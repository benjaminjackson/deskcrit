## Basics: common mistakes

- [ ] **Body text is not smaller than the menu and button text.** Paragraph text is set at least as large as the text of the interface around it, such as menu links and button labels. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Paragraphs are not set in a decorative face.** Paragraphs are set in a face made for reading. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Text does not read as faint.** Text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background, or 3:1 for large text. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Paragraphs are not centered or right-aligned.** Paragraphs of several lines are set flush left. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Content keeps clear of the page edge.** Text and boxes sit inside the type area, with a margin between them and the edge of the page. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Captions and labels are not too small to read.** Captions, labels, and legal text stay large enough to read. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Long text is not set in all capitals.** Capitals suit headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Text is broken into chunks.** Text that people skim is broken up by headings, short paragraphs, and lists. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Boxes do not split or join groups.** Items inside one shared boundary, such as a card, a panel, a tint, or a border, read as a group. → `references/basics.md`
- [ ] **Secondary actions are not styled as strongly as the primary one.** A view has one high-emphasis action. → `references/basics.md`

## Layout, space, grid, hierarchy

- [ ] **One grid governs the page.** Every block takes its edges from one underlying grid of columns, margins, and intervals, so the same column lines recur from the top of the page to the bottom. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Equal intervals between columns.** The space between columns, cards, or grid fields is one fixed interval across the page. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Pictures fit the grid.** An image takes its width from the columns and its top and bottom from the lines of text, so pictures and text share the same edges. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Break the grid decisively or not at all.** An element that leaves the grid does so by a clear amount, so that the break reads as a choice. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Space shows the groups.** The gap between items in one group is clearly smaller than the gap between groups, and a label sits nearer its own field than the one before it. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Unrelated elements do not crowd each other.** Elements that do not belong together keep clear space between them. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **White space is gathered, not scattered.** Ruder holds that the printed and the unprinted areas must stand in tension, and that the tension comes from contrast. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Figure and ground are unambiguous.** Text and key shapes separate clearly from what lies behind them. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Balance.** Visual weight comes from size, darkness, color, and isolation, and where an element sits changes its weight. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **One element dominates.** The order of importance is plain: one element is seen first and the rest follow. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Contrast of scale gives the page tension.** Considered layouts set strong contrasts against each other: one very large element against small ones, a vertical against horizontals, dense against open. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **No central axis by default.** Tschichold rejects the habit of centering everything on one axis, because it fixes the form before the content is read and makes the layout rigid. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Repeated elements follow one program.** Gerstner treats design as a program: a set of rules from which each solution is chosen. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **No rules, boxes, or ornament where space would do.** Lines, borders, and decoration that carry no information compete with the content. → `references/layout.md`
- [ ] **Optical alignment over mathematical alignment.** Round and pointed shapes overshoot, and icons center by visual mass rather than by bounding box, so that they look aligned. → `references/layout.md`

## Typography

- [ ] **Line length (measure).** A single column of paragraph text must not run so wide that the eye loses its place on the way back to the start of the next line. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Leading suits the measure.** Line spacing is chosen for the typeface, the text, and the measure together, and the longer the line, the more space it needs between lines. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Heading lines hold together.** A heading of two or more lines needs tighter line spacing than text, so that it reads as one unit. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Typefaces in concord or in clear contrast, never in conflict.** Type elements relate either by concord, which keeps to one family and varies it little, or by contrast, which makes them plainly different. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Weak contrast is worse than none.** When two elements are meant to differ, the difference must be obvious at a glance in at least one of Dair's seven kinds of contrast: size, weight, structure, form, color, direction, and texture. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Contrast from more than size.** Average work builds every level of the hierarchy from size alone, so headings grow very large while their weight, form, and color stay the same as the text. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Sizes come from a scale.** Text sizes come from a small, deliberate scale, so that each size reads as its own level. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **One change at a time for emphasis.** Emphasis inside text changes one thing: italic, or bold, or small capitals. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Even word spacing in justified text.** Justified lines keep their word spaces even. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Rivers of white space.** Seen from a distance, a block of text shows no channels of white that run down or across several lines. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Rag quality in ragged-right text.** The right edge of ragged text is gently uneven. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Widows, orphans, and runts.** A paragraph does not end with one short word alone on its last line, and a column does not begin with the last line of a paragraph. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Paragraph marks: indent or space, not both.** Paragraphs are marked either by a first-line indent or by space between them, never both. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Headings sit with the text they introduce.** A heading sits closer to the text it introduces than to the text before it. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Lines of text align across columns.** In a considered grid the lines of text in neighboring columns sit on the same baselines, and the top and bottom of each picture line up with lines of text. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Hanging bullets and punctuation.** The left edge of a text block stays crisp. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Display type is spaced tighter than text.** As type gets larger, the space between its letters and words looks wider, so headlines need tighter letter and word spacing than text. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Weight and stroke thickness at small sizes.** Hairline and very light weights are for large sizes. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Letterspacing (tracking) of text.** Lower-case text keeps the spacing its typeface was designed with. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Letterspace capitals and small caps.** Strings of capitals and small capitals are letterspaced, and lower case is not. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Kerning.** At display sizes, the space between letter pairs looks even: no gaps in pairs such as "AV", "To", and "Wa", and no collisions. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Ligatures.** In a face whose f reaches into the next letter, the f joins the following i or l as a ligature. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Real italics, bold, and small caps (no faux styles).** True italic and bold cuts of a family are used. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Typographic details: quotes, dashes, and spaces.** Curly quotation marks and apostrophes, true en and em dashes, ellipsis characters, and one space after a sentence. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Figures in tables and text.** Columns of numbers use tabular figures aligned on the decimal point or the right edge, and running text uses text figures. → `references/typography.md`
- [ ] **Line breaks in headings and short text.** Headings, buttons, and captions break at natural phrase boundaries and do not split a word, a number and its unit, or a name across lines. → `references/typography.md`

## Color

- [ ] **Body text contrast (WCAG AA).** Normal text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Non-text contrast.** UI component boundaries and states (input borders, icons that carry meaning, focus rings) and meaningful chart graphics are at least 3:1 against adjacent colors. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Do not use color alone to carry meaning.** Any information shown by color (error state, chart series, link, required field) has a second cue such as text, icon, pattern, underline, or position. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Links distinguishable from surrounding text.** Inline links that differ from body text only by color need at least 3:1 contrast against the body text color, plus a non-color cue (underline) on hover and focus. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Placeholder, disabled and decorative text exemptions.** Placeholder and helper text still needs 4.5:1; only truly disabled controls and pure decoration are exempt. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Text over images and gradients.** Text on a photo, gradient or textured background keeps its contrast at the worst point behind the text, not the average. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Color-vision deficiency safety.** The design stays readable and its distinctions stay visible when simulated for protanopia, deuteranopia and tritanopia (about 8% of men have red-green deficiency). → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Okabe-Ito palette for categorical data.** When a chart needs distinct categories safe for color-blind readers, use the Okabe-Ito eight (black, orange, sky blue, bluish green, yellow, blue, vermillion, reddish purple), and keep to about 8 or fewer categories. → `references/color.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Limit categorical colors in a chart.** Categorical palettes work for about 5 to 10 hues at most; beyond that, direct labels, small multiples, grouping or highlighting one series and graying the rest are better than more colors. → `references/color.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Sequential data uses one ordered lightness ramp.** Ordered data (low to high) uses a single-hue or multi-hue ramp whose lightness changes monotonically, so lighter means less and darker means more (or the reverse, consistently). → `references/color.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Diverging data uses a neutral midpoint.** Data with a meaningful midpoint (zero, average, target) uses two contrasting hues that darken away from a light neutral center. → `references/color.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Color scales must be perceptually uniform and CVD safe.** Prefer perceptually uniform ramps (viridis, cividis, ColorBrewer) over HSV rainbow ramps, and check that the ramp still orders correctly in grayscale and under color-blind simulation. → `references/color.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Complementary harmony.** A complementary scheme pairs hues about 180 degrees apart on the wheel; use one as dominant and the other as a small accent, and avoid equal-area saturated pairs. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Analogous harmony.** An analogous scheme uses neighboring hues (roughly within 30 to 60 degrees); it reads calm and cohesive but needs lightness or saturation variation, or one contrasting accent, to avoid flatness. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Triadic, split-complementary and tetradic schemes.** Triadic (about 120 degrees apart) and split-complementary (a hue plus the two neighbours of its complement) schemes keep one hue dominant and mute the others in saturation or area. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Monochromatic schemes need value range.** A single-hue design varies lightness and saturation enough to separate its levels (steps at least visibly distinct, text still at 4.5:1) so hierarchy comes from value, not hue. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **60-30-10 proportion.** Distribute color roughly 60% dominant (usually neutral background), 30% secondary, 10% accent. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Accent color restraint.** Use one primary accent for the main action and interactive state; reserve it so that it does not also decorate non-interactive content. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Limited palette.** A piece has a small defined palette: typically one neutral ramp, one or two brand hues, and semantic status colors. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Simultaneous contrast.** The same color looks different on different surrounds; compare colors in context. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Vibrating and clashing combinations.** Avoid fully saturated complementary colors adjacent at similar lightness (red on blue, red on green, magenta on cyan) for text or thin shapes; they vibrate. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Value (lightness) carries structure.** The design should still show its hierarchy and figure/ground in a grayscale render; if two elements merge in grayscale, hue alone is separating them and the pairing is fragile. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Warm and cool temperature.** Warm hues (red, orange, yellow) advance and draw attention; cool hues (blue, green) recede. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Saturation hierarchy.** Saturated color marks what matters; surrounding content uses desaturated or neutral tones. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Semantic color conventions.** Red marks errors and danger, green marks success, amber/yellow marks warning, blue marks information. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Cultural meaning of color.** Color meaning varies by audience (white for mourning in parts of East Asia, red for luck in China but danger in the West, red/green finance conventions reversed in China and Japan). → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Color consistency across a system.** The same role uses the same color everywhere (all primary buttons, all links, all error states). → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Dark mode conventions.** Dark themes use dark gray (not pure #000 with pure #FFF text), lighter and slightly desaturated accents, and express elevation by lighter surfaces rather than shadows. → `references/color.md`
- [ ] **Color for state: hover, focus, disabled.** Interactive states differ visibly from the default (focus indicator at least 3:1 against adjacent colors) and disabled states are lower in emphasis without relying on hue alone. → `references/color.md`

## Art direction and composition

- [ ] **One focal point.** The image has one clearly dominant element that the eye reaches first; if two or more elements compete at equal weight, flag it. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Clear eye path.** The eye can travel from the focal point through secondary elements to the end (for example headline, image, call to action) without hitting dead ends or jumping back. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Emphasis through contrast, not volume.** The emphasized element is set apart by a clear difference in size, value, color, or isolation. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Visual hierarchy has distinct levels.** There are two to four clearly different levels of importance, and each level is distinguishable from the next at a glance. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Balance (symmetrical or asymmetrical).** The visual weight of the composition feels balanced around its center, either by mirroring or by offsetting a large light element with a small heavy one. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Symmetry is chosen, and static by default.** Strict symmetry is used only when stability, formality, or calm is the intended mood. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Contrast of value.** The image uses a clear range of light and dark; the key shapes read in a squinted, grayscale view. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Limited, purposeful palette.** The palette uses a small set of related hues (commonly one dominant, one or two supporting, one accent), and each color has a job. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Color mood matches the message.** The color temperature, saturation, and value support the intended emotion (calm, urgent, playful, serious) and do not contradict the subject. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Dominant scale contrast.** At least one element is dramatically larger or smaller than the rest to create drama and focus. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Unity: parts belong to one whole.** Every element shares at least one visible trait (color, shape language, type, line quality, spacing) with the others so the image reads as one piece, not a collage of unrelated parts. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Variety to avoid monotony.** Within a unified system, there is enough variation in size, shape, value, or spacing to keep interest. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Repetition and rhythm are regular enough to read.** Repeated elements have consistent spacing or a clear progression, and a deliberate break in the pattern marks the point of emphasis. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Proximity groups related items.** Items that belong together sit closer to each other than to unrelated items, and the gaps between groups are clearly larger than gaps within groups. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Alignment: every element has a visible relationship.** Each element aligns to an edge, axis, or grid line shared with another element. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Use a grid.** The layout follows a consistent underlying grid (columns, margins, baseline), so margins, gutters, and module sizes repeat. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Negative space is deliberate.** Empty space is shaped and sized on purpose, gives the subject room, and is not just leftover. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Figure-ground is unambiguous.** The viewer can immediately tell the subject (figure) from the background (ground). → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Rule of thirds and off-center subject placement.** The main subject sits near a third line or intersection, or the image has a clear reason for centering it. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Golden ratio and proportional division (use sparingly).** Major divisions of the frame follow a consistent proportional logic (golden section, root rectangles, simple ratios) rather than arbitrary splits. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Leading lines and directional movement.** Lines, edges, gazes, and pointing shapes lead toward the focal point rather than out of the frame. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Shape language carries emotion.** Shapes support the intended feeling (circles soft and safe, squares stable, triangles and diagonals dynamic or threatening), and the overall shape language fits the message. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Tension and dynamism from diagonals and asymmetry.** If the message is energetic or dramatic, the composition uses diagonals, off-balance placement, or tension between elements. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Line quality and weight are consistent.** Strokes in a set of icons or illustrations share one weight, one cap and join style, and one corner radius. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Consistent level of detail across a set.** All illustrations or icons in the image share one level of detail and abstraction (all simple glyphs, or all rendered scenes), not a mix. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **One perspective and one light source.** All objects in a scene share one viewpoint, horizon, and perspective system, and shadows and highlights agree on a single light direction. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Consistent rendering style across assets.** All imagery shares one treatment (flat vector, line art, photo, 3D render, texture), or mixes them by an explicit system (for example photo cut-outs on flat color). → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Photos treated consistently.** Photos in one design share lighting mood, color grade, crop style, and subject distance. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Cropping and framing are intentional.** Crops do not cut off heads, hands, or limbs at joints by accident, and tangents (edges that just touch) are avoided. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Image quality: sharp, undistorted, at native proportions.** Images are sharp at display size (no visible pixelation or blur from upscaling), keep their original aspect ratio (no stretch or squash), and have no compression artifacts. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Texture and pattern with restraint.** Texture or pattern is used on a limited area or at low contrast so it never competes with the focal point or reduces legibility of text over it. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Every ornament does a job.** Every ornament, flourish, gradient, shadow, or effect serves meaning, hierarchy, or mood. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Less, but better (reduction).** The design says its message with as few elements, colors, and typefaces as possible; each remaining element is refined rather than cluttered. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Honesty: no fake depth or false promises.** The design does not fake material, depth, or quality it does not have (heavy bevels, fake textures that suggest a different medium, claims not supported by the image). → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **One idea per piece (concept).** The piece communicates a single big idea that can be stated in one sentence, and each visual element supports it. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Visual and headline work together.** Image and headline are two halves of one idea: neither repeats the other literally, and neither makes sense alone only when it should not. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Thematic coherence: one mood, one era.** Every element (type, color, imagery, texture, props) fits one mood and one period or world. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Poster: one message, readable at distance.** A poster carries one main message that is readable from several meters away (thumbnail test), with secondary details clearly smaller and grouped. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Brand consistency: voice, mark, and clear space.** The logo has clear space around it, is not distorted, recolored off-palette, or crowded by other elements; colors, type, and imagery style match the brand's other pieces. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Simplicity and wit in marks and symbols.** A logo or symbol is simple enough to be recognized small and in one color, and is memorable through one clear idea. → `references/art-direction.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Gestalt grouping: similarity, continuity, closure.** Elements that look alike are read as a group; the design uses similarity, continuity, and closure on purpose, and does not create accidental groupings or shapes between unrelated items. → `references/art-direction.md`
- [ ] **Foundation exercise: the elements and principles check.** The piece makes deliberate use of the basic elements: point, line, shape, form, space, texture, value, color. → `references/art-direction.md`

## Interface and interaction

- [ ] **Nielsen 1: Visibility of system status.** The design shows the current state (selected tab, active step, progress, saved or unsaved, loading) with a visible indicator. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 2: Match between system and the real world.** Labels, icons, and metaphors use the audience's own words and familiar real-world conventions, not internal jargon or system terms. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 3: User control and freedom.** Every state that can be entered has a visible way out: close, cancel, back, undo, or clear. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 4: Consistency and standards.** The same action, term, color, and component style means the same thing everywhere on the screen or set, and follows platform and industry conventions (logo top-left links home, cart icon top-right). → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 5: Error prevention.** The design removes error-prone conditions before they happen: constraints on inputs (date pickers, input types, masks), sensible defaults, and a confirmation step before irreversible actions. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 6: Recognition rather than recall.** Options, actions, and context are visible on screen. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 7: Flexibility and efficiency of use.** Frequent actions have shortcuts or accelerators (search, filters, keyboard hints, saved or recent items) that do not get in the way of novices. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 8: Aesthetic and minimalist design.** Every element on the screen supports the user's main task. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 9: Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors.** Error messages are in plain language, say what went wrong, say how to fix it, and appear next to the problem field with a visible non-color cue (icon, text), never a bare code. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Nielsen 10: Help and documentation.** Where the task is complex, help is findable and contextual (tooltip, hint text, link near the field) and short and task-focused. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Norman: Affordances.** Interactive elements look like what they do: buttons look pressable (fill, border, shadow), fields look typeable, and non-interactive elements do not look interactive. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Norman: Signifiers.** Clear perceivable cues tell the user where to act and how: underlined or colored links, chevrons on expandable rows, drag handles, labels on icons. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Norman: Feedback.** Each control has distinct visible states for hover, focus, pressed, disabled, and selected. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Norman: Natural mapping.** The spatial layout of controls matches the layout of what they control: a label sits next to its field, a toggle next to its setting, a control near the thing it changes. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Norman: Constraints.** The design limits possible actions to valid ones: unavailable options are disabled or hidden, inputs accept only the right type, and only one path is obvious at each step. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Norman: Conceptual model.** The screen makes the system's structure clear so a first-time user can form a correct mental model: grouping, naming, and navigation show what the product is and how parts relate. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Shneiderman: Enable frequent users to use shortcuts.** Power-user paths are offered: keyboard shortcut hints, bulk actions, defaults that carry forward. → `references/ux.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Shneiderman: Design dialogs to yield closure.** A multi-step task has clear start, middle, and end: step indicator, final confirmation screen, and a stated next step after success. → `references/ux.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Tognazzini: Anticipation.** The design puts what the user is likely to need next in front of them (prefilled defaults, relevant next action, needed information) instead of making them hunt for it. → `references/ux.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Krug: Don't make me think (self-evident and scannable).** Each page is self-evident or at least self-explanatory. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Jakob's law.** Common patterns are used where users already know them (nav at top or side, search with a magnifier icon, cart top right, standard form controls). → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Cognitive load.** The screen asks for one main thing at a time; extraneous elements (decoration, unrelated links, dense text) that add load without helping the task are cut. → `references/ux.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Progressive disclosure.** Advanced or rare options are hidden behind an obvious control (accordion, "More options," details) and primary content is shown first. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Primary versus secondary action.** One primary action per view is visually dominant (filled, high contrast); secondary actions are lower emphasis (outline or text); cancel and tertiary actions are weakest. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Buttons and links must look clickable.** Links are distinguishable from body text by more than color alone (underline or other cue); buttons have a shape, fill, or border. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Destructive action confirmation and safety.** Destructive actions (delete, remove, reset) are visually separated from safe ones, labeled with the specific outcome ("Delete project"), styled as destructive (often red), not the default focus, and followed by a confirmation or undo. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Empty states.** An empty list or dashboard explains why it is empty and offers the next action (a button or example) instead of a blank area. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Loading states.** Content that takes time shows a skeleton screen, spinner, or progress bar in the same place, with layout stable (no layout jump). → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Error states.** The error state is visible, near the cause, in plain words, with a fix, preserves what the user typed, and does not rely on color alone. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Forms: labels above fields and never placeholder-only.** Each field has a persistent visible label, placed above (or very close to) the field; the placeholder is not used as the only label. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Forms: single column layout.** Forms are one column with a clear top-to-bottom order; multiple fields per row only for short, tightly related pairs (city and zip, month and year). → `references/ux.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Forms: inline validation and required markers.** Fields validate when the user leaves them (not on every keystroke, not only at submit) with a visible result by the field; required and optional fields are marked, and optional fields are kept few. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Redundancy and duplicate controls.** The same action is not offered twice on one screen without reason (two "Sign up" buttons beside each other, repeated links in the same block, header and footer copies competing). → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Platform conventions (Apple HIG and Material).** Components, navigation patterns, iconography, and gestures follow the target platform's conventions (tab bar at bottom on iOS, back at top left, system icons for share and delete; Material navigation bar, FAB, app bar). → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Touch target size and spacing.** Touch targets are at least 44x44 pt (iOS) / 48x48 dp (Android) with spacing of about 8 dp between neighbors. → `references/ux.md`
- [ ] **Mobile thumb zones.** On phone-sized layouts, the frequent actions sit in the lower, easy-reach area of the screen (bottom nav, bottom primary button) and not in the top corners. → `references/ux.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Dark patterns to avoid.** The design does not use deceptive patterns. → `references/ux.md` _(off-list source)_

## Accessibility

- [ ] **Text contrast (minimum).** Body text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background; large text (at least 24 CSS px, or 18.66 CSS px bold) needs at least 3:1. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Non-text contrast.** Visual boundaries needed to identify a UI component need at least 3:1 against adjacent colors: an input border, a checkbox, a button edge when there is no other cue. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Reflow.** At a 320 CSS px wide viewport (400% zoom of 1280 px) content reflows to one column with no horizontal scrolling and no loss of information. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Images of text.** Text is real text, not a picture of text, unless the exact look is essential (logotype). → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Use of color.** Color is not the only way to show information: links inside body text, errors, required fields, chart series, and states need a second cue (underline, icon, label, pattern). → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Sensory characteristics.** Instructions do not rely only on shape, size, position, sound, or color (for example, 'click the green button on the right' needs a name or label too). → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Target size (minimum).** Pointer targets are at least 24 by 24 CSS px, or smaller targets have enough spacing that a 24 px diameter circle centered on each does not overlap another target or its circle. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Focus visible.** Every keyboard-focusable element shows a visible focus indicator when focused. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Focus not obscured (minimum).** A focused element is not entirely hidden by author-created content such as sticky headers, cookie banners, or chat widgets. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Focus appearance.** The focus indicator encloses the component or is at least 2 CSS px thick around its perimeter, and changes by at least 3:1 between focused and unfocused states. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Orientation.** Content works in both portrait and landscape unless one orientation is essential (piano app, bank-check deposit). → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Headings and labels.** Headings and form labels describe the topic or purpose of their section or field. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Labels or instructions.** Every input has a visible label or instruction that stays visible while typing. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Link purpose (in context).** Each link's purpose can be told from its text or its surrounding sentence, paragraph, or list item. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Consistent navigation.** Navigation links repeated on multiple pages appear in the same relative order each time. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Consistent identification.** The same function has the same label and icon across pages (a search icon is always called 'Search', not 'Find' on another page). → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Consistent help.** Help links, contact details, or chat appear in the same relative place across pages. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Error identification.** An input error is identified in text, naming the field in error, not only by red color or an icon. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Error suggestion.** When an error is detected and a fix is known, the message says how to correct it ('Enter a date like 03/21/2026'), not just 'Invalid input'. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Apple touch target size.** Interactive controls are at least 44 by 44 points on iOS and iPadOS, with space between them. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Material touch target size.** Touch targets are at least 48 by 48 dp, with at least 8 dp between targets. → `references/accessibility.md`
- [ ] **Dyslexia-friendly typography.** Use a common sans-serif such as Arial, Verdana, or Calibri at 12 to 14 pt (about 16 to 19 px) or larger. → `references/accessibility.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Dyslexia-friendly line length, spacing, and background.** Line spacing of about 1.5, with extra space around headings and between paragraphs. → `references/accessibility.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Low-vision large-print guidance.** For print and posters aimed at low-vision readers, RNIB See it Right advises at least 12 pt, and 14 pt or more for wider reach. → `references/accessibility.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Text alternatives for charts and posters (information not conveyed in image alone).** Key meaning in a chart, infographic, or poster is also stated in text near it (title, summary, data table, caption) rather than only in the picture. → `references/accessibility.md`

## Charts and data

- [ ] **Maximize the data-ink ratio.** Most of the ink in a chart encodes data; heavy gridlines, borders, fills, and decoration that carry no information are removed or muted. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **No chartjunk.** Charts have no ornamental moire patterns, heavy textures, clip art, 3D effects, drop shadows, or decorative backgrounds that compete with the data. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Graphical integrity: keep the lie factor near 1.** The visual size of an effect matches the size of the effect in the data. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Bar charts start at zero.** Bar and area charts have a value axis that begins at zero because bar length encodes value; truncated bar axes are flagged. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Do not distort with 3D.** No 3D bars, pies, or ribbons; extra depth dimension without extra data makes values hard to read and distorts size. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Rank encodings by perceptual accuracy.** The most important comparison uses the most accurate encoding: position on a common scale, then length, then angle or slope, then area, then volume, then color saturation or hue. → `references/dataviz.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Choose chart type by data relationship.** The chart type fits the question. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Use pie charts sparingly.** Pies are only for a part-to-whole of about five or fewer slices where exact comparison is not needed; pies with many slices, similar sizes, or several pies to compare are flagged. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Label directly instead of using a legend.** Series, bars, and lines are labeled at the data itself; if a legend is needed the reader should not have to look back and forth to decode colors. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Use color type that matches the data.** Ordered values use a sequential (single-hue lightness) palette, values with a meaningful midpoint use a diverging palette, and unordered categories use a qualitative palette of distinct hues, limited to about 5 to 7 categories. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Do not rely on color alone and avoid red-green pairs.** Meaning conveyed by color is also carried by label, shape, or position, and palettes are distinguishable under common color-vision deficiency; contrast of marks against background is at least 3:1. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Use color with restraint and emphasis.** Neutral gray carries context and one accent color highlights the point; a rainbow of colors without meaning is flagged. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Titles and annotations state the takeaway.** The chart title or a caption states the finding ("Sales fell 20% after March"), not only the topic ("Sales by month"), and key points are annotated on the chart. → `references/dataviz.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Label axes, units, and sources.** Every chart has labeled axes (or direct labels) with units, a readable scale, and a data source or date where relevant; the reader should never need to guess what a number measures. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Keep scales honest and consistent.** Axes are linear unless clearly marked as log, tick intervals are even, no broken axes without a marker, and charts meant for comparison share the same scale. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Use small multiples for comparison across many series.** Instead of one chart with many overlapping lines or categories, repeat the same small chart with identical scales and layout so differences are visible at a glance. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Use sparklines for compact trend context.** Small word-sized trend lines without axes can sit beside numbers in tables and dashboards to show shape and change; they are not used when exact values are needed. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Sort and order data meaningfully.** Categorical bars are sorted by value (or a natural order such as time, size, or age), not alphabetically or randomly, unless the reader must look up named items. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Dashboards fit on one screen and are glanceable.** A dashboard shows its most important metrics on one screen without scrolling, in a prioritized layout, with the key figures in the top left or the most prominent area. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Give metrics context and comparison.** A KPI number is shown with a comparison (target, previous period, trend) so the reader can tell good from bad; a bare big number is flagged. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Group related items by proximity, enclosure, and similarity.** Related marks or panels sit together with space between unrelated groups; the Gestalt rules (proximity, similarity, enclosure, connection, continuity) make the intended groups, and only those, look grouped. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Choose visual variables to fit the data type.** Position and size suit quantitative data, hue and shape suit categories, lightness and size suit ordered data; do not encode a category with size or a quantity with shape. → `references/dataviz.md` _(off-list source)_
- [ ] **Chart vs table: use tables for lookup of exact values.** When readers need precise values or many different units, use a table; when they need patterns, trends, or comparisons, use a chart. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Show uncertainty and sample context.** Estimates, forecasts, and samples are shown with ranges, error bars, or notes about sample size, and projections look different from observed data. → `references/dataviz.md`
- [ ] **Do not truncate or cherry-pick the time range or sample.** Time ranges and subsets show enough context to avoid misleading trends; a chart that begins or ends exactly where it makes the claim look strongest is flagged for review. → `references/dataviz.md` _(off-list source)_

## Unchecked candidates

From the reading list but not yet checked against the books. Use as prompts to look, not as rules to cite.

- [ ] Color: Albers, *Interaction of Color* (1963). The same color looks different on different grounds, and two colors meant to match can look different next to each other. A visible question asks whether one color, used for one role, looks like two different colors in two places.
- [ ] Color: Albers, *Interaction of Color*. Two strong colors of equal lightness side by side make their edge seem to vibrate.
- [ ] Charts: Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, chapter 5. Moiré hatching, heavy grid lines, and three-dimensional effects in a chart are chartjunk.
- [ ] Charts: Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, chapter 4. Ink that can be erased without loss of data, such as a background fill or a frame, is removed.
- [ ] Composition: Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, chapter IX "Dynamics". Oblique lines and shapes create directed tension, and a layout that is all horizontals and verticals reads as static.
- [ ] Composition: Hofmann, *Graphic Design Manual*, chapter "The dot". A single element placed exactly at the center of a field reads as still, and one placed off center gives the field movement.
- [ ] Systems: Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*. Pieces of one series (slides, posters, screens) follow one program of grid, type, and color.
