**First read.** On desktop the eye goes first to the rust "Sign in" button in the top right corner, because it is the only saturated color on the page. Then it drops to the large headline and moves across to the "Reorder this week" table, which shows quickly that this is a stock tool for bookshops. The "Start a 30-day trial" button barely registers. The page feels calm, warm and plain-spoken, like a good shop counter. On the phone the eye lands on a headline squeezed to one or two words per line, next to a table that runs off the right edge. On widescreen the headline and the table drift apart, with a wide empty gap between them.

**What it's for.** A landing page that gets independent bookshop owners to start a Shelfmark trial. It is a responsive web page.

**Verdict.** On desktop the page nearly does its job. The headline and the live-looking reorder table show the value in seconds, but the strongest call to action is "Sign in", which is for people who already bought. On phone and tablet the layout never stacks into one column, so the page breaks at exactly the widths where an owner might first see it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The layout does not reflow on phone or tablet (defect).** On the phone the page is 789 px wide inside a 390 px viewport, so it scrolls sideways (`page_width`, phone). The desktop two-column hero is kept. The headline is pushed into a 181 px column at 7 lines of about 7 characters each (`characters_per_line` 6.8, `h1`). The paragraph below it runs at 20 characters a line. The three feature lines at the bottom are 58 to 78 px wide, at 5 to 9 characters a line, so each one turns into a tall stack of single words with a staircase right edge. The buttons break as "Start a / 30- / day / trial" and "See / how it / works". The nav breaks as "Shops / using / it" and "Sign / in", and the header rule stops at 390 px. The table starts halfway across and runs past the edge of the screen. On the tablet the same layout overflows by 21 px (789 in 768): the table is clipped at the right, and the hero text is still crammed into a 181 px column while the table takes the rest. The owner has to pan sideways and read a column of single words, and many will leave before they reach the table that makes the case. WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.10 (Reflow). Fix: below a breakpoint, stack to one column. Put the headline and paragraph at full width, then the buttons on one line each, then the table at full width, then the three features one under another. Let the nav collapse or wrap as whole items. Viewports: phone, tablet
2. **"Sign in" outranks the trial, so the page's strongest call to action is for existing customers (defect).** "Sign in" is the only filled, saturated button: white on rust, weight 500, contrast 7.69 (`a.nav-signin`). "Start a 30-day trial" is gray-brown text at weight 400 on a beige fill (`#5c544b` on `#f3eee6`, `a.button.button-primary`). It uses the same text color, size and weight as the outlined "See how it works" beside it, so the two hero buttons read as near-equals. A new owner's eye goes to the button that is not for them, and the action you want takes a second look to find. Material Design 3, "Buttons": a view has one high-emphasis action, and other actions use lower-emphasis styles. Fix: give the trial button the filled rust treatment, keep "See how it works" outlined, and drop "Sign in" to a plain nav link or a quiet outline. Viewports: all
3. **The ask leaves the owner to guess what the trial commits them to, and gives them nothing to compare against (defect).** "Start a 30-day trial" does not say whether the 30 days are free, whether a card is needed, or what happens on day 31. An owner of a small shop with thin margins could read it as "free for a month" or as "the first paid month", and the wrong reading is the one that stops them clicking. The page also gives no way to judge the value on terms the owner already trusts. Nothing sets Shelfmark against how they track stock now, such as their till's own report, a spreadsheet, or a stock book. Below the hero there is nothing to back the claim: the nav promises "Pricing" and "Shops using it", but the page ends after three one-line features. Fix: state the trial terms on the button or in one line under it. Add a comparison with the owner's current way of knowing what to reorder, and bring the price or a named shop onto the page, or at least near the trial button. Only the maker has those facts. Viewports: all
4. **On widescreen the hero splits into two islands (defect).** The text block keeps its 540 px desktop width at the left. The table is pinned to the right margin at x 1377, which leaves about 770 px of empty cream between them. The table's left edge lines up with nothing below it. The feature lines stretch to 79 to 81 characters (`characters_per_line`, `section.facts > p`), past the comfortable 45 to 75 (Bringhurst 2.1.2). The left side is heavy with text, the right side is a small box on its own, and the eye has to cross the screen to connect the headline to its proof (Arnheim, "Balance"; Ruder on gathered space). Fix: cap the content width so the table sits beside the text as it does on desktop, and keep the feature columns on the same column lines as the hero. Viewports: widescreen

**Smaller things.**
- The table's column headers and the "Mon 9 June" date are 14 px at 3.82:1 (`thead > tr > th`, `span.preview-date`), under the 4.5:1 that WCAG 1.4.3 asks for. Darken that gray. Viewports: all
- There are three sizes 1 px apart: 16 px for the hero paragraph and nav, 15 px for the buttons and feature lines, and 14 px for the table (`type_sizes`). Side by side they read as small mistakes, not as levels (Bringhurst 3.1.1). Keep two of them, and make finer distinctions with weight or color. Viewports: all
- The headline ends with "asks" alone on its third line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Break it so "a customer asks" stays together. Viewports: desktop, widescreen
- The "Reorder this week" table shows copies on the shelf and copies sold, but not how many to order. The owner has to work out the order themselves, which is the job the subhead says Shelfmark does for them. If the product suggests a quantity, show it. If not, the table is fine as it is. This is common sense, not a published rule. Viewports: all

**What's working.**
- The table works as the main picture. It shows real-looking output with believable book titles and a Monday date, so an owner sees the product doing their job instead of a stock illustration. Keep it, and keep it beside the headline.
- The copy is specific. "Know what is on your shelves before a customer asks" and "tells you which titles to reorder on Monday morning" talk about the owner's own moment and routine, not about "inventory management".
- The restraint suits the audience. A warm cream ground, one rust accent, one typeface, and three features led by a bold phrase instead of emoji cards. Nothing looks like a template, and it feels like it belongs to a bookshop.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let rust mean "this needs you."** Finding 2 already moves the filled rust treatment from "Sign in" to the trial button. Go one step further and make rust the page's one signal for action. Use it on the trial button and on the "On shelf" cells in the "Reorder this week" table that read 0 ("A Year of Hedges", "The Glass Orchard"), and nowhere else. Right now the table shows the gap between copies sold and copies on the shelf only as plain numbers, so the owner has to scan for it. If those two zeros were in the same color as the trial button, the owner would see a lost sale on the shelf and the way to stop it in one glance. That serves the job of the page, which is to get an owner to start the trial. Taste: Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, puts emphasis on the data that carries the message. Albers, *Interaction of Color*, shows that a single repeated color makes a viewer read the things that carry it as one group.
2. **Set the book titles like book titles.** "The Salt Road", "Small Engines" and the rest are in the same roman as the column headers and the numbers. Bookshop owners spend their working day with titles set in italic in catalogs, distributor lists and reviews. Setting the Title column in italic would make the table read as their own stock list, not as a generic spreadsheet. It also builds on what "What's working" credits: a page that feels like it belongs to a bookshop, which earns trust from an owner who expects software to be made for someone else. Taste: Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, gives italic as the convention for the titles of books.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "Pricing" and "Shops using it" go to other pages or point at sections that do not exist. Finding 3 depends on what is behind them.
- How the layout behaves at widths between 390 and 768, or between 1280 and 1920.
