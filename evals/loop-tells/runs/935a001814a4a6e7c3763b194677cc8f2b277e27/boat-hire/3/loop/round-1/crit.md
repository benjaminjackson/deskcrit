**First read.** The eye lands first on the dark intro paragraph, not the headline. The headline above it is a pale gray line only a touch larger, so it reads like a caption. Next comes the filled green "See boats and dates" button, then the white form on the right with its outlined "Search" button. It reads as a quiet, plain booking page for a canal boatyard. It feels calm but empty: no boat, no water, no price, and a lot of bare background. On the phone the page breaks: the two columns never stack, the nav wraps into a column beside the logo, the intro runs two or three words per line, and the form is pushed off the right edge of the screen.

**What it's for.** The home page of a narrowboat hire site, where people book a boat directly with the boatyard (as told to me).

**Verdict.** On desktop and tablet it works as a plain search page, but nothing on it leads, and it hides its one real selling point. On a phone it fails outright, because most of the booking form sits off the screen. Fix the phone layout first.

**What would move it most**
1. **The phone layout does not reflow, so the booking form is mostly off the screen.** The page is 587 px wide in a 390 px viewport (facts file, phone `notes`: "scrolls sideways"). The form starts at x 270 (`form.search` labels) and the Search button runs to x 564, so a phone visitor sees only a sliver of the form and must scroll sideways to book. The hero text is squeezed into a 119 px column: the intro runs 18 lines at 9 characters per line (`p.lead`, `characters_per_line` 9.1), the headline breaks into five lines, and "See boats and dates" wraps to two lines. The nav stacks into five lines beside the logo, and "Your booking" splits across two. This is a defect: the phone is where many people browse holidays, and here they cannot see the thing they came to use (WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow). Direction: below tablet width, stack everything into one column, with headline, intro, form, then routes. Give the nav a phone treatment that fits on one row or folds away. Viewports: phone
2. **Nothing leads, because the headline is styled as the weakest text in the hero.** The h1 is 22 px, weight 400, in gray `#6b7574`. The lead under it is 20 px in near-black `#26302f`, and the nav is also 20 px (`font_size`, `h1`, `p.lead`, `nav.site-nav`). The darker paragraph wins, and the logo at 24 px competes too. That gives three sizes within 4 px of each other (24, 22, 20) and two more at 16 and 14: five sizes where three would do, and the top three read as one level (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). The h1 also differs from the body only by being lighter, which is the wrong direction for a heading (Dair, chapters 8 and 9). The visitor gets no clear first line to read, so the offer takes a second pass to find. This is a defect, plus a convention break. Direction: make the headline clearly the first thing seen, with a real step up in size and dark color, perhaps a heavier weight or the serif from the logo. Bring the nav down toward 16 px so it stops matching the lead (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). Viewports: all
3. **Two buttons do the same job, and the stronger style is on the weaker one.** "See boats and dates" (filled, `a.button`) and the form's "Search" (outlined, `button`) both lead to available boats. The form already holds the visitor's base, date, nights, and berths, so Search is the action that books. Yet the generic link gets the filled primary style, and the real action looks secondary. The visitor has to stop and work out which one to press, and whether the left button ignores what they set in the form. This is a defect (Material Design, emphasis levels; Nielsen heuristic 8 on duplicate controls). Direction: keep one action. Make the form's Search the filled primary, and remove the hero button or give it a different job. Viewports: all
4. **The copy buries the reason to book here, and the headline contradicts the routes.** "Hire a narrowboat for a week on the canal" could sit on any hire site. The real difference is the last sentence of the intro: "book it here with the boatyard that keeps it. No agency in the middle, and no fee on top of the hire." A visitor who skims stops before they reach it. "For a week" also clashes with the route list just below, which offers 4 nights and 10 nights. And "no fee on top of the hire" gives the visitor nothing to measure it against, because the page shows no hire price anywhere. The visitor cannot see the value for themselves; they must take it on trust. This is a defect. Direction: move the direct-booking point up into the headline or the line straight under it, and drop or loosen "for a week". Show the kind of fact that proves the claim, such as a from-price per week. That figure has to come from the boatyard. Viewports: all
5. **On wide screens the page splits to the edges and leaves a hole in the middle.** At 1920 px the text column stops at x 664 and the form starts at x 1517, so the eye has to cross about 850 px of nothing to get from the pitch to the form. At 1280 px there is a dead band of about 270 px under the hero button, above "Routes from Hartwell Junction". Nothing ties the two sides together, and the form looks like a sidebar instead of the point of the page. This is a defect for the eye path and balance (Arnheim, on balance; Ruder, on gathered space). Direction: put the layout in a max-width container so the text and the form sit as one group, and close the gap above the routes. Viewports: desktop, widescreen
6. **A holiday page with no picture of the holiday.** This is taste, but I would argue it hard. People hire a narrowboat for the boat and the water, and this page shows neither. The design is plain enough to carry a picture well. Direction: add a real photo of the boatyard's own boats or a stretch of a route, and avoid stock pictures. Viewports: all

**Smaller things.**
- The h1 and the gray route details ("7 nights, 38 locks") measure 4.46:1, just under 4.5:1 (`violations`). Darken the gray slightly; the h1 fix in item 2 covers the heading. Viewports: all
- The form field borders look pale gray on white, roughly 1.5:1 by eye (not measured), and on the start-date field the border is the only edge. Darken them to at least 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Viewports: all
- On the tablet the intro paragraph runs 24 characters per line (`p.lead`), far under 45, and ends with "hire." alone on the last line. Widen the text column or narrow the gap to the form. Viewports: tablet
- On the phone the route rows break a number from its unit ("12 / locks", "61 / locks"). Join each number and unit with a non-breaking space, or stack the details under the route name. Viewports: phone
- "Sat 14 June" looks like a free-text field, not a date picker. If it takes typed text, constrain it to a date input or picker (Nielsen heuristic 5). Viewports: all

**What's working.**
- The form is prefilled with sensible defaults (a base, a Saturday start, 7 nights, 4 people), so a visitor can press Search without typing. Keep that.
- The route list shows nights and locks for each route, which is exactly what a canal hirer weighs. "Routes from Hartwell Junction" follows the form's chosen base, which ties the two together.
- The palette is restrained, with one deep green, an off-white ground, and a serif wordmark. It suits a slow, unhurried holiday and looks like no template.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the route list the core of the page.** The list under "Routes from Hartwell Junction" holds the only facts on the page that are about canal life: nights and locks for each route. Today it sits at the bottom like a footnote, set in small gray type. A stronger version would treat the routes as the main way into a holiday, not an extra under the form. The lock count would be the number that leads each row, because it tells a hirer if the trip is a hard one or a lazy one. That serves a direct booking site well: a boatyard knows its own water, and the route list is where it can show that, which no agency can. This is taste. No approved source makes this exact point, so I argue it on its own terms: the page should lead with the thing only this seller can show.
2. **Builds on finding 2. Let the Slowwater serif carry the whole voice of the page, not only the headline.** Today the serif is only in the wordmark, and the sans does everything else. A stronger version would use the serif for every line a visitor reads as a promise: the headline, the "Routes from…" heading, and the route names. The sans would stay for the working parts, such as labels, fields, and buttons. The name already promises a slow, unhurried trip. Give that promise one typeface and the booking tools another, and the page says "holiday" and "book" in two clear voices, so a visitor can see which part to read and which part to use. This is taste (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, on choosing a face that suits the text and pairing faces with clear roles).
3. **Builds on finding 3. Keep the deep green for the act of booking only.** After finding 3 the green moves to the form's Search button. The stronger step is to keep it off everything else, so that across the site the green always means "this books your boat." The palette is already restrained enough for one color to own one meaning. On a page that exists to take a direct booking, a color that always marks the booking step does the job that a second button would do badly. This is taste (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*, on keeping strong color for the primary action; Albers, *Interaction of Color*, on how one color reads differently in different settings).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the route list updates when the Base field changes, and whether the route rows are meant to be links (they carry no link styling, and the facts file lists them as text, not targets).
