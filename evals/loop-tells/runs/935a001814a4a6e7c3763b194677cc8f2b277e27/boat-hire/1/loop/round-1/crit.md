**First read.** On desktop the eye goes first to the dark green "See boats and dates" button. Next it goes to the top-right nav links, which are set larger and darker than the headline. The headline "Hire a narrowboat for a week on the canal" is pale gray and reads like a subtitle, so the page has no title to anchor it. Then the eye jumps across to the white "Find a boat" card and drops through an empty band to a short list of routes. It reads as a calm, honest, independent boat hire site, but it is closer to a wireframe than a page that sells a week on the water. On the phone the two-column layout never collapses: the text squeezes into a strip about nine characters wide and the page runs off the right edge.

**What it's for.** My assumption: to get someone planning a canal holiday to pick a base, dates, and boat size and start a booking.

**Verdict.** The plain, specific copy and the prefilled search are right, but the page fails on the phone, where most of this audience will plan a holiday. The layout does not reflow, the page scrolls sideways, and the copy becomes a column of single words. On every width, the most important line is the quietest thing on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The phone layout does not reflow.** The page is 587 px wide in a 390 px viewport, so it scrolls sideways (`page_width` 587, viewport note "scrolls sideways"). The search card runs to the right edge. The lead paragraph wraps at about 9 characters per line over 18 lines (`characters_per_line` 9.1, `p.lead`). The h1 breaks into 5 lines of about 8 characters each. The nav stacks into 5 ragged lines beside the logo, and "Your booking" splits. The button wraps to two lines, and the route details wrap and come loose from their names. A visitor on a phone has to pan sideways to reach the form and read the pitch one word at a time. Defect, against WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.10 (Reflow). The fix is to stack everything in one column below a breakpoint: headline, lead, search card, then routes. Give the nav a compact form. Tablet is a milder version of the same problem: the lead runs about 24 characters per line over 8 lines (`characters_per_line` 24.1) because the card takes half the width. Viewports: phone, tablet
2. **The headline is the quietest text on the page.** The h1 is 22 px, weight 400, in gray #6b7574 at 4.46:1 (`font_size`, `font_weight`, `contrast`, `h1`). The nav right above it is 20 px in near-black at 12.75:1, and the lead under it is 20 px near-black. The line that says what the business does reads as a caption to its own paragraph, and the eye goes to the menu and the button instead. Defect. Tschichold asks that the order of importance show through clear differences of size (*The New Typography*), and Dair says the step should also change weight or color, not size alone (*Design with Type*, ch. 8–9). The type scale makes this worse: 24, 22, and 20 px (logo, h1, lead and nav) sit too close to read as different levels (Bringhurst 3.1.1). Make the h1 clearly the largest and darkest text, with a real step above the lead and a heavier weight. Bring the nav down to the 16 px body size so it stops competing. That leaves about three levels: headline, 16 to 20 px text, and 14 px labels. Viewports: all
3. **Two calls to action for one job, in two styles.** "See boats and dates" is a filled green button. "Search" in the card is an outlined button with the same green. Both take the visitor to available boats, so the visitor has to work out which one to use and whether they differ. The real primary action, Search with the base, date, nights, and berths already set, looks secondary. Convention (Material Design 3 and Apple HIG button emphasis: one primary per view). Choose one. The usual way: make Search the filled primary, and either remove the hero button or turn it into a text link that scrolls to the form on small screens. Viewports: all
4. **The page falls apart into three pieces with dead space between them.** On desktop about 270 px of empty space separates the hero from "Routes from Hartwell Junction" (h2 at y 612, button ends at y 338). That gap is far larger than any other on the page, so the routes look like a separate leftover, not part of the plan-your-trip story. On widescreen the card sits pinned to the far right edge (x 1539), with about 850 px of nothing between it and the 600 px text column. The page tips left and top, and the eye has to travel across empty space to reach the form. Defect. Space should show the groups (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II), and a heavy side needs a counterweight (Arnheim, ch. I). Cap the content width so the card stays near the text. Close the gap above the routes so they read as the next step from the selected base. Viewports: desktop, widescreen
5. **No picture of a boat or the canal.** The page sells a week on a narrowboat, and nothing on it shows what that looks like: not the boats, not the water, not the bases. The copy makes a good factual case ("No agency in the middle, and no fee on top of the hire"), but nothing makes the visitor want to go. Taste: for a leisure booking, the picture is a large part of the decision. The fix is real photos of the actual boats or routes set into the hero, not a stock or generic image. Viewports: all

**Smaller things.**
- The h1 and the route details ("7 nights, 38 locks" and the others) are #6b7574 at 4.46:1, just below the 4.5:1 minimum (WCAG 1.4.3). Darken that gray a step. Viewports: all
- The nav does not mark the current page. Add one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline, for the active item (Laubheimer, NN/g menu checklist). Viewports: all
- The input and select borders look pale gray on white. Measure them against the 3:1 minimum for component edges (WCAG 1.4.11). Viewports: all
- "Start date" is a text field showing "Sat 14 June" with no year and no visible picker. Use a date input so visitors cannot type an invalid or unclear date (Nielsen 5). Viewports: all
- On the phone the search, which is the main action, sits at the top right in the part of the page that overflows, out of easy reach. Once the layout stacks, put it straight after the lead. Viewports: phone

**What's working.**
- The copy is specific and plain. "Pick a boat from one of our four bases, choose a route that fits your days, and book it here with the boatyard that keeps it" tells the visitor what happens, and "no fee on top of the hire" gives a real reason to book here. Keep that voice.
- The search card comes prefilled with a base, date, 7 nights, and 4 people, each field with its own label above it. The visitor can act on it with one click, so keep the defaults.
- The routes list puts nights and locks next to each route name, which lets the visitor compare effort and length at a glance. It is the most useful thing on the page, so give it a better place, not less space.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Set the headline in the logo's serif.** Builds on finding 2. "Slowwater" is the only serif on the page. Everything else, including the headline, is a neutral sans, so the name is the only place the page has any character. A stronger version would give the headline that same serif. "Hire a narrowboat for a week on the canal" would then carry the brand's slow, unhurried voice, and the sans could stay on the form and the details. The piece exists to make someone want to plan a canal week and start booking. A headline that sounds like the brand helps with the wanting, and the factual copy below it does the rest. Taste. Lupton (*Thinking with Type*) treats a contrast between a display face and a text face as a way to give a page a voice without adding more styles.
2. **Let nights and locks lead the routes.** Builds on finding 4. "7 nights, 38 locks", "4 nights, 12 locks" and "10 nights, 61 locks" are the only real figures on the page. Right now they sit in pale gray at the far right, as if they were an afterthought. A stronger version would make those two numbers the main thing in each row, set as figures you can compare down the column. Then the routes list works as the tool for choosing a week by how hard it is, which is a real part of picking dates and nights before you search. Taste. Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*) argues for showing the data itself plainly and letting the reader compare it directly. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*) wants tabular figures wherever numbers are read in columns.
3. **Put "no fee on top of the hire" where the booking decision happens.** The best reason to book here is the line "No agency in the middle, and no fee on top of the hire." Today it sits at the end of the lead paragraph, far from the "Find a boat" card where the visitor actually commits. A stronger version would repeat that existing promise at the Search button, so the reason to book sits next to the action of booking. The piece is for starting a booking, and someone hesitating at the button is the one who needs reassurance about price. Use the page's own words, not new copy. Taste, argued on its own terms: put the reassurance at the point of commitment, not where the visitor has already moved past it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus indicators on the fields and buttons, and whether the selects open to sensible options. Check both in a browser with the keyboard.
- Whether the routes list updates when the base changes, as its heading "Routes from Hartwell Junction" suggests it should.
