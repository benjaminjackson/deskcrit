**First read.** On desktop the eye lands on the large serif headline. It then jumps right to the two dark green squares, plots 3 and 8, in the plot card. It reads as a calm, plain tool for the volunteers who run a community garden. It does not feel salesy. On the phone the first thing you see is a collision: the menu runs over the logo, the plot card covers the end of the headline, and the four feature cards turn into thin columns of one or two words.

**What it's for.** A landing page that gets community garden coordinators to put their plot waiting list into Plotwise ("Add your garden").

**Verdict.** On desktop and tablet the page does its job. The headline names the spreadsheet the coordinator already uses, and the one button names one exact action. On the phone it is broken, and many coordinators will open the link there first. After that, the feature cards cannot be scanned, and a few lines of copy can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**

1. **The phone layout does not fit the screen.** Defect. The page is 497 px wide in a 390 px viewport, so it scrolls sideways (facts file, phone `notes`). Three menu links wrap onto three lines, and "For / growers" runs into the "Plotwise" logo. Two of those links are only 19 px tall (`violations`, `nav.site-nav > a:nth-of-type(1)` and `(3)`), under the 24 px minimum (WCAG 2.5.8). The plot card sits on top of the headline and hides the end of "spreadsheet", and it touches the right edge with no margin. The four feature cards stay in four columns, so each one reads one or two words per line ("Plot / offers / A free / plot / goes to"). A coordinator who opens this from a group email on their phone gets a page that looks unfinished before they read a word. This fails WCAG 1.4.10 (Reflow), and it breaks the convention that a phone menu folds away (Nielsen, Jakob's law). Fix it this way: at phone width, stack everything in one column (headline, paragraph, button, plot card, then the cards one under another), and fold the menu into one control. Viewports: phone

2. **The feature cards have no titles you can see, so they cannot be scanned.** Defect. "Waiting list", "Plot offers", "Rent reminders" and "Work days" are set at 15 px, weight 400, color #55604f. That is the same as the body text under them (`font_size`, `font_weight`, `color` for `div.card > h3` and `div.card > p`). The section heading "What Plotwise does" (`h2`) is the same too. So each card reads as one sentence that starts with two odd words, "Waiting list Growers join the list online…". A coordinator who wants to know "does it do rent?" has to read all four cards to find out. Dair's rule applies: a heading that differs from its text by nothing looks like an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). Fix it this way: make the four titles differ from their text in a way you see at a glance. Weight or the headline's serif would do it, and the size can stay. Give "What Plotwise does" the same treatment so it reads as a section heading and not as a stray line. Viewports: all

3. **Key lines of copy can be read two ways, and the wrong reading costs a signup.** Defect. "Free" means "empty" here, but the page also talks about rent and has a "Pricing" link. So "offers a free plot to the next person in line the day it comes free" can read as a plot that costs nothing, and the "Free" key in the plot card has the same problem. The rules in the cards also sound fixed: "has a week to say yes", "one reminder before rent is due in March". A coordinator whose garden gives two weeks, or collects rent in April, can read these as Plotwise's own fixed rules and decide it will not fit their garden. Those lines might just be one example garden, but the page does not say so. Fix it by editing, not by adding new claims. Use a word for "free" that cannot mean "no charge" ("the day a plot comes open"). Then make clear whether the week and the March date are the example garden's settings or the product's rules. Only you know which is true. Viewports: all

4. **The plot card shows the part a spreadsheet already does well, not the waiting list the headline promises.** Taste. The headline is about running the waiting list. The paragraph says a plot goes "to the next person in line". But the card shows only which plots are free and which are taken, with no list and no next person. That is the one thing a coordinator's spreadsheet already does fine, so the only picture on the page doesn't show what's different about Plotwise. Fix it this way: let the sample show a free plot tied to the person it is being offered to, using the same Elm Row example. Viewports: all

5. **On widescreen the hero falls apart into two islands.** Defect. The headline block ends at about 584 px, and the plot card starts at 1436 px. That leaves about 850 px of empty space between them, so the card looks like it belongs to a different page. Below them, the feature cards stretch to 439 px each, and their text runs as one long line. The content is not held to a width. Fix it this way: cap the content width near the desktop layout, so the card stays close to the headline it supports. Viewports: widescreen

**Smaller things.**
- The "Taken" swatch in the key is a pale square (#e4e8df) on white, and the taken tiles are close to the white card. Both look well under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Status is also shown only by fill color plus the key (WCAG 1.4.1). Make the taken mark clearly visible, and give the free plots a second cue, such as an outline or a word. Viewports: all
- The headline breaks inside phrases ("for / your garden without the / spreadsheet"). The tablet break ("Run the waiting list / for your garden / without the / spreadsheet") reads better, so break it at phrase boundaries. Viewports: desktop, widescreen
- The "Plot offers" card ends with "yes." alone on its last line. Rewrap it or widen the measure. Viewports: desktop, tablet
- The hero paragraph runs right up to the plot card ("in line the" ends at about 496 px, and the card starts at 499 px). Give the column a gutter. Viewports: tablet

**What's working.**
- The headline names the tool the coordinator already uses and wants to leave ("without the spreadsheet"). It sets the product against something they know, on their own terms. Keep that comparison.
- There is one primary action, "Add your garden". It is the only filled button on the page, and it names one exact thing the coordinator can do. Don't add a second button next to it.
- The restrained palette and type plan (a serif headline, Inter for text, one green, warm neutrals, four sizes) fit a volunteer-run garden and avoid the template look.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Tell the whole page as one garden's story.** Builds on finding 3. The plot card already names a real-looking garden, "Elm Row Gardens, plots 1 to 12". Right now the four feature cards speak about no garden in particular. A stronger version would set all four cards in Elm Row: the waiting list, the offer, the rent reminder and the work day would read as moments in one garden's year. The coordinator would then see their own job laid out in order, not four separate features. This also settles the "is a week the rule?" question, because the week and the March date become Elm Row's settings. The maker has to supply the wording and confirm which settings each garden can change. Taste.
2. **Carry "without the spreadsheet" into every card.** The headline's comparison is the sharpest idea on the page, and it stops after the first line. The page is for coordinators who run their list in a spreadsheet today. Each feature card could name the spreadsheet chore it replaces, so the comparison runs down the page and does not sit in the headline alone. Then a coordinator who scans the cards keeps meeting the same reason to switch. The chores have to come from the maker's knowledge of how coordinators work now. Taste.
3. **Use the green only for "open plot" and the action that fills it.** The dark green appears in exactly two places: the free plots 3 and 8, and the "Add your garden" button. That pairing already says something: open plots and the way to fill them are the same color. Keep it that way as the page grows, with no green section headings, links, icons or accents. The color then keeps one meaning and points the coordinator straight from an empty plot to the signup. Nielsen's heuristic on consistency and standards supports this: one signal should mean one thing everywhere. This needs the second cue from "Smaller things" for the free plots, so the meaning does not rest on color alone. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The widths between those captured, especially between phone (390 px) and tablet (768 px), where the stacking fix in finding 1 needs to start.
