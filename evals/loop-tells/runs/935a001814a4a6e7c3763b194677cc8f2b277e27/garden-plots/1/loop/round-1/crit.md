**First read.** The eye lands on the big serif headline, "Run the waiting list for your garden without the spreadsheet". It then jumps to the two dark green squares (plots 3 and 8) in the card on the right, and then down to the green "Add your garden" button. It reads as a calm, plain tool for whoever keeps a community garden's list, with muted greens on warm off-white, and it reads quickly. On the phone, the page falls apart. The navigation links pile onto the logo, the plot card slides over the end of the headline and off the edge of the screen, and the feature cards become four slivers with one word per line.

**What it's for.** It's a marketing landing page for Plotwise, a tool for booking plots and running waiting lists, aimed at the people who run community gardens. That's as you told me.

**Verdict.** On desktop and tablet it does its job. The headline is specific, the ask is clear, and the plot map shows the product instead of a stock picture. But the phone layout is broken badly enough that a garden organizer who opens this on a phone sees a page that doesn't work. Below the headline, every piece of text is the same size, weight, and color, so the feature section reads as one gray run of text.

**What would move it most**

1. **The layout doesn't adapt to narrow screens.** On the phone the page is 497 px wide in a 390 px viewport (`page_width`), so it scrolls sideways (WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow). The four navigation links wrap into a three-line block that starts right at the logo's right edge and runs into it ("Plotwisegrowers"). "For growers" breaks across two lines, and two links are 19 px tall where the minimum is 24 (`violations`, `nav.site-nav > a:nth-of-type(1)` and `(3)`, WCAG 2.5.8). The plot card sits beside the headline instead of below it. It covers the end of "spreadsheet" and runs past the right edge of the screen. The four feature cards stay in four columns, so their text runs 4 to 6 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `div.cards > div.card > p`), and "who has a week to say yes" becomes eleven lines of single words. On the tablet the same failure is milder. The cards run 12 to 15 characters per line, against Bringhurst's 45 to 75, and the plot cells shrink to slivers about 20 px wide of uneven widths (19 to 26 px, `box`, `div.plan-grid > div.plot`), so the plots stop looking like a regular row of equal plots. To fix it, stack the hero to one column (headline, text, button, then the plot card), collapse the navigation, and stack the feature cards one or two per row below the desktop width. *Defect.* Viewports: phone, tablet

2. **Below the headline, every level of text looks the same.** The section heading "What Plotwise does", the four card titles, the card descriptions, the hero paragraph, and the navigation are all 15 px, weight 400, #55604f (`font_size`, `font_weight`, `color` on `h2`, `div.card > h3`, `div.card > p`). The card title runs straight into its sentence, so "Waiting list Growers join the list online…" reads as one line with a missing period, and the reader can't scan the four features by name. The section heading looks like a caption rather than the start of a new section. This is the half of the page that explains what the product does, and right now it reads as one gray block. Muller-Brockmann and Lupton ask for two to four levels you can tell apart at a glance, and Dair notes that a level can differ from the next by weight or color as well as size. Set the card titles heavier or in the darker #2f3a2c, and give the section heading a clear step up. You don't need new sizes. *Convention.* Viewports: all

3. **On a wide screen the hero comes apart.** At 1920 px the headline column stays 520 px wide and pinned left, while the plot card is pinned to the right edge. That leaves about 850 px of empty band between them (`box`, `h1` at x 64 to 584, `div.plan-title` at x 1457). The headline and its proof read as two separate things, and the eye has to cross the screen to connect them. Meanwhile the feature cards stretch to 440 px each, so most descriptions sit on one line with empty card space beside them. Cap the content width, or let the hero's two halves stay together, so the page keeps its desktop layout at wider sizes. *Defect* (balance, Arnheim; gathered space, Ruder). Viewports: widescreen

**Smaller things.**
- The plot map shows which plots are free and which are taken. The headline and the paragraph under it promise more than that: a waiting list, with the free plot offered to the next person. The card's title, "Elm Row Gardens, plots 1 to 12", names the garden but doesn't tell the viewer what to notice. Let the card show the step the copy describes, the free plot going to whoever is next on the list, using names and data only you can supply. *Taste.* Viewports: all
- "Each grower gets one reminder before rent is due in March" can be read two ways. It can mean rent is always due in March, or that it's due in March only in this example. A garden whose rent is due in another month may decide Plotwise doesn't fit. "One reminder" can also read as a hard limit. If the month and the number of reminders can change, say so in the line. Viewports: all
- The "Taken" swatch in the key and the taken plots are #e4e8df on a white card. By eye that looks well under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for meaningful graphics; the facts file doesn't measure it. The swatch almost disappears, so the key's second entry barely registers. Darken the taken fill or give it an outline. Viewports: all
- "yes." sits alone on the last line of the "Plot offers" card (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Adjust the width or the wording so the line doesn't end on a single short word. Viewports: desktop, tablet
- The page ends after the feature row, with no footer and no second "Add your garden" button. A reader convinced by the features has to scroll back up to act. *Taste.* Viewports: all

**What's working.**
- The headline names the job and the tool it replaces ("without the spreadsheet"), so the viewer compares Plotwise with something they already use. "Add your garden" names the one exact thing to do. Keep both.
- The plot map is a specific, believable piece of the product, not a generic hero picture. A named garden and numbered plots tell an organizer straight away that this was made for their problem.
- The palette and type pairing fit gardening without cliché: one green used only for the action and the free plots, warm neutrals elsewhere, and Source Serif 4 for the voice with Inter for everything else.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the serif carry the explanation too.** Builds on finding 2. Right now Source Serif 4 appears only in the logo and the headline, and everything below the hero is Inter. A stronger version would set the section heading "What Plotwise does" and the four card titles ("Waiting list", "Plot offers", "Rent reminders", "Work days") in the same serif. The serif is the page's voice. If it names each feature, the organizer scanning the lower half meets the same calm voice that made the promise, and the four names stand apart from their sentences by typeface as well as weight. Dair, *Design with Type*, treats a change of typeface as one of the ways to make contrast. *Taste.*
2. **Make the green square the page's one sign for "free plot".** The dark green cells for plots 3 and 8 are the most specific thing on the page, and the crit already credits the rule that green means only the action and the free plots. A stronger version would hold to that so strictly that the green square becomes a mark the organizer learns in one look and sees again wherever the page talks about a plot coming free, such as the "Plot offers" card. The piece is for selling one idea: a free plot goes to the next person without anyone touching a spreadsheet. One sign used the same way every time makes that idea easier to remember. Itten, *The Art of Color*, and Albers, *Interaction of Color*, both treat a color's meaning as something the viewer learns from how consistently it is used. *Taste.*
3. **Tie each feature to the spreadsheet it replaces.** The headline compares Plotwise with something the organizer already uses ("without the spreadsheet"), but the feature row drops that comparison and lists features with no reference to it. A stronger version would carry the comparison through, so each card names the spreadsheet chore that feature removes. That keeps the reader weighing Plotwise against their own way of working, which is the argument the headline started. The chores have to come from the maker's own knowledge of how organizers work today, so I'm not inventing them here. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see whether a menu toggle exists for the phone navigation, or what the focus states of the navigation links and the button look like.
