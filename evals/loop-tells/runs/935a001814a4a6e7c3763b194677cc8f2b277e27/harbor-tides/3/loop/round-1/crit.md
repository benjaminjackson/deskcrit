**First read.** On desktop the eye lands on the headline at top left. It drops to the dark teal button, then looks right and finds nothing: two thirds of the hero are empty white. Next it drifts to the lower row, where the tide table, the one real piece of product on the page, is set in the smallest and grayest type. The page reads as an honest, quiet start page for a small utility. It feels trustworthy but unfinished, as if a picture failed to load. On the phone the eye gets caught on the lower row: four columns that never stack, squeezed into strips one word wide, with the page scrolling sideways.

**What it's for.** A home page that persuades harbor masters and harbor offices to set up Tidewell, so their berth holders can check tides, lock gate times and pontoon depth on one page.

**Verdict.** The copy is specific and the tone suits harbor offices, but the page does not show the product. The one thing that would sell it, a page that berth holders actually read, is a 12 px table tucked under the hero, and the hero beside it is empty. On phone and tablet the layout breaks.

**What would move it most**

1. **The phone layout does not reflow, and the tablet layout crushes its columns.** On the phone the page is 676 px wide in a 390 px viewport (the `page_width` note), so it scrolls sideways. The three point columns are 65 to 69 px wide, which gives 6 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `div.points > div.point > p`). "Notices" and "Dredging," run to the right edge with no margin. On the tablet the same columns are 76 px wide with 6.6 to 7.3 characters per line, and the headings break as "Depth at / the / pontoon" and "Lock / gate / times". A harbor master who opens the link on a phone in the harbor office, or one who taps it in an email, meets a broken page from a product whose whole promise is a page that people check on their phones at the quay. That contradicts the pitch. This is a defect (WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow; Bringhurst on measure: 45 to 75 characters). Fix: below the desktop width, stack the table and the three points into one column at full width, so each paragraph runs at a normal measure. Viewports: phone, tablet

2. **The hero does not show the product, and the proof is the smallest thing on the page.** On desktop and widescreen the right two thirds of the hero are blank. The tide table under it, which shows what Tidewell does, is set at 12 px (`font_size`, `tbody td`), below the 15 px of the nav and body text. Its heading "Today at Porthmellin" is 13 px, smaller than the 15 px headings of the three text points next to it. A harbor master gets told what the page does but never sees it. The page also tips hard to the left (Arnheim on balance), and the empty space reads as something missing, not as a planned rest. This is a defect. If an image was meant to fill that space and did not load, check it in a live browser. Fix: make the page that berth holders see the hero visual, at a size you can read: the tides, the lock gate times and the pontoon depth together, the way a berth holder would see them on the quay. Then the headline and the picture make one argument: "here is the page, and you can stand behind it." Viewports: desktop, tablet, widescreen

3. **The type scale has five sizes, and the hierarchy of the lower row runs backward.** The sizes are 30, 19, 15, 13 and 12 (`type_sizes`). 13 and 12 differ too little to read as two levels. The h3s are the same 15 px as the body text and differ only by weight 500 against 400, so each point reads as one gray block. The most important block in the row, the tide table, gets the weakest heading. This is a convention (Bringhurst, "Don't compose without a scale"; Müller-Brockmann on distinct levels). Fix: keep three or four sizes. Fold 13 and 12 into one size, and give the headings of the lower row one shared level that clearly differs from the body text. Viewports: all

4. **The copy asks for trust but never gives the harbor master a reason to give it.** "Tide times your harbor office can stand behind" is a promise about accuracy, but nothing on the page says where the tide predictions come from or how the gate times are checked. That is the first thing a harbor master, who answers for those numbers, will want to know. "Today at Porthmellin" does not say whether this is a live harbor or a sample, so a stranger cannot tell what they are looking at. "Gate openings are worked out from each tide" drops the verb's subject, so the reader cannot tell whether Tidewell or the office does that work. The button "Set up your harbor" names an action but not what it costs the office in time or money. The page also never sets Tidewell next to what the office does now. The line "posted before the first boat asks" points at the phone calls the office takes today, but stops there. Fix: say the source of the tide data and who works out the gate times (the maker has to supply both facts). Label the table as an example of a Tidewell page. Say what setup takes, next to the button. Viewports: all

**Smaller things**
- The table headers "Tide / Time / Height" have 3.78:1 contrast (`thead th`, `#7a858b`), below 4.5:1. Darken them to the gray of the body text. Viewports: all
- In the table, the times and heights are left-aligned in proportional figures ("1.1 m" is narrower than "4.8 m"). Use tabular figures and set the numbers flush right (Bringhurst 4.4.3). Viewports: all
- The lower row does not keep to one grid: the table column is 300 px wide with a 48 px gap, while the point columns are 247 px (460 px on widescreen) with 32 px gaps. Put all four columns on one set of columns and one gap. Viewports: all
- The 13 px h2 and the 15 px h3s share a top edge, so their baselines miss by about 2 px. The 22.5 px rows of the table also drift away from the 23.3 px lines of the paragraphs beside them. Use one line interval across the row. Viewports: desktop, tablet, widescreen
- On the phone the nav wraps into a 2×2 block beside the logo. Use a menu or a single row that fits. Viewports: phone

**What's working**
- The hero paragraph ends on the viewer's world: "one page that berth holders can check from the quay." That line tells a harbor master exactly who uses the page and where. Keep it.
- The palette is restrained: one deep teal for the single button against warm grays. It feels calm and maritime, and nothing competes with the one action.
- There is one primary action and no crowd of buttons, and the three points name real harbor tasks (lock gates, pontoon depth, dredging notices), not generic feature claims.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 2.** Let one number lead the product picture. The table gives the berth holder four tide rows of equal weight. But on the quay a berth holder asks one question: what happens next, and when? A stronger version would make the next event the biggest thing on the product page, for example the next high water with its time and height, or the next lock gate opening. The other rows would sit quietly under it. This serves the pitch to the harbor master: they see a page that answers the berth holder's question at a glance, so it is plausibly a page that cuts the phone calls to the office. This is taste. The case for it is that a page people check standing up gets one look, and that look should land on the answer.
2. **Builds on finding 2.** Show the day as a tide curve, not only as a table. The piece already promises "the depth for every hour of the day" and gate openings "worked out from each tide". Those are times plotted against water height. One curve across the day, with the gate windows and the pontoon depth marked on it, would show in one picture how the three features connect, which the four rows and three text columns can only describe. For a harbor master deciding whether to set up Tidewell, that picture is the product's whole logic. This is taste, and Tufte argues for it in *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*: show the data so the eye can compare. The page has only four tide points, so the maker would need to supply the hourly heights and the real gate windows. Do not draw the curve from the four points alone.
3. Make the deep teal mean "Tidewell's data". Right now the teal is used only on "Set up your harbor". A stronger version would also use that same teal on the current or next tide in the product picture, and use it nowhere else. Then the color links the action to what the harbor master gets, and on the berth holder's page it marks "now". This keeps the restraint that the crit praises in "What's working", and gives the one color a job. This is taste. Albers, in *Interaction of Color*, shows how much one color can carry when the gray around it is held quiet.
