**First read.** On desktop the eye lands on the large headline at left, then the dark red "Sign in" button at top right pulls it away, and only then does it reach the "Reorder this week" table. The page reads as a quiet, warm stock tool for a small bookshop owner, and the tone suits that person. But the strongest color on the page goes to returning users, and the trial button is a pale beige box that looks hardly stronger than "See how it works". On the phone and the tablet the page does not reflow. The headline is crushed into a column one word wide, the table is pushed to the right and runs off the screen, and the feature notes become thin columns of one or two words per line.

**What it's for.** A marketing landing page that makes an independent bookshop owner understand what Shelfmark does and start a trial.

**Verdict.** The concept is sound: the headline makes a specific promise, and the table shows the product's output, not a stock picture. But the page breaks at phone and tablet widths, and the main ask is the weakest button on the page. Fix the layout below desktop width first. Then make the trial button the primary action.

**What would move it most**
1. **The layout does not adapt below desktop width.** The phone page is 789 px wide in a 390 px viewport, and the tablet page is 789 px in 768 px, so both scroll sideways (facts file `notes`). On both, the hero text column is only 181 px wide (`h1` box). The headline breaks to about 7 characters per line and the hero paragraph to 20 (`characters_per_line`, `div.hero-text > p`), against a comfortable 45 to 75 (Bringhurst, 2.1.2). On the phone the three feature notes fall to 5 to 9 characters per line (`section.facts > p`) and form ragged columns of single words. "Start a 30-day trial" breaks between "30-" and "day", which splits a number from its unit (Bringhurst, 2.4.6). "Sign in" and "Shops using it" wrap onto two and three lines. The header rule stops at 390 px while the content runs on past it, and the table's right edge touches the edge of the page. A shop owner who opens this from a link on their phone sees a broken page before they read the promise. Defect (WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow; Müller-Brockmann on the type area). Direction: below about desktop width, stack the hero in one column with the table under the text, put the three notes in one column, and collapse the navigation into a pattern people know from phone sites. Viewports: phone, tablet
2. **The trial is not styled as the primary action; "Sign in" is.** The only filled, saturated button is "Sign in" (white on dark red, weight 500, `a.nav-signin`). "Start a 30-day trial" is a beige tint with the same gray-brown text (`#5c544b`, weight 400) and the same size (15 px) as the outlined "See how it works" (`a.button.button-primary`, `a.button.button-secondary`). New visitors are the audience of this page, yet the strongest call goes to people who already have an account, and the real ask looks almost the same as the secondary one. This is also one button system used two ways: the nav button and the hero buttons look as if they came from different designs. Convention, and here it costs the page its conversion (Material Design 3 and Apple HIG on emphasis levels; Gerstner on repeated elements). Direction: give the trial button the filled dark red treatment and keep "See how it works" outlined. Then tone "Sign in" down to a text link or an outline so that it does not compete. Viewports: all
3. **The table shows the numbers but not the answer the headline promises.** The paragraph says Shelfmark "tells you which titles to reorder", but the table lists only "On shelf" and "Sold this week". The viewer has to compare the two columns and work out the decision themselves. The rows are in fact sorted by how far sales outrun stock (9−1, 6−0, 7−2, 5−1, 4−0), but nothing on the table says so, so the order looks random. The table is the one piece of proof on the page, and right now it shows the inputs, not the result. Defect of the copy and the content. Direction: show the output itself, such as a column for what Shelfmark recommends, and leave the label and the values to the maker. The ask has a related gap. "Start a 30-day trial" names a concrete action, but nothing sets Shelfmark next to what a shop does now (a spreadsheet, the till's own sales report, a count on Monday). The viewer has to take the value on trust. Add the kind of comparison only the maker can state. Viewports: all
4. **On widescreen the hero splits into two islands.** The text stays at the left (x 64 to about 570) and the table goes to the right edge (x 1377), with about 800 px of empty space between them. The table no longer reads as the illustration of the headline. It reads as a separate widget, and the eye has to travel across the screen to connect the promise to the proof. The three notes below sit on thirds that neither hero block shares. Convention (Müller-Brockmann, one grid governs the page; Ruder on white space that is gathered, not left over). Direction: limit the content width, or keep the hero columns at a fixed ratio so that the table stays next to the text it proves. Viewports: widescreen

**Smaller things.**
- The table column headers and the "Mon 9 June" date have a contrast of 3.82:1 (`thead > tr > th`, `span.preview-date`, `#8a8178` at 14 px), below the 4.5:1 that WCAG 1.4.3 requires. Darken that gray to the body gray `#5c544b`. Viewports: all
- The type sizes 16, 15, and 14 are each one pixel apart (`type_sizes`), so the hero paragraph, the feature notes, the button labels, and the table text read as almost the same level, not as clearly different levels (Bringhurst; Refactoring UI). Use 16 and 14 only, and make further distinctions with weight or color. Viewports: all
- The headline ends with "asks" alone on its third line. Set the measure so that the last line holds more than one word. Viewports: desktop, widescreen
- The second feature note ends with "take." alone on a line, and the notes run 79 to 81 characters per line (`section.facts > p`), which is past the 75 maximum. Viewports: widescreen
- "Returns tracked." has no verb and does not say what it does for the shop owner. "Works with your till" does not say which tills. Restore the verb, end on what the owner can now do, and add the kind of fact only the maker has (the tills it supports). Viewports: all

**What's working.**
- The headline is specific to this audience and says what the product does for them, in their own words ("shelves", "a customer asks"). Keep this voice.
- Using a real reorder list as the hero picture, with real-sounding titles, is far better than a stock image. It shows the product's output on the page itself.
- The palette of warm off-white, two browns, and one dark red holds together and suits a bookshop without imitating one.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the dark red mean "act on this."** Builds on finding 2. Moving the dark red from "Sign in" to the trial button is the fix. The stronger move is to keep that red for one meaning on the whole page: something the shop owner should do now. Then the same red could mark the rows in the "Reorder this week" table that need action, such as the two titles with 0 on shelf. The page is for a bookshop owner who must understand fast what Shelfmark does and then start a trial. If the red means "reorder this" in the table and "start here" on the button, the owner learns the product's main job and the page's one ask from one color. Taste. Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*, argues for one rule applied the same way every time, and that applies here to one color with one meaning.
2. **Make the Monday reorder list the center of the page.** Builds on finding 3. The page already has a small ritual: "on Monday morning" in the paragraph, "Mon 9 June" and "Reorder this week" on the table. A stronger version would build on that ritual. The table would stop looking like a side card next to the headline. It would look like the list the owner actually gets on a Monday: the largest and most finished thing on the page, with the headline as its caption. The owner is deciding whether this tool earns a trial, and the reorder list is the only proof on the page, so it should get the most space and care. Taste. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, argues that the data itself should be the picture, and this list already is.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus indicators on the nav links and the buttons. Check them in a live browser, especially on the beige trial button.
