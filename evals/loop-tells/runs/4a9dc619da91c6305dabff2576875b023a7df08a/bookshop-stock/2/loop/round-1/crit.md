**What it's for.** A landing page that gets independent bookshop owners to start a Shelfmark trial, as you described it.

**Verdict.** On desktop the idea is clear and well matched: a specific headline next to a real reorder list. But the page sends its one strong color to "Sign in", which a new visitor cannot use, and on the phone and tablet the layout is broken outright. Many shop owners will first meet this page on a phone, so the broken small-screen layout is the biggest thing in the way.

**What would move it most**

1. **The layout does not reflow below desktop width.** The page is 789 px wide on the 390 px phone and on the 768 px tablet, so both scroll sideways (`page_width`, phone and tablet `notes`). The two-column hero keeps its desktop split. The headline runs 7 lines at 6.8 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `h1`, phone and tablet). The body paragraph runs 6 lines at 20 characters (`div.hero-text > p`). The trial button wraps to 4 lines, and its text breaks as "30- / day" (`a.button.button-primary`, 86×115 px). On the phone the three feature notes squeeze into 58 px columns of 5 to 9 characters and up to 12 lines (`section.facts > p`). "Shops using it" wraps to 3 lines and "Sign in" to 2. The reorder table, which is the product's proof, starts at x 310 and ends at 789, so on the phone most of it is off the screen and on the tablet its right edge is cut by the screen. The visitor has to scroll sideways to see the one thing that shows what the software does, and the headline reads as a word list. Defect (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.10 Reflow; Müller-Brockmann on keeping content inside the type area; Bringhurst 45 to 75 characters). Fix direction: below a breakpoint, stack the page into one column (headline, paragraph, buttons, then the table at full width, then the notes one under another), and collapse or simplify the navigation so it fits the width. Viewports: phone, tablet

2. **"Sign in" is styled as the primary action and the trial button is not.** "Sign in" is a filled rust button with white text (`a.nav-signin`, background #8a3b24). The button the code itself calls `button-primary`, "Start a 30-day trial", is pale beige (#f3eee6) on a near-white ground (#fffdf9), with the same gray text and regular weight as the outline "See how it works" button next to it (both #5c544b, weight 400). So the strongest call on the page speaks to people who already pay, and the action a new visitor came for looks like one of two equal options. Warm, saturated color pulls forward, and here it pulls the eye to the wrong corner. Convention (Material Design 3, "Buttons": one high-emphasis action per view). Fix direction: give the trial button the filled rust treatment, and drop "Sign in" to a text link or an outline button in the nav. Viewports: all

3. **On wide screens the page falls apart into two edges and an empty floor.** Nothing holds the content to a maximum width. At 1920 px the hero text sits at x 64 to 604 and the table at x 1377 to 1855, with about 770 px of nothing between them. The eye has to cross the whole screen to connect the claim with its proof. All content ends by y 550, and below that roughly half the screen, 1080 px tall, is blank, with no closing ask, no proof from shops, and no footer. The same empty band takes the bottom 250 px on desktop. This empty space is left over, not shaped: the page looks unfinished, and it ends without asking a visitor who has read this far to do anything. Defect (Ruder on gathered rather than leftover space; Arnheim, "Balance"). Fix direction: constrain the content to a centered container about the width of the desktop layout, so the headline and the table stay a glance apart. End the page with a closing section that repeats the trial ask, using what the nav already promises ("Shops using it", "Pricing") if that content exists. Viewports: widescreen, desktop

4. **The copy leaves out the facts a shop owner checks first.** The headline is excellent: specific, in the owner's own words, about a moment they know. The supporting lines are vaguer. "Works with your till" does not say which tills or card readers, and that is the first thing an owner checks before starting a trial, so add the kind of fact that is missing (the systems it supports). "One click sends the reorder list in the format they take": "they" has to be traced back to the distributor, and the claim does not say which distributors. "Returns tracked." has no actor. Say who tracks the returns and what the owner gets ("Unsold copies due back are listed by date" already does this, so the bold lead-in could follow it). The asks also only invite the owner to take the product on trust. Nothing sets Shelfmark next to what the owner does now, such as a stock count, a spreadsheet, or the till's own report. Saying what kind of comparison would help, and letting the maker supply it, would let the owner judge the value on their own terms. Taste, from how a stranger reads the copy. Viewports: all

**Smaller things**

- The table headers and the date "Mon 9 June" are 3.82:1 (`thead > tr > th`, `span.preview-date`, color #8a8178), under the 4.5:1 minimum (WCAG 1.4.3). These are the labels that say what the numbers mean, so darken them to the body gray #5c544b. Viewports: all

**What's working**

- The reorder table as the hero picture. It shows the product's actual output, with real-sounding titles, "On shelf" against "Sold this week", and a Monday date, instead of a stock illustration. The viewer sees the promise of the paragraph ("tells you which titles to reorder on Monday morning") kept on the same screen. Keep it and give it room.
- The headline and paragraph use the shop's own vocabulary: shelves, a customer asking, copies, sold, ordered, returned, till, distributor. A bookseller recognizes their own day in it.
- The palette: cream ground, warm dark brown text, one rust accent. It feels like paper and a bookshop, not a software template. Keep the palette and move the rust to the trial button.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the reorder table show the gap, not just list it.** The table already holds the page's best evidence: "The Glass Orchard" and "A Year of Hedges" have 0 on shelf against 4 and 6 sold this week. Right now those rows weigh exactly the same as "Small Engines" with 2 on shelf, so the visitor has to do the subtraction to see why Shelfmark matters. A stronger version would make the gap between "On shelf" and "Sold this week" the first thing the eye reads in the table, so the empty-shelf rows stand out on their own. That is the headline's moment, a customer asking for a book you don't have, shown in the product's own output. A shop owner who sees it in one glance has the reason to start the trial. Use weight or position for this, not the rust, because the crit is moving the rust to the trial button and two jobs for one accent would weaken both. Taste. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*: show the comparison the data exists to make.
2. **Build the page around sold, ordered, returned.** The paragraph names three things, "counts every copy as it is sold, ordered, or returned", and the three notes below follow that order: the till, the distributor, the returns. That match is already in the piece, but the notes are three equal footnotes in a row. A stronger version would commit to that three-part sequence as the structure of the page below the hero. Each step would get its own section and its own piece of proof of the kind the reorder table gives for ordering, for example what the till import or the returns list looks like. The maker supplies those, since the page doesn't have them yet. Then an owner who scrolls follows a book's whole path through their shop and checks each part against their own week, which is the judgment they make before they start a trial. This goes beyond finding 4's missing facts: it is about how the page is put together, not about the wording. Taste. I argue it on its own terms, because no approved source states it.
