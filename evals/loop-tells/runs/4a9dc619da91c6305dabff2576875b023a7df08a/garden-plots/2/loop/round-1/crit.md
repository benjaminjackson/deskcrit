**First read.** The eye lands on the big serif headline, "Run the waiting list for your garden without the spreadsheet". It then jumps right to the two dark green tiles (3 and 8) in the plot card, and then down to the green "Add your garden" button. The page reads as a calm, plain landing page for a small tool that garden organizers would use. It feels earthy and quiet, not salesy. On the phone the first read is different: the eye lands on a broken page. The nav links pile onto the logo, the plot card hangs off the right edge, and the four feature boxes shrink to columns one or two words wide.

**What it's for.** A landing page that gets people who run community gardens to move their plot booking and waiting list onto Plotwise. They start with "Add your garden".

**Verdict.** On desktop, tablet and widescreen the page does its job. The headline names the user's pain in the user's own words, and there is one clear ask. Below the headline, though, everything is set at one size, weight and color, so the feature section reads as a gray block. On the phone the layout does not reflow at all, and that is the biggest problem.

**What would move it most**
1. **The phone layout is broken.** The page is 497 px wide in a 390 px viewport, so it scrolls sideways (`page_width`, phone). The four nav links wrap into three lines that overlap the "Plotwise" logo. Two of the links are only 19 px tall (`nav.site-nav > a:nth-of-type(1)` and `(3)`, which are below the 24 px minimum in WCAG 2.5.8). The h1 runs 8.8 characters per line, and "spreadsheet" touches the left edge of the plot card. The plot card runs to the right edge of the page with no margin. The four feature cards stay in four columns, so their text runs 4 to 6 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `div.cards > div.card > p`). "Waiting list" breaks after "Waiting", and a reader has to go down one word at a time to read "Growers join the list online…". A garden organizer who opens this from a link on their phone meets a page that looks broken before they read a word. This is a defect (WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow). To fix it, collapse the nav or let it wrap below the logo. Stack the hero to one column with the plot card under the text. Stack the feature cards to one column, or two at most. Viewports: phone
2. **Nothing below the headline has a level of its own.** The section heading "What Plotwise does" (`h2`), the four card titles (`h3`), the card text, the hero paragraph, the nav, and the plot-card title are all Inter 15 px, weight 400, in #55604f. This is the same on every viewport. Each card title sits directly on its text with no gap, so "Waiting list Growers join the list online…" reads as one run-on sentence. The eye cannot scan the four features by name, and the section heading looks like a stray caption. The fix is a convention from Dair, *Design with Type*: a heading differs from the text by weight, form, or color, not only by size. Set the card titles in a heavier weight or in the darker #2f3a2c used for the h1, and add a small gap below them. Give the section heading a real step up, for example the serif at a size between the body and the h1. This adds one level, not several. Viewports: all
3. **"Free" means two things on a page about rent.** "Offers a free plot to the next person in line the day it comes free" and the legend "Free" use the word for "vacant". The next card then talks about rent being due. A garden organizer, or a grower they forward the page to, can read "a free plot" as "a plot that costs nothing". That is a promise about money the garden would then have to walk back. This is a defect in the copy. The direction of the fix is an editorial change of word: use "empty", "open" or "vacant" for the plot state in the paragraph, in the "Plot offers" card and in the legend. Keep "free" out of anything that sits near rent. Viewports: all
4. **The picture shows the plots, but not the waiting list the headline sells.** The headline promises to run the waiting list, and the paragraph promises that a free plot is offered to the next person the day it comes free. The plot card shows only which plots are free and which are taken, with the caption "Elm Row Gardens, plots 1 to 12". Nothing in it shows a list, a next person, or an offer going out. So the one picture on the page repeats the setting and leaves the product's real job, the offer, to the reader's imagination. This is my taste, but I hold it firmly. The plot card is the right idea and should stay. The direction is to make it show what happens to plot 3 or plot 8, so that the picture and the headline make one point together. The caption should then say that point, instead of only naming the garden. The content is for the maker to supply. Viewports: all

**Smaller things.**
- The page stops after the feature row, and the bottom third of the screen is empty cream. On widescreen the headline and the plot card sit at opposite corners about 870 px apart, with nothing between them. Cap the content width, or bring the two halves of the hero closer, so the space looks planned. Viewports: desktop, widescreen
- The feature card text runs 12 to 15 characters per line on the tablet, so the short sentences break every two words. Two columns of cards would read better. Viewports: tablet
- "Add your garden" names the action, but not what it costs or what happens next. Pricing is only in the nav. A short line next to the button that says what the first step involves would help an organizer decide. That fact is for the maker to supply. Viewports: all
- "Rent reminders: Each grower gets one reminder before rent is due in March" reads as a fixed rule. An organizer whose rent is due in October can't tell whether March is an example or a limit. Say which it is. Viewports: all

**What's working.**
- The headline names the tool the reader uses today. "Without the spreadsheet" sets Plotwise against the thing every garden organizer already has and resents, and that lets them judge the value on their own terms. Keep that comparison.
- The palette is small and restrained: one dark green, used only for the button and the free plots, on a warm off-white. The eye goes to the action and to the free plots, and nowhere else. The free tiles also differ strongly in lightness and get bold white numbers, so the free and taken states still work without color vision.
- The serif headline with the plain sans text gives the page a quiet, specific character, close to a community notice board. The page does not look like a stock software template. The contrast is good throughout (lowest is 5.33:1 on the taken plot numbers).

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 4.** Let "Plot offers" lead the feature row. The four cards ("Waiting list", "Plot offers", "Rent reminders", "Work days") have the same size, the same weight and the same place in the order. But the headline and the paragraph sell one job: the next person in line gets the open plot. "A week to say yes" is the only concrete promise in the row, and it is the thing that sets Plotwise apart from a spreadsheet. A stronger version would put that card first, or make it bigger than the other three, and treat the other three as support. An organizer who skims the row would then see why to switch, not a list of features of equal weight. This is my taste. It follows the idea in Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, that the order and size of the parts must show how important each one is.
2. **Builds on finding 2.** Make the serif the voice for every named thing on the page, not only the headline. Today the serif appears once. The garden name in the plot card ("Elm Row Gardens"), the section heading and the four card titles are all set in the same Inter as the running text. If every name and heading is in the serif and Inter is kept for the sentences, the community notice board character that "What's working" credits would continue down the page. It would stop after the hero no longer. That serves a page whose job is to make a garden organizer feel this tool was made for gardens, not for software buyers. This is my taste, and I argue it on its own terms. The pair of typefaces is already in the piece, and this only gives each one a single, steady role.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus states on the nav links and the button.
- Where the layout breaks between the tablet (768 px) and the phone. Check the widths between 390 and 768 in a live browser.
