**First read.** On desktop the eye lands on the big serif headline, then jumps right to the two dark green squares in the plot map, then drops to the green "Add your garden" button. It reads as a small, calm software product for community or allotment gardens, made for whoever runs the waiting list. It feels honest and unfussy, if a little thin below the fold. On the phone the eye has nowhere stable to land: the menu wraps into the logo, the plot map hangs off the right edge, and the four feature cards turn into slivers one or two words wide.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a landing page that persuades the person who runs a community garden's waiting list to sign their garden up.

**Verdict.** On desktop and tablet the top of the page does its job: you can tell what it is, who it's for, and what to click. The phone layout is broken outright, and that's the biggest thing in the way. Below the headline, the section and card titles look exactly like body text, so the feature row reads as four run-on sentences.

**What would move it most**

1. **The phone layout doesn't fit the screen.** The page is 497 px wide on a 390 px viewport, so it scrolls sideways (facts file, phone `page_width`). The menu wraps onto three lines and runs into the logo, so it reads "Plotwisegrowers". The plot map sits against "spreadsheet" and runs off the right edge. The four feature cards stay in four columns, so the text sets at 4 to 6 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `div.cards > div.card > p`). Card titles break as "Waiting / list", and the fourth card is cut off by the screen edge. The nav links "For gardens" and "Pricing" are 19 px tall and overlap their neighbors, below the 24 px minimum (`violations`, `nav.site-nav > a`). A grower or garden manager who opens this from a phone link meets a page that looks unfinished. Defect, against WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow and 2.5.8 Target size, and Müller-Brockmann on keeping content inside the type area. Fix direction: at phone width, stack everything in one column (headline, text, button, then the plot map at full width, then the cards one per row) and fold the menu into a menu button. Viewports: phone

2. **Nothing below the headline reads as a heading.** "What Plotwise does" and the four card titles ("Waiting list", "Plot offers", "Rent reminders", "Work days") are 15 px, weight 400, color #55604f. That's identical to the body text under them (`h2`, `div.card > h3`, `div.card > p`), and each title sits right on its sentence with no gap. So "Waiting list Growers join the list online…" reads as one sentence with a stray capital, and a skimmer can't pick out the four features. The page has a 40 px level and then everything else is one level. Defect, against Müller-Brockmann and Lupton on distinct levels and Arnheim on space showing the groups. Fix direction: make the card titles bolder or darker rather than bigger, following *Refactoring UI*. Give the section label its own treatment, and open a small gap between each title and its text. Viewports: all

3. **"Free" can be read two ways on a page that also talks about rent.** The paragraph under the headline says Plotwise "offers a free plot to the next person in line the day it comes free", and the map's legend says "Free". You mean "vacant". But a reader can just as easily take "a free plot" to mean one that costs nothing, and the Rent reminders card tells them plots do cost money. A garden manager may wonder what Plotwise is promising growers, and a grower may expect a plot with no rent. Where the page talks about what people pay, a phrase with two readings is a defect. Fix direction: an editorial edit. Use the word you mean, for example "offers an empty plot to the next person in line the day it comes up", and change the legend to match. Viewports: all

4. **The hero's two columns don't scale with the screen.** At widescreen the text column stays 520 px wide on the left and the plot map moves to the right edge, which leaves an empty band of about 850 px between them. The headline and its picture stop reading as one unit, and the left side carries all the weight (Arnheim, on balance). At tablet the map gets squeezed to 163 px. Its title wraps, the plot squares come out at uneven widths from 19 to 26 px (`div.plan-grid > div.plot`), and the hero paragraph ends 3 px from the map's border. Defect. Fix direction: give the hero a maximum width so the map stays next to the text on wide screens. At tablet width, either give the map more room or stack it under the text, as on the phone. Viewports: tablet, widescreen

**Smaller things.**
- The plot map's left edge (x 796 on desktop) lines up with nothing else on the page, and the hero split has no relation to the four card columns below it. Take the map's edge from the card grid. Viewports: desktop, widescreen
- "yes." sits alone on the last line of the Plot offers card. Adjust the card width or the wording so that a word does not stand alone on the last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Viewports: desktop, tablet
- Free and taken plots differ only by fill color. The lightness gap is large, so color-blind viewers can still tell them apart, but WCAG 1.4.1 asks for a second cue. Use a pattern, an outline, or a word on the free plots. Viewports: all
- "Each grower gets one reminder before rent is due in March" reads as if every garden's rent is due in March. It can also read as if growers get only one reminder. If the month depends on the garden, say so in the words you already have. Viewports: all

**What's working.**
- The headline compares the product with the spreadsheet the manager already uses. The reader can judge the value against a tool they know, without having to trust a claim. Keep that comparison.
- The plot map is a specific picture of the product in use: a named garden, numbered plots, and two that are free. It is not a stock illustration, and it shows the moment the product exists for.
- The palette keeps the one strong green for free plots and the button. The eye links "a plot came free" with "add your garden". Everything else is warm neutral, and all text passes contrast (lowest 5.33:1).

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the Elm Row Gardens plot map the thread of the whole page.** The map already shows the exact moment Plotwise exists for: plots 3 and 8 have just come free. Right now it is a picture next to the headline, and the four cards below explain the product in the abstract. A stronger version would tie each card back to that map. The waiting list, the offer, the rent reminder and the work day would all be things that happen to Elm Row's plots, so the manager follows one garden through the product instead of reading a feature list. The page is there to persuade the person who runs the waiting list that this fits their garden, and one concrete garden followed from start to finish argues that better than four general claims. To do this, the maker needs content the page does not have yet, such as who is next in line for plot 3, and that content is theirs to supply. Taste. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, argues for showing the data in place of describing it.
2. **Builds on finding 2: carry the headline's serif down to one more level.** The serif appears only in the logo and the headline, and everything else is set in the sans. Finding 2 fixes the card titles. The further commitment is to give "What Plotwise does", and any section heading added later, the same serif at a smaller size. The page then speaks in two voices: the serif makes the case and the sans explains how it works. A manager who skims would get a short spoken argument from the serif lines alone, and that is the reader this page has to win over. Taste. Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, on contrast between typefaces to separate levels.
3. **Carry the spreadsheet comparison through the feature cards.** The headline's "without the spreadsheet" is the strongest idea on the page, but it stops at the headline. The cards say what Plotwise does and not what it replaces. A stronger version would set each card against the manual job it removes, such as the phone round when a plot comes free or chasing rent. The manager would then judge every feature against work they already do, which is what makes the headline persuasive. The maker knows what that manual work really is, so the wording is theirs to write. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the phone menu is supposed to collapse behind a menu button that failed to load, or was never built. Check this in a live browser at 390 px.
