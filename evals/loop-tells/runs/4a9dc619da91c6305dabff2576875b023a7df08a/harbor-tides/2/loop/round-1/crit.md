**First read.** On desktop the eye lands on the headline at top left, drops to the dark teal "Set up your harbor" button, and then has nowhere to go. The right two thirds of the first screen are empty white, and the small tide table and three text columns below read as an afterthought. It reads as a plain software page for harbor offices: calm and legible, but thin, nearer a wireframe than a page that sells. On the phone the bottom band never stacks. The three text columns squeeze into strips one or two words wide, and the page runs off the right edge of the screen.

**What it's for.** It must persuade harbor masters and harbor offices to set up Tidewell, so their berth holders can check tides, lock gate times, and pontoon depth on one public page.

**Verdict.** The words are good and the type is quiet and readable, but the page does not show the product and is broken on phones. The only evidence of what Tidewell makes is a 12 px table tucked under the hero, while the space where it should be shown stands empty. On a phone, which is where berth holders will "check from the quay", the layout runs off the screen.

**What would move it most**
1. **The phone layout does not reflow, and the tablet layout fails the same way.** At the phone width, the page is 676 px wide on a 390 px screen (`page_width`, phone), so it scrolls sideways. The three blocks "Lock gate times", "Depth at the pontoon" and "Notices" stay side by side in columns about 65 px wide. Their text runs 6 to 7 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `div.points > div.point > p`), 10 to 12 lines deep, with staircase rags and headings broken as "Depth at / the / pontoon". At the tablet width the page fits, but those columns are 76 px wide with the same 6 to 7 characters per line. The nav on the phone also wraps into a two-by-two block beside the logo. For this product it is the worst possible failure. The page promises a page people read on a phone on the quay, and a harbor master who opens this page on a phone sees one that has broken. This is a defect (WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow, and Bringhurst's 45 to 75 characters for a line). Fix: below a tablet width, stack the bottom band into one column (table, then the three points) and let the nav collapse or sit on its own row. Viewports: phone, tablet
2. **The product is not shown, and the space for it is empty.** The headline claims "tide times your harbor office can stand behind", but nothing next to it lets the reader see those tide times. At desktop and widescreen the hero's right side is blank, and at widescreen the bottom half of the screen is blank too. All the weight sits in a 400 px column at the left edge, so the page looks lopsided and unfinished (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Meanwhile the one piece of real proof, the "Today at Porthmellin" table, is set at 12 px (`font_size`, `tbody td`), smaller than the body copy. Its 13 px title (`h2`) is smaller than the 15 px headings beside it, so it reads as a footnote. A harbor master deciding whether to trust this needs to see what berth holders will see. Fix: make the public harbor page itself the hero visual. Put the table, the gate times, the depth and a notice together, large enough to read, in the space now empty beside the headline. That is a defect, and the empty right side is a convention break with no payoff. Viewports: desktop, tablet, widescreen
3. **The copy never lets the visitor compare Tidewell with how they post tides now.** The ask, "Set up your harbor", is specific and good. But nothing on the page sets Tidewell against what the office already does: a printed tide table in the window, a chalkboard, a PDF, phone calls to the office. So the visitor has to take "can stand behind" on trust. The table is also not called an example, so a stranger cannot tell whether Porthmellin is a real customer, a demo, or their own harbor. Fix: say plainly that the table shows the page berth holders see. Add the comparison with the visitor's current method, and name where the tide predictions come from, the fact "stand behind" depends on. Only the maker has that fact. This is about the copy, not a checklist rule. Viewports: all

**Smaller things.**
- The table headers "Tide", "Time" and "Height" are at 3.78:1 (`contrast`, `thead th`), under the 4.5:1 minimum. Darken them to the body gray, #4d585e. Viewports: all
- The type sizes 13 px (`h2`) and 12 px (table) are too close to read as two levels, and the h2 over the table ranks below the 15 px h3s next to it. Drop to four sizes and give the table title the h3 size. Viewports: all
- The h3s differ from the body only by weight 500 against 400 at the same 15 px, so the feature headings barely separate from their text. Add a clearer weight or color step (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 9). Viewports: all

**What's working.**
- The headline and lead paragraph speak the harbor master's own language: "berth holders", "visitor pontoon", "from the quay". They say exactly what Tidewell does in one sentence. Keep that voice.
- The one teal button is the only color on the page and the only primary action, so the next step is never in doubt.
- The body text is comfortable on desktop: 15 px Inter at 53 characters per line with 1.55 leading in the hero (`div.hero-text > p`), at 7.31:1 contrast.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Draw the day's tide as a curve.** Builds on finding 2. The "Today at Porthmellin" table already holds four turning points: 1.1 m, 4.8 m, 0.9 m and 5.1 m at 00:20, 06:12, 12:31 and 18:40. Beyond moving the table up and enlarging it, show those points as one curve across the day. Then put the lock gate times and the pontoon depth on that same curve, instead of in three separate text blocks. The feature copy already promises "the depth for every hour of the day", and a curve is how that promise looks. A harbor master who must decide whether to trust Tidewell sees in one glance when there is enough water, and does not have to read four rows and do sums. The real hourly figures and gate times must come from the maker, not from the designer. Taste. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, argues that a time series reads best as a line and not as a table of points.
2. **Let one tide figure lead.** Builds on finding 2. Right now the four rows in the table carry the same weight. A berth holder on the quay asks one question, "when is the next high water, and how high?", so pick one row from the table, the next tide, and make it the largest figure on the page. Set the rest below it at a clear lower level. The page exists to sell a harbor office on a page its berth holders will actually check, and showing the answer they come for first is the most direct proof. Which tide is "next" depends on the time of day, so the maker must decide what the page shows. Taste. Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*, argue that you give one element emphasis by making the others quieter.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the nav links and the button.
