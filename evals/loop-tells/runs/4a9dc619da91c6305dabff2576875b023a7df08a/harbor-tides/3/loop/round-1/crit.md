**First read.** The eye lands on the headline at top left, "Tide times your harbor office can stand behind", and then drops to the dark teal "Set up your harbor" button. After that it falls into a hole: the right half of the hero is empty white. Below that hole, a small tide table and three short feature notes sit in one low row, set so small that they read as a footnote. It feels calm, plain, and believable, but thin, as if something has not loaded. On the phone the page does not stack. It runs sideways off the screen, the nav wraps into a 2×2 block beside the logo, and the three feature notes break into columns one or two words wide.

**What it's for.** Get a harbor master to sign up for Tidewell to post tides, lock gate times, and pontoon depth for their harbor.

**Verdict.** The words and the restraint are right. The layout fails the job on two counts. On a phone it breaks, and a phone is the device this product is about ("check from the quay"). On a wide screen it hides the one thing that sells it, a real day's tides at a real harbor, at caption size under an empty half page.

**What would move it most**

1. **The page does not reflow on narrow screens.** The phone render is 676 px wide in a 390 px viewport, so it scrolls sideways (page notes, `page_width` 676). The lower row keeps four columns at every width. The table column keeps its full 300 px, so the three feature notes get squeezed to about 65–76 px. On the phone they run 4 to 7 characters per line and 10 to 12 lines each (`characters_per_line`, `div.points > div.point > p`). On the tablet they run about 7 characters per line and 9 to 11 lines. The headings break as "Lock / gate / times" and "Depth at / the / pontoon". The Notices column runs right to the edge of the phone page with no margin, and the nav wraps into two ragged rows beside the logo. A harbor master who opens this link on a phone sees a page that looks broken, on the device the product promises to serve. That costs more trust than anything else here. This is a defect (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.10 Reflow; Bringhurst on measure; Hochuli on staircases). The fix: below a tablet width, stack the lower row into one column: the table, then each note at full width. Let the nav collapse or sit on its own line. Viewports: phone, tablet

2. **The proof is the smallest thing on the page, and the hero has a hole where it belongs.** The "Today at Porthmellin" table is the product itself: what berth holders will actually see. But it is set at 12 px, with a 13 px heading (`h2`). That heading is smaller than the 15 px headings of the three feature notes next to it (`div.point > h3`), so the most concrete thing on the page ranks below three generic labels. Meanwhile the hero text takes up 400 of 1152 px on desktop, and the rest of the hero is empty. On widescreen the hero still hugs the left 400 px, and the bottom 40% of the screen is blank. The page tips hard to the left, and the empty area reads as a picture that failed to load, not as chosen space. If a picture is meant to be there and did not load, check that first. As built, the page fails here. The viewer has to find the evidence for themselves, and most will not scroll their eye down to 12 px gray type. This is a defect (Arnheim on balance; Ruder on gathered space; Müller-Brockmann and Lupton on distinct levels). The fix: give the sample of the berth holders' page the empty right half of the hero at a size people can read. Make its heading at least the level of the feature headings, and say that this is what berth holders see. Viewports: desktop, widescreen, tablet

3. **The headline promises trust, and nothing on the page backs it.** "Tide times your harbor office can stand behind" asks a harbor master to put their office's name on these numbers. But the page never says where the tide predictions come from, or who checks the gate times. "Gate openings are worked out from each tide" has no actor: worked out by whom, the harbor master or Tidewell? The reader has to guess, and the wrong guess is either "more work for me" or "a black box I can't vouch for". A stranger needs the verb's subject restored ("Tidewell works out gate openings from each tide…", if that is true) and the kind of fact that earns the headline: the source of the tide data, and what the office checks or approves. Only the maker can supply those. The ask, "Set up your harbor", is specific, which is good. But nothing sets Tidewell next to what the office does now (a chalkboard, a printed almanac, answering the phone), so the viewer has to take the value on trust. Name that comparison; do not invent it. This is a defect in the copy. Viewports: all

**Smaller things.**
- The table headers "Tide / Time / Height" are #7a858b at 3.78:1 (`contrast`, `thead > tr > th`), below 4.5:1. Darken them to the body gray (#4d585e, 7.31:1). Viewports: all
- The feature headings are 15 px, the same size as their body text, and differ only by weight 500 against 400 and a slightly darker gray. They barely separate from it. Make one difference stronger: a heavier weight or a clear size step (Dair). Viewports: all
- The type scale has 13 px and 12 px both in the table area (`type_sizes`). These sizes read as one level picked twice. Keep one of them. Viewports: all
- The gutter after the table column is 48 px, and between the feature columns it is 32 px. Use one interval for both (Müller-Brockmann). Viewports: desktop, widescreen

**What's working.**
- The copy is specific to harbors and speaks the audience's own words: berth holders, the quay, visitor pontoon, sounding, dredging, fuel dock hours. "Enter one sounding and Tidewell shows the depth for every hour of the day" is the best line on the page. It says who does what and what the office gets. Keep that pattern.
- The palette is one muted teal and grays, with the teal used only on the button. The page feels calm and official, which suits an office that posts safety information. The single primary action is unmistakable.
- The left edge at 64 px holds the logo, headline, body, button, and table on one strong line at every width.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the sample match the three promises.** Builds on finding 2. The "Today at Porthmellin" table shows only the four tides. But the three notes next to it promise three more things on the berth holders' page: lock gate times, depth at the pontoon, and notices. A stronger version would let the sample carry all three: a gate opening, an hourly depth, and one notice, set where each note can point to it. Then each feature note is a caption on something the harbor master can see, not a claim they have to picture. That serves the sign-up directly, because the harbor master is choosing what their berth holders will read, and here they would see the whole page before they set it up. The gate times, depths and notice text have to come from the maker, from a real or clearly marked sample harbor. Do not make them up. Taste. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, argues for showing the data itself, not a description of it.
2. **Show the sample at the size it will be read on the quay.** Builds on findings 1 and 2. The hero copy says berth holders "check from the quay", which means a phone in one hand. When the Porthmellin sample moves into the empty right half of the hero, commit to showing it at the size and proportion a berth holder will actually see on that phone, not as a wide desktop table. A harbor master judges whether they can stand behind these numbers by picturing someone reading them on a wet pontoon. Seeing it at that size answers the question before they ask it. Taste.
3. **Give every note the same plain shape: what the office does, then what Tidewell does.** Builds on finding 3. The best line on the page, "Enter one sounding and Tidewell shows the depth for every hour of the day", already has this shape. A stronger version would write all three notes the same way: one sentence for the office's part and one for Tidewell's. Then a harbor master can add up their own workload across the row. That matters for this reader, because "can stand behind" is a promise about who answers for each number. The maker has to supply the facts about who does what for the gate times and the notices. Taste. Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*, on letting the reader see the answer without having to work it out.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The nav and button focus states, and whether the hero's right half is meant to hold an image that did not load. Check that in a live browser before acting on finding 2.
