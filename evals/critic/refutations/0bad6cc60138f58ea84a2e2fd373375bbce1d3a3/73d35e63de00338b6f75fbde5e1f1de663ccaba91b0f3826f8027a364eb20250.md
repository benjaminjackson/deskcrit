**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It starts at the top-left line only because that's where reading starts, and then moves through nine lines of grey text that all look the same. Partway down you work out that every other line is a question heading, and that these are help answers for a bakery's pickup orders. It feels calm and plain, but you have to read it before you can see how it's organised.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is an FAQ or help block on a bakery's online ordering site. Customers who already have an order scan it to find the one answer they need, such as changing a pickup time, sending someone else, or cancelling.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. An FAQ has to be scannable, and here the headings look the same as the answers. A customer can't jump to "Cancelling an order" without reading their way down to it. The second problem is that the two lines customers will hold the bakery to, the cancellation deadline and who can collect, can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings look like body text, so the list can't be scanned.** The four headings ("Changing your pickup time" and the rest) are the same weight and colour as the answers, and look a pixel or two *smaller*. Eight blocks of text appear as one undivided run, with no title above them. Someone looking for cancellation has to read every line to find it, which defeats the point of an FAQ. This is a defect. Dair (*Design with Type*, ch. 8) says a difference between two elements must be obvious at a glance in size, weight, form or colour, and that a one-step difference looks like an accident. Fix: make the headings clearly different from the answers. Semibold or bold is the cheapest way, and a slightly larger size helps. Add one page title ("Help with your order" or similar) above them so there's a place to start, as Tschichold says: one element first, the rest stepped back by size. Three levels are enough: the title, the questions, the answers.
2. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways.** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." Ahead of what: the pickup time, the bake, or the pickup day? Does "a day" mean 24 hours before the slot or any time the day before? A customer with a 9am Saturday pickup could reasonably think they can cancel on Friday evening. If the bakery means 9am Friday, that customer is refused, or charged for bread they thought they'd cancelled. That is the line people argue over at the till. This is a defect. Fix: state the deadline as a time the customer can check, for example "You can cancel until 24 hours before your pickup time," or "…until 6pm the day before."
3. **"Along with a name" is ambiguous at the point of collection.** Whose name, the collector's or the person who placed the order? If staff expect the orderer's name and the friend gives their own, the friend may be turned away at the counter. This is a defect, and it's minor in wording but costly in the moment. Fix: say it outright: "They'll need to show the order number and give your name."

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the answers run about 84–92 characters per line across an ~820 px measure, above Bringhurst's 45–75 range. With answers this short it's tolerable, but narrowing the column to about 600–650 px would bring it into range and cost nothing.
- Widow: "anything." sits alone on the second line of the allergies answer. A narrower measure or a small rewrite ("Ask the baker if you're unsure.") gets rid of it.
- Copy: "Open your order" doesn't say where. Name the place ("in your account" or "from the link in your confirmation email"), or make it a link.
- Copy: "holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops before the consequence. Say what happens to the customer after that: is the order given away, and do they get a refund?

**What's working.**
- The spacing already groups each item correctly: about 10 px from heading to answer and about 35 px between items. Keep those gaps when you make the headings bolder. The grouping is right, it just isn't visible yet.
- The answers are short, direct, and written in a person's voice. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule in terms the customer accepts instead of just stating a policy. That's the right tone for a small bakery, and the other answers should match it.
- The near-black text on white, one face and one alignment, is clean and readable. Don't add decoration when fixing the hierarchy.

What matters first: 3 lines, 1 yes, 2 no (one element dominates; distinct text levels)
