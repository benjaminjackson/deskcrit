**First read.** My eye goes first to "How can we help?" in white serif on the navy band, then straight down to the white search field. That part reads quickly and calmly. Next, before any other card, the eye jumps to the bright credit card under **Billing and invoices**, then to the green shield under **Privacy and security**. In the first few seconds those two topics look more important than the other six, and no part of the page explains why.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of a product help centre. Its users arrive with a problem and need to either search or pick the right topic quickly.

**Verdict.** This is a competent, tidy help-centre home page, and the search-first layout does its job. What hurts it most is the icon set. Two of the eight topic icons come from a different, full-colour illustration style. That breaks the grid's even weighting and makes the page look assembled rather than designed.

**What would move it most**
1. **Two icons from a different set pull false emphasis (defect).** Six topic icons are thin navy line drawings: the lightning bolt, key, box, plug, bell and life ring. Two are not:
   - The **Billing** card is a filled illustration in blue, yellow and orange.
   - The **Privacy** shield is filled green with a gloss gradient and a check mark.
   
   They are the only saturated colour below the header, so they become the second and third things the eye lands on. That gives Billing and Privacy a priority the content doesn't claim. The two icons also bring in four hues the rest of the palette doesn't use, outside a navy-and-grey system. And the shield's shine adds fake depth that nothing else on the page has (that last point is taste). A row of cards should follow one program, with a variant only where the rules call for it (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*). The fix is to redraw the card and the shield as navy line icons with the same stroke weight, caps and size as the other six. If Billing or Privacy really does deserve emphasis, show it through position or a label, not a stray icon style.

2. **The page is unclear about what product it supports (defect, mild).** "Set up your workspace and invite your team" and "Connect calendars, chat tools, and storage" describe a SaaS collaboration tool. "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" describes a shop. A first-time visitor can't tell whether these are two products, a product that also sells hardware, or a leftover from a template. The navigation and grouping should show what the product is and how its parts relate (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2). Fix: if both really apply, make the description say what is being ordered ("Track a hardware order…"). If not, remove the card.

**Smaller things.**
- Card descriptions end on lone short words: "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages." (and "your team.", "and storage."). Most of the cards look like they have a runt last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrite the descriptions to fit one line, or balance the line breaks so the second line carries a phrase.
- Nothing on the first screen speaks to the problem a visitor most likely came with: no popular articles, no known issues, no link to Status beside search. If those sit just below the fold this is fine. If not, add three or four top articles under the search field (common sense; the most common question shouldn't need a search).

**What's working.**
- The navy band with one serif headline and one wide search field gives the page a single clear starting point and one obvious action. Keep it uncluttered.
- The grid is disciplined. The logo, "Browse by topic" and the left card column share one edge, the cards share the right edge with "Contact us", and the gutters are even. That is why the page feels trustworthy at a glance.
- Card copy is specific and written in the user's terms ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Fix sync problems and error messages"). It names what you'll do, not just a category, so picking a card takes no guesswork.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The focus indicator on the search field and the cards, plus their hover states. Check them in a live browser: on a white field over navy, a weak default focus ring is easy to miss.
