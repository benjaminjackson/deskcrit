**First read.** The eye lands on "About the trust" in bold, drops into an 11-line grey paragraph, and has nowhere to go after that. It reads as the About section of a small countryside charity's website. It feels calm and tidy, but anonymous: nothing in it says moor, footpath, or this village. The most useful thing in the text, that anyone can come and help on a Saturday, sits in the bottom third of the block where a skimming reader won't reach it.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the About section of a local footpath trust's website. Its job is to tell walkers and neighbours what the trust does and get some of them to turn up to a work day.

**Verdict.** The type is set well, but the section doesn't do its job. Everything is in one paragraph, so the invitation to volunteer is buried and can be read two ways, and nothing tells the reader when or where to show up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The volunteer ask is buried and vague.** It comes in at line 7 of 11, after the history and the staff numbers, and it never says where to meet, what time, or how to sign up. A reader skimming for "can I help?" either misses it or gets an invitation they can't act on. *Defect.* Lift the work-day offer out of the paragraph as its own short block, and say what to do: the next date, the meeting point, the start time, and a link or contact. The empty right half of the frame is a natural place for it.
2. **"Every second Saturday" can be read two ways.** It can mean every other Saturday (fortnightly) or the second Saturday of each month. A volunteer who picks the wrong reading turns up on a day when nobody is there. This is a date people will act on, so the ambiguity is a *defect*, not a style note. Pick one wording ("every other Saturday" or "on the second Saturday of each month") and give the next date.
3. **The text is one unbroken 11-line block.** It mixes three jobs: what the trust looks after, its history and size, and the invitation. All three get the same grey weight, so the reader can't scan for the part they came for. *Convention* (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web": text people skim is broken into short paragraphs and headings). Split it into those three parts. Put the invitation in the reader's terms ("You don't need any experience. We bring tools, gloves and a hot drink."). It's the one place the copy talks to the reader, and right now it does it in passing.

**Smaller things.**
- The voice changes partway through. It starts in the third person ("It was set up… it has four staff") and switches to "We provide" in line 8. Pick one, and "we" suits a volunteer appeal better.
- "About the trust" could sit on any charity's page. A heading that says what the trust does ("Keeping the moor's paths open since 1987") would tell the reader more in the same space. *Taste.*
- The look is generic: grey sans serif on white, with no picture of the paths, the stiles, or the volunteers. The reader can't tell this is a moorland trust until they read it. One real photo of a work day would do more than any styling change. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- The reading column is comfortable: about 60 characters per line, roomy line spacing, a clean ragged right edge, and dark text at strong contrast. Keep that column width and spacing when you split the text.
- The heading stands clearly apart from the text through both size and weight, and it sits close to the paragraph it introduces. The hierarchy is clean, so splitting the text won't need any new type sizes, only space and maybe one subheading.
- The copy is concrete: eleven miles, two woods, 1987, ninety volunteers, drains, stiles, gorse. Those specifics are what make the trust sound real, so keep them.
