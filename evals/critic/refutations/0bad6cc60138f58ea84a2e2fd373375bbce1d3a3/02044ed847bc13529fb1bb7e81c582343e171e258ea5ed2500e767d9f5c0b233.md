**First read.** My eye goes to the bold serif "Harbour Lights festival", then drops into three grey blocks of the same weight with nothing to pick out. It reads as a local events notice, with a friendly, well-set serif and a calm, bookish feel. The middle column starting lower catches the eye straight away and looks like a slip. After a few seconds I still can't say when the lantern walk is or where I need to be.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or notice telling local families what's on at the festival, and when and where, so they turn up.

**Verdict.** It looks tidy but doesn't do its job well. Three events with times and places are written as unbroken prose, so a reader has to read every word to learn what's on Saturday. The dropped middle column also makes a three-column layout look careless.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three events have no headings and the facts are buried. Defect.** Each column is one event (the festival overview, the Saturday lantern walk, the Sunday pie contest), but nothing says so. Someone scanning for "when is the lantern walk?" has to read the middle block to find "Saturday night… at dusk… town hall". The pie contest's hand-in time ("by one o'clock") and the announcement time ("at four") sit mid-sentence. People skim notices like this, and dense unbroken blocks are the pattern they skip (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Headings that name what each section holds are also WCAG 2.4.6. Direction: give each column a short heading that names the event and its day ("Lantern walk, Saturday at dusk"). Then pull out the what, when and where (date, start time, meeting point) as one line or a short list at the top of each column, so the prose becomes the colour, not the only way to get the facts.
2. **The middle column sits about 14 px lower than its neighbours. Defect.** The first and third columns start level at about y=145; the middle one starts at about y=159. Its lines therefore fall between the lines of the other two columns rather than level with them. Müller-Brockmann's grid puts the first line of every column on the same line. Here the offset is small enough to look like a mistake rather than a choice, and it is the first thing a designer's eye snags on. Direction: give all three columns the same top, probably by removing a stray top margin or padding on the middle one.

**Smaller things.**
- Gutters are lopsided. Each rule sits about 20 px from the text on its left and about 11 px from the text on its right, so the columns look pushed right against their dividers. Centre each rule in its gutter, or drop the rules and use a wider, even gap (Müller-Brockmann: one fixed interval).
- The first column ends with "seagulls." alone on the last line, the longest paragraph ending on one word. Retouch the copy or the measure so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The copy is pleasant but also generic. "Volunteers are still needed" is the one real ask on the page, and it has no next step: no name, number or place to sign up. Give it one, or the request goes nowhere.
- The title says "festival" but never says which year or the actual date. "The first weekend of September" makes the reader work it out. Put the dates in the title area.

**What's working.**
- The type itself. A readable text serif at a comfortable size, with generous leading and lines of roughly 40–48 characters, which suits multi-column text (Bringhurst gives 40–50). Keep the face and the setting.
- A clear two-level scale. The bold title is far enough above the body in size and weight that nothing competes with it. When you add event headings, make them a third step between the two, not a near-copy of either.
- Restraint. One ink colour on white and hairline dividers. The page feels like a well-made parish notice, which fits a small-town festival.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The empty lower ~40% of the frame is probably just the end of the content in this viewport. Check whether more follows, such as a map or a volunteer sign-up, in a live browser. How the three columns reflow on a phone: they will need to stack, and headings matter even more once they do.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 6 yes, 2 no (one look for repeated elements; headings name their sections)
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 14 lines, 11 yes, 3 no (one grid with equal gutters; no near-misses; baselines across columns)
Text: 30 lines, 28 yes, 2 no (clean paragraph breaks; text broken into chunks)
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: skipped, no images
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: skipped, no controls
