**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big serif headline, "The finest bread in the valley". It drops to the one-word second line, "valley", then down to the two-line subhead. Then it stops. There's nowhere to go after "Mill Street." and the bottom 40% and right half of the screen are empty cream. It reads as the opening screen of a small, old-fashioned local bakery's website. It feels calm, warm and literary, but also unfinished, like a hero section waiting for its picture and its button.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the first screen of a desktop website for an independent bakery. Its job is to get local people to visit the shop or order bread.

**Verdict.** The typography is well made but the screen doesn't do the job. It never tells anyone what to do next: no hours, no address beyond a street name, no button. Its main claim is a superlative any bakery could print. The biggest problem is the missing next step.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no next step (defect).** The eye path ends at "a wood-fired oven on Mill Street." and runs into empty space. Nothing tells a hungry local when the shop is open, where exactly it is, or how to order. They have to scroll or leave to find out, and on a first screen many will just leave. The empty lower-left, under the subhead, is where the answer belongs. Put one specific action there, in the reader's terms: "Open today 7–2 · 14 Mill Street", or "Order tomorrow's loaves by 6 pm". Common sense rather than a cited rule: the eye should go headline → detail → action without hitting a dead end.
2. **The headline could be on anyone's page (defect).** "The finest bread in the valley" is a claim the reader has to take on trust, and every bakery in every valley makes it. All the real distinction is in the small subhead: sourdough, rye and white tin loaves, wood-fired, baked every morning, on Mill Street since 1962. Move a concrete fact into the 100-px line: the wood-fired oven, the 60-odd years, the loaf you're known for. Then the reader can judge you against the supermarket loaf they already buy, not just be told you're the best.
3. **Nothing shows the bread (taste, but it costs a lot here).** People buy food from a bakery because of how it looks. This screen asks them to imagine crust from a sentence. The empty right side and bottom have room for a photograph of the loaves or the oven, which would also give the headline something to work against. My view as a designer, not a rule.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "The finest bread in the / valley". That leaves one word alone on line 2 and splits "the valley". Either break after "bread" ("The finest bread / in the valley") or change the measure so the phrase stays together (Bringhurst on etiquette of line breaks; the source for heading breaks specifically isn't confirmed, so treat it as common sense).

**What's working.**
- The type pairing: a classical text serif for the headline against a plain sans for the eyebrow and subhead. It's clear contrast with no conflict, and the serif with its joined "fi" sets the old-craft tone the bakery wants. Keep it.
- A clean three-level scale: a small tracked brown-caps eyebrow, the very large headline, and about 20-px body text, all on one left edge at 110 px. Every level has an obvious job.
- The subhead is the best writing on the page: specific, concrete and local. Its lines run about 60 characters with comfortable spacing. Treat it as the model for the rest of the copy.
