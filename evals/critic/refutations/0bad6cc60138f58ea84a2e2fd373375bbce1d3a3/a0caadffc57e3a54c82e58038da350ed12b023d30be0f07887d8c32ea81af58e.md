**First read.** My eye goes to the first lines of the brush script before it reaches the heading. The script is heavier, darker and busier than the bold sans title above it. It reads like a café chalkboard or a wedding favour card. Only when I start reading do I see it's practical ferry information: times, bikes, winter service. The feel is cheerful, but reading it is slow work.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or info panel that tells ferry passengers how the service works: frequency, crossing time, bikes and the winter timetable. Readers will scan it to plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. Every word passengers need to read is set in a connected brush script, so they have to decode the text letter by letter. The tone also fights the content. On top of that, the copy is vague exactly where passengers need it to be exact.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in a script face.** All three paragraphs use a connected brush script at body size. The joined, slanted letters and heavy strokes blur word shapes: "twelve minutes", "lower deck" and "before dusk" each take a second look to read. Someone checking times on a phone at the landing will skim and misread, or give up. Script is for a few words of display. Paragraphs need a face made for reading (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1). The fix is to set the paragraphs in a plain text face. The sans already used for the heading would work at regular weight. If you want the friendly tone, keep the script for a few words at most, such as a short tagline. **Defect / convention.**
2. **The title doesn't lead.** "Notes for ferry passengers" is bigger, but the script below it has more ink, more texture and more movement. So the title and the text compete for the first look, and the page doesn't open on what it is. Tschichold asks that the order of importance show plainly. Item 1 fixes this on its own: once the body is in a quiet text face, the bold title clearly comes first. **Defect.**
3. **The heading and body don't feel like one piece.** A crisp, neutral UI sans sits on top of holiday-card brush lettering. The page reads like two templates stuck together, and the script makes timetable information feel like decoration. Passengers want this to read as reliable and official, not cute. Choose one voice. A calm sans or a sturdy serif throughout fits a public transport notice. **Taste.**
4. **The copy is vague where passengers will act on it.** "Between early morning and late evening", "a little longer when the tide turns" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" give no times. A passenger can't tell whether the first boat is at 6:00 or 8:00, or whether a winter "before dusk" means 15:30 or 16:30. If they guess wrong, they miss the last boat. "Every forty minutes" also has no anchor: forty minutes from when? Put in the real first and last departure times and the winter cut-off time, or at least a starting time for the 40-minute cycle. If the times change with the season, say where to find them online, not only "in the shelter at each landing". **Defect.**

**What's working.**
- The three short paragraphs each cover one topic: service, bikes, winter. That's the right way to chunk this information, so keep it when you change the face.
- The flush-left column with a generous left margin and plain near-black on white gives an easy, high-contrast base. It will read well once the face changes.
