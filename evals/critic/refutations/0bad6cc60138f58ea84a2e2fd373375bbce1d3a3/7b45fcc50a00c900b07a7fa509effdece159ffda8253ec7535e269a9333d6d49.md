I critiqued it as the first screen of the homepage for a team weekly-planning product called Weekplan. You didn't say what it's for, so that's my assumption.

**First read.** My eye lands on the big black headline, reads it as two lines, drops to the grey subhead and stops on the blue "Start a free trial" button. After that there's nothing left to look at, and I notice the empty right half and the empty bottom third. It reads as a clean, competent SaaS landing page, calm and neutral, but it could belong to almost any startup. I come away knowing the promise and not what the product is.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the first screen of a marketing homepage. It should get team leads or engineering managers to understand what Weekplan does and start a trial.

**Verdict.** It's clear and well set, but it only half does its job. The subhead promises "one honest picture of the next five days," and the page never shows that picture. The viewer has to take the main claim on trust, and the layout is left lopsided with nothing in it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The product the copy describes is missing.** The subhead says the product combines tickets, meetings and leave into one picture of the week. The whole right half next to the headline and everything below the button (roughly y 530–800) are blank, so the viewer never sees that picture. That costs two things:
   - **Defect:** someone who is curious can't check the claim and has to start a trial to find out what "honest picture" means.
   - **Composition:** all the visual weight sits in the top-left, and nothing on the right or bottom balances it. The frame looks top-heavy and unfinished, as if an image failed to load (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance").

   The fix: put a real five-day view in that space, beside the headline or straight under the button. It should show a ticket, a meeting and a person on leave colliding on one day, so the "actually works" contrast is visible at a glance. Then the screen both makes the case and finds its counterweight. If the white space is deliberate, it can only work once the page has something to hold up against it. Empty, it reads as missing content, not restraint.
2. **The ask asks for trust, not a comparison.** "Start a free trial" is the button text any SaaS page would use. It asks the viewer to commit before they've seen the value, and it doesn't say what the trial involves (length, card required). The headline already sets up the comparison: the week you planned against the week your team actually works. Give the ask the same kind of comparison, using something the viewer already has. For example, "See this week's real plan from your Jira and calendar." Then they judge Weekplan against their own week, not against your claim. At minimum, add the trial length and card terms under the button, because those are what people hold you to. This point is a copy defect, not a checklist rule.
3. **The copy is generic.** The headline is good: "the week your team *actually* works" has a real point of view. The subhead is close but abstract, because "honest picture" is a label, not something that happens. Say what it does for the reader, such as "so you can see who's actually free before you promise the date." That puts the consequence in their terms. This one is taste.

**What's working.**
- **The type hierarchy.** There are three clear levels: the very large, tightly spaced bold headline, the regular-weight grey subhead, and the bold button text. They differ in size, weight and colour together, so there's never doubt about where to start. Keep it.
- **One left edge.** The wordmark, headline, subhead and button all sit on the same line at x≈120, and the nav ends exactly on the right margin. Whatever you add should keep to that frame.
- **Restraint.** There's one accent colour, used only on the single primary action, and the subhead runs about 70 characters per line in a comfortable size and grey.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button and nav links. Also how the headline wraps at narrow widths, since a three- or four-line headline at mobile size could push the button off the first screen.
