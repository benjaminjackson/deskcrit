**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month", then down to the tall June bar, then right to $4.82M. It reads as a credit union's half-year report to members: calm, orderly, trustworthy. What you notice next is the diagonal hatching in the bars, which shimmers.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling members of Brookhollow Credit Union how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026, in a form they can take in at a glance.

**Verdict.** It mostly works: the layout is clean, the headline states a finding, the figures add up (the six bars sum to the $4.82M total), and every number has a comparison. What holds it back is the chart, which pulls the eye to the wrong thing. The striped fill shimmers, and the bar that dominates the picture, June, is mostly an exception that only the footnote explains.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June bar tells a story the page doesn't stand behind (defect).** The June bar is about 33% taller than May, while the other months rise by about 7–10% each. It is the first thing the eye lands on after the headline, so the takeaway becomes "deposits surged in June". The only explanation is in the grey source line under the card: "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll." Few members will read that far, so they will remember a jump that is really a one-off. Put a short note on or next to the June bar itself, something like "includes bonus payroll". Better still, show the bonus as a separate, lighter part of the bar, so the underlying growth is what the eye compares. This is common sense, not a rule from an approved source: the note belongs where the eye already is.
2. **The headline claim has nothing to measure it against (defect).** "Rose every month" only shows six months of one year. If deposits rise every January to June, for example because of tax refunds and then the bonus, the headline says nothing about how this year did. The real news is in the top-right card: "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025". That year-on-year comparison is what members can judge the credit union by, and the chart doesn't show it. Add 2025 as quiet grey bars or a line next to each month, or change the headline to the claim the card supports. Again, this is my reasoning, not a cited rule.
3. **The hatched fill is chartjunk (convention).** The six bars are filled with tight blue and white diagonal stripes. They carry no data, they make the bars vibrate on screen, and they make the bar tops look softer and harder to compare. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5, and Few call this chartjunk (moiré patterns). Use a flat blue fill. If the stripes are meant to show something, such as a provisional figure, that meaning needs a legend, and then it belongs on one bar, not all six.

**Smaller things.**
- Units are mixed: the chart says "$1150k" and "$1200k", but the card says "$4.82M". Use "$1.15M" and "$1.2M" on the chart, or the same style everywhere, so members don't have to convert.
- "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025" and "Up $11 on Jun 2025" read awkwardly to a stranger, because "on" can sound like a date. "Up 6.1% from Jan–Jun 2025" or "vs June 2025" removes the doubt. This matters because these are the figures members will quote back.

**What's working.**
- The headline states a finding, not a topic, and the subtitle says exactly what is being counted ("New money paid into member savings accounts, by month"). Keep that habit.
- Every figure on the bars has a direct label, the bars start at $0, and the gridlines are pale, so apart from the fill the chart is honest and easy to read.
- Each of the three cards follows the same pattern: label with period, big number, green change against last year. That makes them quick to scan, and each one answers "compared to what?"
