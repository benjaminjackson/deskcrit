**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board" in heavy near-black, then drops into three short paragraphs. Within a second it reads as a passenger info page for a river ferry, for someone about to travel. It feels calm and plain, maybe a little generic, but it's easy to read. The type does its job. The words are where it falls short.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or panel that tells first-time ferry passengers what they need to know before they get on.

**Verdict.** The layout and type work: there's one clear place to start, a clean three-level hierarchy and an easy column to read. The real failure is the copy. It describes the service but leaves out the facts a passenger plans a trip around, so after reading it they still can't plan the trip.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable copy gives no times a passenger can act on.** *Defect.* In the first paragraph, "between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat leaves. Someone heading for the last crossing has to guess whether "late evening" means 9 pm or 11 pm. A wrong guess strands them on the wrong bank. "Every forty minutes" has no starting point, so they can't work out when the next boat goes. In the third paragraph, "in winter" has no dates, so in November a reader can't tell whether the hourly timetable applies yet. The line then sends them to "the shelter at each landing" for printed timetables, which is the place they are trying to plan for and haven't reached. These are the times and dates people hold an operator to, so vague wording here costs more than a style slip. Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing (for example "first boat 6:20 from north landing, last 22:40 from market quay"), the minute the 40-minute cycle starts, and the months the winter timetable runs. Put the full timetable, or a link to it, on this page and not only in the shelter.

**Smaller things.**
- **Line length:** the first paragraph's lines look like about 83–84 characters, past Bringhurst's 45–75 range (*Elements of Typographic Style* §2.1.2). The paragraphs are only two or three lines long, so the cost is small. Narrow the column by about 100 px so lines land near 66 characters.
- **Bicycle paragraph (taste):** it describes what "riders" do in the third person ("Riders wheel them on…"). Speaking to the reader would make it an instruction they can follow: "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on and lock it to the side rail."
- **Eyebrow (taste):** "Passenger notes" is a category label. It tells the reader nothing the heading doesn't already say. Either drop it or replace it with the route name ("North landing – Market quay") so the page says which ferry it is about.

**What's working.**
- **Hierarchy:** there are three clearly separate levels: a small tracked-out caps eyebrow, a large bold heading and regular body text. The heading differs from the body in both size and weight, so you know at once where to start. Keep that scale and don't add sizes in between.
- **Spacing:** the eyebrow sits tight to the heading, and the heading sits close to the body. The paragraphs are separated by space alone, with no indent. Groups read correctly without any rules or boxes.
- **Plain setting:** it's a readable sans in dark grey on white, comfortable line spacing and flush-left lines with a gentle rag. Nothing gets in the way of reading.
