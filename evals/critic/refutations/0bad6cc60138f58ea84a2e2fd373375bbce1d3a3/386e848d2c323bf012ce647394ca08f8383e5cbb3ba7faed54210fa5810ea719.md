**First read.** The eye lands on "Plan the week with your whole team", then jumps across to the blue "Create account" bar. Nothing else competes. It reads as a sign-up page for a team planning tool. It feels calm, tidy and competent, and it is also generic enough to belong to any of a hundred SaaS products.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the sign-up page of a team planning product, meant to turn a visitor who already showed some interest into a new account.

**Verdict.** The layout and form craft are sound, and the page will work for someone who has already decided to sign up. What holds it back is the words. The headline says "your whole team" and the line under it says "up to five people", and the left half never shows or says what makes this tool worth the sign-up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The offer contradicts itself where the reader decides (defect).** The headline in the left column promises planning "with your whole team". The line below caps the free workspace at "up to five people" and then says "You can invite the rest of the team later." That last sentence can be read two ways:
   - you can add more people later for free, or
   - inviting anyone past five means paying.

   This is the free-plan limit, the line a sign-up will later hold you to. A team of eight who reads it the first way and then hits a paywall will feel misled. A reader who suspects the second reading will hesitate here. Say plainly what happens at the sixth person, for example "Free for up to five people. Larger teams start at $X per person." Then either make the headline fit that limit or drop "whole".
2. **The left half makes no case for the product (taste).** That column is a wordmark, one headline and one sentence, with a large empty area below it. "Plan the week with your whole team" could sit on any calendar, task or standup tool, so a visitor comparing options learns nothing about why this one is different. The empty space is deliberate and works as a resting area, so the fix is not to fill it with clutter. Put one specific thing there, such as a small glimpse of the weekly board or one concrete line about what a team sees on Monday. The best version sets it against what the viewer uses now, like "Replaces the Monday planning spreadsheet", so they can judge the value on terms they already know.
3. **The form makes people guess its rules (convention).**
   - The password rule "At least 10 characters" lives only in the placeholder. It disappears as soon as the user starts typing, which is exactly when they need it (Nielsen, heuristic 6: recognition rather than recall). Move it under the field as helper text that stays visible.
   - None of the five fields is marked required or optional. "Company" in particular looks like it might be skippable, and a user only finds out on submit (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms"). Add "(optional)" after any optional label, or asterisks plus a one-line legend.

**Smaller things.**
- "Sign in" at the bottom of the card has the same grey, weight and size as "Already have an account?" and no underline, so it doesn't look clickable. Returning users are the ones who need it. Underline it or give it the link colour (Nielsen Norman Group on flat links; WCAG 1.4.1).
- The input borders look very pale grey on white, probably below the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for on field edges. Measure the border colour against white and darken it if it falls short.

**What's working.**
- There is one clear primary action. The full-width blue button is the only saturated element on the page besides the wordmark, so the eye always knows where the form ends.
- The type has three clean levels (headline, card title, labels and body) separated by weight and size, with no near-duplicate sizes. The two columns start on a shared top line: the wordmark and "Create your account" sit level.
- The form is well built. Every field has a visible label above it, the fields are in one column, and the only side-by-side fields are first and last name, a short related pair. The email placeholder "name@company.com" quietly tells people to use their work address.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings and hover and error states on the fields and button. For a sign-up form, check in a browser that focus is clearly visible and that errors name the field.
- Whether the email and password fields use the right input types.
- How the two columns reflow on a phone, where the pitch might push the form below the fold.
