**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks", then drops to the orange button. It reads as a consumer language-learning app's landing page: calm, tidy and friendly. It also feels anonymous. The teal speech-bubble panel on the right registers as "illustration goes here", and the grey "Keep your streak going" card under the button makes you stop and wonder whether it's a notification you're meant to dismiss.

**What it's for.** My assumption: it's the hero of a marketing homepage, and it has to get adult beginners who've never heard of Parlo to start the free week.

**Verdict.** The basics are sound: one clear promise, one clear action, a three-step explanation. But the piece doesn't yet sell. The one thing that could make it different, a tutor that listens and gives feedback on your speech, is described in words and never shown. Meanwhile the right half is spent on a generic icon, and the spot right under the button goes to a message written for people who already use the app.

**What would move it most**

1. **The illustration repeats the headline instead of proving it.** The teal panel (top right, about 400×300 px) shows two empty speech bubbles. "Conversation" is already in the headline, so the picture adds nothing. It's also the part of the page a sceptical visitor would look to for evidence, and here it's a placeholder you'd find on any chat, support or messaging product. The claim that would sell this is "Say it back and get feedback on each word", and nobody can see it. The fix is to use that space to show the product doing its job: a phrase, the learner's attempt, and per-word feedback marked right or wrong. Then the picture and the headline make one argument, "you'll be talking in ten weeks, and here's how it catches your mistakes", rather than saying the same thing twice. *Taste, argued on its merits. It's also the biggest lever on the page.*

2. **The streak card is aimed at the wrong person and sits in the wrong place.** The grey box directly under the CTA has the heading "Keep your streak going". A first-time visitor has no streak. The heading names a habit-tracking feature, not what the box holds, which is a statistic about who finishes the course (WCAG 2.4.6 asks headings to describe their content). The copy also leaves its number hanging: "twice as often" as whom? Because it's set as a tinted, rounded box about the size of the button, it looks like a second control or an in-app toast. It sits in the most valuable spot after the button and slows the step from the button to the decision. Either cut it from the hero, or rewrite it as reassurance aimed at the newcomer: "Five minutes a day is enough: learners who practise five days a week finish twice as often as those who don't". Then set it as plain text under the button, not as a box. *Defect: it confuses the intended viewer.*

3. **The hero doesn't sit on the grid that the rest of the page uses.** The three steps below divide the page into clean thirds, from 56 px to 1224 px. The illustration panel runs from 676 px to 1076 px, so it lines up with none of those columns and stops about 150 px short of the right edge, where "Log in" and step 3 both end. Its bottom edge (around 410 px) doesn't meet the button or the card either. The result is a light, unfinished right side and a top half that looks placed by eye while the bottom half looks gridded (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*; Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on balance). Run the visual to the right margin. Start it on the step-2 or step-3 column line. End its bottom on a line the left column also uses. *Convention, and here it costs something: the page looks less finished than it is.*

4. **The copy leaves the buyer to fill gaps at exactly the points they'll later hold you to.**
   - "Start your free week": does it need a card, and does it turn into a paid plan on day 8? A visitor who isn't sure will either hesitate or feel tricked later. Put one line next to the button, for example "No card needed", or "Then $X/month, cancel anytime".
   - "Short daily lessons" is a length with no unit. Say "10 minutes a day".
   - "A tutor that listens" can be read as a person or as software. That changes what the visitor expects from the price and from the experience, so say which it is.
   - "Finish with a short chat about your day": chat with whom?

   Each of these is a word or two. Each one removes a reason to hold back from clicking. *Defect: these lines can be read two ways at the point of commitment.*

**Smaller things.**
- The "1. Listen / 2. Speak / 3. Use it" headings are about 17 px bold, and the card heading is about 18 px bold, next to an 18 px subhead. They're told apart by weight, not size, which works, but tie the card and step headings to one size.
- The step descriptions are generic ("Hear a short phrase spoken by a native speaker"). One concrete example phrase per step would do more than a description of the step.

**What's working.**
- The headline is a specific, checkable promise with a timeframe. That's far better than "Learn a language the fun way". Keep its size and its tight two-line setting.
- There's exactly one primary action, and it's the only warm colour on the page. The eye goes from headline to button without any competition. Keep the orange for that button only.
- The three-step strip with hairlines above each step is quiet and evenly spaced, and it explains the method in a few seconds. Its grid is the one the hero should adopt.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the page continues below the fold. The empty band at the bottom suggests more content but doesn't show it.
- Hover and focus states on the button and the navigation.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 5 yes, 3 no (image and headline make one idea; every element does a job; headings name their section)
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 11 yes, 2 no (one grid; visual weight balances)
Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: 12 lines, 12 yes
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 32 yes, 1 unsure (focus indicator)
