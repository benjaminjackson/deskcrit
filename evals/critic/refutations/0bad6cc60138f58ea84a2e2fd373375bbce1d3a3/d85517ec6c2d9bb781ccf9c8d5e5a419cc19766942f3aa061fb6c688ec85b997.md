The checklist and deep read are done; I'm writing up the final critique now.

**First read.** The eye goes to "Revenue overview", then to $4.82M in the first card, then across to 112% and 1,246, and then down the blue bars climbing to the right. Within three seconds it reads as the home screen of a SaaS finance tool, telling a founder or finance lead that revenue grew all year. It feels calm, competent and trustworthy. Nothing shouts.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a SaaS revenue dashboard that lets a founder or finance lead check annual revenue, retention and growth at a glance, and trust the numbers enough to repeat them.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, disciplined dashboard that does its job: the hierarchy is clear, the chart is honest and every number has context. The one real problem is that the numbers don't agree. The "Revenue by plan, 2025" table adds up to the ARR figure, not to the 2025 revenue the chart shows. On a screen people quote from, that matters more than everything else here combined.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plan table's figures don't match its label or the chart (defect).** The table on the right is headed "Revenue by plan, 2025" and its Total is $4.82M. That's exactly the "Annual recurring revenue, Dec 2025" figure in the first card. Add up the twelve monthly bars, which are labelled recognised revenue, and you get roughly $4.49M (about 310 + 328 + 340 + … + 447 thousand). A reader who checks will find two different 2025 revenue totals on one screen. One of two things is happening:
   - the table is really ARR by plan and the heading is wrong, or
   - it really is 2025 revenue and the figures are wrong.

   Either way, whoever repeats $4.82M as "2025 revenue" in a board deck will be wrong, and the screen loses trust everywhere else too. These are figures people act on, so it's a defect, not a wording note. Fix: make the heading say exactly what the table sums. If it's ARR, call it "ARR by plan, Dec 2025". If it's revenue, the total has to match the chart, with a footnote if the two definitions really differ. (WCAG 2.4.6 asks that headings describe their content; here the heading describes something else.)

**Smaller things.**
- The "Revenue by plan, 2025" heading sits about 4 px below "Monthly revenue, 2025" across the gutter. It's a near miss, not a choice. Put the two headings on one baseline (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). A subtitle line under the table heading, to match the chart's "Thousands of US dollars…", would also explain what the table counts.
- The chart title only names the topic. Something like "Monthly revenue grew 43% from January to December" would say the finding the bars already show. That's taste, not a rule: the takeaway is the reason the chart is there.
- The plan table has no comparison, unlike the three cards. A small "vs 2024" column would tell the reader which plan is driving the 18% growth (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").
- The "Jan to Dec 2025" pill at top right could be a date-range control or just a label. It has no chevron or icon, and its pale grey fill looks well under 3:1 against white. If it's a control, give it a visible edge and a dropdown cue (WCAG 1.4.11). If it's only a label, drop the pill shape so it doesn't look clickable.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards.** Each pairs a precise label with a period ("Net revenue retention, 2025"), one big figure, and a signed change against last year ("+3 pts vs 2024"). That's what makes the numbers readable at a glance. Keep this pattern exactly, and use it in the plan table too.
- **An honest chart.** It's one blue series on a zero baseline, with light gridlines, units in the subtitle and a source line naming "closed invoices only". Nothing extra competes with the data (Tufte; Wilke).
- **A tight type scale.** I count about four sizes: page title, KPI figures, section headings, and body/labels. Grey and weight do the finer distinctions. The sidebar shows the current page with a quiet tint and no extra colour.
