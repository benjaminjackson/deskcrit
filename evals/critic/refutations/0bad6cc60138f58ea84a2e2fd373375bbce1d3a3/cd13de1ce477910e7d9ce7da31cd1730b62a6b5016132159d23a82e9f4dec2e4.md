**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, "How the river ferry runs in winter," then reads down two short paragraphs to the pale grey box at the bottom. It reads as a plain, calm notice from whoever runs the ferry. It's quiet and orderly, and nothing fights for attention. The lower half of the frame is empty, which looks like the page simply ends there.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or notice that tells riders when the winter ferry runs, what can delay it, and what vehicles pay. A rider should be able to find a crossing time at a glance.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and sensibly built, but it falls short of its main job. Someone checking when the ferry leaves has to read sentences to find the times, and the lines are too long to read comfortably. Both problems are easy to fix.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable is buried in prose, and the times are spelled out in words.** The first paragraph holds everything a rider comes for: the season, the 40-minute interval, the first boat and the last boat. It's all run into two sentences as "every forty minutes," "seven in the morning" and "six in the evening." A rider scanning for "when's the last boat?" can't spot it. They have to read the paragraph, then turn "six in the evening" into 18:00 in their head. People skim a page like this rather than read it (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"), and numbers are what they skim for. Times and dates are also what riders act on and later hold the operator to. Make them findable without reading: set the times as figures (07:00, 18:00, every 40 min) and pull them out of the sentence into a short labelled block, such as "First boat / Last boat / Every." Give that block more weight than the ice-delay note, because it's the most important information on the page. This is a defect: it slows down the one task the page exists for.
2. **Lines run about 100–105 characters.** Both paragraphs stretch across the full 880 px column. For example, "From November to March the ferry leaves the north landing every forty minutes. The first crossing is at" is one line. Bringhurst puts the comfortable range at 45 to 75 characters (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). The text is only two lines per paragraph, so the cost is small here. It will grow as soon as the page holds more than these few sentences. Narrow the text column to roughly 600–640 px at this size, keep the headline and box on the same left edge, and let the box narrow with the text. This breaks a published convention, and the cost is mild.

**Smaller things.**
- Grey box: the space below the text (about 45 px) is close to twice the space above it (about 24 px), so the box looks like it lost a line. Make the padding even on all sides. This is common sense, not a cited rule.
- "Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue" is the only instruction to the rider, and it's tucked at the end of a paragraph. If ice delays are common in winter, it deserves its own line. This is taste.
- "Cars pay the standard fare" assumes the reader already knows the fare. Give the amount, or link to it. A rider deciding whether to drive on needs the number.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: one bold headline and one body size, set apart by both size and weight. That keeps it obvious where to start. Don't add in-between sizes when you add the timetable block; use weight for that.
- The headline is in plain language and says exactly what the page covers.
- The tinted box is used once, for one group (vehicle rules), and lines up on the same left edge as everything else. It sets that information apart without adding clutter.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone. At narrow widths the line-length problem goes away, but check that the box keeps its padding.
