**First read.** The eye lands on "Account settings", then moves down the four white cards, scanning bold title to grey line to blue button. Then it catches the tinted "Billing" in the sidebar and stops, because the page isn't about billing. It reads as a settings hub for a team SaaS product. It feels calm, competent and generic.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the overview of account settings in a workspace tool for teams: an admin or member lands here to find and change one setting quickly.

**Verdict.** The craft is clean and the layout mostly works, but the navigation is wrong. The sidebar says you're on Billing while the page shows an overview, and the sidebar and the cards list different sets of sections. That makes the viewer stop and wonder where they are and where things live, which is exactly what a settings page should never cause.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sidebar marks the wrong page.** "Billing" (left column, third item) has the selected tint and bold blue text, but the content is the "Account settings" overview with Billing as one card of four. The viewer gets two answers to "where am I?" and has to work out which one to trust. They may also click "Billing" expecting nothing to change. *Defect* (Nielsen heuristic 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist", guideline 5). Fix: mark the current page in the sidebar with one clear cue that matches the heading. Either this overview gets its own sidebar entry ("Overview" or "All settings") and that entry is selected, or this screen is Billing and the content changes to match.
2. **The sidebar and the cards describe two different products.** The sidebar lists Profile, Notifications, Billing, Members, Integrations and Security. The cards list Profile, Notifications, Billing and Sessions. "Sessions" has no sidebar item, and Members, Integrations and Security have no card, so the viewer can't tell whether Sessions is part of Security or a separate area, or why half the sections are missing from the overview. *Defect* (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). Fix: give both lists one structure with the same names, in the same order, covering the same set. If Sessions belongs under Security, name the card "Security" and keep "Sessions" in its description.
3. **The Billing card holds back the facts a viewer acts on.** "Your plan renews on 1 March" gives no amount, no plan name and no year. "The address on file" doesn't say which address. Renewal is where money moves, and the viewer has to click "View invoices" to learn what they'll be charged, which that button doesn't promise to show. *Common sense* (the catalog's entry here has no approved source). Fix: put the plan, the amount and the full date in the line, e.g. "Team plan, £240, renews 1 March 2027", and name the address or the billing email.

**Smaller things.**
- "You are signed in on three now" (Sessions card): say "three devices" so the number has its unit.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: a large title, bold card titles, grey body text, with clear steps and weight doing the fine distinctions. Keep it.
- Each card pairs one plain sentence that tells you what the setting does to you ("Your name and photo appear on comments and in the member list") with one specifically named action ("View invoices", "Review"), not a generic "Settings". That's the right copy pattern; carry it to the missing sections.
- Four equal outlined buttons and no forced primary action is the right call for a hub of peer settings.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards and buttons show a visible focus ring for keyboard users.
