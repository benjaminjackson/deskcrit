**First read.** My eye goes to the headline, then straight to the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%, then across the other three bars. It reads as a clean product-analytics page comparing how often each plan's trials turn into paying accounts. It feels calm and trustworthy, maybe a little too calm: nothing tells me whether these numbers are good, bad, or changing.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal analytics page where a product or growth team reads Q3 trial-to-paid conversion by plan and decides where to act. Correct me if that's wrong.

**Verdict.** It's well made and honest in form, but it doesn't yet do its job. It shows four rates with no comparison and no sense of how reliable they are, so a reader will rank the plans on differences the data may not support. The definition of "converted" can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**

1. **The bars look like a clear ranking, but the data mostly can't support one.** The table's counts tell the story. Enterprise's 3.4% rests on 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. At that size, by my rough calculation, the 95% range is about ±1 point, roughly 2.3% to 4.5%, which overlaps every other plan. Growth's 4.0% (118 of 2,950) is about ±0.7 points. Only Starter is tight, at about ±0.35. Meanwhile the bars fill most of a 0–5% axis, so a 0.9-point gap looks like a big difference in height. A reader will come away thinking "Growth converts best, Starter worst" and may move budget on that. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) asks for samples to be shown with their uncertainty. Add a thin interval to each bar, or at least a line saying which gaps are real. *Defect.*

2. **Nothing tells the reader whether 3–4% is good.** Every bar stands alone: there's no previous quarter, no target, and no overall rate across plans. The reader can compare the plans with each other but can't tell whether conversion got better or worse, or whether it's on plan. That's usually the question someone opens this page to answer. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") flags metrics shown without a comparison. Fix: put the period and a comparison next to each number, such as a Q2 marker on each bar or a "vs Q2" change under each label, plus a reference line for the blended rate. *Convention, and it costs a lot here.*

3. **The definition can be read two ways, and the numbers depend on which one is right.** The subtitle says "within 30 days." The note says a trial converts "when the first invoice is paid" and that trials "still open on 30 October are counted as not converted."
   - **Reading A:** each trial gets its own 30-day window, and 30 October just closes the window for trials started on 30 September.
   - **Reading B:** 30 October is one cut-off for everyone. Then a July trial had about three months to pay while a late-September trial had 30 days. That would make the subtitle wrong and push the rates up.

   This is the line people will quote and hold the page to, so it has to be exact. Say it outright: "A trial counts as converted if its first invoice is paid within 30 days of the trial starting." *Defect (copy).*

4. **The headline names the topic, not the finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" is a label. Once items 1 and 2 are settled, the title should say what the reader should take away, for example "Conversion held at 3–4% across plans; no plan clearly ahead," or whatever the data actually supports. Right now the reader has to work it out, and the tallest bar will do it for them, probably wrongly. *Taste.* I'm arguing this on its own terms, not citing a rule: on a decision page, the title is the sentence people repeat in meetings.

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honest and sparse.** The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, the value labels sit right on the bars, and there's one colour because there's one measure. Keep all of that when you add intervals and comparisons.
- **The table next to the chart.** It shows the counts behind each rate (the rates check out: 286/9,210 = 3.1%, and so on), the figures are right-aligned, and the plans are in the same order as the chart. That's what makes the sample-size problem visible at all. Consider adding a rate column so the reader doesn't have to divide.
- **The navigation shows where you are.** "Conversion" is marked by weight as well as colour.
