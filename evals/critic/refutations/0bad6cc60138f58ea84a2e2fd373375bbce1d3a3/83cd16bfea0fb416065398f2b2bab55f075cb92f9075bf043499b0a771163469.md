**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline, "Notes from the boat shed", and drops to the left paragraph. It reads like a quiet local journal: unhurried, warm, a bit literary. Only when the eye reaches the end of the right column does it find out this is really an invitation to volunteer, and by then the page has ended, leaving about 60% of the frame blank under it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page for a community boat-restoration group, meant to get local readers to turn up and help.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and calm, but the page doesn't do its job. It invites people in without telling them where, when, or how. The headline and layout present it as a journal entry, so the invitation reads as an aside instead of the point.

**What would move it most**
1. **The ask has nothing to act on.** In the right column, "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings" gives no time, no address beyond "the end of the lane", and no contact or way to say you're coming. A stranger who wants to help still has to find out which lane, what time, and whether to just show up. Most won't bother. Krug's test is that a page tells people within seconds what they can do, and this one doesn't. Fix: put the specifics in words a newcomer can act on, such as "Come any Thursday from 6 to 9 pm at [address]. Just turn up, or text [number] first." Set it apart from the descriptive paragraph so it can be found at a glance. *Defect.*
2. **The headline frames this as a diary, not a welcome.** "Notes from the boat shed" names a genre, not what the reader gets. It tells them they're about to read musings, so they read the volunteer line as colour rather than an offer. The two columns carry equal weight, so nothing marks the right column as the purpose. Fix: either make the headline or a subhead say the offer (something like "Help us varnish three old dinghies this spring"), or keep the journal title and give the invitation its own clearly heavier block below the two paragraphs. *Defect; arguing from common sense, since the "one idea" entry has no approved source.*
3. **The richest copy has no picture.** The writing is sensory (tar, cut cedar, clinker dinghies on trestles), but the page shows none of it, and the blank lower half is where a photo of the shed or the boats would naturally go. One honest photo would do more to bring a stranger in than any line of copy. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.** None worth listing. The text setting holds up on every line.

**What's working.**
- The type: one serif family, a bold headline against regular text, so the two levels differ in weight as well as size. Body text looks about 17 px with comfortable line spacing, and both columns run about 60–65 characters a line, inside Bringhurst's 45–75. Keep all of that.
- The two columns share baselines and sit on clean, equal margins. The page feels ordered without any rules or boxes.
- The copy itself is good. "There is always a kettle on, and a spare apron on the hook by the door" is the line that will win people over. It already speaks to the reader's real worry, that they have no experience. Keep it and build the ask around it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether there's more page below the fold, such as a map, contact details or a sign-up. If there is, the blank lower half of this capture means none of it shows on first view, and the ask in finding 1 should still move up.
