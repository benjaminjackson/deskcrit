**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three short paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry: calm and tidy, but airy to the point of looking unfinished. The body text doesn't read as paragraphs. It reads as a stack of separate lines spaced evenly down the left side of the screen.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers when the boat runs and how to bring a bicycle, read quickly by someone planning a trip.

**Verdict.** The page works as plain reading matter. Two things hold it back. The line spacing is so loose that the paragraphs fall apart. And the copy leaves out the one thing a passenger came for: actual times they can act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The line spacing breaks the paragraphs apart (defect).** In all three paragraphs the lines sit about 45 px apart on roughly 18 px type, which is about 2.5 times the type size. Each line floats on its own, so at the end of a line the eye has to search for the start of the next one, and the text reads as a list of lines instead of sentences. The lines run 65 to 72 characters, which needs a little more than the default spacing, not this much (Bringhurst §2.2.1; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, p. 47: the leading should suit the line length). Fix: bring it down to about 1.5 times the type size so each paragraph reads as one block.
2. **Paragraph breaks barely show (defect).** The gap between paragraphs (about 64 px) is only about 20 px more than the gap between lines (about 45 px). Because of that, the three topics (schedule, bicycles, winter) don't separate at a glance. Grouping by nearness depends on the gaps inside a group being clearly smaller than the gaps between groups (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II). Tightening the line spacing fixes most of this. Keep the paragraph gap at about one full line, or more.
3. **The copy gives no times you can plan around (defect, copy).** "Early morning and late evening", "every forty minutes" with no starting time, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" all leave the passenger to guess. "Before dusk" could mean an hour before dusk or five minutes before. Whoever guesses wrong is stuck on the wrong bank. These are exactly the details people act on and later hold the operator to. Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing, with exact times, and the winter dates and last-boat time. A small timetable would do this better than prose.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" is generic. Something like "Times, bikes and winter service on the river ferry" would tell readers what's on the page before they start reading.
- The bicycle paragraph doesn't say what riders should do with their bike while waiting, or whether space on the lower deck is limited. It's worth stating if either is true.

**What's working.**
- There's one clear focal point. A bold heading in the same family as the body is enough hierarchy for a page this short, so keep the two-size scale.
- The lines are a comfortable length, set flush left in a plain, readable sans in near-black on white. The contrast and the type choice need no changes.
- The short paragraphs with one topic each are the right structure. They just need the spacing to make the breaks visible.
