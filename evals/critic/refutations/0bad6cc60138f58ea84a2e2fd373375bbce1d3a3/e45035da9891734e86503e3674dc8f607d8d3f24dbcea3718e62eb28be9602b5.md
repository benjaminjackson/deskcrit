**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline first, then gets pulled hard to the first card. Its photo is the only dark, detailed, photographic thing on the page: a wood-panelled room with leaded windows at night. From there the eye goes across the four cards, lands on the green "From $" prices, and drops into an empty cream band with nothing more to do. The page reads as a calm, trustworthy home-services site for homeowners. The first card looks like it came from a different website.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a local home-services company, meant to get homeowners to pick a service and book a visit.

**Verdict.** This is a clean, competent page that mostly does its job: you know what it is, what's on offer, and roughly what it costs within seconds. The one thing badly in the way is the Window cleaning photo. It breaks the card set, it's dark and dated in a page that is light and friendly, and it doesn't show the service. Second: the cards end at a price with no way forward.

**What would move it most**
1. **The Window cleaning photo breaks the set and sells the wrong thing.** It's top left, in the first of four matching cards. The other three use flat mint-panel illustrations. This one is a dim, tungsten-lit photo of what looks like a college common room, with the windows black from night outside. Because it's the only photo and the only dark area on the page, it grabs attention before the headline is done, and what it shows is an old, gloomy room, not clean glass. Someone looking for window cleaning sees dark windows. It also tells them the set was assembled rather than designed, which costs a little trust on a page that asks strangers into their home. This is a **defect** (it pulls focus and misleads), backed by **convention**: elements of one kind share one style, and a variant appears only where the rules call for it (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*). The direction of the fix: replace it with an illustration in the same flat style as the other three, such as a window with a squeegee. If you want photos, make all four cards photos with one light, bright grade.

2. **The cards stop at a price and leave the viewer with nowhere to go.** The subhead promises "Pick a service, choose a day", and the cards are the "pick a service" step. But each card ends at "From $89" with no action, and nothing marks the cards as clickable. Below them is about 150 px of empty cream. The only booking route is the small "Book a visit" button in the top-right corner, which is the one place the eye has already left. A viewer who has just picked Laundry pickup has to work out for themselves where to click. This is a **defect** (it slows the main task) and the argument is common sense, not a cited rule. Direction: give each card its own specific action, such as "Book window cleaning", or make the whole card an obvious link to that service's booking step. Then the page carries out the two-step promise the subhead makes.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline, top left: it breaks with "schedule" alone on the second line. Widen the text box a little or rebalance the break so the headline reads as one unit, for example "Help around the house, / on your schedule".
- "From $" prices: "From" with no unit leaves Laundry at "$24" open to reading as per load, per bag, or per week. Add the unit ("From $24 a load") so the viewer knows what they are being held to.

**What's working.**
- The headline and subhead. "On your schedule" plus "a vetted crew arrives with their own supplies" answer the two worries a homeowner has (timing and trust) in specific, plain words. Keep that voice.
- The type and colour system. A sturdy serif for the brand and headline, a plain sans for everything else, one dark teal for the brand and button, and green reserved for prices. It is calm, warm, and easy to scan, and the price colour makes the cost findable across all four cards in one sweep.
- The card structure: picture, name, one line of detail, then price, with the prices aligned along one baseline. It compares cleanly. Keep it when you fix card 1.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards are clickable or have hover states, which would partly answer finding 2. How the four-card row reflows on a phone.
