**First read.** The eye lands on "Landing stage rebuild", drops into the intro paragraph and the flat illustration, and then moves right to "Get timetable changes" and the blue Sign up button. It reads as a short news item from a small ferry operator with a subscription box next to it. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. On the way down, the blue boat in the illustration flashes as a second button.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an article page for a ferry service that tells passengers about the rebuilt landing and gets them to sign up for timetable-change notes.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and spacing are clean and do their job. But the sign-up form can't work as shown: it promises to "send one short note" and never asks where to send it. The one action on the page is broken.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form has no email or phone field.** The card on the right asks only for "Your name" and "Home landing". The note under the button then says "We send one short note when the timetable changes." A passenger who fills this in has given the service no way to reach them, and a careful reader will notice before pressing Sign up and lose trust in the whole box. *Defect* (common sense; no approved source covers it). Fix: add the contact field and make it the first or most prominent one. If the service offers more than one channel, let people choose (email or text). "Your name" is the field you can drop, because nothing on the page needs it.
2. **The boat in the illustration looks like the Sign up button.** The blue rounded bar on the water, at the right of the figure, is the same saturated blue, the same height and the same corner radius as the Sign up button. It's the only saturated colour on the page apart from the button. Elements that look alike are read as related (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*), so the eye pairs a drawing with the only action, and on a screen it reads as something to click. *Convention.* Fix: keep the brand blue for things you can act on. Draw the boat in a different blue or a darker tone, or give it a hull shape so it can't be mistaken for a button.
3. **"Home landing" is a blank text box for a fixed list of places.** The service has a known set of landings, but people have to type theirs freely. They'll misspell it, abbreviate it, or type a landing that doesn't exist, and the timetable match fails without anyone noticing. Nielsen's heuristic 5, error prevention, says to constrain the input. *Defect.* Fix: use a select menu or radio list of the landings, ideally with the north landing from this article pre-selected.

**Smaller things.**
- Footer: "Ferry timetables are a sample for a fixture image. All names and places are invented." This is placeholder copy and has to be replaced or removed before the page ships.
- Form fields: none are marked required or optional. With only two or three fields this costs little. Once you add a contact field, put "(optional)" after any field people can skip, such as name (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group).

**What's working.**
- **Clear type levels.** There's one dominant bold heading, a smaller bold panel heading, comfortable body text at about 60–65 characters a line with generous leading, and small captions. Each size has an obvious job.
- **The second paragraph lands on the reader.** "The ramps now meet the deck at every tide, so prams and wheelchairs roll straight on" turns a construction fact into what it means for a passenger. Keep that line, and consider moving it up, because it's the strongest sentence on the page.
- **The sign-up card is self-contained.** One bordered group with labels above the fields, a single primary button, and an honest reassurance line ("You can stop the notes at any time") right under the button. It's a sound pattern once the contact field is added.
