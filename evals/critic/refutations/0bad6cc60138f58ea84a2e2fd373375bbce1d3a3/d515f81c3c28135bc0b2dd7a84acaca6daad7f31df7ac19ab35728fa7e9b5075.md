**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "The allotment at the end of the bus line", and drops straight into the opening paragraph. It reads as a long-form feature from a small, calm local magazine. It feels clean and quiet, maybe too quiet: nothing on screen looks like an allotment, a bus stop or a person. Below "Most of them are still in use." the bottom quarter of the screen is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the opening screen of a magazine feature, which has to pull a general reader into a story about a community garden and keep them reading.

**Verdict.** The typography is competent and reading it is easy, but the page doesn't yet do the job of a feature opener. It credits "Photographs by the residents" and shows none, so a visual story about pallets, bath tubs and forty households opens as a grey block of text that could be any blog.

**What would move it most**
1. **The promised photographs are missing, so the story has no picture to sell it** (defect). The byline under the headline says "Photographs by the residents", but the screen has no image at all. A reader either thinks something failed to load or sees a page with nothing in it about this place: no beds, no tap queue, no Halima Osei. A feature opener usually relies on its lead image to make a stranger care, and this one makes the headline do all the work. If an image should sit here and didn't render, check it in a live browser. If the design is text-first on purpose, the byline is promising something the first screen doesn't deliver. Direction: put one strong resident photograph between the byline and the first paragraph, or full-bleed above the headline. Pick a picture that adds to the headline rather than repeating it, like the bath-tub beds or the Saturday queue. This is common sense and taste; no approved source covers it.
2. **The body lines are too long** (convention). The paragraphs run about 83 characters a line ("Ten years ago the plot behind the last stop was a car park nobody used. Today forty"). That's past Bringhurst's 45–75 range, and the ideal is about 66. The leading is generous, so it reads acceptably now. Over a long feature, though, the eye's return trip to the next line gets tiring, and this page is made for long reading. Direction: narrow the text column by about a fifth, to around 600–620 px at this type size, or go up a step in size at the same width. Measure the characters per line to confirm.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "bus / line" leaves "line" alone on the second line and splits the phrase "bus line" (Bringhurst 2.4.2 on short last-line words). Rebalance the break so it falls after "allotment" or "at the end", for example with balanced wrapping or a narrower headline box.
- Nav: "Features · Food · Places · Subscribe" doesn't mark the current section, even though the page is labelled "Feature". Give "Features" one cue, such as weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Copy, taste: "Subscribe" is the site's only ask, and it's set exactly like the section links, so it reads as a fourth category. Set it apart a little, even if it stays a text link.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: headline, body, then byline and nav, with the green "Feature" kicker as the one accent. Every level is obvious at a glance, and the colour is used with restraint. Keep the scale.
- The opening copy is very good. "The queue for the shared water tap is the best place in the district to hear the news" and "So the first beds were pallets and old bath tubs" are concrete and human, and they pull you on. The design should serve that writing, so it's worth getting the picture right.
- The spacing groups things well: kicker, headline and byline sit together, and the paragraph gaps are even with no indents (space or indent, not both).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The empty band under the last paragraph. It could be the end of the content, a lazy-loaded image, or a capture cut-off. Check in a browser what fills it.
- Hover and focus states on the nav links.
