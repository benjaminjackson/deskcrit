**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif heading, then gets pulled through the body by the three bold runs: "labelled with their ingredients", "collected by four", "on the field only". The two italic times, "arrive before ten" and "three o'clock sharp", are lighter, so they're the easiest to skim past. It reads as a short, calm notice for people helping at a village fete. It's tidy and well set.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming a web page or email that tells stallholders and helpers the practical rules for the day, so they arrive on time, follow the rules and park in the right place.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is good. The notice falls short as a set of instructions: the emphasis ranks the rules the wrong way round, and the copy leaves out the date and place that every rule depends on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out the facts that make it usable** (defect, common sense; no checklist entry covers it). "Arrive before ten" and "three o'clock sharp" don't say which day. "The field", "the trestle by the door" and "the box" assume the reader has already been there. It also doesn't say who each rule is for. Arriving early and labelling cakes are for stallholders. The raffle and parking rules are for anyone coming. A helper who wasn't at the planning meeting can't act on this without asking someone. These are the lines people hold you to on the day. "Before ten" with no date is the one most likely to be read wrong, for example by someone who arrives at 9:55 on the wrong Saturday. To fix it, put the date and place right under the heading, and say who each rule applies to ("Stallholders: please arrive…").
2. **Bold and italic are both used to mark deadlines, so the emphasis puts the wrong things first** (defect; Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind share one style). There are three times in the text. Two are italic ("before ten", "three o'clock sharp") and one is bold ("collected by four"). The bold also marks rules that aren't deadlines (ingredients, parking). The reader can see that bold means something, so the bold runs read as the main points. That leaves the earliest and most important deadline, the 10 o'clock arrival, as one of the quietest phrases on the page. Five emphasised phrases in four sentences also dilute each other. Pick one emphasis for the things people must act on, probably bold for the times. A better option is to pull the times and rules out into a short list with the time at the start of each line, so the notice can be scanned standing at a gate.

**Smaller things.**
- Heading: "fete" without the circumflex is fine in British usage. Use "fête" if the rest of your material does.

**What's working.**
- The text setting reads comfortably. Lines run about 70 characters, the line spacing is generous, there's clear space between the paragraphs, and the rag is even. Keep this measure and spacing if you add a list.
- The heading is clearly the first thing you see. It's a heavier, larger cut of the same serif family, so it contrasts by weight as well as size, and the page has one voice.
- The restraint suits the job. With one colour and one family, it feels neighbourly, not like an ad.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The apostrophe in "o'clock" looks straight, but at this size and in italic I can't tell. Check the character in the source.
