**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a visitor-information section for the lighthouse: it should make a reader want to go and tell them how.

**Verdict.** The setting is clean and the text reads well, but as a visitor page it doesn't do its job. The practical facts are vague and sit last in a soft paragraph. The image block also floats out of line with the text column, so the layout looks placed by eye rather than built.

**What would move it most**

1. **The visit is the least clear thing on the page (defect).** Everything a visitor needs is in paragraphs 2 and 3, as asides: "open to visitors on summer weekends", "Tickets are sold at the cottage by the car park".
   - The reader can't tell which months count as "summer", what the hours are, what a ticket costs, or where the car park is.
   - Someone planning a trip has to look elsewhere, or turns up at the wrong time.
   - The page has no ask at all; the last thing it says is about scones.
   - Fix: keep the history paragraph as the hook, then give the visit its own short block with:
     - dates and hours, e.g. "Open Saturdays and Sundays, June to September, 10–4"
     - the price
     - where to buy tickets and park
     - one exact next step, such as directions or booking a time.
   - The line about the view from the gallery is the best selling copy on the page ("you can follow the ferry all the way across the bay"). Keep it next to the visit details, where it can tip the decision.
   - This is common sense, not a published rule.

2. **The copy contradicts the picture (defect).** The first sentence says "the white tower at Gull Point", but the illustration shows a tower with a broad red band and a red cap. A reader who notices will wonder which one is right. A visitor looking for "the white tower" may doubt they've found the right one. Fix: make the illustration match the real tower, or change the words to describe it. The picture otherwise only repeats the headline, so matching it to the real tower would also give it a job.

3. **The image and the text share no edge (convention).**
   - The illustration is roughly 230–573 px tall on the page and looks centred on the canvas (its middle is around 400 px).
   - The text column starts at the headline, about 145 px, and ends around 497 px, so its middle is around 320 px.
   - So the image's top, middle and bottom each sit about 80–90 px off the text's. It drops below the paragraphs and starts well under the headline. It reads as placed independently, not as part of the same layout.
   - A placement that is neither clearly aligned nor clearly offset makes the eye waver (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I; Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*, on one grid governing every block).
   - Fix: align the image top with the cap height of the headline, or with the first line of body text, and let the text column set the vertical span.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 1 ends with "room." alone on its last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrap it, for instance with a non-breaking space in "lamp room", or trim a word earlier in the paragraph.

**What's working.**
- **The text setting.** The serif body is a comfortable size, the lines look about 65–70 characters long, the line spacing is generous, and the short paragraphs are evenly spaced. Keep all of that when you add the visit block.
- **The colour.** It's a small palette: pale blue sky, two sea blues, and warm red and yellow kept to the lighthouse. The warm accent pulls the eye to the subject and the cool sea sits back.
- **The headline.** "The light at Gull Point" is specific and calm, and its heavy weight plus large size makes a clear single top level above the body.
