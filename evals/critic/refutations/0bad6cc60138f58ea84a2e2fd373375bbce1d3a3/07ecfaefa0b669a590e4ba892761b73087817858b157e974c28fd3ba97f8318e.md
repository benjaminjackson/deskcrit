**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then down into three plain paragraphs. It reads as a quiet, tidy information page for people using a small river ferry. It feels calm and honest, with nothing sold. But if I came here as a passenger wanting to know when the next boat leaves, I'd finish reading without an answer.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or notice that tells passengers when the ferry runs, how long a crossing takes, and the rules for bicycles, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound, but the page doesn't do its job. A passenger can't get a single departure time from it, and the facts they would act on are buried in descriptive prose they have to read in full. The design problem here is in the words and how the information is structured, not in the styling.

**What would move it most**
1. **No time anywhere on the page tells a passenger when to turn up.** This is a defect in the copy. Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", and paragraph 3 says "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk." A stranger can't turn either into a clock time. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00. "Before dusk" moves by hours across the winter. "Every forty minutes" doesn't help without a first departure to count from. Then the last sentence sends the reader somewhere else: "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing". Someone reading this at home learns that the real information is at the quay. These are the lines people plan around and later hold the operator to, so vague wording here costs missed boats. Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing, in clock times, for summer and winter. Better still, list the actual departure times so nobody has to do the arithmetic.
2. **The facts are set as an essay when they should be scannable.** This is a defect. Each paragraph is really a topic: times and crossing length, bicycles, winter service. But nothing marks them, so finding the bicycle rule means reading the whole sandbank sentence first. The first paragraph's second sentence runs to about 50 words and ends on detail about the crew's course and the harbour wall, which matters to the crew, not the passenger. In one line, all the passenger needs is "allow up to X minutes at low tide." Krug's point about scannable pages applies: people scan for the item they came for. Fix: give each topic a short label ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter"), or turn the times into a small table. Cut paragraph 1 down to the part that affects the passenger.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 1 ends with "tide." alone on its last line, a short-word widow (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Fixing the copy will probably remove it. If it doesn't, rebreak the line or edit a word.
- Paragraph 2 never says it's about bicycles from a passenger's side. "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" explains the order but not the consequence for you: a cyclist should expect to board last. Spell that out to them. This is copy, not a checklist line.
- Two things a first-time passenger needs are missing: the fare for foot passengers (the page mentions "no extra charge" for bikes but never the base fare) and where the north landing and market quay actually are.

**What's working.**
- The type setting is good. Body text looks like about 19 px, with a measure of about 65–70 characters and leading near 1.5. That sits well inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range and reads comfortably. Keep that measure and leading when the content is restructured.
- The hierarchy is clean: one bold heading clearly above the body, one family, dark text on white, nothing decorative. That restraint suits a plain public notice. Keep it, and add structure through labels and a table rather than through extra styling.
