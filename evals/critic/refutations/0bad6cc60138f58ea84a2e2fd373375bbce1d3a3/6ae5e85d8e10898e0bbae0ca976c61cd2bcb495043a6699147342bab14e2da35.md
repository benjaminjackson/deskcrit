**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a short information page for people visiting a reservoir. It shows recent rainfall (perhaps so they know what conditions to expect) and how to get there.

**Verdict.** It doesn't work. The rainfall chart is the biggest thing on the page and says nothing: no axis, no units, no category labels, no takeaway. And the layout ties it to directions that have nothing to do with rainfall. Until the chart can be read, the page's title promises information it never gives.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart can't be read** (defect). The three bars in the left panel have no value axis, no units, no labels under the bars and no date range. A visitor can see that the middle bar is tallest, but not whether it's a month, a year or a week, or whether 40 mm is a lot. It's the largest and most prominent element on the page, so the page's main item is empty. Every chart needs labeled axes or direct labels with units and a period, so the reader never has to guess what a number measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few). The fix: put the period under each bar ("Sep / Oct / Nov") and the value with its unit on or above each bar ("62 mm"). Then add one reference point, such as the usual rainfall for that month or the reservoir's level, so a visitor can tell wet from dry (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Three bars also show very little range. If the trend is the point, show enough months to make it one (common sense).
2. **The title and chart don't say what the rainfall means for the visitor** (taste). "Rainfall at the reservoir" names a topic and stops. Someone planning a visit wants the consequence: "October was the wettest month this year, so expect muddy paths by the water" or "The reservoir is full after a wet autumn." Put the finding in the title or a one-line caption under the chart, said to the visitor. As it stands, even a fully labeled chart would leave them to work out why it's there.
3. **The directions read as the chart's caption, and the page title covers both** (defect). "Getting there" starts level with the top of the chart and sits about 60 px from it, in the only text on the page. The eye pairs them and looks for a link between rainfall and the number twelve bus that isn't there. The page title, "Rainfall at the reservoir", sits over both blocks, so half the page's content falls under a heading that doesn't describe it (WCAG 2.4.6, headings describe their content). The fix is to separate them. Either give the chart its own caption block and move "Getting there" below as a separate section with clearly more space above it, or retitle the page for what it really is (for example, the reservoir's name) and give the chart its own heading. Proximity is reading related things as related (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II), and right now it's linking two unrelated things.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart frame is a dark gray border about 2–3 px wide on a tinted panel, darker than the axis line, so it competes with the bars. Remove the frame, or keep only the pale tint, so the bars are the strongest marks (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*; Lupton, *Thinking with Type*).
- The directions copy spells out "number twelve bus" and "four in the afternoon". These are the details a visitor will look for and act on, so set them as "No. 12 bus" and "after 4 pm" so they can be found at a glance (taste).

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (title, section heading, body) with clear steps, and the headings are bold as well as larger. Keep it when you add chart labels, and set those in a fourth, smaller size rather than squeezing in another near-match.
- The "Getting there" paragraph is well set: plain sans, flush left, comfortable leading, a line length around 45 characters, and no stray short word on the last line. It also says exactly who does what and where.
- The single blue for the bars, on a neutral page, is restrained and right for one series. Don't add colors when you add labels.
