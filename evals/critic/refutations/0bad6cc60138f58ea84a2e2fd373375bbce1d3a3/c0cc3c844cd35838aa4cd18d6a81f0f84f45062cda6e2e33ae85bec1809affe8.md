I ran the full checklist on this screenshot of the Quillpost sign-in card. The one finding that matters is that the email field is marked as wrong with a red border and nothing else.

**First read.** My eye lands on the purple "Sign in" button first, then jumps up to the red-outlined email field. It reads as a plain, tidy sign-in card for a product called Quillpost, shown in an error state. The feeling is calm and competent, and it looks much like every other sign-in card.

**What it's for.** I assumed this: an existing user signs in quickly, and when what they typed is wrong, the screen tells them what to fix. The image is clearly showing that error state.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job, but the error state, which is the moment this screenshot is about, doesn't. The email field is flagged by a red border alone. A user who looks at it sees something is wrong but not what, and a user who can't tell red apart sees nothing at all.

**What would move it most**
1. **The error has no words** (defect). The only signal on the email field is a red border. There's no message, no icon, and the label doesn't change. The user has to hunt for the problem themselves. Here it's a typo ("exmaple") plus a missing ".com", which is easy to read past because people see what they meant to type. Someone with a colour-vision deficiency may not see that anything is wrong, and will press Sign in again and again. This breaks WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.1 Error Identification and SC 1.4.1 Use of Color, and Nielsen's ninth heuristic. To fix it, put a short message directly under the field that names the problem and how to fix it. Keep the red border as a second cue. For this exact case, the line can point at the actual mistake: "This email address looks incomplete. Did you mean dana.ortiz@example.com?" A suggestion that names the user's own likely mistake fixes it in one click, while a bare "Invalid email" makes them find it again. That covers WCAG SC 3.3.3 Error Suggestion. Also make sure the field keeps what they typed, as it does here.
2. **"Create an account" doesn't look clickable** (convention). The footer line is all one grey, so the link and the "New here?" in front of it look the same. The other link on the card, "Forgot your password?", is purple. For a new visitor this is the only way in, and it reads like a caption. Following WCAG 1.4.1, and NN/g (Moran) on flat links drawing less attention, give "Create an account" the same purple as the other link. Underlining both links would also stop them relying on colour alone.

**Smaller things.**
- The password field's grey border looks faint against the white card, possibly under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for on input borders. Measure it, and darken the border if it falls short.

**What's working.**
- There's one clear primary action. The filled, full-width purple "Sign in" is the only strong element on the card, so nobody wonders what to do. Keep every other action at link weight.
- Labels sit above each field, close to their own field and clearly apart from the next one. They aren't placeholder text, so they stay visible once the user types.
- The scale is restrained: a purple wordmark, a bold "Sign in" heading, bold small labels, and regular text. Every size has a clear job, and the brand purple is used only for the logo, the link and the button.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the fields and the button. Check that they're visible, at least 2 px, and 3:1 contrast.
- Whether the error is shown on blur or only after submit, and whether screen readers announce it.
