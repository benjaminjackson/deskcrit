**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", and drops straight down the left column to the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the hero of a small local ferry's website, aimed at day-trippers and locals. It feels calm, tidy and polite, and also bare: everything is pushed into the left half of the screen, and the right side looks like it is waiting for a picture that never arrived.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the landing screen for a local ferry. Its job is to make a casual visitor want to take the crossing and then get them to the departure times.

**Verdict.** It's competent and clear, but thin. The copy and type do their job. The page fails the visitor on two fronts: it gives them nothing to plan with (no times, no price, no location), and it shows nothing of the view it is selling. Both leave the visitor with work the page should have done.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts a ferry visitor came for are missing or left vague.** The paragraph says boats leave "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". That leaves the visitor guessing: is the first boat at 6 or at 8? Is the last one at 8 pm or at 11? There is also no fare, and "the north landing" assumes they already know where it is. A tourist deciding whether they can make the crossing today has to click through before they learn anything usable. The button then makes it worse: "See the timetable" is a clear ask, but it is the only way to get information this page could have shown directly.
   - Direction: state the real hours ("first boat 7:10, last boat 21:30") and the fare. Make "north landing" a place the visitor can find, such as a street name or a map link. Best of all, show the next departure on the screen itself. The button then confirms what the visitor already knows instead of hiding it.
   - Type: defect, argued as common sense. The anticipation principle behind it has no approved source.

2. **The layout tips left, and the empty right half reads as missing content, not as calm.** All the content sits in a column roughly 640 px wide against the left margin. The right ~45% of the frame and the bottom third are blank white. Nothing on the right balances the dark headline, so the page looks unfinished, like a template with its hero image removed (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). It also costs the pitch. The best line on the page, "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck", describes something the visitor never sees, and with no picture and only a stock blue-and-Inter palette, this could be any company's page.
   - Direction: put the bay on the right, either a photo from the deck or a simple route map with the 12-minute crossing marked. That gives the page its counterweight and proves the promise.
   - Caveat: if an image was supposed to load there and didn't in this capture, check it in a live browser. If the page really ships like this, the finding stands.
   - Type: defect (balance) plus taste (sense of place).

**Smaller things.**
- **Body and list sizes are too close.** The paragraph looks about 20 px and the three-line list below it about 18 px. That gap is too small to read as a deliberate level, so it looks like a mistake (Bringhurst, 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). Set the list at the body size, or make it clearly smaller and lighter.
- **The list doesn't read as a list.** "Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board" has no markers or icons, so it reads as a ragged extra paragraph. Add simple markers, or set the items in a row, so they scan as three perks. This is taste.
- **The headline breaks between the number and its unit.** "twelve / calm minutes" puts the key number at the end of line one and its unit on line two. Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" so the promise stays in one piece. This is common sense; Bringhurst 2.4.6 supports keeping numbers with their units.

**What's working.**
- **The headline is specific.** A real number ("twelve") and a feeling ("calm") make a promise no other ferry page would write word for word. Keep both.
- **The type hierarchy is clear.** There is one dominant headline, the body text is readable, and the lines run about 60–68 characters. Everything shares one left edge, so the eye path from headline to button is effortless.
- **The call to action is concrete.** "See the timetable" names exactly what you get, and it is the only primary button, sized well above the minimum target.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the button has visible hover and focus states.
