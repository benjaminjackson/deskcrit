**First read.** The eye lands on the title, drops to the dark-blue block of weekday mornings, then goes right to the three big numbers. In about two seconds it reads as a support report: tickets pile up on weekdays between about 9 and 16, and weekends are quiet. It feels calm, sober and trustworthy. Then it hits a snag. The darkest cells, Monday and Friday, sit under the 11:00 column, but the number beside the chart says "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00".

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a Reports screen in a helpdesk product, for a support lead who decides staffing and shifts from when tickets arrive.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made, restrained heatmap that nearly does its job. The one thing in the way is that its headline number and its picture disagree about the busiest hour. On a screen people staff shifts from, that costs more trust than any styling problem would.

**What would move it most**
1. **The "Busiest hour" stat contradicts the heatmap.** *(Defect.)* Read by its labels, the 10:00 column is 25–29 on every weekday. The 11:00 column is 25–29 on Tuesday to Thursday and 30+ on Monday and Friday, so it is at least as busy on every day. A lead who checks the stat against the chart will either doubt the whole report or plan the peak shift an hour off. There are three possible causes:
   - The stat is wrong.
   - The chart is wrong.
   - The hour ticks are misplaced. "09:00" sits left-aligned over one cell, so it could mark that cell or the boundary before it.

   Find which one, then make the number and the picture say the same thing. Also mark the busiest hour on the chart itself, for example an outline on that column or a short label, so the reader can see the match without working it out.
2. **The hour axis is ambiguous.** *(Convention: Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, on clear labelling.)* There is a tick every three hours, left-aligned above a cell, and nothing says whether "12:00" means the hour 12:00–12:59 or the line between two cells. For a chart whose whole point is "which hour", the reader shouldn't have to guess. Centre each label over its cell, or label every hour in short form ("9", "10", "11"), so each column has a name.

**Smaller things.**
- The title "When new tickets arrive" names the topic, not what the chart shows. Something like "Tickets peak weekday late mornings; weekends stay under 15 an hour" would state the finding outright. *(Taste; no approved source.)*
- Check that 3,412 tickets fits the chart. The bins suggest roughly 200+ tickets per weekday, which over four weeks looks higher than 3,412. Also, "New tickets, September" covers the whole month, while the chart covers 1 to 28 September. Make both use the same period, or label the difference.

**What's working.**
- **The colour ramp.** It is one blue, going from light to dark, with seven labelled bins. It survives greyscale, and the weekday-versus-weekend shape is clear in under a second. Keep it.
- **The stats column.** Each number has a plain label above it and a comparison with August below it, in words ("Up 4% on August", "Down 6 min on August"), not just a red or green arrow.
- **The source line.** It says up front that times are local and that hours mean when a ticket was opened, not when it was answered. That prevents the most likely misreading of the chart.
