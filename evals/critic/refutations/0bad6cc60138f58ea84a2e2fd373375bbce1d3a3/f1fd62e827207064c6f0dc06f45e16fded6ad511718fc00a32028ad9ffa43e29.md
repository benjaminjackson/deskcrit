**First read.** The eye lands on the big "Pricing" heading, drops to three prices of equal weight ($9, $29, $79), runs down three short feature lists, and then has nowhere to go: the cards stop and about 230 px of white sits under them. The blue "Start free" button in the top-right corner is the only thing to click, and it is nowhere near the plans. It reads as a clean, generic SaaS pricing page. It's calm and tidy, but it doesn't help anyone choose.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the pricing page of a scheduling and booking tool ("Tidewell"). It should help a visitor pick a plan and start on it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. It shows prices but never asks the viewer to pick one. No card has an action, no plan is recommended, and the plan differences are left for the visitor to work out. Everything is neatly set, so the problem isn't craft. The page is missing the parts that turn reading into choosing.

**What would move it most**
1. **None of the plans has an action, so the page ends in a dead end.** Each card ends at its last feature line ("Email reminders", "Reports", "Priority support") and there is no button or link inside it. A visitor who has decided on Team has to look up to the header's "Start free", and that button doesn't say which plan it starts. The eye path from price to features to action breaks at the last step, and that's where the empty band at the bottom comes from. *Defect.* The control is far from the thing it acts on (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, natural mapping), and pricing cards without a per-plan button also break a pattern visitors expect (Nielsen, Jakob's law). Fix: give every card its own button at a shared baseline, worded for that plan (for example "Start Team free" if there's a trial), with only one of them styled as primary.
2. **No plan stands out, so the visitor has to make the comparison alone.** The three cards match in size, border, weight and colour, and the three prices are the same size. The biggest thing on the page is the word "Pricing", which tells the viewer nothing they didn't know when they clicked. *Convention:* when three or more elements compete equally, nothing is seen first (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). Fix: make the plan most buyers should pick (probably Team) the clear first look, using a tinted or bordered card, a "Most popular" label and its primary button. Set the other two back.
3. **The feature lists don't say how the plans relate.** Solo lists "Booking page" and "Email reminders", and Team lists neither. Does Team include them? Only Business mentions SSO, so do the others lack it? On a page that sells plans, people hold you to what each one includes, so a list that can be read two ways is a defect. One reading is "each tier includes the one below". The other is "these are different products", and that reading makes Team look like it lost the booking page. *Defect* (Nielsen, recognition rather than recall: the viewer has to piece the full picture together). Fix: start the higher tiers with "Everything in Solo, plus:", and state the limits that set each tier apart in the same terms on every card (calendars, users, support).
4. **The copy could belong to anyone.** "Simple plans for teams of every size. Change or cancel whenever you like." could sit on any SaaS page, and nothing on this page says what Tidewell does. A visitor arriving from search or a shared link only learns it's about calendars and booking from the feature bullets. *Taste / common sense*, and it matters because the page has to sell as well as list. Fix: replace the generic sub-line with one that names the job and the benefit in the viewer's terms, e.g. what booking without back-and-forth email saves them. Make the heading do more than say "Pricing".

**Smaller things.**
- The "Pricing" item in the top navigation looks the same as Product, Features and the rest, so nothing marks the page you're on. Give the current item one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline bar (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- The centred sub-line wraps so that "you like." sits alone on its second line (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Widen the measure or rewrite it so it fits on one line.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is tight and clean. Heading, plan names, prices and body text are clearly separate levels, and "/ month" is set small and grey next to the big price, so a visitor can read the prices across the row at once. Keep that price treatment.
- The three-column grid is exact: plan names, prices and feature lines sit on shared baselines across the cards, so comparing across the row is easy.
- The restrained palette (one blue, kept for the wordmark and the single button) leaves room for a highlighted plan to stand out once you add one.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the navigation and the button, and how the three cards stack on a narrow screen. Check that the recommended plan comes first or is clearly marked once the cards are stacked.
