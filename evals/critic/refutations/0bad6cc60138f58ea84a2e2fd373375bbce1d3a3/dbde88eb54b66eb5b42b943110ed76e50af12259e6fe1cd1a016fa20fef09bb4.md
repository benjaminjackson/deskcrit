**First read.** I see the four prices first: $0, $12, $39, $99. They're the biggest, blackest marks on the page, so they win over the headline. The eye then moves left to right along a row of four identical cards with four identical blue buttons, and nothing tells it to stop anywhere. It reads as a clean, competent SaaS pricing page that could belong to almost any product. It feels calm and neutral, but it doesn't guide you.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the pricing section of a collaborative design or document tool's website. Its job is to let a visitor from a freelancer up to a company see which plan fits them and pick it.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and legible, but it doesn't steer anyone toward a choice. The four plans are treated with exactly the same weight. On top of that, the editor counts make the Free plan look more generous than the first paid plan, which will stop a careful buyer cold.

**What would move it most**
1. **The editor counts look like a mistake.** The second feature row says Free has 3 editors and Solo has 1. A visitor comparing the first two columns sees that paying $12 takes away two editors. They'll either assume the page is wrong or stop trusting the rest of the numbers. Prices and allowances are what people hold you to, so this is a **defect**, not a style note. If it's intended (Solo is a single-seat plan), the row has to say so, for example "Just you" instead of "1 editor", and it may need to explain why Free allows 3. If it's not intended, fix the number.
2. **Nothing marks which plan to choose.** The four cards share the same size, border and weight. The four prices are the same size, and the four buttons are identical solid blue. Every column asks for attention equally, so the visitor has to do all the comparing themselves, row by row, across 16 cells. Tschichold asks that the order of importance be shown by differences of size; that's a **convention**. Material Design and Apple's HIG both say one filled primary button per view. Here there are four, and that's a **defect**: the button that matters gets no more weight than the others. Make the plan most visitors should pick (probably Team, if that's where the revenue is) clearly dominant. Use a stronger border or tint, a "Most popular" label, or a slightly raised card. Keep its button solid and turn the other three into outline buttons.
3. **"/ month" doesn't say what is being charged.** "$39 / month" next to "15 editors" can mean $39 for the whole team or $39 per editor. That's a 15× difference, and a buyer will budget on whichever reading they take. This is a **defect** on the one line people will hold you to. Say "per team / month" or "per editor / month". If annual billing exists, show it here too. The subtitle "Change or cancel at any time" is a good start toward reassuring people about money, but it doesn't settle this.

**Smaller things.**
- Every card has a dead band of about 70 px under its button, and the page has about 130 px of empty space below the cards. That leftover space reads as unfinished rather than intended. Either end each card at its button or use that band for something useful, such as "No card needed" under Free.
- The headline "Plans for every size of team" could sit on anyone's pricing page and adds nothing the cards don't already say. Use it for the thing that sets this product apart, or for the reassurance people need before they pay. This is **taste**.
- "Talk to us" under a stated $99 price is confusing. Is $99 the real price, or a starting point for negotiation? Either label it "From $99" or make the button "Start Company" and move sales contact to a secondary link.
- The feature rows have no labels, so "Full history" against "1 year of history", and "Named contact" against "Priority help", leave the visitor to work out which row is which. It's legible now, but it won't scale past four rows.

**What's working.**
- The feature rows line up across all four columns on the same baselines, with the same hairline dividers. Comparing a row across plans is easy because of that, so keep it however the cards change.
- The type scale is tight and clear: big bold prices, medium plan names, body-size features, and small grey descriptions. That's four levels with real steps between them, all in one family. Nothing collides.
- The palette is restrained, with neutrals and a single blue. That leaves room for one accent on the recommended plan without adding any new colour.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the four buttons.
- How the four columns reflow on a phone, and whether the recommended plan still comes first or stays visible there.
