**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", the bold heading at top left, and glances up at the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label. Then it hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals. They read as an even grey block with no word shapes, so you have to decode them letter by letter. It reads as a calm, plain info page for ferry passengers, but the body sounds like a notice being shouted at you.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page of practical notes that passengers read before a river-ferry trip, to learn when boats run, how long a crossing takes, and what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** The frame is sound: one clear heading, a sensible column, calm spacing. But the body text, which is the whole point of the page, is set in all capitals and is much slower to read than it should be. The copy is also vague on the times a passenger would plan around. Fix those two things and this is a good page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body is set in all capitals.** This applies to all three paragraphs, from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING." Capitals take away the ascenders and descenders that give words their shapes, so every line becomes a row of same-height rectangles. Readers fall back to reading letter by letter, and on a page of nothing but prose that cost lands on every sentence. It also changes the tone from helpful notes to a posted warning, which fights the friendly heading "Before you board". Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says not to capitalise whole paragraphs and to keep capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) notes that text in capitals is particularly hard to read. The fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. The "PASSENGER NOTES" label can stay in capitals. It's short, letterspaced, and doing a label's job. *Defect.*
2. **The copy is vague exactly where a passenger will act on it.** "Between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing. Someone planning a 6:30 commute or a 10 pm trip home can't tell whether a boat will be there. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say from when, so they can't work out a departure time. "In winter" has no dates, so in early November nobody knows which timetable applies. The page then sends readers to "the shelter at each landing" for printed timetables, which they can only reach after they've already travelled. These are the lines passengers remember and later hold the operator to. As written they leave the reader guessing, and a wrong guess means a missed last boat. The fix: give the first and last departure times, an anchor for the 40-minute cycle (for example, "on the hour, :40 and :20"), and the dates winter service starts and ends. Also link the timetable from this page instead of pointing only to the shelters. *Defect (copy).*

**What's working.**
- The heading is the one clear place to start. It's about three times the body size and bold, it sits tight on the text it introduces, and it says in plain words when this page matters ("Before you board").
- The three paragraphs each hold one topic: schedule, bicycles, winter. They're separated by space alone, with no indents or rules. That's the right way to chunk short practical notes. Keep it when you rework the copy.
- The kicker, heading, and body form a clean three-level scale with big steps between them, all on one left edge. The structure needs nothing added.
