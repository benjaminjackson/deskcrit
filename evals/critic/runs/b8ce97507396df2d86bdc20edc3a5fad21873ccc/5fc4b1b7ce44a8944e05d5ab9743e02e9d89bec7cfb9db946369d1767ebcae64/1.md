**First read.** The eye goes first to the three big numbers (3.8%, €64.20, 12.4k) and reads them left to right. Then it drops to the orange bars, where the tall Saturday bar and the short Sunday bar stand out. It reads as the owner's overview screen for a small online bakery. It feels calm, orderly and a little warm.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a quick monthly check for the shop owner on sales, conversion and subscriptions, plus which delivery days carry the week.

**Verdict.** It does its job well: it's honest, clear and easy to scan. The one thing in the way is that the page never states a single time period, so the owner can't be sure the cards and the chart describe the same stretch of time, and can't change it.

**What would move it most**
1. **Give the page one stated period that the owner can change.** The period is spread across four small grey lines in three different wordings:
   - "Last 30 days" on the conversion and order-value cards
   - "As of 30 September 2026 … vs 31 August" on the subscribers card
   - "orders delivered in September 2026" in the source line under the chart

   The owner can't tell whether "last 30 days" ends today or on 30 September, so they can't trust that the €64.20, the 12.4k and the bars describe the same month. The question that comes right after a glance at this screen is "and last week, or last year?" Nothing here answers it, so the owner has to leave the page. This is a defect, because it confuses the comparison the page exists for. It also breaks a convention: Nielsen's heuristic #7, flexibility and efficiency of use. Fix: put one period control, showing the active range (e.g. "1–30 Sep 2026"), at the right end of the "Shop overview" title row. Have it drive the cards and the chart together, then cut the per-card period lines down to the comparison alone.

**Smaller things.**
- **Chart title:** "Orders by delivery day" names the topic, not the finding. Add a one-line takeaway such as "Friday and Saturday carry the week; Sunday is a third of Saturday." This is taste, not a rule I can cite.
- **KPI card labels:** "Checkout conversion rate" and the "Last 30 days" line under the number look almost the same in size, weight and grey. The label doesn't read as the card's heading. The chart title also uses a second heading style for the same level. Set the card labels and the chart title in one darker or medium-weight panel-heading style, and keep the lighter grey for the period and comparison lines (Bringhurst §3.1.1; Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8).

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards follow one strict pattern.** Each has a label, a big bold figure with a smaller, lighter unit (%, €, k), a period, and a green delta with ▲ and +. All four rows line up across the three cards, so once you've read one card you can compare all three. "+0.4 pts" correctly marks a change in a rate rather than a percentage change.
- **The bar chart is honest and quiet.** It has a zero baseline, even 300-step ticks, faint gridlines, one flat colour, no legend, the days in natural order, and a source line. The weekend peak and the Sunday drop show at true scale.
- **Colour and grid discipline.** The bakery orange appears only in the wordmark and the bars, and green only on good-news deltas, which are also marked by shape. Every block sits on one 48 px left edge with equal 24 px gutters, so the screen reads as one system. Keep both rules as the page grows.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The focus indicator on the nav links: tab through them in a browser and check for an outline of at least 2 px with 3:1 contrast.
- Whether the KPI cards and the chart link through to Orders and Subscribers. Nothing on them looks clickable.
- The contrast of the small grey axis labels and source line (about 11–12 px). They look fine, but check the actual grey against white for 4.5:1.
