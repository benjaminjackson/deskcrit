**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline "Notes for ferry passengers" at the top left, then drops into four short serif paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for people taking a river ferry. It feels calm and orderly. Each paragraph opening looks fussy, though, and the right side of the page is a wide empty band.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming a short web page that tells ferry passengers the practical facts: how often boats run, the bicycle rules, the winter timetable and the landings.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. It's clear, quiet and easy to read. The biggest problem is the copy: the times a passenger would plan around are vague. After that, the paragraph setting gives every paragraph two "new paragraph" signals.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times you'd act on are vague.** Paragraph 1 says boats run "between early morning and late evening" (around y≈176), and paragraph 3 says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk" (around y≈444). A passenger planning a winter trip home can't work out when the last boat goes. "Before dusk" could mean any time in the last hour of daylight. If they read it as later than it is, they miss the boat. This page is mostly about schedules, so the vagueness sits right where the reader needs a hard answer. **Fix:** give the first and last departure times for each season, or say plainly that the printed timetable at each shelter is the one to trust, and say where to find it online. **Defect.**
2. **Paragraphs are marked twice, with an indent and with space.** All four paragraphs start with a deep indent of about 47 px (roughly 2.5 em). There is also about 20 px of extra space between them. The first paragraph, right under the headline, is indented too, which cuts a notch into the top-left corner of the text block. The reader gets the same signal twice, the left edge looks ragged, and the page looks like default settings rather than a choice. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*) and Butterick (*Practical Typography*) both say to use one marker, not both, and to set the first paragraph after a heading flush left. **Fix:** on a short web page, keep the space and drop the indents. If you'd rather keep indents, close the gaps, shrink the indent to about 1 em and set the first paragraph flush. **Convention.**

**Smaller things.**
- **Balance (taste):** all the content sits in the upper left (x≈120–770, y≈65–560). About 510 px on the right and 240 px at the bottom are empty, so the page looks like it's missing a sidebar. Make the space look intentional: move the column nearer the middle, or use the right side for something light and useful, such as the next departure, the landing addresses or a small map.
- **Hard to scan (taste):** the four paragraphs carry equal weight with no lead words. A passenger hunting for one fact, like the bicycle rules or the winter timetable, has to read prose to find it. Short run-in labels such as "Bicycles." or "Winter." would let them jump straight to it.

**What's working.**
- **The headline:** it stands apart in size, weight and typeface (bold sans over a regular serif). With only two text levels, far apart in size, the entry point is obvious without any decoration.
- **The reading column:** lines run about 60–72 characters with generous line spacing, in a soft near-black on white. That's well within Bringhurst's 45–75 range and comfortable for continuous reading.
- **One shared left edge:** the headline and body start at the same x≈120, and the headline sits close to its text, so the page reads as one ordered block.
