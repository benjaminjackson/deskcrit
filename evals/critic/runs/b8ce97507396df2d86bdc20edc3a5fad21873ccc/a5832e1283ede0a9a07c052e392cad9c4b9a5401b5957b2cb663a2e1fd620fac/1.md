**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Help for ferry passengers", drops to "Before you board", and reads down the left list. It reads as a plain help page or notice for passengers on a small ferry service: tips before boarding, and steps for reporting a problem. It feels calm, orderly and useful, and a little unfinished, because the right column stops halfway down and leaves the bottom third empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help page or printed notice that tells ferry passengers what to do before boarding and what to do when something goes wrong.

**Verdict.** It does most of its job well: the type is clear, the copy is plain and specific, and the two lists are easy to read. The weak spot is the right column. Its heading promises help when something goes wrong, but all it gives is a slow written complaint, and that comes at exactly the moment a passenger most needs the page to be right.

**What would move it most**
1. **"If something goes wrong" promises more than the section gives.** The right column heading (top right) speaks to anyone in trouble: someone who missed the last boat, lost a bag, got hurt, or had a bike damaged. The three steps under it only explain how to file a written complaint: note the crossing, describe the problem, then leave the note at the quay office or send it "to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter". A passenger with a problem right now finds no way to get help on the spot: no "tell a crew member", no phone number. They also don't learn what happens after they send the note, whether they get a reply or a refund, or when. Even the address is a pointer to somewhere else, so someone reading this away from the landing can't act on it at all. WCAG 2.4.6 asks that headings describe their topic. Fix: either rename the heading to what the section holds ("To report a problem after your crossing"), or make the section live up to the heading. That means a first line for urgent problems ("Tell a crew member straight away"), the actual address or contact on the page, and a closing line that says what the passenger can expect back. Defect.

**Smaller things.**
- Lines in the two columns stop lining up after the first item. Both start level, but from the second item on, a line in one column sits level with a gap between lines in the other. The space between items isn't a whole or half multiple of the line spacing. Make it a whole or half line so both columns keep one rhythm (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*). Convention, minor, because people read each column down rather than across.
- The page leans to the upper left. The title and the longer left column carry the weight, and the right column ends at about 380 px, which leaves the lower right and the bottom third empty (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on balance). If this is a fixed frame, centre the block vertically or give the right column more weight. The urgent-help line and contact details from finding 1 would do that naturally. On a scrolling web page, only the uneven columns matter. Taste.

**What's working.**
- **Each list's form matches its content.** The tips are bullets because they can be done in any order, and the reporting steps are numbered because they happen in sequence. The reader can tell "any order" from "do this, then this" without being told.
- **Plain copy that gives a reason.** Each tip pairs the action with a reason from the passenger's own world: "because winter sailings run once an hour and the last boat leaves before dusk", "so that the gangway is kept clear". A stranger can act on every line without filling a gap.
- **A clear, quiet type system.** There are three sizes, the headings are set apart from the body mainly by bold weight, and the measure is about 50 to 60 characters with generous line spacing. The bullets and numbers hang outside the text, and near-black on white holds up in print or on a screen outdoors. Keep all of this through any revision.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this is a fixed frame or a scrolling page, which decides how much the empty bottom third counts. Also how the two columns reflow on a phone, which matters for passengers reading at a landing.
