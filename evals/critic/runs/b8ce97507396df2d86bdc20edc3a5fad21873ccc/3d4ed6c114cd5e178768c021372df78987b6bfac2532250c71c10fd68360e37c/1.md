I've merged the section reports and checked the apostrophe in a close crop; here is the critique.

**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif title "Notes for the summer fete", then jumps between six underlined italic phrases in the body. The underlines make them look like links. It reads straight away as a short practical notice for fete helpers. It feels calm and bookish, but the emphasis makes it look busy.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short web or email note telling volunteers and stallholders the times and rules they must follow on the day.

**Verdict.** It nearly works. The structure is clean and the text is easy to read. The one real problem is how the important details are marked: six phrases are both italic and underlined. They look like links, and because they are all treated the same, nothing stands out.

**What would move it most**
1. **The emphasis looks like links and marks everything the same.** These six phrases are set in italic and also underlined: "arrive before ten", "labelled with their ingredients", "three o'clock sharp", "collected by four", and "on the field / only".
   - **What goes wrong:** On a screen, underlined text means a link. Readers will try to click these, or wonder what they're missing. If they really are links, the wording doesn't say where they go. They also cover about a third of the body text, so the one rule with a real cost ("or the prizes go back in the box") gets no more weight than the parking note. After the title, the eye finds no clear next step.
   - **The rules it breaks:** Emphasis should change one thing at a time, and underline is not a way to emphasise typeset text (Bringhurst; Butterick, *Practical Typography*). Underline means a link on the web (Nielsen Norman Group). A mark should tell the truth about what can be done with it (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*).
   - **Fix:** Drop the underline, then cut the emphasis back to what people must act on: the times and the deadline. Better still, pull those out as a short list that starts with the time ("10:00 Arrive and set up stalls"). Then they need no emphasis at all.
   - **Type:** A broken convention that does real harm here, so I count it as a defect.

2. **The raffle deadline can be read two ways.** The line is "all tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box."
   - **Reading one:** winners must claim their prizes by four.
   - **Reading two:** helpers must gather up the ticket stubs by four.
   - **Why it matters:** People will hold the organisers to a deadline. Someone who reads it the wrong way loses a prize, or argues about one at ten past four.
   - **Fix:** Say who does what, for example "Winners must claim their prizes by four, or…". Defect.

**Smaller things.**
- **"only."** It sits alone on the last line, and it is a one-word underlined stub cut off from "on the field". Rewrap the text, or tie the last two words together with a non-breaking space.
- **The apostrophe in "three o'clock sharp"** looks like a straight typewriter mark. Use a curly ’ and check it at full zoom.
- **The underlines cut through descenders** in "sharp", "by", "field" and "only". Removing the underline (finding 1) fixes this too.

**What's working.**
- **Two clear levels of type.** The bold serif title is about twice the size of the regular body text, with no competing typefaces. Keep that gap if you add a list or subheads.
- **Easy-to-read paragraphs.** The body is large, near-black on white, aligned to one left edge at about x 180, generously spaced between lines, and split by space alone. It is comfortable to read on a phone or a printout.
- **Colour doesn't carry meaning.** The key phrases are marked by type style, so they still show for colour-blind readers and in black-and-white print. Keep that when you switch to a single kind of emphasis.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Whether the underlined phrases are real links.** That decides which fix applies: plain emphasis, or links whose text names where they go. Check the cursor and what happens on click on the live page.
