**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers" at the top left, then drops into three grey paragraphs of the same weight that run almost the full width. It reads as a plain service notice for ferry riders and feels calm and orderly, but it looks like a document. Nothing in the body catches the eye, and the bottom half of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long a crossing takes, and the rules for bikes and the winter timetable, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It's clean and readable in its parts, but it doesn't yet do its job. The very long lines make a short notice feel tiring to read. Worse, the facts a passenger needs to plan a crossing are vague, and they sit hidden inside sentences.

**What would move it most**
1. **The text column is far too wide (defect / convention).** All three paragraphs run from about x 80 to x 1196, which comes to roughly 140 to 150 characters per line. On every line the reader has to sweep across the whole screen and then find the start of the next one. With line spacing this tight for such a long line, it's easy to drop onto the wrong line, and that happens in exactly the paragraphs that carry the times. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, §2.1.2) gives 45 to 75 characters, about 66 ideal. The same slab causes the second problem: one full-width block of type over an empty lower half (from y≈420 down) makes the page look top-heavy and unfinished, as if something below didn't load (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on balance). Fix: cap the column at about 65 to 75 characters, roughly 600 to 700 px at this size, still hung from the x≈80 margin. The text then runs further down the page, and the open space becomes a deliberate field on the right rather than a void underneath. That one change fixes both problems.
2. **The facts people will act on are vague and buried (defect).** Take "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening": it gives no first or last departure and no departure minutes. "In winter" names no months. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" doesn't say which quay (north landing or market quay) or what time. A passenger who guesses "late evening" or "before dusk" wrong is stranded at the landing. The page also sends them to a printed timetable in the shelter, which is no help to someone planning from home. These facts also sit mid-sentence, so someone scanning for "when is the last boat?" has to read every paragraph to find it. Fix: state the actual first and last departure times from each landing, the winter dates, and the last winter boat from each side. Pull those times out where they can be scanned, such as a short list or small table after the headline, and leave the prose for the bike etiquette. Link to the timetable online, not only in the shelter.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above. The empty lower half is folded into finding 1.

**What's working.**
- **A clear two-level type scale.** The bold headline is about twice the body size, so the page needs nothing more to show where to start and what it is. Keep it to these two sizes when you add the timetable.
- **Spacing that shows the groups.** The gap under the headline (about 35 px) is larger than the gaps between paragraphs (about 18 to 20 px), and those are larger than the line spacing. Each paragraph reads as one topic: crossings, bicycles, winter. Keep these ratios when the column narrows.
- **One firm left edge and a single near-black ink on white.** There's no decoration competing with the text, and that plain, calm tone is right for a practical service notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The empty band from y≈420 to 800 could be the end of the content or a page that hadn't finished loading. Check in a live browser that nothing, such as a timetable or a map, is missing below the third paragraph.
