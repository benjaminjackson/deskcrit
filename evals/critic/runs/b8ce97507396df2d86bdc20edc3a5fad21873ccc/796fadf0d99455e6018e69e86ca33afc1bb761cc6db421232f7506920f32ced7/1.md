**First read.** The eye lands on the large black heading, "Update the card that pays for your subscription", then moves down through two paragraphs to the bold subhead "When the new card is declined". The top nav and the left sidebar stay quietly in the background. It reads as a plain, calm help-centre article for someone paying for a SaaS product. It feels trustworthy and tidy, but a reader who came to change a card gets to the bottom without finding out how.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help article for a customer who needs to change the card that pays their subscription: they should confirm they're in the right place, make the change, and know what happens if the new card fails.

**Verdict.** The visual design is sound: clear hierarchy, strict alignment, and one link colour used for one job. But the article doesn't do its job. It never tells the reader how to change the card, and the one deadline on the page can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page promises a how-to but gives no steps, and no way to reach the place where the card is changed.** The heading and the sidebar item "Update a payment card" promise a task. The only direction is "from the billing page" in the first line (around x 900–1035, y 247). That phrase isn't a link and doesn't say where the billing page is. Four side topics are linked: billing history, roles, invoices and restoring a plan. The reader learns the rules around the change (open invoices, who has permission, declines) but has to leave and find the actual control themselves. Many will hunt through the app or open a ticket, which is the cost a help article exists to prevent. The second paragraph even sends invoice payers to "follow the steps in paying by invoice", which shows this page has no steps of its own. This breaks Nielsen's recognition-rather-than-recall heuristic: the next action should be on the screen in front of the reader. Fix: put a short numbered procedure straight under the heading, starting with a direct link to the billing page (or a single "Go to billing" link), and move the open-invoice and permission rules below it. *Defect.*

2. **The decline-retry deadline can be read two ways, and it decides when the customer loses their plan.** This is in the paragraph under "When the new card is declined" (y ≈ 525–610). "After three days and after seven days" doesn't say what the days count from: the first decline, or the previous retry? "After the second attempt" could mean the second charge overall (the day-3 retry) or the second retry (day 7). A customer can't tell whether they have about 3 days or about 7 days (or 10) before the workspace drops to the free plan. That's a date they'll act on and later hold the company to, and anyone who picks the wrong reading is either caught out or complains. Fix: anchor every day to one event and name the outcome to the reader, for example: "We retry 3 days and 7 days after the first decline. If the day-7 retry fails, your subscription moves to the free plan." *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- **Sidebar and top nav don't show where you are.** "Update a payment card" (x 96, y 178) looks exactly like its siblings, and "Billing" in the top nav looks like the other three items. Only the small grey breadcrumb shows location. Mark the current item with weight, a bar or a background (Nielsen 1; NN/g menu-design checklist). *Convention.*
- **Body lines are a little long.** At about 82–85 characters (column x 380–1050), they're above the 45–75 range (Bringhurst, 2.1.2). Narrowing the column to about 600 px brings them near 66. There's room to spare on the right. *Convention.*
- **The space above "When the new card is declined"** (y ≈ 487) is barely larger than the normal gap between paragraphs. The section break only shows through the bold weight. Make the space above subheads roughly twice the paragraph gap and keep the space below tight (Arnheim, proximity). *Convention.*
- **No search anywhere on the page.** In a help centre, search is the usual way in. Without it, a reader whose question isn't one of the five sidebar titles has to guess the topic. Put a search field in the header (Nielsen 7). *Convention.*

**What's working.**
- **The heading speaks the customer's language.** "The card that pays for your subscription" is a full sentence in the customer's own terms, not a product label. The subhead "When the new card is declined" names exactly the case it covers, so a worried reader finds the failure case by scanning. Keep that voice when you add the steps.
- **One hue for one job.** The blue appears only on the inline links, and every link is also underlined. So "blue" always means "clickable", and the links still work without colour (WCAG 1.4.1). Each link's text names where it goes.
- **Strict, quiet structure.** Two left edges (x ≈ 96 and 380) hold everything, and the sidebar label and breadcrumb share one top line. The large heading is the only big element, and the body is bigger than all the chrome. Nothing competes with the article.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the links, especially the grey breadcrumb and sidebar items, show a visible focus indicator. Check with the keyboard in a browser.
- Whether a sticky header would cover focused links when the page scrolls.
