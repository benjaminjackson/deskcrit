**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a local bakery's website, and its job is to make a nearby visitor want to come in or order. Tell me if it's something else.

**Verdict.** The typography and palette are well made and feel right for a bakery that has been going since 1962. But as a hero it stops at mood: it shows no bread, gives no reason to believe the claim, and offers no next step. The biggest problem is that the visitor finishes reading with nowhere to go.

**What would move it most**
1. **No next step, and an empty lower band where one belongs.** All the content sits in the top-left, roughly x 110–1085 and y 120–500. The bottom 300 px is empty across the full width, and so is everything to the right of the description (x 720–1280). There's no "visit us", no opening hours, no order link and no picture of a loaf. The visitor learns that the bakery exists and is good, then has to work out alone when it's open and how to get bread. The empty band doesn't read as chosen space. It reads as something missing, and the frame looks top-heavy because the dark headline has nothing below it to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). **Fix:** put the one action a local visitor most needs right under the description, for example "Open from 7am · 14 Mill Street · Get directions", or "Order for collection" if they take orders. If you can, add a photo of the bread or the oven in the empty space. That gives the page its counterweight and makes "finest bread" believable at a glance. If the live page already shows a button, an image or the next section in this band, the balance problem goes away; the capture shows none. *Defect* (the missing action), *convention* (balance).
2. **The headline is the most generic line on the page.** "The finest bread in the valley" is the biggest thing here, and almost any bakery could say it. It asks the visitor to take a superlative on trust. What actually makes this bakery different is in the small text: a wood-fired oven, baked every morning, Mill Street, since 1962. So the eye lands first on the line that says least. **Fix:** put one concrete fact in the headline so the line the eye lands on carries the proof, such as "Bread from a wood-fired oven, every morning since 1962". Then let the description add the rest. *Taste*, argued on its own terms: the headline is the one line every visitor reads, so it should be the one that can't be swapped onto another bakery's site.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line break (x 110–350, y 320–410): "valley" sits alone on line 2 and line 1 ends on "the", splitting the phrase. Break at the phrase and balance the lines ("The finest bread / in the valley") with a manual break or `text-wrap: balance`, and check it at every width. Bringhurst covers the lone word; the phrase-break part is common sense.
- The apostrophe in "HARTLEY'S" in the eyebrow (x≈175, y 132) looks like a straight typewriter mark. Use a real curly ’. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- **The type plan.** A refined old-style serif at about 110 px over a plain sans at about 20 px gives one obvious place to start, roughly five times the body size. Two clearly different faces each do their own job. The eyebrow is close in size to the body but stays separate through bold tracked caps and brown colour. Keep that ratio and the pairing.
- **One palette, one mood.** The warm cream ground, near-black text and a single bread-crust brown limited to the eyebrow feel homely and old without a single photo. The accent marks the brand and the date without competing with the headline.
- **One strong left edge.** The eyebrow, the headline and the description all hang from x≈110, which makes the asymmetry look deliberate. Keep that edge for whatever you add below.
