**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline at top left, then drops into three narrow justified serif columns. The first thing it notices there is the holes between words ("minutes between early", "bicycle during the"), before any sentence registers. It reads as a short information page about a local ferry. It feels plain and unfinished: the text hugs the top left, with an empty strip on the right and an empty bottom third.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a short web page that tells ferry passengers how the service works: the route, how often boats run, bicycles, and winter changes.

**Verdict.** The page is calm and correctly ordered, but its body text is hard to read. Three justified columns this narrow are full of holes and white channels, and that is the main thing in the way. The headline also promises a story about the seasons that the text mostly doesn't tell.

**What would move it most**
1. **The columns are too narrow to justify, so the text is full of holes.** All three columns (about 260 px each, roughly 28–32 characters a line) are justified with no hyphenation. To fill the line, the browser stretches the spaces between words:
   - Column 1: "minutes between early", "weather, and a little", "longer when the tide", "sandbank near the old".
   - Column 2: "Riders wheel them on" and "bicycle during the", where each gap is wider than a word.
   - Column 3: "In winter the timetable" and "each landing, and the".

   Where loose lines stack, as in columns 1 and 2 around the tenth and eleventh lines, the gaps join into white channels running down the column. The even grey of each column breaks into spots, the eye trips over each gap, and the reader goes back to the left edge every four or five words.

   Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.1–2.1.2) warns that short justified lines lead to exactly this, and puts multi-column text at about 40–50 characters a line. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 34) says that under about 50 characters, even word spacing becomes difficult if not impossible.

   Fix: set the columns flush left, ragged right. Then widen them toward 40–50 characters, using the roughly 270 px empty to the right of column 3, or go to two columns. Keep justification only with wider columns and hyphenation turned on. Defect (word spacing) and convention (line length), and here both cost the reader.

2. **The headline promises something the page doesn't deliver, and nothing helps you find a topic.** "The river ferry through the seasons" suggests an account of how the service changes over the year. In fact:
   - Column 1 covers the route, how often boats run and how long a crossing takes.
   - Column 2 covers bicycles.
   - Only column 3 is about a season (winter).

   A reader who came for the seasonal story finds one paragraph out of three. A reader who wants times or bicycle rules gets no sign of them, and with no subheads they have to read each column to find out what it holds.

   WCAG 2.4.6 says headings describe their topic. Fix: either retitle the page to what it holds (times, bicycles, winter changes), or make the body actually compare the seasons. In either case, put a short label over each column so a reader can go straight to the one they need. Convention, partly a judgment of the copy.

**Smaller things.**
- **The layout is lopsided.** The text block sits top left: about 120 px of margin on the left, about 268 px on the right, and about 290 px empty below the longest column. It looks dropped into a wider frame rather than designed for this one. Widening the columns (finding 1) fixes most of it. Otherwise centre the block or give the right margin a job (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on balance).
- **The times are vague** (my judgment, about the copy). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines a passenger acts on, and both leave the actual time to guesswork. Someone who reads "before dusk" as 6 pm in December misses the last boat. Give the first and last departure times, or point clearly to where the timetable is.

**What's working.**
- **The headline clearly comes first.** A bold sans at about twice the body size, over a regular serif, makes the two levels distinct by weight and letterform, not size alone. Keep that contrast when you add column labels: make the labels a step between the two, not a third competing size.
- **The grid is strict.** The three columns are equal widths with equal gutters, the headline aligns with the left edge of column 1, and baselines line up across the columns. That's why the uneven column endings look calm. Keep the grid when you widen the columns.
- **Restraint.** Near-black on white, no ornament, no accent colour. That is right for quiet service information.
