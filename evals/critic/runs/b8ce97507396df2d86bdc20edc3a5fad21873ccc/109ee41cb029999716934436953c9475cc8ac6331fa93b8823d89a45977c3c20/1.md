**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold "Before you board", picks up the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it, then reads down three short paragraphs. It reads as a practical notice for river ferry passengers. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. The text sits in the upper-left and the rest of the frame is empty white.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or a section of one that tells foot passengers and cyclists what to expect before they take the ferry.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are clean and easy to read. The problem is the copy: it doesn't give passengers the one fact they came for, which is when they can catch a boat. The design itself has only small issues.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times are too vague to plan a trip with.** This is a defect. The first paragraph says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" but gives no first or last sailing. The third paragraph says "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" without saying which months count as winter or what the hourly times are. Then it sends the reader to "the shelter at each landing" for a printed timetable. So the main question stays unanswered: "When is the last boat back?" Each reader will guess their own "late evening", and someone who guesses wrong misses the last crossing. Times and season dates are what passengers act on and later hold the operator to, so vague wording here costs real people, not just style. This is common sense about copy, not a published rule.
   - **Fix:** state the first and last departure from each landing (for example "first boat 6:20 from the north landing, last 22:40 from the market quay") and the winter months ("November to February").
   - **Fix:** put the timetable on the page or link to it, instead of only pointing to a shelter the reader isn't standing in yet.

**Smaller things.**
- **Lines run a little long.** Full lines in the body column (x ≈ 120–850) hold about 80–85 characters, for example the first line ending "every forty minutes". That's just over Bringhurst's 45–75 range (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, ideal 66). Narrowing the column to about 600–640 px gets it to roughly 65–70 characters, and the empty right side means this costs nothing. This is a convention.
- **The page tips to the upper-left.** All the content sits in the top half and the left 57% of the frame. With nothing below y ≈ 400, the empty space looks like the page ran out rather than space left on purpose (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on balance). If this is the whole page, give the lower area a job. The timetable from finding 1 is the obvious candidate, set on the same x = 120 edge. A footer that anchors the bottom also works. This is taste, and it's moot if the capture is only the top of a longer page.

**What's working.**
- **The three text levels can't be confused.** The label is small, bold, spaced-out capitals in grey. The title is large, bold and near-black. The body is mid-size, regular weight and dark. Each step changes size together with weight, form or colour, so the title is the only place to start and the label reads as a tag, not a second headline. Keep these three treatments.
- **Everything hangs from one left edge (x = 120), and the spacing groups it correctly.** The label sits tight above the title, there's a bigger gap before the body, and the paragraph gaps are even. "PASSENGER NOTES / Before you board" reads as one unit and the three notes read as one body.
- **The palette is restrained.** Near-black on white with a single grey step. Nothing competes with the information, which suits a public-transport notice.
