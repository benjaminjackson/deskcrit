**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then down through the paragraph, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a local ferry company's homepage, aimed at day-trippers and locals. It feels calm and orderly, but sparse: everything sits in the left half and the right half is empty white.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top section of a ferry service's homepage, meant to make the crossing appealing and send visitors to the sailing times.

**Verdict.** The message and the next step are clear within seconds, but the page looks unfinished. Half the frame is empty, and nothing shows the bay, the boat or the open deck the copy is selling. The biggest thing in the way is that missing picture and the lopsided layout it leaves behind.

**What would move it most**
1. **The empty right half: no picture, and the page tips to the upper left.** Everything sits in one column from about x 160 to 790. The right side (about x 800–1280) and the band below the button (y 570–800) are blank white. A visitor reads a promise of "calm" and of watching "the harbour slide past from the open deck", and has nothing to look at. The headline does all the persuading alone. In that same blue, this could be any company's page. The empty area also doesn't read as space left open on purpose. It reads as a picture that failed to load. A heavy side with nothing to balance it makes the frame look like it's tipping (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Fix: put a counterweight on the right, ideally a picture that finishes the headline instead of repeating it. The view from the deck mid-crossing would show the "calm" that the headline only claims; a stock photo of a ferry would not. If an image was meant to be there, make sure it always loads and has a fallback. If the page really is meant to be text-only, make the emptiness look chosen: scale the type and column up, or move the block toward the optical centre. Defect if the image is missing, taste otherwise.

2. **The sailing times are vague, and the actual times are one click away.** The paragraph says boats leave "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". That's the line a rider plans around, and it can be read many ways. A 6 am commuter and a 9 am day-tripper will each assume their own hour, and nobody can tell at what minute past the hour the boats leave. There's no fare either. So the only ask is "go and look", and the visitor can't judge the trip from what's on the page. Fix: show the next two or three departures from the north landing (or the first and last boat and the minutes past the hour), plus the fare, right in this section. Keep the button for the full timetable. Defect: a timing line that can be read two ways sends someone to the landing at the wrong time.

3. **There's no top bar or navigation.** The only sign of the operator is the small blue "Coastal Ferry Co." label at about 160,100. It looks like a label, not a link home. There's no route to fares, routes, the other landing, tickets or contact. A visitor whose question isn't "when does it leave" has nowhere to go, and can't even tell whether this is the home page. Transport sites put the logo and main links across the top, and visitors expect them there (Nielsen, Jakob's law; Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2). Leaving them out gains nothing. Fix: add a standard top bar with the logo as a home link on the left and the few things riders need (Timetable, Fares, Getting to the landings, Contact) on the right. Convention.

**Smaller things.**
- **Feature list size:** the three feature lines (y about 388–475) look one step smaller than the paragraph above, in the same weight and colour, so they read as a slightly shrunken paragraph rather than a separate level (Bringhurst). Either set them at the paragraph's size, or make them clearly different with bullets or icons, a different weight, or the three points side by side.
- **Headline break:** the headline breaks as "Cross the bay in twelve / calm minutes", which splits the one fact it's selling. The first line ends on a dangling "twelve". Break it as "Cross the bay in / twelve calm minutes", with a non-breaking space or a slightly narrower headline box, which also evens out the line lengths.

**What's working.**
- **The purpose is clear in one read.** The label names the company, the headline gives a concrete benefit with a number, and the button names one exact next step ("See the timetable", not "Learn more"). Keep that directness.
- **One accent colour, used twice.** The same blue marks the brand name and the single action, and the button is the only filled shape on the page, so the eye ends on it without any help from size. The page still reads in the right order in greyscale. Keep the blue to those two places; blue bullets or links elsewhere would dilute it.
- **Clean grouping on one left edge.** Everything starts at x 160. The label sits tight above the headline, the blocks are separated by even gaps, and the list is set closer together than the gap above it. The groups read correctly without rules or boxes, and the restraint suits the word "calm".

- How the headline break and the paragraph's line length hold up at narrow widths.
