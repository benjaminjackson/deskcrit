**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, then straight to the tallest bar, Q3 2026 at 705, then along the row of heavy blue 3D blocks. It reads as one page from a board sales deck: tidy and well labelled, but the chart looks like a default spreadsheet graphic dropped into a modern page.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a slide in a board pack that shows directors a seasonal pattern in shipments, so they can plan around it, read in a meeting or skimmed beforehand.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The headline makes a claim, and the labelled bars let a director check it. But the chart shows a forecast as if it already happened, and it doesn't point to the bars the headline is about. Those two things matter most on a board page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast looks like an actual result.** The Q4 2026 bar (far right, 560) has the same solid blue, 3D shape and bold label as the seven real quarters. The only sign that it's a forecast is a bracket in the subtitle and the source line at the bottom. A director skimming the chart takes 560 as having happened. They'll quote it as year-on-year growth over Q4 2025's 480, or read the drop from Q3 2026 as confirmed. A board member is exactly the reader who will later hold the company to that number. Few and Wilke both say forecasts must look different from actuals. Fix: give the bar its own look (outline only, a pale tint or hatching), label it on the bar itself ("560 forecast"), and show a range if one exists. *Defect.*
2. **The chart doesn't show what the headline claims.** The headline says "peak each third quarter", but all eight bars are the same blue. The reader has to find Q3 2025 and Q3 2026 by reading the axis labels. Fix: colour the two Q3 bars in the accent and set the other six in a muted tint or grey, so the chart proves the headline in one glance (Few and Wilke on using colour for emphasis). Do this together with item 1, so the forecast treatment and the Q3 highlight don't clash. *Convention.*
3. **3D bars.** Every bar has a lit top face and a dark side face. The top face sits above the true height, so you can't tell whether the front or back edge marks the value against the gridlines. The bevel also clashes with everything else on the page, which is flat and current: the serif headline, the hairline rules and the letterspaced wordmark. So the chart looks pasted in from an older deck, and the review looks less careful than it is. This breaks Tufte's rule that nearly all the ink in a chart should be data (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*). Fix: use flat 2D bars. The direct value labels already carry the exact numbers. *Convention, with a small cost to accuracy and to trust.*

**Smaller things.**
- Headline claim: "Each third quarter" rests on two cycles, and the strongest point on the chart is left out: every 2026 quarter beats the same quarter of 2025 (Q3 went from 610 to 705). Either soften the claim ("Q3 was the peak in both years") or show a third year. Also think about putting the growth in the headline, since a board will ask about it.
- Subtitle copy: "eight quarters to Q4 2026 (Q4 2026 is the forecast)" says Q4 2026 twice and reads like a patch. Tighten it to "…eight quarters from Q1 2025; Q4 2026 is a forecast."
- Text levels: the subtitle and "Units shipped by quarter" look the same size, but the chart title is bold black and the subtitle is mid-grey. After the headline, the eye skips the subtitle for the chart title. Make the subtitle clearly the second level, or quiet the chart title. Folding the unit into it ("Units shipped by quarter, thousands") also stops "Thousands of units" looking like the same boilerplate as the header and footer.
- Grouping: the chart heading sits closer to the subtitle above (about 27 px) than to the plot below (about 40 px to the "800" label), so it reads as the end of the intro instead of the label for the chart. Open the gap above it and close the gap below it.
- Grid: every other block runs from x 80 to x 1200, but the chart's plot and baseline stop at about 1140, short of the footer rule directly under it. Run the plot to 1200, and hang the axis labels in the margin or align them to 80 (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).
- Balance: all the content ends at the footer (y≈680), leaving an empty band about 110 px tall at the bottom, so the page looks top-heavy and cut short. Give the chart that height or centre the content block vertically. Ignore this if the band only comes from how the screenshot was taken.

**What's working.**
- **The headline makes a claim, and the chart lets you check it.** "Shipments peak each third quarter" is a statement, not a label, and the direct value labels let a director confirm it in seconds without reading the gridlines. Keep the approach of a title that states the finding.
- **Every supporting line names what it holds.** The products and time span, "Units shipped by quarter / Thousands of units", the axis starting at zero, and the source line together mean nobody has to guess what a bar measures or where the numbers come from.
- **The type and colour are restrained.** There is one bold serif for the headline, one plain sans for everything else, and one blue family running from the navy wordmark to the bars, with greys for the supporting text. All the colour weight goes to the data, which leaves room for the Q3 accent and forecast tint in items 1 and 2.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the empty band at the bottom is part of the slide or comes from the capture viewport. Check it in the source deck at its real aspect ratio.
