**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" and then drops into three paragraphs whose lines run into each other. Each paragraph looks like a smeared grey band, not sentences, so reading stops almost at once. It reads as a plain information page for river-ferry riders, and it looks broken rather than designed.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or notice that tells ferry passengers the timetable, the bicycle rules and the winter schedule, read at a glance before a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The heading and type plan are sound, but the body lines are set closer together than the type is tall, so the facts a passenger needs are close to unreadable.

**What would move it most**
1. **The lines in every paragraph overlap.** This affects all three paragraphs (x ≈ 120–750, y ≈ 137–360). The type looks about 18–19 px, but the baselines are only about 15 px apart, so descenders hit the next line's ascenders:
   - "forty minutes" runs into "takes about".
   - "on after foot passengers" runs into "the side of the deck".
   - "crossing leaves the quay" runs into "the shelter".

   The reader has to pick each line out of the overlap. On the way back to the left edge their eye slips onto the wrong line, and some words are partly covered. These are times and rules people act on (every forty minutes, last crossing before dusk), so a passenger will skip them or misread them. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.2.1) and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) both say lines of this length need more space between them than average. This text has less than none.

   **Fix:** set the line height to about 1.4–1.5× the type size, roughly 26–28 px for 19 px text. Then make sure the gap between paragraphs stays clearly larger than the new line spacing, so the three topics still read as separate blocks. It looks like a CSS `line-height` below 1, or a fixed pixel value meant for smaller text; check the computed value. **Defect.**

**Smaller things.**
- The three paragraphs cover separate topics (timetable, bicycles, winter) with no subheads, so a passenger can't jump to the one they need. Short subheads such as "Timetable", "Bicycles" and "Winter" would make the page scannable. Taste: no rule requires it, but people use notices like this to look things up.

**What's working.**
- **The heading clearly leads.** "Notes for ferry passengers" is bold and about twice the body size, and it is the only element at that level. A stranger knows at once what the page is and who it's for. Keep the two-level scale: one family, a big step and no in-between sizes.
- **The column is right for reading.** The heading and all three paragraphs sit on one left edge at x ≈ 120. Lines run about 60–70 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45–75, with a gentle rag. Once the line spacing is fixed, the text will read comfortably at this width.
- **The copy is concrete and complete.** It gives real numbers and rules a passenger acts on: every forty minutes, about twelve minutes, bicycles free on the lower deck, one boat an hour in winter. Each paragraph covers one topic.
