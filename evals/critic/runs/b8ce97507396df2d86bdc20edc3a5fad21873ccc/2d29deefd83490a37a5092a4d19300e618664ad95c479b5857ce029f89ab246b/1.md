**First read.** The eye goes straight to the near-black heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into two paragraphs of pale grey text that look washed out next to it. The last line, the winter timetable warning, is the smallest and faintest thing on the page and easy to skip. It reads as a plain information page for ferry riders. It feels calm and tidy, but faded, almost like placeholder text.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells passengers when the river ferry runs, how bikes travel, and that winter times differ. People may read it on a phone, possibly outdoors at the landing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't yet do its job. The structure is sound, but all the actual service information is set in a grey too pale to read comfortably. The one line a passenger has to act on, the winter warning, is styled as the least important thing on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is too faint to read.** Both paragraphs (x≈120–776, y≈150–335) and the winter note (y≈362–382) are a light cool grey that looks like about #9CA3AF on white. That's roughly 2.5:1 contrast. Only the heading reads at a glance. The ferry details look disabled, and on a phone in daylight at the landing, or for older passengers, they will be hard to read. WCAG 1.4.3 asks for 4.5:1 for text this size, and at about 19 px regular the body doesn't count as large text. Check the real colour with a picker. Fix: set the body in a dark slate of at least 4.5:1, around #4B5563 or darker. Let the heading stand out through size and weight, not by fading everything else. *Defect, convention (WCAG).*
2. **The winter warning is styled as a footnote, but it's the line that stops a wasted trip.** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard…" is set about 3 px smaller than the body, in the same pale grey and weight, at the bottom of the page. It's too close in size to read as its own level, so it looks like a third paragraph that came out smaller by accident (Bringhurst: sizes only a few points apart read as mistakes). A skimming reader drops it. Fix: decide what it is. It's a warning, so set it at body size or larger and separate it with weight or a label, such as a bold "Winter timetable". Don't make it fainter than the body. *Defect.*
3. **The times and dates are too vague to plan by.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing, so someone planning the last crossing home can't tell whether 21:00 counts as "late evening". "Timetables change in winter" doesn't say when winter starts or what changes. It also sends people to a noticeboard they'll only see once they've already arrived. Times and dates are what passengers act on and later hold the operator to. Fix: give the first and last departure times and the dates of the winter timetable, or say where to check before leaving home. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- All the content sits in one block in the top-left (x 120–780, y 80–385), and about 60% of the frame is empty. That makes the page look unfinished rather than calm. Either give the space a job, for example by putting the winter warning in the right column, or set the column on a deliberate margin so the space looks chosen. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- The heading says exactly what the page holds and who it's for, and its weight, size and darkness make it an obvious place to start.
- The measure and spacing suit reading: about 72–76 characters per line, line spacing of about 1.5, a steady gap between paragraphs, and one shared left edge at x≈120. Once the grey is darker, the copy will read as one calm, continuous notice.
- The palette is restrained: one sans family, one dark neutral and one grey, with no ornaments. That's the right tone for plain service information.
