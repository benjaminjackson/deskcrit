**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then drops into three plain paragraphs in a left column. The right ~40% and the bottom third are empty. It reads as a short notice page for ferry riders. It feels calm and clean, but it is prose you have to read rather than a notice you can scan.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that lets ferry passengers quickly find when the boat runs, how long the crossing takes, the bike rules and the winter changes.

**Verdict.** It's legible and well set, but it doesn't do its job well. The facts a passenger plans around are vague or buried mid-sentence, so the page can't answer "when is the last boat?" without sending them to a printed timetable at the landing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times passengers act on have no clock times.** "Between early morning and late evening" (paragraph 1) and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" (paragraph 3) are exactly what a rider plans a trip around. Neither tells them whether there's a 7:00 or a 21:30 boat, or when "before dusk" is in December. The page then points them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", so they only learn the real times once they're already standing there. The last sentence of paragraph 1 also runs two conditions together: the tide turning against the boat, and low tide on most mornings. A reader can't tell when the crossing is slower, or by how much. Defect (common sense): it leaves people unable to plan and risks a missed last boat. Fix: give the first and last crossing times, the winter last crossing, and the date winter hours start. Split the tide sentence into plain statements, such as "At low tide, most mornings, the crossing takes about N minutes."
2. **Key facts are buried in running prose with nothing to scan by.** The three paragraphs cover three separate topics (frequency and crossing time, bicycles, winter timetable), but nothing labels them. "Every forty minutes", "about twelve minutes" and "one boat each hour" sit mid-sentence at the same weight as everything else. Someone looking for one answer has to read the whole page. Taste, argued from the job: passengers scan service notices, they don't read them through. Fix: add short run-in labels ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter") or bold the key figures, using weight rather than another type size, so the clean two-level scale stays intact.

**Smaller things.**
- Composition: all the content sits in the top-left block (x≈120–752, y≈75–530), and the right ~528 px and the bottom ~270 px are empty with no job. The page tips up and to the left and looks like an unfinished template (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on balance). Keep the column width and either centre it or give the right-hand space a counterweight that earns its place, such as a small timetable or a map of the two landings. This is minor if it's one width of a responsive page.
- Paragraph 1 ends with "tide." alone on the last line (y≈297), a runt (Bringhurst, §2.4.2). Splitting the run-on sentence fixes it, or use `text-wrap: pretty` or a non-breaking space before the last word.

**What's working.**
- The body text is properly set: one plain sans at about 19 px, near-black on white, lines of roughly 62–70 characters, and leading of about 1.5. It reads without effort, so keep the measure when you change the layout.
- The type scale is clean: a bold heading about twice the body size and nothing else, so the start point is obvious. Add structure with weight, not more sizes.
- The heading names its reader ("ferry passengers"), so the dominant element says at once what the page is and who it's for. Space alone separates the paragraphs, with one consistent step.
