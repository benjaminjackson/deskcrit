**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Help for ferry passengers," drops to "Before you board," and works down the left column before crossing to the right. It reads as a plain, calm, legible help page, with nothing decorative in the way. Two things catch the eye after that. The wrapped lines of every list item run back under the bullet or number, so the left edges look ragged. And the bottom third of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help page ferry passengers read to get ready for a crossing and to find out what to do when something goes wrong.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The structure is clear, the reading setting is comfortable, and the page says at once what it is. Two things hold it back. The lists are set without hanging indents, so the bullets and step numbers sink into the text. And the right-hand column promises help but only gives a way to file a complaint.

**What would move it most**
1. **Hang the bullets and numbers.** In the left column, each bullet sits at x≈90 and its text starts at x≈115, but wrapped lines ("because winter sailings…", "the machine is slow…", "boarded and lock them…") go back to x≈90, under the bullet. The numbered steps on the right do the same: wrapped lines start at x≈660, under "1.", "2.", "3.", not under the first word at x≈681. As a result each item looks like a small paragraph with a mark stuck on its first line, and the wide gap after the bullet makes the first line look indented. The step numbers are the one thing a passenger with a problem should be able to scan down, and they disappear into the text. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 4.4.2) says to make lists clear through their position on the page and to hang the numbers in the margin. Lining wrapped lines up with the first word is the common-sense part of that. Fix: use a hanging indent, so every wrapped line aligns with the item's first word and the markers sit alone in the margin. On the web, real `ul`/`ol` markers with `list-style-position: outside` do this. Then close the gap between bullet and text to about a word space. *Convention.*
2. **"If something goes wrong" promises more than the column gives.** The heading (top of the right column) suggests help with any problem. The three steps under it only tell you how to report a problem afterwards: write down the crossing details, describe what happened, and leave the note at the quay office. A passenger who has just missed the last boat or can't get a pram on board will land here and find a complaint form. The last step also sends them to "the address on the noticeboard" instead of giving the address, which turns help into another errand. WCAG 2.4.6 asks that headings describe the topic or purpose of their section. Fix: name what the section does, for example "Report a problem with your crossing". If the page is also meant to help in the moment, put the immediate action first (ask the crew, or call the quay office) and print the address itself. *Defect.* It sends a passenger in trouble to the wrong place.

**Smaller things.**
- Lines in the two columns don't share baselines. They match through the headings and first lines, then drift: right step 2 starts at y≈271, between the left column's lines at 257 and 298. Make the space between items a whole line or half a line (about 27 px or 13 px), so both columns stay on one baseline grid.
- The weight sits in the top left. The right column stops at y≈380, the left at y≈550, and about 30% of the frame below that is empty. On a scrolling web page this is just leftover viewport and doesn't matter. On a fixed screen or posted notice, scale the type up to fill the frame or give the short right column something to balance it, such as the quay office contact set apart. *Taste.*
- Bullet 4 ends with "clear." alone on its last line (y≈542). Rewrap it, or use `text-wrap: pretty` or a non-breaking space. Fixing the hanging indent may solve this by itself.

**What's working.**
- **The reading setting.** The body is a plain sans at a comfortable size, near-black on white, with lines of about 55–60 characters and generous line spacing. It reads easily on the move or in a dim shelter.
- **A tight type scale.** There are three levels, each with a clear job: the bold title at about twice the size of the headings, bold headings only a little larger than the body, and regular body text. Weight separates the headings from the body rather than an extra size, which is the "bolder, not bigger" approach in Wathan and Schoger's *Refactoring UI*. Keep it.
- **Bullets for tips, numbers for steps.** The two columns follow the order a passenger needs, and the choice of marker tells the reader which part is a sequence.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the empty bottom band is part of a fixed frame or just viewport on a scrolling page. That decides whether the balance note matters.
