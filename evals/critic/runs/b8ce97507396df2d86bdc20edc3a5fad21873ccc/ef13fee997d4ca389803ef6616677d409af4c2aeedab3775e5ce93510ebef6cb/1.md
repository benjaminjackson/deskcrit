**First read.** The eye lands on the big white headline, "Ferns and shade plants for every corner", and then has trouble with everything under it. The subline and the outlined button sink into the leaf pattern, and the eye keeps getting pulled into the bright leaves on the right, where there is no text. It reads as the homepage hero of a small local plant nursery. It feels fresh and green, but loud and restless.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a nursery's website, there to tell visitors what the nursery grows and get them to browse the stock.

**Verdict.** It does not do its job yet. The message is clear and well written, but the background is as loud as the text, so the subline and the one button lose to the pattern. And the picture doesn't show the ferns or the shade the headline promises.

**What would move it most**
1. **The background fights the text.** The leaf pattern covers the whole hero, from just under the white header bar to the bottom of the screen, at the same density and contrast everywhere. That includes the area right behind the headline, subline and button (about x 96–645, y 265–627). The leaves run from dark olive to near-white lime, so pale leaves sit right against the white letters.
   - **The headline** survives because it is huge and bold, though its edges still blur behind "shade" and "every".
   - **The subline** "Grown on the hill and ready to plant this weekend." is about 20 px and regular weight. Leaf edges break up its letters, so it takes effort to read, and it is the line that makes the offer.
   - **Saturation:** about 90% of the screen is saturated green. Nothing is left that colour can use to point at the important part, and the eye has nowhere to rest.
   - **Grayscale:** the pale leaves come close to white, so the layout has no value structure to hold the text up.
   - **Sources:** WCAG 1.4.3 asks for text contrast at the worst point behind the letters, not on average (4.5:1 for the subline, 3:1 for the headline). At the palest leaves both look below that. Albers and Refactoring UI say structure should come from value, and Itten covers contrast of saturation.
   - **Fix:** make a deliberate calm area behind the text column. A dark scrim or gradient over the left half would do it, as would a solid panel, or a pattern that thins and darkens toward the text. Keep the full pattern on the right, where nothing sits over it. Then check contrast at the palest leaf behind each line.
   - **Kind:** defect.
2. **The one action on the page is its weakest element.** "Browse the greenhouse" (about x 96–356, y 569–627) is the only call to action, but it has a thin white outline and no fill, which is how a secondary button is normally styled (Material Design, Apple HIG). Over pale lime leaves the outline looks well under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks of a control's edge. So the button breaks up into the pattern and looks like a framed caption, not something to press. The visitor has read the offer and then can't find the next step.
   - **Fix:** give it the primary treatment, such as a solid white fill with deep green text, or a deep green fill with a clear edge, so it doesn't depend on what is behind it.
   - **Kind:** convention, with a defect consequence.
3. **The picture doesn't show what's for sale, and it sets the wrong mood.** The headline promises ferns and shade plants. The image is hundreds of generic flat ovals in sunny lime and grass greens, tilted every which way. There are no fronds, no shade, and no plant you could buy. It reads as green confetti, the kind of hero any "green" brand could use. A shade-plant specialist calls for cool, dim and calm, and this feels like a spring-sale banner.
   - **Why this matters:** for a nursery, the picture is the strongest evidence of the product, and here it adds nothing to the words.
   - **Fix:** use a real photo of the stock: a fern-filled shady corner, or the greenhouse itself. Ideally it would also give you the calm area behind the text that finding 1 needs. If it stays an illustration, draw recognisable fronds in deeper, cooler, less saturated greens.
   - **Kind:** taste for the subject matter. Using colour temperature and saturation for mood is backed by Itten.

**Smaller things.**
- **Two left edges:** the logo starts at x≈64 but the hero column starts at x≈96. That 32 px jog looks accidental (Müller-Brockmann, one grid). Put the hero on the logo's edge, or indent it by an amount that is clearly deliberate and repeated down the page.
- **Headline line breaks:** it breaks as "Ferns and shade / plants for every / corner", which splits "every corner" and leaves one word alone on the last line. Break it as "Ferns and shade plants / for every corner" (Bringhurst on breaks in display text).
- **No search or basket in the header:** if the site sells online, someone who already knows which fern they want has no quick way to find it (Nielsen, heuristic 7). Add search and a basket at the top right. If it's a catalogue for in-person visits only, ignore this.

**What's working.**
- **The copy does the job.** The headline says exactly what's sold. The subline adds that it's local and ready now, and "this weekend" is a real, specific hook. Keep these words and give them a background they can be read on.
- **One clear type scale.** There is one sans family. The headline is about three times the size of anything else, the subline and button are mid-size, and the nav is small and grey. The eye always knows where to start.
- **A calm header.** The deep green wordmark and four plain nav links (Plants, Pots, Care guides, Visit) sit on a quiet white bar. The labels use a gardener's own words, and the logo's green ties the brand to the palette.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Focus and hover on the button:** whether the button and nav links show a visible focus ring. A white outline over leaves will make a default one hard to see too.
- **Below the hero:** whether anything shows that the page scrolls. The hero fills the full 800 px.
- **A changing pattern:** whether the pattern animates or changes on each load. If it does, the worst-case contrast behind the text will change too.
