**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" and drops into three paragraphs of near-black body text. It reads at once as a plain notice for river-ferry riders, and it feels calm and practical. Two things catch on a second look. The right edge of the text looks hand-broken, with the first paragraph's last line sticking out past the rest. And the facts a rider needs are buried mid-sentence in the prose.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short web or print notice that tells ferry passengers how the service runs, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read. The notice still fails at its main job: a passenger who has read it can't plan a crossing, because the times are vague and sit inside running prose.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger plans around are missing or vague (defect).**
   - **Where:**
     - Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", with no first or last departure and no starting time for the 40-minute cycle.
     - Paragraph 3 says "in winter" with no dates, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". Dusk moves through the season, and the ferry has two quays, so "the quay" doesn't say which one.
   - **What the passenger experiences:** they can't work out when the next boat leaves or whether they've missed the last one. The notice then sends them to printed timetables "in the shelter at each landing", so they only get the answer once they're already standing on the quay. That defeats the point of the notes.
   - **The tide sentence:** paragraph 1's second sentence runs six lines and ends with "on most mornings at low tide". It's unclear whether the longer crossing comes from the tide turning or from low tide, and the notice never says how much longer it takes.
   - **Fix:**
     - Give the first and last departures, the winter dates, and the last winter crossing from each landing.
     - Pull those numbers out of the prose into a short list or small timetable at the top. That's my taste, and it's the usual way to do it: those numbers are what people scan for and come back to check.
     - Split the tide sentence so it says when crossings run longer and by roughly how much.
     - Point to where the timetable can be found before the trip, not only at the landing.

**Smaller things.**
- **Uneven right edge (convention; Bringhurst, *Elements* 2.1.3; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*):**
  - In paragraph 1, line 1 stops at "market" even though "quay" would fit. The first five lines end around x≈620–650, then the last line ("sandbank… low tide.") runs out to x≈689, so the longest line sits at the bottom.
  - In paragraph 2, line 2 stops early at "boarded,".
  - The breaks look like manual line breaks. Remove them and let one shared width wrap all three paragraphs.
- **Lopsided page (convention; Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"):**
  - All the content sits in the top-left block (x 120–690, y 75–530). The right ~46% and the bottom ~270 px are empty, so the page looks like it lost a sidebar or hasn't finished loading.
  - Keep the reading width. Either give the right side a job (the timetable summary from finding 1, or a small map of the two landings), or move the column so the empty space reads as a margin you chose.

**What's working.**
- **The heading** names exactly what this is and who it's for, and it's the only dominant element. It's bold and about 2.5× the body size, with nothing competing, so the eye knows where to start.
- **The body setting** is comfortable: about 19 px, roughly 50–62 characters per line, and line spacing near 1.5, all inside Bringhurst's range. Space alone separates the paragraphs: the gap between paragraphs is about twice the line spacing, so the three topics (crossings, bicycles, winter) read as separate units without rules or boxes.
- **The colour** is one dark charcoal on white for heading and body. It gives strong contrast without the glare of pure black, and it keeps the page in one calm voice. If you add an accent later, save it for something the passenger acts on, such as the last winter crossing.
