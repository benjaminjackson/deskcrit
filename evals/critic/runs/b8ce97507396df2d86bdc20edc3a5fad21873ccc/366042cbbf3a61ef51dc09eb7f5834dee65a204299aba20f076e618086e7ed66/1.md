**First read.** The eye lands on the centred masthead, "The Wexley Gazette", drops past the double rule, and then runs left to right across three headlines that all look the same. Nothing tells it where the news starts. Gaps between words show in all three narrow justified columns, and the bottom 45% of the frame is blank. It feels calm and traditional, but flat, like a list more than a front page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the front page of a small local paper or its website, for residents who want to scan the town's news.

**Verdict.** It looks like a newspaper but doesn't do a front page's main job, which is ranking the news. Three identical stories under the masthead, with half the page left empty, give the reader no lead story and no reason to start anywhere but the left.

**What would move it most**
1. **No lead story.** The three stories are identical in every way: the same bold headline at about 24 px (y≈155), the same body size, and the same 330 px column at x 100, 470 and 840. The masthead only just leads, at about 1.4× the headline size. So the reader reads left to right out of habit, and the page never says what today's main news is. Size should show order of importance (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). When every main element is the same size, the page has no tension (Hofmann, *Graphic Design Manual*, on contrast of scale). Fix: choose the lead (the bridge reopening is the likely one, since it affects the most readers) and make it clearly the first thing seen. Give it a much larger headline and a wider measure, such as two columns or the full width above the others, and set the other two back as secondary stories. Defect.
2. **The bottom half is empty, so the page looks cut off.** Everything ends by about y 415, and from there to y 800 there is nothing across the full width. The column ends don't line up either (left and middle at about y 404, right at about y 378), so their uneven bottoms become the lower edge of the whole layout. All the weight sits at the top, with nothing below to hold it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). The reader sees a page that looks unfinished or still loading. Fix: use that space. The lead story in point 1 is the natural thing to fill it, along with a second row of stories, a picture, or a dateline or footer band. If three short items really are the whole issue, make the canvas fit them. Defect, unless this is a capture of a longer page (see below).
3. **Short justified lines leave holes between words.** Each column holds about 34–42 characters per line, which is short of 45–75 and under Bringhurst's 40–50 for multi-column text. Justifying so few words opens clear gaps, for example "months of repairs to its arches." (left, y≈247), "the old corn exchange from next" and "a stage for local bands on the first" (middle), and "a regional prize for the vegetable" (right). The grey of each column turns blotchy, and the stretched lines read in jerks. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 34) says that under about 50 characters, even word spacing becomes "difficult, if not impossible", and Bringhurst (§2.1.1–2.1.2) calls the result "white acne". Fix: set the text ragged right, or hyphenate if you keep it justified. Widening the lead story (point 1) also gives it a better measure. Defect for the spacing, convention for the measure.

**Smaller things.**
- The double rule runs from x 100 to 1180, but the text block ends at about x 1170, so the right edges almost line up but don't. Shorten the rule to 1170 or widen the columns to fill 1080 px.

**What's working.**
- **A clear three-level type scale in one serif family.** The masthead is about 34 px bold, headlines about 24 px bold, and body about 18 px regular. No two sizes are close enough to confuse, and weight backs up size. Keep this scale when you add a lead size; add one clearly larger step, not another size close to an existing one.
- **One ink, and a coherent period feel.** One near-black on white, the serif, and the double rule under the masthead all belong to the printed newspaper tradition, so the reader knows within a second what this is, and it holds up in grayscale or print. Don't break it with a sans or accent colours.
- **Real headlines and a shared baseline grid.** "Bridge to reopen in spring", "Market moves indoors" and "School wins garden prize" each say who does what, so a reader gets the news from the top line alone. The body lines in all three columns sit on the same baselines, which keeps reading across the page orderly.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty lower half is the actual end of the page or a capture taken before more content loaded. Check the live page or the source file.
- If this is a web page, whether the headlines link to full stories. If they do, they need to look clickable by more than colour and show a visible focus state.
