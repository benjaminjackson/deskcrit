**First read.** The eye goes first to the big brush-script "Crossing the river". Next it jumps across to "Good to know", set in the same script, before it reads either section in the main column, then drops to the blue button. This reads as a passenger guide for a small river ferry. It feels friendly, but somewhat like a costume: script, serif and sans all compete in one short screen.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page that tells ferry passengers how the north landing to market quay crossing works and sends them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** Mostly yes. The structure is plain, the route is named, and there is one clear action. What gets in the way is the type: three unrelated typefaces with mixed-up jobs. That makes the page look assembled from parts, and it pulls the eye to the side box ahead of the main content.

**What would move it most**
1. **Three type voices with crossed roles, and a script that makes the side box outrank the page.**
   - **Where:** The brush script sets "Crossing the river" and "Good to know". A serif sets the grey subtitle, the two section headings and the side-box paragraph. A sans sets the nav, the main paragraphs and the button.
   - **What the viewer gets:**
     - The roles cross over. The page has two kinds of section heading (script in the box, serif in the main column) and two kinds of body text (serif in the box, sans in the main column). The box reads as a different kind of content pasted in from another site, not as part of the same guide.
     - "Good to know" is about 44 px of script, larger and louder than the about 28 px serif headings. Its paragraph is also larger than the main body. So after the title the eye goes to the side note before "When the boats run", and the page seems to have two starting points.
     - The script gives a timetable page a party-invitation tone. That works against the trust a passenger wants from travel information.
   - **Sources:** Dair, *Design with Type*: faces should either agree or contrast, with clear jobs. Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*, and Nielsen's consistency heuristic: one kind of element looks one way. The point about tone is taste.
   - **Fix:** Give each face one job. Use the script for the h1 only, as a single deliberate accent, or drop it. Set "Good to know" in the h2 serif at or below h2 size, and set the box text in the main body face and size. The border and the button then set the box apart, and the type no longer does. This is a convention break plus taste on the script, and it hurts the reading order.
2. **The service hours are vague exactly where passengers act on them.**
   - **Where:** "The ferry leaves every forty minutes between early morning and late evening… the last crossing leaves before dusk."
   - **What the viewer gets:** Someone planning a trip can't tell from this whether a 7:00 or a 21:00 crossing exists. "Before dusk" moves through the year, so a winter passenger could read it as 17:00 or as 16:00 and miss the last boat.
   - **Fix:** Give the first and last departure times, summer and winter, in the sentence: "From 6:40 to 21:20; in winter, hourly from 7:00, last boat 16:00." Keep the timetable link for the full detail. This is a defect: these are the times passengers hold the operator to.

**Smaller things.**
- **Side-box measure:** the box paragraph runs only about 16 to 27 characters per line at about 20 px, which makes six short strips out of one sentence. Set it at body size or widen the card.
- **Side-box line spacing:** the leading looks about 30 px on 20 px type, so the lines drift apart. Tighten it to about 1.3 to 1.4, or match the main body.
- **Lone last word:** "week." sits alone on the last line of the box paragraph. Rewrapping as above will probably cure it.
- **Subtitle line break:** the break leaves "to" at the end of the line and splits "north landing to / market quay". Break before "to" so the route stays on one line. This is taste.
- **"Good to know" says nothing about what's in the box:** a passenger looking for where changes are posted gets no hint (WCAG 2.4.6). Try "Printed timetables and changes".
- **Card's right edge:** it stops at about x 1149, about 35 px short of where the nav ends (about 1184), and the right margin doesn't match the left. Put the card on the header's column line (Müller-Brockmann).
- **Card's top edge:** it sits at about y 146, just off the top of the headline, so it looks like a missed alignment. Align it exactly with the headline, or drop it clearly lower.
- **Lines across the two columns:** the box lines step about 30 px and the main text about 27 px, so the two columns don't share rows. This is optional for a boxed aside.

**What's working.**
- **One colour, one action.** Everything except "See the timetable" is neutral, so the only blue thing on the page is the one thing to do. It sits right under the copy it follows from, and it is comfortably large.
- **Plain structure in the passenger's own words.** The subtitle names the exact route, and the headings "When the boats run" and "Bicycles and luggage" plus the nav labels "Timetable", "Fares" and "Landings" let someone go straight to their question.
- **Main body text.** A plain sans at about 18 px, with lines of about 65 to 70 characters and comfortable leading, is easy to read. Keep it and spread it to the side box.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Focus rings:** check that the nav links and the dark-blue button show a visible focus ring that the button's fill doesn't hide.
- **Sticky header:** if the header stays fixed on scroll, check that it never covers a focused link or the button.
