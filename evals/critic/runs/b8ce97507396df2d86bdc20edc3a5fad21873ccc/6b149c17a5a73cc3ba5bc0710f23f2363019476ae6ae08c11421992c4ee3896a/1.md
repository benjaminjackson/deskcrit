**First read.** The eye goes to the bold headline, then down two short paragraphs into a pale grey box. It reads as a plain public notice about winter ferry service. It feels calm, sober and orderly. Two things catch on a second look: the grey box has much more empty space under its text than above it, and the lines of text run very wide.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public information page telling ferry passengers when the boat runs in winter, what can delay it, and what vehicles pay.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job, but the copy doesn't. The timetable in the first paragraph can't be true as written, and the timetable is the one thing a reader acts on. Fixing the words matters far more than anything visual here.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable contradicts itself and leaves out half the trip.** In the first paragraph (y≈145–195), the ferry leaves "every forty minutes", "the first crossing is at seven in the morning and the last at six in the evening". Forty-minute steps from 7:00 give 17:00, 17:40 and 18:20, but never 18:00. A reader can't tell whether the last boat is at 17:40 or 18:00, or whether the gap changes during the day. The sentence also gives departures from the north landing only, so "the last at six" could mean the last boat out or the last boat back. Someone planning the last trip home acts on this line. If they choose the wrong reading, they miss the boat or get stranded on the far bank. **Defect.** Fix: give the real departure times, or a rule that adds up, such as "every forty minutes from 7:00; last boat 17:40". Then add the last return from the other landing.
2. **The lines are too long for a page people scan for times.** The paragraphs and the box text run about 100–105 characters per line. For example, "From November to March … The first crossing is at" is about 103 characters, well past the 45–75 Bringhurst gives. At that width the eye loses its place moving back to the next line, and a reader hunting for one time or rule has to re-read to find it. **Convention, with a real cost here.** The generous line spacing softens it, but the text is still hard to scan. Fix: narrow the text column to roughly 640–680 px at this size, or raise the body size. Measure `characters_per_line` to confirm.

**Smaller things.**
- Grey fare box (x≈200–1080, y≈290–411): about 26 px of padding above the text but about 45–50 px below it, so the box looks unfinished and the text sits high. Make the bottom padding equal the top by removing the trailing empty line, margin or min-height. (Common sense. Check in a live browser that nothing meant to sit there failed to load.)
- Fare box copy: "Bicycles ride free on weekdays" doesn't say what bicycles pay at weekends. "Cars pay the standard fare" gives no amount and no place to find it. State both, or link to the fares.
- "Check the board at the ticket hut" (second paragraph) is the only instruction on ice delays. Say where else a reader can check before leaving home, if there is such a place.

**What's working.**
- **One clear entry point and a simple two-level scale.** The bold headline is about twice the body size and is the only heavy thing on the page. It also says exactly what the page covers, so a first-time reader knows what this is within a second. Keep the scale this simple.
- **A restrained fare box.** The pale cool-grey tint sets the vehicle and fare rules apart as an aside without adding an accent colour. Its edges sit on the same column lines as the text (x≈200 and x≈1080). Someone asking "can I bring my car or bike?" finds the answer without reading the paragraphs. If the box needs more weight, add a short label, not a stronger colour.
- **Calm, even spacing.** The gaps between paragraphs and the box are consistent, and the heading has a little more room below it, as a page title should.
