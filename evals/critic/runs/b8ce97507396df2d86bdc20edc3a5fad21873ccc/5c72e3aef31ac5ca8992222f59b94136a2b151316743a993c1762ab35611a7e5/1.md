**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Update the card that pays for your subscription" and then drops straight into the body text. It reads as a SaaS help-centre article about billing, and it feels calm, plain and trustworthy. By the end of the page, though, you still don't know where to click to change the card.

**What it's for.** You didn't say, so I'm assuming this is a help article for a Ledgerly account owner or billing admin who wants to swap the card on file, often because a payment has just failed.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are clean and do their job, but the article doesn't do what its title promises. It explains the rules around changing a card and never says how to change it. Its most important dates and outcomes can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The how-to is missing.** The title promises a task. The body (y 235–620) gives policy instead: when the new card is charged, who may change it, and what happens when it's declined. The only direction is "from the billing page" in the first sentence, and that's plain text, while four side trips ("check your billing history", "roles and permissions", "paying by invoice", "restore your previous plan") are links. A reader who came to do the task leaves still hunting for the page and the control, or contacts support, which is exactly what a help article should prevent. Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: the next action should be self-evident. Nielsen's "recognition rather than recall" says the same. Fix: link "the billing page" directly to where the card is changed, and put a short numbered path naming the real controls above the policy paragraphs (for example Settings → Billing → Payment method → Replace card). If the page is meant to be policy only, retitle it to match. *Defect.*
2. **The dates and outcomes a customer will plan around can be read two ways.** These are all under "When the new card is declined" (y 515–620), except the last one:
   - "after three days and after seven days" doesn't say what the days count from. It could be the first decline, or each retry, which puts the last retry on day 7 or day 10.
   - "after the second attempt" could mean the day-7 retry. If the original charge counts as attempt one, it means the day-3 retry, and the downgrade to the free plan comes four days sooner than the reader expects.
   - In the first paragraph, "any invoice that is already open keeps the old card until it is paid" doesn't say what happens when the old card is the one failing. That's the most likely reason anyone is reading this page.

   These are the deadlines and consequences people later hold the company to. A wrong reading means a surprise downgrade or an invoice that is never paid. Fix: tie every date to one event and name the outcome, for example "We retry 3 and 7 days after the first decline. If the day-7 retry fails, your subscription moves to the free plan." Also say what happens to an open invoice the old card can't pay. *Defect.*
3. **Links in the body are marked by colour alone.** The four inline links are medium blue next to near-black body text, with no underline and no other cue. The two colours look close in lightness, probably under the 3:1 that WCAG accepts when colour is the only difference. Readers with colour-vision deficiency, or anyone on a poor screen or in glare, may not see them as links. On this page the links lead to the prerequisite steps (billing history, getting the billing role). WCAG 1.4.1, Use of Color (failure F73). Fix: underline links in running text. The nav links can stay plain, because their position already marks them. Measure link against body colour to confirm. *Convention, and it costs something here.*

**Smaller things.**
- No search anywhere in the header (y 0–63). Most help-centre visitors arrive with a question in their own words, so add a search field to every article.
- The current page isn't marked: "Update a payment card" in the side nav (y≈178) and "Billing" in the top nav look like their neighbours, so only the small grey breadcrumb says where you are. Give both an active state using weight, a bar or a tint, and make the side-nav label match the H1.
- Body lines run about 80–85 characters (x 380–1050), a bit past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the column to about 600–620 px. There's plenty of empty space on the right to absorb the change.
- The H1 breaks as "…pays for your / subscription", leaving one word stranded on the second line. Break before "for your subscription", or shorten the title to "Update your payment card", which also matches the side nav. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- **A clear, restrained hierarchy.** The bold H1 easily dominates. The bold subhead stands apart from body text that's only slightly smaller. The breadcrumb and nav sit clearly lower. Each level has an obvious job and nothing competes with the title. Keep the bold on the subhead, because it's the only thing separating it from the body.
- **One grid and good spacing.** The logo and sidebar share a left edge at x 96. The breadcrumb, title and body hang from x 380. The breadcrumb and "In this section" sit on the same line. The gap above the subhead is about twice the gap below it, so the subhead clearly belongs to its paragraph. The empty space sits in three large areas, not scattered gaps, so the page reads as calm, which suits an anxious billing reader.
- **Plain, second-person copy that speaks to the reader's worry.** "Your workspace stays open during this period" tells the reader what it means for them, not just how the system behaves. The blue is used only for links, so every bit of colour means "click here".

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the inline links get an underline or other non-colour cue on hover and focus, and whether focus indicators are visible. This affects whether finding 3 fails WCAG outright.
- How the two columns reflow on a phone. Readers often open this kind of article from a payment-failure email.
