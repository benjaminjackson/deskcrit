**What would move it most**
1. **The headline's line spacing is too loose** (headline, top left). The type looks about 48 px on a line pitch of about 72 px, so roughly 1.5× leading. That is about what you'd give body text. At this size it leaves gaps between the lines nearly as tall as the letters, and the gap above the byline is only a little larger. The reader takes "The farmers who are letting their / fields flood on purpose, and the / birds that came back" as three lines in a row, not one thought. The headline also takes about 30% more height than it needs and pushes the body text down the screen. Direction: close the headline's leading to about 1.1–1.2× its type size, keeping the descenders of one line clear of the ascenders on the next (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, p. 37, on how far a title can be tightened). The headline will then read as one block and the opening paragraph moves up. *This is taste and common sense rather than a sourced rule.* The approved sources cover the opposite fault, lines that collide. The general rule that heading lines need tighter spacing than text hasn't been checked against a source.

**Smaller things.**
- "a hand's depth" in the first paragraph uses a straight apostrophe. Swap it for a curly one (’) and check that the publishing pipeline converts quotes automatically. This is common sense and not tied to a checked source. At body size it costs very little.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is clean.** I count three levels: the headline at about 48 px, the body at about 19 px, and the kicker and byline sharing a small size of about 15 px. Weight and typeface carry the rest. The headline is a heavy serif, the body is a book-weight serif and the metadata is a grey sans. Every level has an obvious job, and the green "Environment" kicker separates itself by colour, not by adding a fourth size.
- **The body column is set for reading.** Lines run about 66 characters, which is Bringhurst's ideal. Line spacing is generous and paragraphs are marked by space alone. Keep this column width when the headline is retightened, and don't stretch the body out to match the headline's wider measure.
- **The copy pulls the reader in.** The headline gives you the counterintuitive hook (flooding on purpose) and the payoff (the birds came back) in plain words. The opening paragraph is concrete and physical: a grey February morning, "a hand's depth of water", the air "loud with lapwings". The second paragraph then turns it into a story with "the farm is paid to keep them wet." No line reads like it could sit on anyone else's page.
