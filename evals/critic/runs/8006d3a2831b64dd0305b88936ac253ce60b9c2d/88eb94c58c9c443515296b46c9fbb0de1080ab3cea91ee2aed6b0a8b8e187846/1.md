**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", and drops straight into four serif paragraphs. Before reading a word, the left edge looks jagged: every paragraph starts with a deep indent of about 47 px and also sits after a blank gap, so the column looks notched four times. It reads as a calm, plain information page for people who use a small river ferry. It feels quiet and trustworthy, a little like a printed notice.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a web page that tells passengers, new ones and regulars, how the ferry works so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The type is well made and easy to read, but the page doesn't do its job. A passenger can't plan a trip from it, because it gives no actual times. On the design side the only real fault is the doubled paragraph marking, which makes the column look stepped.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on** (defect). The first paragraph says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". The winter paragraph says "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". Then the reader is told the printed timetables "are kept in the shelter at each landing". A reader can't tell whether the first boat leaves at 6:00 or 8:00, or whether "before dusk" in December means 15:30 or 16:30. The only way to find out is to walk to the landing, which defeats the point of a page like this. Missing a last boat is exactly the mistake these notes should prevent. Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing, the winter dates, and the winter last-boat time, or link straight to the timetable. Say plainly whether each time is from the north landing or the market quay.
2. **Paragraphs are marked twice, and the first one is indented too** (convention). Each of the four paragraphs has both a first-line indent of about 47 px (roughly 2.5 em) and a gap of about 20 px above it. The opening paragraph, right under the heading, is indented as well. Either signal alone would show where a paragraph starts, so using both just adds the stepped left edge you see at first glance. The opening indent also pulls the first line away from the heading it belongs to. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.3.1) sets the first paragraph after a heading flush left, since the indent there is superfluous. That "never both" is the better choice is common sense rather than a rule he states: two signals for one break is redundant. Fix: keep the space between paragraphs and drop the indents, which suits a web page of short notes. Or keep a smaller indent of about 1 em, close the gaps, and set the first paragraph flush.

**What's working.**
- The measure is right. Body lines run about 60–65 characters with comfortable line spacing, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Keep the column narrow even on wide screens, and don't stretch it into the empty right side.
- The heading stands apart from the body in more than size: it's a bold sans against a regular serif. That gives a clear two-level hierarchy without making the heading huge. Hold onto this if you add subheads.
- The text is near-black on white with plenty of margin, so the page reads easily and nothing distracts from it.
