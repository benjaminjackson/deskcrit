**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the landing section of a ferry operator's site. Its job is to make a visitor want the crossing and send them to the timetable to plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The structure works: one clear headline, one clear button, nothing extra. It falls short on content. It promises a crossing without saying where to, when the boats run or what a ticket costs, and it sells a view without showing one.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out the facts a traveller acts on.** *Defect.*
   - "Cross the bay": which bay, and to where? The paragraph gives a starting point ("the north landing") but no destination.
   - "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" is the line people will plan around, and it can be read two ways. "Early" could mean 6:00 or 8:00. A commuter who guesses wrong misses the boat or doesn't come at all.
   - There is no fare anywhere. "Bicycles travel free" has nothing to be free next to.

   The button "See the timetable" is a good ask: specific, and it names exactly what you get. But it forces a click to learn the basic facts the hero could give in one line.

   Fix: name both ends ("from North Landing to [town]"), give real times ("first boat 6:40, last 22:20"), and add the adult fare. Let the timetable hold the detail, not the basic promise.
2. **Nothing on the page shows what it's selling.** *Taste, but it costs something.* The pitch is all feeling: "calm", "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck", picnics, prams. It is set as dark grey text on white in a stock sans-serif, with a stock blue button. A visitor gets no picture of the crossing and no sense that this ferry is different from any other. The empty band of about 250 px below the button shows there is room. Fix: add one real photo taken from the open deck with the harbour behind, beside or behind the headline, so the image proves "calm" and the words don't have to.

**Smaller things.**
- Every block, including the three-line paragraph and the button, sits on one centre axis. Tschichold treats that as a habit rather than a choice. It costs little in a hero this short, but if you add a photo, an off-centre arrangement with the text flush left would read more easily. *Convention.*
- The paragraph (about 20 px) and the feature lines (about 18 px) are both plain regular text, just a couple of pixels apart. They read as a mismatch, not as two levels (Bringhurst: sizes come from a scale). Set them the same size, or make the features clearly different through weight or a list form.
- The headline breaks as "twelve / calm minutes", which splits the number from its unit. A break like "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" keeps the promise in one piece. *Common sense; the source for this is unconfirmed.*

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete, numeric promise ("twelve minutes"), and "calm" sets the tone in one word. Keep both.
- The hierarchy is clear. The headline is far bigger and heavier than everything else, and the only saturated colour is on the one button, so the eye finds the action without effort.
- "See the timetable" names exactly what the click gives you. Keep that kind of ask when you add the missing facts.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The empty band below the button could be the real end of the hero or a capture of a page that was still loading content. Check in a live browser.
