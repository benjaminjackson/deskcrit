**First read.** The eye lands on "Help for ferry passengers", drops to the two bold headings, and splits the page cleanly: tips on the left, a complaint procedure on the right. It reads as a plain, calm help page from a small ferry operator. It's sober and trustworthy, and a little bare. On the second look the left column's lists feel lumpy: the wrapped lines slide back under the bullets, so the items don't read as separate blocks.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a help page on a small local ferry service's website, for passengers planning a trip or reporting a problem. It might also be a printed notice.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound. There's one clear focal point, three type levels that are easy to tell apart, and a sensible two-column split. What lets it down is a list setting that blurs the left edge, plus copy that sends the reader somewhere else for the facts they actually need, like when the last boat leaves and where to send a complaint.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page holds back the two facts people will act on.** This is a copy defect. "The last boat leaves before dusk" isn't a time. It's a deadline, and passengers will plan around it. One reader takes it as about 4 pm, another as 6 pm, and the one who guesses late is stranded. Step 3, "send it to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter", makes someone who is reading a help page go and stand in a shelter to find an address the page could just print. Give the last winter sailing time, or point to the timetable on this page. Put the actual postal or email address in step 3.
2. **The lists don't hang, so neither column has a clean left edge.** This is a convention break. In the four bullets on the left, and in steps 1 to 3 on the right, the wrapped lines go back to the margin under the bullet or number instead of lining up with the first word. Each item stops looking like its own block, and the markers get lost in the text. Skimming is the whole point of a list here, and it gets harder. There's also a wide gap between each bullet and its text (about the width of three letters) that the numbered list doesn't have, so the two columns are set by different rules. The fix is to hang the markers: put them in the margin, line the wrapped lines up with the first word, and use the same gap from marker to text in both lists. Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style* §4.4.2, supports pointing out list items and hanging the numbers in the margin. The rule that wrapped lines should line up with the first word is common practice rather than something a checked source states.
3. **The complaint steps don't say what happens next.** This is a copy finding. "If something goes wrong" sets out three steps for the reader and then stops. Nothing says who reads the note, when they'll hear back, or what they can get (a refund, lost property returned). Without that, a frustrated passenger can't tell whether writing a note is worth the trouble. Add one closing line that tells them what to expect, for example "We reply within X days" and what can be resolved.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading "If something goes wrong" is broad. If this column is mainly for complaints and lost property, say so in the heading so people can find it.

**What's working.**
- A tight type scale: title at about 40 px, section headings at about 22 px bold, body at about 18 px. Each level does one job, and the headings stand apart by weight as well as size.
- Numbers for a sequence and bullets for independent tips is the right choice, and the reader picks it up without being told.
- Every bullet starts with a verb (Check, Bring, Wheel, Ask) and gives its reason ("so that the gangway is kept clear"). That's good, people-first instruction copy, so keep it.
