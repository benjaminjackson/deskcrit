**First read.** I look first at the bold sans headline, then read down three calm serif paragraphs. It reads as an information page for a small river ferry. It feels quiet, tidy and trustworthy, like a well-set noticeboard. Nothing competes, and nothing is wrong with the setting.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells someone planning to cross the river when the ferry runs, how long the trip takes, and what to do with a bike, so they can turn up at the right time.

**Verdict.** The typography does its job almost perfectly. The page as a whole doesn't, because the words leave out the facts a passenger acts on: they still can't tell when to be at the landing, and in the end the page sends them to a printed sheet at the landing itself.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times aren't stated** (paragraphs 1 and 3). This is a defect. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" has no first or last departure and no anchor time, so a reader can't work out whether a boat leaves at 9:00 or 9:20. "A little longer" doesn't say how much. "In winter" doesn't name the months. "Before dusk" moves by about an hour across the season. These are exactly the lines a passenger acts on and later holds the operator to, and each can be read more than one way. If they read it wrong, they wait 40 minutes, or in winter they miss the last boat and are stuck on the wrong bank. Then "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends them to the place they're trying to plan the trip to. The fix is to give the real first and last departures and the departure minutes ("from 6:20, then at :00 and :40"), the winter dates, and the winter last-crossing time or a rule such as "last boat 4:00 pm, Nov–Feb". If you put up the printed timetable, put it on this page too.
2. **The highest-stakes fact is buried** (the end of paragraph 3). This is taste, but I'd argue it hard. The one thing that can actually hurt a passenger is the early last crossing in winter. It sits in the middle of the final paragraph, in the same weight as how bikes are locked to the rail. Give it a line of its own, or put it near the top next to the frequency, so someone skimming in December sees it. Don't add colour or a warning box. Moving it is enough.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Notes for ferry passengers" could head any ferry page. Something that names the route ("Crossing from the north landing to the market quay") would tell readers at once that they're in the right place.
- "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" reads as a description. It lands better said to the reader: "wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on".

**What's working.**
- The type pairing and the scale: a bold sans headline over a serif body gives exactly two clear levels, and they differ in face and weight as well as size. Keep that and don't add in-between sizes.
- The text column runs about 68 characters a line, with generous leading and paragraphs separated by space alone. It's comfortable to read and should stay that width if the page grows.
- The restraint: one colour and one alignment edge, with no boxes or ornament. It suits a public-service notice and makes the page feel reliable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone. Most people checking a ferry time will be on one.
