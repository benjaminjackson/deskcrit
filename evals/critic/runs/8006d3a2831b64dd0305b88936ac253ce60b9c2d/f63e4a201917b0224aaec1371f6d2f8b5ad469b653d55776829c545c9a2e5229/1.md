**First read.** The eye goes to the headline "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks" first, then drops straight to the purple "Keep your streak going" box, which looks like the heaviest button on the page. Only after that does it find the orange "Start your free week" button. The big teal panel on the right takes a glance and gives nothing back: two empty speech bubbles. It reads as a clean, friendly language-learning start-up landing page. It feels competent but generic, and the colours are a little noisy.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is the top of the marketing home page for a language-learning app, aimed at adults who haven't signed up yet, and its job is to get them to start the free week.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The promise is specific, the call to action is clear, and the type is tidy. The biggest problem is the purple streak box. It is a message for people who already use the app, set in the loudest block on the page, and it pulls attention away from the one action that matters.

**What would move it most**
1. **The streak box is aimed at the wrong person and is styled like a button** (defect). The purple panel under the button (about x 56–456, y 390–498) talks about keeping "your streak" to someone who has never done a lesson. It's a retention message on a page meant to sign people up. It also has rounded corners, a saturated fill and bold white text, just like the orange button, but it's larger and sits right below it. So it looks like a second action: people will try to click it, or have to work out which block is the real ask. Things that look alike get read as the same kind of thing (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*). The fact inside it is useful: "people who practise five days in a row finish twice as often." Recast it as a quiet line of proof for a newcomer, something like "Five minutes a day, five days a week, is what gets people to the finish", in plain text or a light tint. It should never use a filled, button-like shape. Or cut it.
2. **Three vivid colours compete, and the largest one does nothing** (convention). Teal (the panel), purple (the box) and orange (the button) are all at full strength and fill big areas. No single colour leads, so the orange button doesn't stand out as clearly as it should. The teal panel is the biggest coloured area on the screen, and it holds a picture with no information in it. Itten (*The Art of Color*) says to keep one hue in charge and tone the others down. The fix: make orange the only strong colour and keep it for the button. Turn the panel into a light tint or neutral, and take the purple out (this goes with finding 1).
3. **The picture only repeats the headline** (taste). Two empty speech bubbles say "conversation", which the headline already says in words. Half the hero is spent on a symbol that could sit on any chat, support or language site. This is the main reason the page feels templated: nothing on the right side tells a visitor what's different about Parlo. Use the space to show what the copy promises. For example, a real exchange in the learner's target language with the tutor's feedback on one word, or a glimpse of what week ten sounds like. Then the image adds something the headline can't.
4. **The copy leaves out what a sceptic would ask next** (defect, in the lines people will hold you to). "Short daily lessons" gives no length, but "ten weeks" is a promise, and it only means something if the visitor knows the daily cost (10 minutes? 30?). "A tutor that listens" can be read two ways: a human tutor or software. Readers who assume a person will feel misled when they meet an AI, and readers who assume software may wonder what the free week includes. Give the minutes, and say plainly what the tutor is. The ask "Start your free week" is specific, which is good. But nothing lets the visitor test the promise against something they already know. A line like "Try one lesson and hear how you sound on day one" would let them judge the value for themselves.

**Smaller things.**
- The teal panel's edges (about x 676 and 1076) don't line up with any other column. The steps below split at 461 and 867, and the right margin of the nav and steps is at 1224. Put it on the same columns as the step row, or run it to the right margin.
- The empty space is leftover rather than planned: a 150 px strip to the right of the panel and a blank band under the steps. Gathering it, for example by widening the panel to the margin, would make the white space look deliberate.

**What's working.**
- The headline is specific and checkable: "a real conversation" and "ten weeks" are an outcome and a deadline, not a vague claim. It clearly dominates the page, and the tight two-line setting holds together. Keep it.
- The type is tidy: four clear sizes (headline, intro text, step titles, step text and nav), one family, and weight doing the fine distinctions. The intro paragraph runs about 60 characters a line.
- The three steps (Listen / Speak / Use it) are plain, parallel and quick to scan. Each says who does what, and the thin rules above them group them without boxing them in.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the purple box is actually clickable. If it is, finding 1 becomes a second competing button, not just something that looks like one.
