**First read.** My eye lands on the bold serif title "Harbour Lights festival", then drops into three equal grey blocks of text with nothing to pick one out. It reads as a local festival notice, maybe a council or community web page. It feels calm and tidy but flat: closer to a typed memo than an invitation to a night of lanterns and paper boats.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page meant to get local families to come to the festival, know when and where each event happens, and sign up to volunteer.

**Verdict.** The page is cleanly typeset, but it doesn't do its job. All the facts a visitor needs (dates, times, places) and the one thing it asks of people (volunteer) are buried in 34 lines of continuous prose with no headings. Someone planning a weekend has to read every word to find out what time anything starts.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nobody can scan the three events.** Each column is about a different thing: the overview, the Saturday lantern walk and the Sunday pie contest. But none has a heading, and each is one unbroken paragraph of 11–12 lines. Someone looking for "when is the boat race?" has to read the whole page, and the boat race is never actually given a time. The two type sizes are clearly different, so the hierarchy is clean, but it only has two levels: the title and everything else. **Defect** (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web": text people skim is broken up by headings, short paragraphs and lists). Fix: give each column a heading that says what it is and when, such as "Lantern walk · Saturday at dusk" and "Pie contest · Sunday, judging at 4". Then pull the hard facts (day, time, place, what to bring) out of the prose into a few short lines under each heading. Keep the prose for the colour around them.
2. **The facts people will act on are vague or missing.** "The first weekend of September" gives no year and no dates. "At dusk" is not a start time. The boat race on the mill pond has no day or time. Stalls open "at ten each morning", but which mornings? People plan around these details and hold the organiser to them, so leaving them for the reader to work out is a defect. Fix: state actual dates (for example "Saturday 5 and Sunday 6 September") with the year, and give every event a clock time and a place.
3. **The volunteer request has no way to say yes.** "Volunteers are still needed to hand out programmes, carry chairs, and guard the cake tent" sits mid-paragraph in column 1. It names no person, link, form or deadline. The one thing the page asks of anyone goes nowhere, so even willing readers can't act. **Defect.** Fix: lift it out of the paragraph into its own short block with one exact action, such as "Sign up for a two-hour shift" with a link or a named contact and a date to reply by. The seagull line is charming, so keep it.

**Smaller things.**
- Column 1 ends on "seagulls." alone on the last line. Rewrap it so at least two words share the line (Bringhurst, §2.4.2).
- The gutters are lopsided. Text starts about 11 px to the right of each vertical rule, but its longest lines stop 20–27 px to the left of the next one. The rules look jammed against the column that follows them. Centre the rules in the gutter or widen the gap on the right of each rule.
- Mood (**taste**): a lantern festival called "Harbour Lights" is set entirely in charcoal on white, with no warmth, light or image. One warm accent or a single picture of the lantern walk would tell people this is an evening out and not an announcement. Keep it restrained.

**What's working.**
- The type is good for reading. The serif text face is comfortable at this size, the line spacing is generous, and the column widths of about 40–47 characters are right for multi-column text (Bringhurst gives 40–50). Lines sit on the same baselines across all three columns, which is why the page looks calm. Keep this when you add headings: put them on the same baseline grid.
- The title clearly dominates through size and bold weight, with even margins and no clutter. The page has one obvious place to start, so don't add competing elements at the top.
- The copy has real local character: paper boats, the prize for "the pie that makes the judges laugh", every lantern sold buying library books. That makes the festival feel like this town's own event and not a generic one. Restructure around it; don't cut it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the three columns stack into a sensible order on a phone. With no headings, a stacked version would be one 35-line block.
