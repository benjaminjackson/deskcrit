**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline and then straight to the big navy 38% slice. It moves on to the teal and orange slices, then starts going back and forth between the pie and the legend, and gives up somewhere in the fan of thin slices at the top left. It reads as a tidy, calm annual-report page about cost breakdown. The type and spacing look careful. The chart itself looks like a spreadsheet default.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an annual-report page telling shareholders and staff where Harrowgate's operating money went in fiscal 2026 and what that says about the business.

**Verdict.** The page is well made, but the chart doesn't do its job. A 14-slice pie with a separate legend turns a simple message ("people and premises take over half; everything else is small change") into a colour-matching puzzle. It also never says that message, or how it compares with last year.

**What would move it most**
1. **Wrong chart for 14 categories** (convention, and here a defect). Nine of the 14 slices are thin slivers of 1–3%, packed into the top-left. A reader can't compare them or tell them apart, and the eight 2–3% slices are effectively the same size. Pies only work for about five slices or fewer (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization* ch. 10.1). You already have the data sorted by value. Show it as a sorted horizontal bar chart with the category name and percentage on each bar. Or keep the pie and cut it to the four largest slices plus one "Other (10 categories), 25%" slice, with the breakdown in a small table below.
2. **The legend makes the reader match colours, and the colours can't carry it** (defect). Fourteen hues means pairs like Training/Travel (brown/red), Utilities/Software licences (two blues) and Insurance/Bank fees (two olive-greens) are hard to tell apart even with full colour vision, and impossible for many colour-blind readers. Each sliver is linked to its name only by colour (WCAG 1.4.1). The labels outside the pie make it worse: they show "2%" and "3%" with no category name, so the reader still has to decode the colour. Each percentage is printed twice, once on the chart and once in the legend, and neither copy is enough on its own. Label at the data (Tufte) and the legend can go. The palette can then drop to one colour, with a second colour kept for the one point to highlight (Few). For example: salaries in navy, everything else in one grey.
3. **The headline names the topic, not the finding** (taste, argued). "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" leaves the reader to work out the point. An annual report is read by people skimming for the story, and the chart has a strong one: "Salaries and rent take more than half of what we spend" (38% + 16% = 54%). Put that in the headline or the line under it, and the chart becomes the evidence rather than the puzzle.
4. **No comparison, so no figure means anything** (defect for this audience). A shareholder can't tell whether 38% on salaries or 12% on cloud hosting is up, down, or normal, so the page can't support any judgement about how costs are managed (Few: a figure needs an earlier period or a target beside it). Add fiscal 2025 shares next to 2026: a second bar or a change column in the table. Consider total costs in currency too, because shares alone hide whether the total grew.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing. The rest of the checklist passed.

**What's working.**
- **Type:** a serif headline and subtitle over a sans for labels, with clear steps between sizes, all set flush left on one margin. It feels editorial and trustworthy. Keep it.
- **Sorting:** the data is sorted by value and the legend percentages are right-aligned in bold. Those carry straight over to a bar chart.
- **Context:** the subtitle gives the exact period, and the source line notes the rounding. A stranger knows what every number means.
