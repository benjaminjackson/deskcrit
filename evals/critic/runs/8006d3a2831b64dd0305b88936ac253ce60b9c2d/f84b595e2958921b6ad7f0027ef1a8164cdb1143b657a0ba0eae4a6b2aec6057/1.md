**First read.** The eye goes straight to the dark middle card. It's the only dark area on a white page, and its heading is in a different typeface. Then it moves out to the two white cards, which look like each other. It reads as a short list of walking routes on a council, park, or tourism site, and it feels calm and tidy. But the middle card looks like it was pasted in from another website, so the first question is "why is that one different?", and nothing on the page answers it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web section that lets someone pick one of three local walks and open its details. The audience is the general public deciding where to walk.

**Verdict.** It doesn't quite do its job. The two outer cards are clean and readable, but the middle card breaks the system in five ways at once with no stated reason. So the page shows a preference ("this one!") it never explains. The second problem is that the three walks are hard to compare, because distance and difficulty are buried in sentences.

**What would move it most**

1. **The middle card is a different design, and nothing says why** (defect; convention). "Woodland loop" differs from its neighbours in five ways:
   - a dark slate fill instead of white with a pale border
   - square corners instead of rounded ones
   - a serif heading in a sans page
   - about half the inner padding (text starts about 16 px from the edge, not about 34 px)
   - an outlined yellow pill button instead of a filled blue one

   The viewer experiences this in three ways:
   - It wins the eye purely by contrast, so the page seems to recommend the woodland walk. But there is no "Recommended", "Easiest", or "Featured" label, so the reason is left to guesswork. That is meaning carried by colour alone (WCAG 1.4.1, Use of color).
   - Its button is an outline, which on the web usually means a secondary action. So the card that looks most important has the weakest-looking button.
   - The serif heading against sans everywhere else is a single unexplained mix of faces. Dair would call it conflict rather than contrast, because it doesn't come from a type plan.

   Gerstner's rule applies: items of one kind share one program, and a variant appears only where the rules call for it (*Designing Programmes*). The fix depends on intent:
   - If it isn't featured, make it match the other two exactly.
   - If it is featured, say so in words with a label, and keep the shared structure: same padding, corners, heading face, and button shape. Mark it with one change only, such as a tint or a heavier border, plus the label. Its button should stay the strongest on the page.

2. **The routes can't be compared at a glance** (defect; this is about the copy, not a checklist line). The facts someone needs to choose are distance, effort, surface, and amenities. They are written as prose, and each card puts them in a different order:
   - "Six kilometres… two steep climbs… a cafe"
   - "Four kilometres… flat and shaded… benches"
   - "Ten kilometres… paved… good for bicycles"

   To answer "which is shortest?" or "which is flat?", the viewer has to read and remember all three paragraphs. Fix: pull distance and difficulty out as one short, matching line on every card, in the same position (for example "6 km · two steep climbs"). Keep the sentence for character.

   Each "View route" button only invites a click on trust. A more specific label would also tell the viewer what they'll get, such as "See map and directions".

3. **Each card has about 200 px of dead space at the bottom** (defect). All three boxes run to about y=600, but their content ends around y=390. That leaves the lower 40% of each card empty, inside a border, where it reads as something missing (an image or map that didn't load?) rather than calm. Ruder's point is that empty space should be gathered on purpose, not left over (*Typography: A Manual of Design*). Fix: either size the cards to their content, or use the space. A small route map or photo would earn its place on a walking page. If an image is meant to be there, check that it loads.

**Smaller things.**
- The middle card's heading, paragraph, and button sit higher than the matching items in the outer cards, so the rows don't line up across the cards (Müller-Brockmann). This goes away once the card shares the same padding.
- "end." sits alone on the last line of the Cliff path text, and "metres." does the same in Woodland loop (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Fix the wrap or tweak the wording.

**What's working.**
- The grid: three equal columns with equal gaps, a generous left margin, and a page title that lines up with the cards' left edge. It's steady and easy to scan, so keep it.
- The outer cards' type scale is clear and properly varied. The title is about 32 px bold, card headings are about 24 px bold, body text is about 16 px grey regular, and button text is bold. Each level differs from the next in more than size.
- The copy has a voice. Lines like "benches every few hundred metres" and "a cafe at the far end" are the details a walker actually cares about. Keep them when you pull the facts out.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Keyboard focus style on the "View route" buttons, and whether they all lead to the same kind of page.
