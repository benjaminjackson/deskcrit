**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then drops into three paragraphs of body text in a column on the left. The right half of the frame is empty. It reads as a calm, plain information page for a small local river ferry. It feels quiet and trustworthy, a bit like a parish notice.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a public information page that people read before or while waiting for the ferry, so they can plan a crossing: when boats run, how long a crossing takes, and what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** The type is clean and easy to read, but the page doesn't do its main job well. The facts a passenger needs to plan around are given as vague phrases instead of times, and they're buried in prose. A reader comes away knowing roughly how the ferry works, but they still can't say when the first or last boat leaves.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times are vague (defect, about the copy).** All three paragraphs have this problem: "between early morning and late evening", "In winter", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk", "a little longer when the tide turns". These are exactly the details a passenger plans around and later holds the operator to, and none of them can be acted on.
   - "Before dusk" moves by more than an hour across a winter, so the reader has to guess.
   - "The quay" could mean the market quay or both landings. A reader at the north landing could take it to mean their last boat too. If they're wrong, they're stranded on the far bank.
   - The page also points people to the noticeboard at the landing, which they only see once they're already there.

   **Fix:** give clock times for the first and last boats in each season, the dates winter starts and ends, and the direction of the last crossing. Replace "a little longer" with a number, such as "up to 20 minutes".
2. **The key facts are written as prose, so you can't scan them (taste, argued).** There are about six facts a passenger actually checks: how often boats run, the first and last boat, how long a crossing takes, the winter timetable, that bikes travel free, and where changes are posted. All of them are inside running sentences, so you have to read three paragraphs to find one number. The heading calls these "notes", which suits prose, but a passenger checks this page in a hurry, often on the landing. Krug's point that people scan rather than read applies here. **Fix:** pull the times and the crossing length out into a short block or a two-row table (summer / winter) at the top, and keep the prose below for the bike rules and the explanation about the sandbank.

**What's working.**
- The type is set with real care: one family, a clear two-level scale with the heading set apart by weight as well as size, lines of about 70 characters, generous leading, and spacing between paragraphs instead of indents. Keep all of that when you restructure.
- The bicycle paragraph is the strongest writing on the page. It tells the rider what to do, in what order, and why ("so the deck stays clear for people walking to the stairs"). That's the voice and level of detail the timetable paragraphs need.
