**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline, then goes straight to the six underlined italic phrases. They look like links, so for a moment this reads as a web page with six things to click. Then it settles into what it is: a calm, well-set note for people helping at a village fete. It feels friendly and orderly. The underlines are the only thing that jars.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a short instruction note read on screen by stallholders and volunteers, so they turn up on time, label their cakes, and handle the raffle and parking correctly.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound, and the note mostly does its job. Two things hold it back. Every key fact is both italic and underlined, which makes the facts look like links and blunts the emphasis. The copy also leaves out the facts a helper would act on: the date, whether "ten" means morning, and who collects what by four.

**What would move it most**
1. **Six phrases are italic and underlined, so they look like links and the emphasis is spread thin.** This covers "arrive before ten", "labelled with their ingredients", "three o'clock sharp", "collected by four" and "on the field only" (which wraps onto a second line). On screen an underline means "link", so the reader's first take is that these are clickable, and anyone who taps one gets nothing. Stacking two signals on one phrase also shouts. Lupton says emphasis "usually requires only one signal", and Hochuli warns that underlining "hinder[s] linear reading". With five of about ten clauses marked, nothing actually stands out. *Convention, with a real cost here.* Fix: drop the underline and keep the italic. Better still, pull the times out of the paragraphs into a short list (10:00 arrive, 15:00 raffle draw, 16:00 tickets collected), where they need no emphasis at all.
2. **The copy leaves out what the reader has to act on.** There's no date anywhere, and "Notes for the summer fete" doesn't say which fete or who the notes are for. "Before ten" doesn't say whether that's morning, though that's probably safe to assume. "All tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box" can be read two ways. Either winners must claim their prizes by four, or someone must gather up the unsold tickets. If a winner reads it the wrong way, they lose their prize, and they'll hold the organisers to it. "Parking is on the field only" doesn't say for whom. *Defect:* these are times and conditions people will act on. Fix: add the date to the heading ("Saturday 14 June: notes for stallholders"), write "10 am", and rewrite the raffle line to say who does what, for example "If you win, collect your prize from the raffle table by 4 pm, or it goes back in the box."

- "only." sits alone on the last line and splits the phrase "on the field only". Rebreak the line, or the problem goes away once the underline and the list change are made.
