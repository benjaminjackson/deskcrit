**First read.** The eye lands on the large blue slice, then goes up to the serif headline, then across to the legend to find out what blue means. Within two seconds it reads as a page from an annual report showing how a company's operating costs split by category. It feels calm, well set and trustworthy. It is also flat: it gives you five numbers and no point.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a page in a company's annual report that tells shareholders and board members where the 2026 operating money went, and what is worth noticing about that.

**Verdict.** It is clean and correct, but it only lists the numbers. The headline states the topic, there is no total and no last-year figure, and the second-biggest slice is an unexplained "Other". So a reader comes away knowing the shares but not what they mean.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline names the topic, not the finding.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" is a label. The reader has to work out the story from the slices, and the obvious one ("salaries are the biggest cost, at 38%") is not worth a page. In an annual report the reader wants to know what changed or what matters: did cloud hosting grow, did salaries' share fall? Make the headline state that, in a full sentence with a verb, and keep the current line as the subtitle. *Taste, argued as common sense:* the reference entry for this has no approved source, but a report page that only restates its own chart wastes the reader's attention.

2. **"Other" is 25%, a quarter of all costs and bigger than three of the four named categories, and nothing explains it.** At the top-left of the pie the grey slice is the second-biggest shape on the page, and the legend says only "Other". A shareholder will read that as either sloppiness or something being hidden. Grouping only works for small leftovers. Either split out its largest items until "Other" is clearly the smallest slice, or add a one-line note saying what it contains (for example, "Other: depreciation, travel, legal and professional fees…"). *Defect:* it confuses the reader and can mislead them.

3. **No total and no comparison.** Shares on their own don't let a reader judge anything: 12% on cloud hosting could be up or down, large or small. There is no total cost in money and no fiscal 2025 figure anywhere. Put the total under the title (e.g. "Total operating costs: £X m") and show last year's share next to each legend row, such as "38% (41%)" or a small up/down change. That comparison is what turns this page from a record into information (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a number needs a unit, a period and a comparison). *Convention.*

4. **The legend repeats the chart and leaves the page left-heavy.** Every percentage appears twice, once in the slice and again in the legend. The category names appear only in the legend, about 100 px away, so the reader has to match colours back and forth five times. The legend is also a thin strip at the top right, which leaves a large empty area in the right half below it (roughly y 380–680). The fix: put the category names on or next to the slices (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*: label on the graphic) and use the freed right column for the change-from-last-year column and a note on what "Other" contains. That solves this finding, the balance and the colour-matching problem together. *Convention and taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- The white "12%" on the orange slice looks to be about 2.8:1 contrast, below even the 3:1 allowed for large text. The white "16%" on teal and "9%" on purple look to be around 3–4:1, so they pass only if they measure as large text (WCAG 1.4.3). Measure them, and use dark text on the orange or darken the fills.
- Category is told only by hue. Blue and purple, and teal and grey, may be hard to tell apart for colour-blind readers (WCAG 1.4.1). Direct labels on the slices (finding 4) fix this.
- The type sizes crowd together around 14–18 px: page header about 14, legend about 15, slice labels about 17, subtitle about 18. Sizes that close read as accidents rather than levels (Bringhurst). Bring the header and legend to one size, and separate the subtitle by weight or colour rather than by a near-identical size.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing: a firm serif for the headline and subtitle and a quiet sans for data and furniture, with a small-caps "4.2 COSTS" section label. It sets a clear order and feels right for an annual report.
- The chart is built honestly. It is flat, with five slices, starts at 12 o'clock with the biggest slice first, gives "Other" a neutral grey, has no gridlines, the shares add to 100, and a source line notes the rounding. Keep all of that when you add labels.
- The legend's percentages are right-aligned with tabular figures and the rules between rows are faint, so it already works as a small table. That makes it a natural place for the prior-year column.
