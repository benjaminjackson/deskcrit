**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, then drops into the italic standfirst, and the standfirst is good enough to pull you into the body. It reads as a long-form feature on a small independent magazine site. It feels calm and competent, and anonymous: apart from the words, nothing tells you this is a story about forty households, bath tubs and a water-tap queue.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the opening screen of a long-form web feature. It has to make a casual visitor commit to reading.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The writing carries it and the hierarchy is clean. The biggest problem is that a story about a particular place, photographed by the people who live there, opens with no picture and in a default typographic voice, so the screen gives up the strongest hook it has.

**What would move it most**
1. **The residents' photographs are missing from the opening screen.** The byline promises "Photographs by the residents", but the first screen is all text, and the bottom ~220 px is empty. The headline sets up a place you want to see (the allotment, the last bus stop), and the page never shows it, so the reader has to take the story's charm on trust from the prose. *Defect* against this job: the headline and the image should make one idea together, and here there's only half of it. If a lead image exists and didn't load in this capture, this is fine. If it doesn't exist, put one resident photo, full column width or wider, between the byline and the standfirst, or directly under the headline.
2. **The lines are too long for comfortable reading.** The body and the standfirst look like they run about 85–90 characters a line ("It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to"). Bringhurst's range is 45–75, about 66 ideal. On a long feature that cost adds up: readers lose their place at each line return and tire sooner. *Convention, and it costs something here.* Narrow the text column to about 600–640 px at this size, or raise the body size a step, until a line holds roughly 65 characters. The headline can keep the wider measure.
3. **The typography has no voice for this story.** Stock Inter in near-black, a grey byline and a small green "Feature" label could belong to any product blog or docs site. This is a story about neighbours, salvaged bath tubs and Saturday gossip, and the setting says "software". The reader gets no sense that Groundwork is a particular publication with its own point of view. *Taste.* Give the headline (and ideally the standfirst) a face with more character, such as a serif or a humanist display face, and keep Inter for navigation and metadata if you like. Pair that with the photography from point 1.

**Smaller things**
- The headline breaks "bus / line" and leaves "line" alone on the second line. That's a weak runt that splits a noun phrase. Rebalance it as "The allotment at the end / of the bus line" with a balanced wrap or a manual break.
- The navigation doesn't show the current section. This is a Feature, but "Features" in the top bar looks like every other link. Mark the current section with weight or an underline.

**What's working**
- **The copy.** The standfirst ends on the queue at the water tap as "the best place in the district to hear the news", which is concrete and specific to this place. The first paragraph opens on a person, a date and a condition ("nothing was built that could not be moved"). Keep this level of writing; it's doing the design's job for it.
- **A clean, settled hierarchy.** Kicker, then headline, then byline, then italic standfirst, then body. Each level is distinct and the steps are clear, so the eye never has to hunt for where to start.
- **Restrained colour.** One green accent on the kicker and everything else neutral, which keeps attention on the words.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- The empty bottom of the screen: it may be a lead image or later paragraphs that hadn't loaded when this was captured. Check it in a live browser.
- The height of the nav links' click targets (they need at least 24 px).
