**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline, "Plan the week your team actually works". It then drops to the grey subhead and lands on the blue "Start a free trial" button. Within two seconds it reads as a planning tool for team leads. It feels clean, confident and calm, but also bare: the right half and the bottom third are empty, so the page looks like a hero whose product shot never arrived.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the hero section of a SaaS landing page, meant to get team leads and engineering managers to start a trial.

**Verdict.** The words and the hierarchy are good, and the order of what you see first is right. What holds it back is that it asks for a trial without showing anything. There is no picture of the "one honest picture of the next five days", so the viewer has to take the claim on trust. That empty space is also what makes the page feel unfinished.

**What would move it most**
1. **Show the honest week.** The page promises a picture, then shows none. The empty area to the right of the text and below the button is where it should go. As it is, the viewer can't check "honest" against anything, and "Start a free trial" asks them to commit before they've seen what they would get. The empty right side also makes the page lopsided: all the visual weight sits top-left and nothing on the right balances it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). The fix: put a real five-day view of a plausible team in that space, with tickets, a meeting block and someone's day off visible together. The viewer can then hold it up against their own sprint board and calendar and see the gap themselves. **Defect** (it costs trust and conversion); the balance point is a **convention**.
2. **Make the ask specific.** "Start a free trial" could be on anyone's page. It names neither what happens next nor what the viewer gets. A better button says what they will see, in their own terms: for example "See next week for your team", or "Connect Jira and see your week". The best version sets the result next to something they already trust, such as their current sprint board or calendar, so they can judge the difference themselves. **Taste**, argued from what makes a viewer click: a specific promise is easier to act on than a generic one.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline tracking: at about 90 px bold, the headline looks set with text spacing, and the word spaces in "Plan the week your" look wide. Tighten the letter and word spacing a little so the two lines read as one block (Bringhurst 2.1.1; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, p. 34).

**What's working.**
- The copy. "Plan the week your team actually works" names a real pain: the gap between the plan and the actual week. The subhead names the inputs (tickets, meetings, leave), the output and the time frame. A stranger knows who it's for and what it does. Keep both lines word for word.
- The hierarchy and alignment. There's a clear jump from the headline (about 90 px) to the subhead (about 20 px) to the nav and button (about 16 px). The logo, headline, subhead and button all share one left edge. The single blue is used only on the one action. Keep that discipline when the product image goes in.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the button and nav links, and how the hero reflows on a phone.
