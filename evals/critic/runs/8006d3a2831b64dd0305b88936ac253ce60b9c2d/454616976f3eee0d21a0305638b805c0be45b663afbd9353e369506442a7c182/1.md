**First read.** The eye lands on "Revenue overview" and then moves straight across the three big numbers: $4.82M, 112%, 1,246. Then it drops to a steady blue staircase of bars that obviously climbs. It reads as a calm, well-made SaaS finance dashboard for a founder or a finance lead. It feels trustworthy. That makes the one thing that doesn't add up more damaging.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal revenue dashboard that lets a founder or finance lead check annual growth at a glance and trust the figures enough to repeat them.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart craft are solid. But the numbers on the page don't agree with each other, and on a finance screen that's the failure that matters. Fix the labelling of the plan table and its link to the chart before anything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **The figures contradict each other.** *Defect.* The table on the right says "Revenue by plan, 2025" and totals $4.82M. That's exactly the ARR figure in the first card. But the twelve bars in "Monthly revenue, 2025" add up to roughly $4.49M (about 312 + 328 + … + 447 thousand). And a December of about $447K, run forward for a year, would be about $5.4M ARR, not $4.82M. A finance reader who adds up the bars, or just notices that the table equals the ARR card, now has two readings and no way to choose:
   - (a) the table is ARR by plan and the heading is wrong;
   - (b) it's 2025 revenue and the chart is wrong.

   Either way they stop trusting every number on the page, and a dashboard is only worth what people trust in it. The direction: decide what the table measures and name it exactly. If it's ARR, call it "ARR by plan, Dec 2025". Then make sure every total on the page can be traced to one basis. If the chart's recognised revenue is meant to differ from ARR (one-off fees, for example), say so in the subtitle so the gap is explained rather than discovered.
2. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding.** *Taste.* The catalogue's source for this is off-list, so it's my judgment. "Monthly revenue, 2025" makes the viewer read the bars to find the story: up about 43% from January to December, with small dips in April and July. One line that states it, such as "Monthly revenue rose from $312K to $447K in 2025", hands that story over in a second and gives the viewer something to repeat in a meeting. Keep the units line as it is.

**Smaller things**
- "Revenue by plan, 2025" sits about 4px lower than "Monthly revenue, 2025" in the next column. That near-miss reads as a slip. Put both headings on one baseline.
- The plan table has no comparison, unlike every other figure on the page. Add a share or a change from last year per plan (Dashboards, Few), so the viewer can see which tier drives the growth.
- The right column ends at the table, leaving an empty block from about y430 to the bottom. That makes the page heavy on the left. Either let the table's column end with the chart or give the space a purpose, such as plan share or the change per plan.
- The "Jan to Dec 2025" chip at the top right could be a date picker or a plain label. If it changes the range, give it a chevron or calendar icon. If it doesn't, drop the fill so it reads as text.

**What's working**
- The KPI cards: each has a label naming the metric and its period, a large figure, and a delta with an explicit baseline ("+3 pts vs 2024", "vs Dec 2024"). Every headline number comes with context, as Few asks. Keep that pattern exactly.
- The chart's honesty and restraint: one blue, a zero baseline, faint gridlines, units stated in the subtitle, and a source line that says what's counted ("closed invoices only"). That's Tufte's data-ink ratio done properly.
- The type scale is tight: title, KPI figures, section headings, body/labels and a small caption size. Lower levels are separated by weight and grey rather than by more sizes (Refactoring UI).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date chip opens a picker, and hover/tooltips on the bars. With a summed axis this rough, exact values on hover would matter.
