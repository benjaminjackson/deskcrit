**First read.** The eye goes straight to the headline. It is big, black, and the only heavy shape on the page. From there it drops to the green button, then crosses to the invoice card on the right, then the three cards below. Within two seconds I know this is invoicing software for freelancers and small studios. It feels calm and trustworthy, and a little quiet.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero of a marketing landing page. Its job is to get a freelancer or small-business owner to start the free trial.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, there is one ask, and the copy is specific. The biggest thing holding it back is the invoice card. The headline promises speed, and the card only shows a finished invoice, so the right half of the hero adds no evidence for the claim.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invoice card shows the result, not the claim** (taste, argued). The headline promises speed ("the time it takes to pour coffee"), and the subhead promises that the product "fills in your clients, rates, and tax" and chases late payments. The card on the right shows none of that. It is a finished, static invoice with no sign of what was filled in for you, how long it took, or whether a reminder is queued. A viewer looks at it and learns only that the product makes invoices, which they guessed from the word "invoice." It is also small and pale next to a 3-line headline, so it reads as decoration, not proof. Make the picture carry the subhead's promise. For example, mark the auto-filled fields (client, rate, tax), or add a line under the total such as "Sent · reminder scheduled for 3 June." Then the viewer sees the time saved instead of taking it on trust.
2. **The button asks for a commitment, not a comparison** (copy). "Start free for 14 days" is clear and specific, but it is the ask on every SaaS page. It asks the viewer to trust the coffee claim and sign up to test it. The stronger ask lets them check the claim against something they already know: their last invoice, or the spreadsheet they use now. Something like "Make your next invoice now, free" turns the trial into a test the viewer runs on their own work. The 14-day detail can sit in a line under the button.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line breaks (common sense; the reference's source for heading breaks is unverified): "in the / time" splits a phrase, and "coffee." sits alone on line three. Narrow or widen the text box so it breaks at phrases, e.g. "Send an invoice / in the time it takes / to pour coffee."
- Feature cards: each card's text fills the top half and leaves the bottom third empty, so the white space is scattered across three boxes instead of gathered. Either shorten the cards to fit the text or give the space a job, such as a small product detail per card.

**What's working.**
- One clear entry point. The headline is the only heavy element, the dark green button is the only coloured one, and everything else steps back in grey. Keep that restraint when you add proof to the invoice card. Don't let the card compete with the headline in weight.
- The copy speaks the viewer's language and lands on them: "chases late payments for you," "written in your own words," "Clients pay from the email with two taps." Each line says who does what.
- The grid is clean. The hero text, the invoice card, and the three feature cards all share the 120 px and 1160 px content edges. The invoice figures are right-aligned, and the arithmetic adds up ($640 + $45 + 8% tax = $739.80). A wrong total there would undercut the whole pitch.
