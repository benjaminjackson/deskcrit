**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph to the blue "See the timetable" button, and then stops on the last line: "Design review: failed, everything on this page is centred". That line changes the read. It stops being a ferry page and starts to look like a draft someone left a note on. Apart from that, it feels quiet, tidy and plain, close to a stock template, with everything packed into the left 60% of the screen.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a desktop landing page for a local passenger ferry. It's for day-trippers and commuters, and its job is to get them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The layout underneath mostly works: the headline is clearly the first thing you read, there's one clear action, and the type is clean. But it isn't ready to publish. An internal review note is printed on the page in body type, the times a rider would plan around are left vague, and there's nothing on the right half of the screen to tell you this is about the sea.

**What would move it most**
1. **An internal note is on the public page** *(defect; common sense, no catalog entry)*. The line under the button, "Design review: failed, everything on this page is centred", is set in the same type as the content, so a rider reads it as part of the page. It tells them the page "failed", which costs trust right before they click. It's also wrong: every block is set flush left on one edge at x≈160 and nothing is centred. Remove it. If someone believes the layout has a centring problem, take that up in review, not on the page.
2. **The hours are left vague** *(defect, copy)*. "From early morning until late evening" is the fact a rider actually plans around, and it can be read two ways: is the first boat at 6:00 or 8:00, and is the last one at 19:00 or 22:30? Someone who guesses wrong misses the last boat home. "Every forty minutes" is exact, so the vague hours next to it stand out more. Give the first and last sailing ("First boat 6:40, last 22:20"), and the fare if it's simple. "See the timetable" is a good ask: it names exactly what you get. A rider who already has the times they need won't have to click.
3. **The right half of the screen is empty, and nothing says sea** *(convention plus taste)*. All the text and the button sit in a column from x≈160 to 790. The remaining ~490 px on the right has nothing in it, so the page leans left and looks like a picture failed to load (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). The copy promises "the harbour slide past from the open deck", but the page shows an Inter headline and a blue button that could belong to any software product. My view as a designer: one picture on the right, the boat, the deck or the bay, would balance the page and do the selling the paragraph is trying to do. If there's really no picture, move the column so the empty space looks chosen rather than left over.

**Smaller things.**
- Type sizes: I count about six. The body (~19 px), the feature list (~18 px) and the stray line (~20 px) are a pixel or two apart, so they read as mistakes rather than separate levels (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Set the list in the body size, or make it clearly smaller and change its colour.
- The feature list ("Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board") has no markers, and its line spacing is close to the paragraph's. It reads as leftover lines rather than a list. Add bullets, ticks or small icons, or a clear change of weight.
- The headline breaks between "twelve" and "calm minutes", which splits the number from what it counts. "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" keeps the promise in one piece.

**What's working.**
- The headline is a specific, concrete promise: a time ("twelve minutes") plus a feeling ("calm"). It's clearly the largest and heaviest element, so the eye knows where to start. Keep both the wording and the size jump.
- One primary action with a precise label. "See the timetable" says exactly what the click gets you, and the button is large and obviously pressable.
- Restraint in the system: one blue used only for the brand name and the action, near-black text, one family, a paragraph at about 68 characters a line, and one clean left edge.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's focus and hover states, and how the column reflows on a phone.
