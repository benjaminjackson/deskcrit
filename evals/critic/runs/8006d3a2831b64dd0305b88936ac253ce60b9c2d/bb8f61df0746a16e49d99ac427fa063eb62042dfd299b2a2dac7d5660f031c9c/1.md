**First read.** The eye lands straight on the big serif headline, "The finest bread in the valley", then drops to the two-line description and stops there. The small brown "HARTLEY'S BAKERY · SINCE 1962" label is picked up last, or not at all. It reads as the top of a small, traditional bakery's homepage: calm, warm and well set. Then you notice the lower third of the screen is empty and there is nothing to do.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the first screen of a local bakery's website, and its job is to get a neighbour to come in or to order, which means showing the bread and saying where, when and how.

**Verdict.** It's a handsome piece of type, but it isn't yet a working homepage. The biggest problem is that the viewer is given nothing to do next: no picture of the bread, no hours or address, no way to order. Meanwhile 40% of the screen is empty.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's nowhere to go after "Mill Street."** (Defect.) The eye goes headline → subline → empty cream. There's no button, no hours, no way to order and no navigation, and everything below about y=500 is blank. That blank area isn't framing anything; it's just where the content ran out. A local visitor arrives wanting to know "is it open, where is it, can I order a loaf", and this screen answers none of those questions. The fix: end the eye's path on one specific action from the visitor's own day, such as "Open today 7–2 · 14 Mill Street" or "Reserve tomorrow's sourdough", and use the empty area for it or for a picture (Arnheim on the eye's path through a composition). A vague "Get in touch" or "Visit us" would not fix it.
2. **The headline makes a claim the page never backs up, and the copy that would persuade is buried.** (Defect in the copy; the image point is common sense, not a cited rule.) "The finest bread in the valley" could be on any bakery's site. It asks you to take "finest" on trust, and there's no picture of the bread to judge for yourself. The proof is already on the page but set small: a wood-fired oven, baked every morning, on Mill Street, since 1962. Either promote one of those concrete facts into the headline ("Baked in a wood-fired oven on Mill Street every morning since 1962" is the kind of claim a reader can check) or pair the headline with a photo of the loaves, so the viewer sees the evidence instead of reading an adjective.
3. **The headline breaks badly: "The finest bread in the / valley".** (Convention, Bringhurst 2.4.2 on a short word left alone on the last line; that source is about paragraphs, and applying it to headings is my judgment.) At the largest size on the page, one word sits alone on line 2. It looks accidental, and it splits "the valley" across the break. Break at the phrase: "The finest bread / in the valley", or shorten the line so it fits on one.

**Smaller things.**
- The apostrophe in "HARTLEY'S" looks straight, not curly. Use the typographic ’ (no checked source; this is common sense).

**What's working.**
- The type pairing: a soft old-style serif for the headline against a plain sans for the body. They contrast clearly in form and size, the headline clearly dominates, and the "fi" ligature is joined properly at display size. Keep both faces.
- The restrained palette: cream, near-black and a single brown used only for the label. It feels warm and traditional without decoration, so keep the brown for that one job.
- The subline is the best copy on the page. It names the actual loaves, the oven, the time of day and the street. That level of detail is what the headline should reach.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether a navigation bar, image or call to action sits just below the fold or loads in later. If one does, check that it shows within this first screen at common laptop heights.
