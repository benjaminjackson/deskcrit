**First read.** Your eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", reads down the left column through the paragraph and the three perks, and stops at the blue "See the timetable" button. Within two seconds it reads as a ferry company's homepage hero, for locals and day-trippers. It feels tidy, sensible and a bit bare: a column of text pushed against the left side of a big white screen, with nothing to see.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about the piece, so I'm assuming it's the top of a small ferry operator's website, and its job is to get a visitor to decide to take the crossing and open the timetable.

**Verdict.** The headline and the order of the content are good. As a page, though, it looks unfinished. The right half of the screen is empty, and there is no picture of the boat, the bay or the "open deck" the copy promises. A product sold on calm and scenery is being sold with words alone, so the piece doesn't make anyone want to go.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right 40% of the screen is empty, and nothing shows the crossing.** All the content sits between about x=160 and x=790. Everything to the right of that, and everything below the button, is plain white. The page leans left and looks like a template whose image didn't load, not a deliberate empty space. Visual weight needs a counterweight on the lighter side (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Empty space works when it is shaped on purpose against the content, not left over (Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*, "Form and counter-form"). The bigger cost is what's missing: "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck" is the reason to go, and the viewer has to picture it themselves. Fix: put a photo of the deck, the bay or the boat in the right half (or run it full-bleed behind a calm area for the text), so the headline and the picture make one idea between them. If a picture is meant to be there and failed to load, this finding still stands until someone checks that it really appears. *Defect* (balance is a convention; the missing picture costs persuasion).
2. **The copy leaves out the two facts a ferry passenger acts on: the price and the sailing times.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" makes the viewer guess. 6am or 8am? Last boat at 9 or at 11? The fare isn't mentioned anywhere on the page. These are the facts people plan around and later hold the company to. "Late evening" can be read two ways, and the wrong reading strands someone on the far shore. Fix: give the first and last sailing ("First boat 6:40, last 22:20") and the adult fare next to the "Bicycles travel free" line, so the viewer can say yes without clicking. Also, "the north landing" only means something to locals; name the place. *Defect.*
3. **The paragraph and the feature list are almost the same size.** The paragraph looks about 20 px and the list about 18 px. That isn't different enough to read as a different level, so it looks like a slip rather than a choice. Count the sizes and you get four: the eyebrow, the headline, the paragraph and the list. Two of them collide. Fix: set the list the same size as the paragraph and set it apart with weight, a small icon or a tick, or set it clearly smaller in a muted colour. Bringhurst covers this under sizes from a scale, and Refactoring UI says to make things bolder rather than bigger. *Convention.*

**Smaller things.**
- The page has no header or navigation and no logo, only the blue text "Coastal Ferry Co." That's fine for a hero crop, but on a real homepage, add the mark and a way to reach fares and routes.

**What's working.**
- **The headline** is specific and promises a benefit: a place ("the bay"), a number ("twelve") and a feeling ("calm"). Keep it word for word.
- **The order and grouping**: eyebrow, then headline, then paragraph, then perks, then one action. Each gap is wider between groups than inside them, so the page reads in one clean pass.
- **The call to action** "See the timetable" names exactly what happens when you press it. It's the only blue block, so it's clearly the primary action.
