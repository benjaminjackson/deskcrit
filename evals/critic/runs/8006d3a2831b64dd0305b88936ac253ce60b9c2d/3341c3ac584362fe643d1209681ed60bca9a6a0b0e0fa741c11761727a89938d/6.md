**First read.** The eye goes to the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then down through the paragraph to the blue button. It reads straight away as the top of a ferry company's homepage. It feels calm and orderly, which suits a ferry, but it is also sparse to the point of feeling unfinished. The right half of the screen is blank, and nothing on the page shows the bay, the boat or the harbour the copy describes.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a local ferry's website. Its job is to tell residents and day-trippers that the crossing is quick and pleasant, and to send them to the departure times.

**Verdict.** It works on the basics: the hierarchy is clear, the type is clean and the ask is specific. But it looks like a page with its picture missing. The empty right half and the lack of any departure times or fare mean it sells the experience only in words and leaves out the facts a rider needs before going.

**What would move it most**
1. **Empty right half, no picture.** Everything sits in the left 60% of the frame. The space from about x=790 to the right edge holds nothing, and no image of the boat, the deck or the bay backs up "watch the harbour slide past". The page tips to the left, and the empty half looks like an image that failed to load rather than a choice (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). The copy sells a view and the page shows none, so this is also where it loses the viewer's attention. **Defect** unless a picture really does load there. In that case check it in a live browser, and this finding becomes "make sure that picture is the open deck on the water". If not, put one strong photograph in that space that shows the calm the headline promises.
2. **The page leaves out what a rider acts on.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no first or last sailing. There is no fare, and nothing says which side the "north landing" is on. To plan a trip, the viewer has to click away just to find out whether they can catch a boat now. The button "See the timetable" is a good ask: it names one exact thing to do. But the page could do part of that job itself. Showing the next departure and the first and last sailing next to the button ("Next boat 14:20 · first 06:40, last 22:00") gives the reader a fact they can check against their own day. **Defect** (copy). The figures are made-up examples; use the real times.
3. **The three-line list repeats the paragraph and looks like loose text.** "Bicycles travel free" restates "Bring a bicycle", and "Seats on the open deck" restates "the open deck". The lines have no bullets or icons. Their size is a step down from the body text, but only slightly, and the spacing between them is close to the paragraph's own line spacing. They read as a fragment of the paragraph, not as a list of benefits. **Defect** (common sense: every element should earn its place). Either cut the matching clauses from the paragraph so the list carries those facts on its own, or use the list for facts the paragraph lacks: fare, journey time, step-free boarding. Then make it look like a list.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count five sizes: label about 14, headline about 56, paragraph about 20, list about 18, button about 18 bold. The paragraph and list are only about two pixels apart, so they read as a mistake rather than two levels (Bringhurst, 3.1.1 "Don't compose without a scale"). Set the list at the body size, or separate it by weight or colour.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** It is concrete and about the reader: it gives a time ("twelve") and a feeling ("calm") in five words. It is set large and heavy with tight line spacing, so it is clearly the first thing you see.
- **The button.** "See the timetable" says exactly what happens next. It is the only blue block on the page and matches the blue of the "Coastal Ferry Co." label, so colour does one consistent job.
- **The paragraph.** The text is easy to read: lines run about 68 characters, with generous line spacing and one clean left edge for every block.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether an image or other content is meant to fill the empty right half. Check in a live browser.
- How the left column reflows on a phone.
