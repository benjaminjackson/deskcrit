**First read.** The eye lands on the black headline "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. Within a second it reads as the hero of a small ferry operator's website. It feels clean, quiet and trustworthy, but also bare. Nothing shows a boat, a bay or a harbour, and the right 40% of the frame and the bottom quarter are empty white, so it looks like a picture failed to load.

**What it's for.** Assumption, since I was told nothing: this is the desktop landing hero for a local passenger ferry, and its job is to get day-trippers and commuters to check sailing times and decide to go.

**Verdict.** The typography and the order of reading are solid, and the headline is good copy. But the hero sells a calm crossing using nothing except words, and it holds back the one fact a ferry passenger needs to act on: when the boats actually leave. The biggest problem is that there's no departure time and no picture of the trip.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy hides the times and the price people need in order to go.** In the paragraph, "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" can't be planned around. The reader can't tell whether early means 6:00 or 8:00, or whether late means 21:00 or 23:30. Someone who reads "late evening" as 23:00 and turns up at 22:15 for a last boat that left at 21:40 is stranded on the wrong side of the bay. Sailing times are exactly what passengers hold the operator to, so this is a defect, not a style point. The page also never says what a crossing costs, yet it tells you bicycles travel free.
   - **Fix:** state the first and last sailings ("First boat 6:40, last boat 22:00, every 40 minutes") and the adult fare.
   - **Make the ask concrete:** "See the timetable" is specific enough, but it sends people away to find what they came for. The next two departures from the north landing, shown right by the button, would let them judge "I can make the 10:20" in their own terms. *Defect.*

2. **The trip itself isn't shown, and the layout feels left-heavy and unfinished because of it.** All the weight (headline, text block, button) sits in the top-left 60% of the frame. Nothing on the right or below balances it, so the page tips left and reads as a broken image slot rather than as chosen white space (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). A calm crossing, an open deck and the harbour sliding past are things people want to see, and here they're only told. Nothing marks this out as this ferry rather than any service's page.
   - **Fix:** fill the right side with a photo of the open deck or the bay at the size of the text block, aligned to its top and bottom. That gives the page its counterweight and puts the promise in front of the reader.
   - **Or:** if the stark page is deliberate, pull the content toward the vertical centre so the emptiness looks chosen. *Defect* (the empty right half looks like a load failure). Treating the photo as the best counterweight is *taste*.

**Smaller things**
- **The list repeats the paragraph.** "Seats on the open deck" restates "watch… from the open deck", and "Bicycles travel free" partly restates "Bring a bicycle". Cut the repeats, or make the paragraph carry only the feeling and the list only the facts (fare, bikes, accessibility, drinks). This is common sense, not a cited rule.
- **The type has one size too many.** I count five sizes: label ~14 px, headline ~52, paragraph ~20, list ~18, button ~18. The paragraph and list are only about 2 px apart, so they read as a slip, not as two levels (Bringhurst, §3.1.1). Set the list at the paragraph size and mark it as a list with bullets or icons, or drop it to the label size if it's meant as detail. Don't split the difference.
- **The headline breaks "twelve / calm minutes".** The number is split from its unit across the line break (Bringhurst §2.4.6, keep a number with its unit). Rebreak it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes", or keep "twelve calm minutes" together with non-breaking spaces.

**What's working**
- **The headline.** "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes" names the trip, gives a concrete number and sets a mood in seven words. It's the one line here that couldn't sit on anyone else's page. Keep it word for word.
- **A clear order of reading.** One heavy headline, a lighter paragraph and one saturated button, all on a single left edge at x≈160. The eye never has to search, and the button is the only colour, so the ask is obvious.
- **Body text that's easy to read.** The paragraph runs about 60 characters a line with generous line spacing, close to Bringhurst's ideal. Don't widen the column when a picture goes in beside it.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Hover and focus states on "See the timetable", and how the hero reflows on a phone. On a phone the headline will rewrap, so check where the line break falls.

**Checklist**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 7 yes, 1 no (every element does a job)
- What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (distinct text levels)
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 12 yes, 1 no (balance)
- Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (heading line breaks)
- Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
- Pictures: skipped, none
- Charts and data: skipped, no chart
- Interface: 33 lines, 32 yes, 1 unsure (focus indicator)
