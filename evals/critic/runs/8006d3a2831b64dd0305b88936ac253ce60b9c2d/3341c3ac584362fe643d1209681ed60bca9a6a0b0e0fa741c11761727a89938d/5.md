**First read.** My eye goes straight to the big headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then down through the paragraph to the blue "See the timetable" button. Within two seconds I know this is a ferry company's landing page. Then I notice the right 40% of the screen and the bottom third are blank, and the page feels unfinished, as if the main image never loaded.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is the top of a ferry operator's homepage, there to persuade locals and day-trippers to take the crossing and send them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The text half works: the hierarchy is clear, the type is clean and the ask is specific. But the page is missing the half that sells the trip. Nothing shows the bay, the boat or the open deck, so a "calm" crossing is only a claim, and the layout tips into the top-left corner.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half is empty, so the page tips and the promise has no picture behind it.** All the content sits in a column on the left, roughly x 160–790, y 90–570. To its right and below it is plain white. The weight piles into one corner with nothing to balance it, so the screen looks lopsided and unfinished rather than calm (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). It costs persuasion too. "Calm minutes" and "watch the harbour slide past" ask the viewer to imagine a view the page never shows. A visual that pairs with the headline, such as the deck with the harbour going by, would let the viewer see the calm instead of being told about it. **Defect.** If an image is meant to fill that space and failed to load in the capture, this point is about the loading, not the layout; check it in a live browser. If no image is planned, the column needs a counterweight: a picture, a timetable panel, or a wider measure set nearer the centre.
2. **The copy gives a frequency, not the next boat.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" leaves the viewer to work out when they can actually go, and "early" and "late" are vague right where people make plans. The button then sends them off to find the answer. The most useful thing this screen could show is the next departure ("Next boat 10:40 from the north landing") plus the real first and last times. The viewer could decide on the spot, and the timetable link becomes a backup rather than the only way to an answer. **Taste / common sense**, but it carries weight here because times are what people act on.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: the paragraph (about 20 px) and the three feature lines (about 18 px) are almost the same size, so the list reads as a slip rather than a separate level (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Either set them the same size and let space mark the list, or step the list down clearly, or add weight or bullets to it.
- Headline break: "twelve / calm minutes" splits the key phrase across the line break. Break after "bay" ("Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes") so the promise reads as one unit.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete promise with a number in it ("twelve calm minutes"). It says what the trip is like, not just what the company is, and at its size and weight it clearly comes first.
- The call to action is specific and has no competition. It's one solid blue button whose label names exactly what you get, and it reuses the blue from the "Coastal Ferry Co." label, so the colour reads as the brand.
- The left column is set well. The paragraph runs about 60 characters a line with comfortable spacing, everything shares one left edge, and the gaps between groups are clearly bigger than the gaps inside them.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether an image or panel is meant to sit in the empty right half and failed to load. Check in a live browser.
