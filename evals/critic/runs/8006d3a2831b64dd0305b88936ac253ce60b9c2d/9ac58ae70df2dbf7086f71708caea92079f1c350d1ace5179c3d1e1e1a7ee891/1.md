**First read.** The eye lands on the "Pricing" heading, drops to the three large prices ($9, $29, $79), then reads the short feature lists, and stops. After the third list there is no button or next step, and the bottom third of the screen is empty. It reads as a tidy, generic SaaS pricing page for a scheduling tool. It feels calm but unfinished, as if the end of each card got cut off.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is the pricing page of a booking and calendar SaaS. Its job is to get a small-business visitor to pick a plan and start a trial or sign up.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The page shows prices but doesn't let anyone choose one: no card has a button, no plan is recommended, and the visitor can't tell what each price covers. The layout and type are clean, and the gaps are in what the page asks for and what it explains.

**What would move it most**

1. **None of the plan cards has an action (defect).** The cards end at the third feature line. A visitor who has just picked "Team" has nothing to click there. The only way forward is the "Start free" button in the top-right nav, which sits far from the decision and doesn't say which plan it starts or whether it applies to all three. So the path ends exactly where the visitor has decided, and they have to guess whether "Start free" means $9, $29, or a trial. Nearly every pricing page puts a button in each plan card, so visitors look for one there (Nielsen, Jakob's law); a page that leaves it out needs a clear reason, and this one has none. Fix: give each card its own button at the bottom, naming the action and plan, e.g. "Start 14-day Team trial". If there is a free trial, say on the card how long it lasts and whether a card is needed.

2. **The page doesn't say what each price buys (defect: these are the numbers a buyer later holds you to).**
   - **What "/ month" covers.** "$29 / month" can be read two ways: $29 for the whole team, or $29 per user. If the visitor assumes a flat price and finds out at checkout that it's per seat, that's a broken promise.
   - **Whether bigger plans include smaller ones.** Team lists "Up to 10 calendars / Shared availability / Reports" but not "Booking page" or "Email reminders". A stranger can fairly ask whether Team drops the booking page Solo has.
   - **What "calendar" means.** Is it one person, one room, or one connected Google calendar?

   This is the Nielsen 6 problem: the visitor has to fill in what the page left out, and may fill it in wrong. Fix: put the billing unit under each price, e.g. "per workspace, billed monthly". Open the Team and Business lists with "Everything in Solo, plus:" and "Everything in Team, plus:". Write "calendar" in the visitor's terms, e.g. "1 person's calendar".

3. **Three equal cards and no recommendation (taste, argued from common sense).** The three cards have the same border, weight, and colour, so the visitor has to weigh all three with no help. That's most work for exactly the buyer the page is probably built for, a small team that could fit Solo or Team. Fix: pick the plan you want most people on, probably Team, and make it stand out. Give it a blue border or tint taken from the "Start free" blue, a short "Most popular" or "For teams of 2–10" label, and the only filled button among the three cards. Make the other two buttons outlined.

4. **The copy could be anyone's (defect).** "Simple plans for teams of every size. Change or cancel whenever you like." fits any SaaS on the internet, and the heading just repeats the nav label. The visitor learns nothing about why Tidewell, or which plan fits them. Fix: write a heading or subtitle that answers the visitor's real question, such as who each plan is for or what they'll stop doing by hand. Keep "cancel anytime", but as a reassurance near the buttons, not as the page's main promise.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav: "Pricing" looks exactly like Product, Features, Customers, and Contact. Mark the current page with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Subtitle: it breaks so that "you like." sits alone on the second line. Rewrite it, or narrow the box so both lines carry a fair share.
- Type sizes: I count about six. Several sit only a step apart:
  - feature text (~15 px) against "/ month" and nav (~16 px);
  - subtitle (~18 px) against plan names (~20 px).

  Merge each pair into one size and separate levels by weight or colour instead (Bringhurst, "Don't compose without a scale"; *Refactoring UI*).

**What's working.**
- The prices are the biggest text in each card, bold and dark, with a light "/ month" beside them. The thing a buyer scans for is the thing they see first. Keep that when you add buttons and labels.
- The cards are evenly spaced and the inside of each follows the same order: name, price, list. That makes comparing across the row easy, so keep the order once the content changes.
- Colour is held back. One blue for the brand and the action, everything else neutral. That leaves room to use the blue to mark the recommended plan and its button.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the nav and on the buttons you add: check them in a browser.
- Whether the empty bottom third is the real end of the page or just the edge of the screenshot. If it really ends there, the page needs a footer, FAQ, or comparison table so it doesn't trail off.
