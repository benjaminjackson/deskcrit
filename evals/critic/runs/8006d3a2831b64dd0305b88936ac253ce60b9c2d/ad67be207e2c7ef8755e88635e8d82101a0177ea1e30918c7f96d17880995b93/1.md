**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board" (bold, dark, the only large thing on the page), picks up the small "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it, then drops into three short paragraphs. It reads as a page of practical notes for people using a river ferry. It feels calm and orderly, though plain. Once you start reading, you want to know when the boats actually leave, and the page doesn't say.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about it, so I'm assuming this is an information page that tells someone about to take the ferry what they need to know to catch it and board, bike included.

**Verdict.** The layout and type do their job: it's clear, legible and well ordered. The page still fails at its main task, because the words never give the times a passenger needs. "Early morning", "late evening" and "in winter" can't be planned around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule isn't stated as times or dates (defect, in the copy).** Paragraph 1 says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says "in winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour". These are exactly the lines a passenger acts on and later holds the operator to, and each can be read more than one way. Is the first boat at 6:00 or 7:30? Is "winter" December to February, or the day the clocks change? Does "each hour" mean on the hour? Someone who guesses wrong misses the last boat or waits on the quay for 40 minutes. The page then sends them to "the shelter at each landing" for printed timetables, which is the one place they aren't while reading this. To fix it, give the first and last departure from each landing, the exact dates of the winter timetable, and the departure minutes (":00, :40, :20" or "on the hour"). If the full timetable doesn't fit, link to it here rather than to a shelter.
2. **The bicycle paragraph describes other people instead of telling the reader what to do (defect, in the copy).** "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail" reads like an observation, yet it's an instruction the reader must follow. Write it to them: "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike onto the lower deck and lock it to the side rail. Bring your own lock." It also never says whether there's a limit on bikes or whether space can run out, which is what a cyclist will want to know next. The page also gives no fare for foot passengers. That's a fair gap for a page called "Before you board".

**Smaller things.**
- In the first paragraph, lines run about 82–85 characters, above Bringhurst's comfortable 45–75. The text is only three short paragraphs, so the cost is small, but narrowing the column by about 15% would bring it into range (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2).

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean, with three distinct levels: a small grey letterspaced label, a large bold heading and regular body text. Each level differs in both size and weight, so nothing competes with the heading (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). Keep this.
- "Before you board" is a good heading. It says who the page is for and when to read it, in plain words.
- The page is set with restraint: one family, one flush-left edge, even space between paragraphs and generous margins. That suits a utility notice, and it's easy to read on a quayside phone screen.
