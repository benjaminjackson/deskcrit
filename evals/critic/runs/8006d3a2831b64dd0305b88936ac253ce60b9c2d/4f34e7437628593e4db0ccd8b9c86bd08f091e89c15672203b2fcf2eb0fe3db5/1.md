**First read.** My eye goes to the three big KPI figures first, then down to the blue bars. It reads as a SaaS revenue dashboard for a founder or a finance lead, and it feels calm and trustworthy. Once I start checking the numbers against each other, that trust starts to slip.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about it, so I'm assuming it's the overview screen of a billing or analytics product. Its job is to let an exec see at a glance how revenue did in 2025, and to give figures they can repeat to someone else.

**Verdict.** It's a competent, clean dashboard, and the layout mostly does its job. The biggest problem is that its own numbers don't seem to agree with each other. On a screen people will quote to a board, that costs more than anything visual.

**What would move it most**

1. **The plan table and the chart contradict each other** (defect). "Revenue by plan, 2025" totals **$4.82M**, which is exactly the "Annual recurring revenue, Dec 2025" figure. But the monthly bars, which are labelled "recognised revenue," add up to about **$4.49M** for the year by eye. Also, December's bar is about $447K, and twelve times that is about $5.4M, not $4.82M. A CFO will see one of these gaps within a minute. After that, they won't trust any figure on the page. The likely cause is that the table shows ARR by plan but is titled "Revenue." If so, call it "ARR by plan, Dec 2025." If it really is 2025 revenue, the total has to match the chart, or a note has to explain the difference (for example, non-recurring revenue, or closed invoices only). Either way, every heading should name the exact measure and date it holds.

2. **The chart frame and gridlines are as heavy as the data** (convention: Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*). A black box surrounds the plot, and near-black gridlines run all the way across every $100K. They're the darkest lines in that panel, so they cut each bar into slices and compete with the bars for attention. Remove the frame, and make the gridlines thin and pale grey (or keep only two or three), so the blue bars are the strongest thing in the chart.

3. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding** (taste; the sources for this rule aren't on the approved list). "Monthly revenue, 2025" makes the viewer work out the story from twelve bars. The story is steady growth, from about $310K to about $450K a month (roughly 40%), with small dips in April and July. Put that sentence in the title or the subtitle. That's what the exec will take into the meeting, and it also gives the chart a text alternative.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: the page title "Revenue overview" (~26 px bold) and the KPI figures (~30 px bold) are almost the same size, so neither clearly leads. The 14, 13 and 12 px body, label and axis sizes also run together. Make the KPIs clearly bigger or the title clearly smaller, and drop one of the small sizes.
- "Revenue by plan, 2025" sits about 4 px lower than "Monthly revenue, 2025" across the gutter. Put the two headings on the same baseline.

**What's working.**
- The KPI cards are done right. Each figure has a metric name, a date, and a comparison with last year ("+18% vs Dec 2024"), so the viewer can tell good from bad without hunting (Few). Keep that pattern for any figure you add.
- The chart's labelling is honest: the axis starts at zero, there's a units line ("Thousands of US dollars, recognised revenue"), and there's a source line. That's exactly what makes problem 1 fixable: the labels already tell you what each number is supposed to be.
- The restraint holds it together: one blue for the data, green only for positive change (with a + sign as well), and a plain sidebar with a clear active state. Leave it this quiet when you fix the chart.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the "Jan to Dec 2025" pill is a date picker or just a label. If it's a picker, it needs something that shows it can be clicked, such as a chevron.
