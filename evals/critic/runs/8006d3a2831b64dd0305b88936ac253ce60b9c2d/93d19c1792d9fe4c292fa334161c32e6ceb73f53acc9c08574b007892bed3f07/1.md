**First read.** The eye lands on the bold question, "Save changes to the shared calendar?", then drops to a row of four identical blue buttons. It stalls there, reading each label from left to right to find the answer to the question. It looks like a clean, competent confirmation dialog until you have to press something. Then it feels like a quiz.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web confirmation dialog for an editor of a shared calendar, maybe a choir organiser, who has changed rehearsal times and is deciding whether to publish them to all followers.

**Verdict.** The heading and body copy are good, but the button row lets the dialog down. Four equal-weight filled buttons hide the answer to the question, and "Discard changes" sits right next to "Save changes", dressed exactly like it, one slip away from losing someone's work.

**What would move it most**
1. **No primary action: all four buttons are equal** (defect). The four buttons along the bottom have the same fill, colour, weight and height. The question asks for a yes or no, but nothing tells the viewer which button is the yes. They have to read all four labels, and they're reading left to right, so they hit "Export as file" and "Discard changes" before they reach "Save changes". On a dialog people dismiss on autopilot, that costs time and causes mistakes. Material Design and Apple HIG both say a view should have one visually dominant primary action, with secondary actions lower in emphasis and cancel weakest; two equal filled buttons already counts as a fault, and this has four. The fix: keep "Save changes" as the only filled button, make "Keep editing" an outline or text button, and drop the others further, as below.
2. **"Discard changes" is next to "Save changes" and styled like a safe choice** (defect). Discarding throws away the rehearsal edits, and nothing on screen offers undo. It sits second in the row in the same blue as Save, with only a normal gap between buttons. The user has to read its label to know it's dangerous, and a mis-click or a hurried tap loses work. Apple HIG ("Alerts") and Material Design ("Dialogs") both say destructive actions should be set apart from safe ones and styled as destructive. The fix: move Discard to the far side of the row, away from Save, give it a destructive style (red text or outline, not a blue fill), and make sure it isn't the default.
3. **"Export as file" competes with the decision it doesn't belong to** (convention: Nielsen, Progressive Disclosure; Nielsen heuristic 8). The dialog asks one question: publish these times or not. Exporting answers neither side of it, yet it takes the first and most-read spot at full weight. The decision grows from two options to four, and the viewer has to work out how exporting relates to saving. The fix: take it out of this dialog, or reduce it to a quiet text link set apart from the decision buttons. That leaves a plain Save / Keep editing choice, with Discard off to one side.

**Smaller things.**
- The button row's left edge (about x 276) sits a few pixels outside the left edge of the heading and body text (about x 280). Either align the button edges to the text or make the offset obviously deliberate.

**What's working.**
- The heading is a real question about a specific object, "the shared calendar", and the body line spells out the consequence for other people: "will be shown to everyone who follows this calendar". That's exactly the stakes a person needs to hear before publishing. Keep both lines as written.
- The button labels are verb plus object ("Save changes", "Discard changes", "Keep editing"), not "OK" and "Cancel", so each one says what will happen. Keep the wording when you change the styling.
- The type is restrained: a bold heading, one regular body size, and button text. There's no size clutter, and the heading stands out clearly.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
