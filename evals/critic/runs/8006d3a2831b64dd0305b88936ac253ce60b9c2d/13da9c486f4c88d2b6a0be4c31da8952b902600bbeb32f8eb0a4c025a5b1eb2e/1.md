## Design critique: "Notes from the boat shed"

**First read.** Your eye lands on the bold serif headline, "Notes from the boat shed," then reads the left column and moves right. It reads like the opening of a blog post or newsletter from a community boatyard. It feels calm, plain and well set. Then it stops: after "anyone who wants to help." there's nothing else, and the lower 60% of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page from a volunteer boat-restoration group, and that its real job is to get new people to come along on a Thursday. Correct me if it's only a diary entry.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean, with no real defects. As a way to recruit volunteers, though, it fails. The second paragraph invites people but gives them no time, no place and no first step, so a reader who's interested has nothing to act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invitation can't be acted on (defect, copy).** The right column says "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings… a spare apron on the hook by the door for anyone who wants to help." Read as a stranger would, it leaves out what anyone needs before turning up:
   - **When:** what time on Thursday, and is it every week?
   - **Where:** "the end of the lane" only makes sense to someone who already knows the lane. There's no address or town.
   - **What to do:** just walk in, or get in touch first? There's no contact.

   The warmth (the kettle, the apron) does its job of lowering the barrier. Then the line ends on a general "anyone who wants to help" instead of one thing to do. To fix it, end on one specific step with the facts attached, for example: "Come by any Thursday from 6:30 pm, [address]. No need to book; just walk in." The place to act should be impossible to miss, not tucked into the end of the second paragraph.
2. **The headline and layout hide the ask (taste, argued).** "Notes from the boat shed" promises a diary, and the two columns give the description and the invitation the same weight. Someone skimming sees an atmospheric note and leaves without noticing they've been invited. If recruiting is the point, give the invitation its own weight. That could be a subhead such as "Join us on Thursdays," a separate block, or a headline that says volunteers are welcome. Keep the descriptive paragraph as the hook.

**Smaller things.**
- Every line of the copy is sensory (tar, cedar, three clinker dinghies on trestles), but nothing on the page shows the shed. One honest photo of the dinghies or the slipway would do a lot of the persuading. It belongs in the empty lower half (taste).

**What's working.**
- **The type is calm and readable.** A bold serif headline sits over a regular serif body from what looks like one family. Lines run about 58 characters, the line spacing is generous, and the two columns sit on shared baselines. Keep this setting when you add the missing content.
- **The copy has a real voice.** "There is always a kettle on" lowers the barrier better than any "No experience needed!" badge would. Keep the tone and add the facts.
- **There's a clear order:** one dominant headline, then two equal columns on a simple halves grid, with generous margins.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I can't tell whether the empty lower 60% is just the end of a short web page, which would be fine, or a fixed frame such as a slide, which would leave it top-heavy and unfinished. Check how it's actually shown.
