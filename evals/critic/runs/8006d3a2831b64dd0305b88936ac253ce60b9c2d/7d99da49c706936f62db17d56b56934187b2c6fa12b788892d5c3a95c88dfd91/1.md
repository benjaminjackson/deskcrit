**First read.** Nothing tells the eye where to start, so it falls on the first line at the top left and reads down as if this were one block of prose. There are four question-and-answer pairs, but on the first pass they look like eight lines of equal weight. You only see the pattern by reading the words. It feels calm, clean and plain. It also feels unfinished, like a draft before anyone styled it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is a help or FAQ section on a bakery's ordering site, for customers who have already ordered and want to change, hand over, check or cancel that order. Someone with one question should be able to scan straight to it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The headings are as heavy as the answers, so a reader can't scan for their question. Two answers that people will act on (the cancellation deadline and what the collector must show) can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings look like the answers, so you can't scan for your question** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two headings use the same weight and colour as the text under them, and they look a pixel smaller if anything. All eight lines carry the same strength, so nothing leads. A reader looking for "cancel" has to read every line instead of glancing down the left edge. Dair's point applies: a contrast must be obvious at a glance, or it looks like an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). The approved fix: make each heading differ from its text in a way that is obvious at a glance, in size, weight, structure, form or colour, not by one step. Here, setting the headings in semibold or bold at this size would do it, or setting the answers in a lighter grey. A page title such as "Questions about your order" would also give the eye a place to start (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance).
2. **Two answers that people act on can be read two ways** (defect, copy).
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead": ahead of what? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup time or any time on the day before. Someone who cancels at 9 a.m. for a 10 a.m. pickup the next day falls between those readings and may find they've paid for bread they can't get. It also doesn't say *how* to cancel, while the first answer does say "Open your order". State the exact cutoff, such as "until 6 p.m. the day before your pickup", and add the step.
   - "show it at the counter along with a name": whose name, the person who ordered or the person collecting? If the collector gives their own name and staff expect yours, they leave with nothing. Say "your name".
   - "holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact. Add what happens to the reader if they're late: is the order sold, kept, or refunded?
3. **The allergy answer sends an online reader to a counter they aren't at** (defect, copy). The answer is "a card by the till". Someone checking on the site before they order, which is the person most likely to read this, can't see that card. Without allergen information on the site, they either guess or don't order. Point them to the ingredients on each product page, or say how to reach the baker before ordering.
4. **The lines are too long for comfortable reading** (convention). The answers run across about 820 px, roughly 95–100 characters a line ("Give the person your order number… along with a name."). Bringhurst gives 45–75 characters as the comfortable range (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, §2.1.2). The answers are short, so it costs less here than in long text, but it adds to the flat look. Narrow the column to about 600–640 px at this size.

**Smaller things.**
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergy answer. A narrower column or a small rewrite will fix it (Bringhurst §2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- Spacing does the grouping: each heading sits clearly closer to its own answer (about 10 px) than to the answer above it (about 25 px). Keep those ratios when you add weight to the headings.
- The headings name the task in the reader's own words ("Collecting for someone else"), not system terms. That makes them good scan targets once they look different from the answers.
- The answers are plain, short and friendly ("the bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule instead of just stating it). Keep that voice when you add the missing details.
