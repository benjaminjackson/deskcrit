**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It starts at the top-left line ("Changing your pickup time") only because that's where reading starts, then works down the block line by line. All the text looks the same, so it reads as one long stretch of help copy. It turns out to be pickup help from a bakery's online ordering, but you only learn that from the third or fourth sentence: there's no title, logo or label to say whose page this is. It feels calm and plain, and also unfinished.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help or FAQ panel for customers who have ordered bread online. They come to it with one question and want to find the answer by skimming.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The four answers are clearly written, but the questions look just like the answers, so you can't skim this page; you have to read it. Two of the answers also leave the deadline and the pickup rule open to two readings.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings can't be told apart from the answers** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two headings are in the same face, weight and near-black as the paragraphs. They are about the same size too, maybe a pixel smaller. Someone looking for "can I cancel?" can't run their eye down the headings, because nothing marks them as headings. They see eight lines of the same grey and have to read from the top. Dair calls this weak contrast, and it's worse than none: a difference too small to see looks like an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). The fix is to make the headings obviously different from the text, and to use more than size to do it: semibold or bold at about the same size is usually enough here, and you could add a slightly quieter colour for the answers (Dair, chs. 8a–8g and 9). The page also needs a title above the list ("Collecting your order" or similar) that says what the page is and gives the eye a place to start.
2. **The deadline and the pickup rule can be read two ways** (defect, copy). "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead": a day ahead of what? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup time, or any time on the day before. A customer who picks the wrong one tries to cancel at 9 pm the night before a 10 am pickup, gets refused, and pays for bread they don't want. Give the rule an exact time ("You can cancel until 24 hours before your pickup time"). "Show it at the counter along with a name" has the same problem: does the collector give their own name or yours? Say it plainly: "They'll need your order number and your name."
3. **The allergy answer sends an online customer to the shop** (defect, copy). "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps only someone already standing at the till. The person reading this is ordering online, and that's when they need to know what's in the bread. Put the ingredients on the order page and point to them from here, or give a way to ask before ordering.
4. **The lines are too long** (convention). "Give the person your order number…" and "Orders can be cancelled…" run to about 95 characters, well past the 45–75 range (Bringhurst, 2.1.2). The cost is small with one- and two-line answers, but those two long lines also run past the wrap point of the other two answers, so the text block's right edge looks loose. Narrow the column to about 600–640 px at this size.

**Smaller things.**
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Narrowing the column will probably fix this; if not, rewrite the line.
- The copy is mostly clear, but several answers stop at the fact. "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" would do more if it told the reader what happens after that ("after that, your order goes back on the shelf").

**What's working.**
- The answers are short, written in the second person, and start with what to do ("Open your order and choose a new slot"). Keep that voice.
- The groups are spaced well: the gap between a heading and its answer is clearly smaller than the gap between items, so each question sits with its answer. Keep that rhythm when you make the headings bold.
- One sans serif face, near-black on white, left-aligned on a clean left edge. It's quiet and readable, and it doesn't need decoration.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the headings are links or expandable sections, and how the column reflows on a phone. The column is about 820 px wide here, so on narrow screens the line-length problem should go away by itself.
