**First read.** Nothing catches the eye first. It lands on the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time", only because that's where reading starts, and then moves down a column of grey text that's the same weight all the way. Within a few seconds it's clear this is order help for a bakery: four questions and four answers. It feels calm and plain, but it reads as one long paragraph rather than four answers you can scan. There's no title and no bakery name.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help or FAQ panel for a bakery's online pre-order service. Customers who have already ordered come here to find one answer quickly: how to change, hand off or cancel an order, or what's in the bread.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The words are mostly clear and the spacing groups each question with its answer correctly. But the headings look the same as the answers, so the page can't be scanned, and that's the whole point of a FAQ. The cancellation deadline can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings look like body text** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are in the same face, the same regular weight and the same near-black as the answers. At about 18 px they even look a touch smaller than the answers, which look about 19 px. A visitor looking for "cancel" has to read every line, because nothing marks where a topic starts. The only cue is the slightly larger gap above each heading. Dair's point is that a difference meant to be seen has to be obvious at a glance, and a one-step difference looks like an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). Make each heading clearly different from its answer, and do it with more than size: set the headings semibold or bold at the same size or one step larger, or keep them regular and make the answers a lighter grey. Then the four topics become a list the eye can scan.
2. **"Up to a day ahead" is a deadline that can be read two ways** (defect). Under "Cancelling an order", "a day ahead" could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time on the day before. If a customer reads it the generous way and tries to cancel at 9pm the night before a 7am pickup, they lose their money and blame the bakery. A cancellation deadline is exactly the kind of line people hold you to later. State the cut-off exactly, for example "until 6pm the day before your pickup" or "up to 24 hours before your pickup time". End on what it means for the reader: "after that, we've already baked your bread and can't refund it" (only if that is the policy).
3. **No title and no starting point** (taste). The panel opens straight on the first question. Nothing says whose help page this is or that the page is about orders, so someone arriving from a link has to work it out from the questions. A short title such as "Your order: common questions" would give the eye somewhere to start and confirm they're in the right place. This matters less if the page has a header that wasn't captured. If it doesn't, add one.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the one-line answers ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run about 95 to 100 characters, over Bringhurst's 45 to 75. That's tolerable for a single line, but narrow the column to about 640 to 680 px so longer answers stay comfortable to read.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer. Narrowing the column or rewording the answer will move the break.
- Copy, "along with a name": whose name, the person's or yours? Say "and your name" or "and their own name", or the person collecting may be turned away.
- Copy, "Open your order": the line doesn't say where. Link the words to the order page, or name it ("Open your order from the confirmation email").
- Copy, allergies: the answer ends at "ask the baker", which only helps someone who is already in the shop. For someone ordering online with an allergy, either link the ingredient list or say how to ask before they order.

**What's working.**
- The spacing groups each question with its answer: about 10 px between a heading and its answer, and about 25 px between topics. Keep that ratio when the headings change.
- The answers are short, use plain words, and speak to the reader ("Give the person your order number", "baked for you"). That is the right voice for a small bakery. Keep it.
- One typeface, one colour and one left edge. The page is quiet in a way that fits help text. It only needs one clear level added for the headings, not more decoration.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether "Open your order" and the question headings are links, and whether the headings expand and collapse like an accordion. If they're interactive, they need to look like they can be clicked.
