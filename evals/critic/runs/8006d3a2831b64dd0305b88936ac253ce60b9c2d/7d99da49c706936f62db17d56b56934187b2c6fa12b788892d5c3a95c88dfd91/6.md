**First read.** Nothing catches the eye. It starts top-left only because that's where reading starts, then goes down a grey column of eight lines that all have the same weight. After a second or two I can tell this is help text for a bakery's click-and-collect orders, but only because I read the words. The layout itself doesn't show any structure. It feels calm and plain, and close to unfinished: it reads like a text file more than a designed help page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help or FAQ section on a bakery's online ordering site. A customer who has placed an order, or is about to, scans it to find the one question they have.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The headings look exactly like the answers, so a reader scanning for their question has to read every line. Also, the two lines a customer will act on (the cancellation deadline and what to bring to pickup) can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings look like body text, so the page can't be scanned.** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two headings are in the same face, weight and near-black as the answers below them, and if anything a pixel or two smaller. Since nothing stands out, the eye has nowhere to start. A reader looking for "cancel" has to read the four headings and the four answers as one run of text to find it. Spacing does the grouping, but it can't do it alone. Dair's point is that a small difference reads as an accident; it needs to be obvious at a glance. Make the headings plainly different from the answers. The cheapest way here is weight: semibold headings at the current size, or one clear size step up plus weight. Also consider dimming the answers slightly so the questions come first. *Defect* (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8; Tschichold, *The New Typography*).
2. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways.** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." Ahead of what: the pickup slot, or the bake? And does "a day" mean 24 hours before the slot, or by the end of the day before? A customer who reads it the generous way and tries to cancel at 9 am for a 10 am pickup tomorrow is refused. They'll remember the wording as a broken promise. The answer also never says *how* to cancel. State an exact cutoff tied to the slot, such as "You can cancel until [time] the day before your pickup", and say where to do it, the way the first answer does. *Defect.*
3. **"Along with a name" leaves the person collecting unsure what to bring.** Whose name: the buyer's or the collector's? Someone standing at the counter with the wrong one is the failure this answer is meant to prevent. Say it outright, e.g. "They'll need your order number and your name." *Defect.*
4. **The answers run too wide.** The one-line answers under "Collecting for someone else" and "Cancelling an order" look to be about 100 to 105 characters, well past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. That's tolerable for one line, but this column will grow as more questions are added. Narrow the text column to about 65 to 70 characters. The empty right side of the page can take the slack. *Convention* (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2).

**Smaller things.**
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the Allergies answer. Narrowing the column will likely re-wrap it; if not, adjust the break (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The page has no title. A short heading such as "Your order: common questions" would tell a visitor arriving from a link what this is before they read anything.
- The Allergies answer only helps people who are already in the shop. Someone ordering online can't see "a card by the till". If allergen information exists online, link it here; that's the moment the customer decides whether to order. (This is copy, not a checklist item.)
- "Open your order" assumes the reader knows where that is. A link to it would save a step.

**What's working.**
- The spacing already groups things correctly. Each heading sits much closer to its answer (about 8 px) than to the previous answer (about 30 px), so once the headings get weight, the structure will read immediately. Keep those gaps.
- The answers are short, use direct verbs, and speak to the reader ("Give the person your order number"). "The bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule in a warm, human way. Keep that voice when you tighten the two ambiguous lines.
- One typeface, one colour and a flush-left column suit a utility page. Don't add decoration to fix the hierarchy; weight is enough.
