**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three grey-black paragraphs of even weight. It reads as a plain, calm information page for a small river ferry. It's tidy and quiet. But nothing below the title stands out, so a passenger looking for "when's the next boat?" has to read every sentence.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and what to do with bikes, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and has nothing wrong with it. The page still mostly fails at its job, because it never gives a time a passenger could plan around. The details people act on are vague ("early morning", "late evening", "before dusk"), and the page points them to a printed timetable in a shelter.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times you'd plan around are missing or vague** (defect). In paragraph 1, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't give a first boat, a last boat, or which minute past the hour the boat leaves. A passenger can't tell whether a 6:30 or a 22:00 departure exists. In paragraph 3, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" is a deadline with no clock time, and dusk moves by over an hour across the winter. Getting it wrong means being stranded on the wrong bank. "The quay" can also be read two ways: the market quay only, which leaves the last boat from the north landing unstated, or both landings. The fix is to give real times, such as first and last departure from each landing in summer and winter, and the departure minutes. If the times really change week to week, say so and say where the current times are online, not only in the shelter.
2. **The facts are buried in prose that you have to read in full** (taste). The few facts people act on (how often, first and last boat, crossing time, winter changes, bikes free) all have the same weight as the sentences around them. Someone checking on a phone at the landing can't scan for them. Put the schedule in a short, scannable form, like a small table or a two- or three-line summary under the title, and keep the prose for the bike and luggage rules. Bold, not a larger size, is enough to lift a key time if it stays in the text (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).

**Smaller things.**
- The title "Notes for ferry passengers" is accurate but could head any ferry page. Naming the route ("North landing ↔ Market quay ferry") tells passengers right away that they're on the right page.
- Paragraph 1: "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" gives no idea how much longer. If it can matter for a connection, give a rough figure ("up to 20 minutes").

**What's working.**
- The text setting is right: the column is about 65–75 characters wide, line spacing is generous, paragraphs are separated by space alone, and the right edge is ragged. That makes it comfortable to read. Keep the measure and spacing when you add the schedule.
- The hierarchy is restrained: one bold title, one body size, near-black on white. There are only two type sizes, with a clear step between them and a weight change. Don't add sizes when you add the schedule; use weight or a table.
- The bike paragraph is clear, step-by-step copy: who does what and when (foot passengers board first, then riders wheel bikes on and lock them to the rail). The schedule copy should be written that way too.
