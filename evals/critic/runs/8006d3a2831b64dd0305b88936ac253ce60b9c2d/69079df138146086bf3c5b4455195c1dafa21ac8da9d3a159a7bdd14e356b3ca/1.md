**First read.** The eye goes straight to the black headline "Notes for ferry passengers". Then it hits a wall of pale grey and has to slow down to read it. The page reads as a short information notice for people taking a river ferry, which is clear. It also feels faded and unfinished: the headline is the only thing on the page with any weight, and everything the passenger actually needs looks like a disabled placeholder.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or panel that tells ferry passengers how the service works, so they can plan a crossing and turn up at the right time with the right expectations.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The structure is sound, but all the useful text is set too pale to read comfortably. The most important line, the winter timetable warning, is the smallest and faintest thing on the page. The copy also never gives the times a passenger would plan around.

**What would move it most**

1. **All the body text is faint.** *Defect.* Both paragraphs and the winter note are light grey on white, roughly the grey of a disabled control. The heading is the only dark thing on the page, so the page has a top and nothing else. A passenger checking the page on a phone at the landing, in daylight, will struggle to read it, and low-vision readers may not manage at all. It looks well under 4.5:1; measure the grey against white to confirm. Darken the text or change its background until it reaches at least 4.5:1 (WCAG 2.2, SC 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum)). In practice, use a near-black or dark grey for the paragraphs, and keep paler greys for text that really is secondary.

2. **The line a passenger acts on is set as the least important.** *Defect.* "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." is the only warning on the page and the only line that changes what someone does. It sits last, smaller than the paragraphs and just as pale, so it reads like a footnote. A winter traveller who skims the two paragraphs gets the summer schedule and misses the warning. Promote it: set it at body size or larger and darker, or give it its own marked block near the top. Only the hierarchy needs to change; the layout can stay.

3. **The copy gives no times you can plan with.** *Defect.* "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" leaves you to guess two things: when the first and last crossings are, and which minutes past the hour the ferry leaves. Someone deciding whether a 6:30 or 22:00 crossing exists can't tell. The winter line has the same gap: it says the times change but not how, then sends you to a noticeboard you can only see once you're already at the landing. Give the first and last departure times and the departure pattern (for example, "on the hour and at :40"). Say what changes in winter, such as fewer crossings or a later start, and give the dates. If you can, point to a winter timetable people can check before they leave home.

**What's working.**
- The single left-aligned column with a generous left margin works. The lines look like about 70 to 75 characters, which is within Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range, so the paragraphs would be comfortable to read once the text is darker.
- The headline differs from the text in weight and colour as well as size, so its role is obvious without being huge.
- The copy is short and divided by topic: crossings, then bicycles, then the caveat. The bicycle paragraph is good plain instruction: it says who does what, in what order, and where.
