**First read.** My eye lands on the bold headline at top left, then drops straight into three short paragraphs. Within two seconds it reads as a plain information page for people taking a river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, like a notice from a council or harbour office. On the second read, looking for when the boat actually leaves, I find no times.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about the piece, so I'm assuming it's a web page that tells ferry passengers when and how to travel, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The page is set cleanly and is easy to read. It still fails its main job, because a passenger can't plan a trip from it: no departure time, no last boat, no fare and no winter dates. The words are what hold it back, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule gives no times a passenger can act on (defect).** The first paragraph says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", and the third says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk." Someone standing at the north landing at 7:10 can't tell whether they've missed the first boat, where the forty-minute cycle starts, or when to be at the quay. "Before dusk" moves by more than an hour across a winter, so two passengers will read it two ways. The one who guesses late gets stranded on the far bank. Departure times are exactly what a passenger acts on and later holds the operator to, so a vague line here is a defect, not a style note. Fix: give the first and last departures as clock times, the minutes past the hour the boats leave from each landing, and the exact winter last-boat time, or a short table of them by month.
2. **The timetable is split in two, with the bicycle rules in between (defect).** The normal schedule is in paragraph 1 and its winter exception is in paragraph 3. A reader who stops after the first paragraph, which is what most people do on a notice like this, comes away with the wrong winter timetable. "In winter" also names no months. Fix: keep all the timing together in one block, with the winter change directly under the normal schedule and its start and end dates stated. Then put bicycles in their own block, ideally under a small heading of their own. That's three things a passenger might scan for: when, how long, and bikes.
3. **The copy leaves out facts the passenger needs and describes rules instead of talking to them (defect for the fare, taste for the voice).** "Bicycles ride… at no extra charge" implies there's a fare, but the fare isn't given anywhere, and neither is where or how to pay. "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" reads as a description of other people. It would land better spoken to the reader: "If you have a bike, wait until foot passengers are aboard, then lock it to the lower-deck rail." Fix: add the fare and how to pay, and rewrite the rules with "you" so each line says what the reader should do.

**What's working.**
- **The reading column.** Lines run about 65 to 70 characters, the body type looks about 19 px with roughly 1.5× line spacing, and the text is dark grey on white. It reads easily with no effort.
- **A simple, clear hierarchy.** There are two type sizes. The bold headline is clearly dominant, and the body sits well below it. Paragraphs are separated by space only, with no indents. Keep this scale when you add a sub-heading: make it bold at body size, not a third in-between size.
- **No decoration getting in the way.** There are no boxes, rules or ornaments. Every mark on the page is content.
