**First read.** The eye lands on "Travel lighter with the Wayfarer pack", drops to the grey paragraph, and stops on the black "Shop the pack" button. It reads as the top of a product launch page for a backpack. Then it looks for the bag, finds a blank right half and a blank lower third, and the page feels unfinished. It's tidy and calm, but nothing on it says "travel".

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a desktop product page, meant to get frequent flyers to look at and buy a new carry-on backpack.

**Verdict.** The words and the type do their job. As a page that sells a physical product, though, it fails: it never shows the bag, and that gap leaves about half the screen empty. Until the pack is on the page, the viewer is asked to buy something they can't see.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no picture of the product, and it should go in the empty right half.** Everything on the page sits in the left 60% (x≈120–860). The right side from about x=860 to the edge, and everything below y≈500, is blank. The headline promises "lighter", the paragraph promises "fits under a seat" and "opens flat like a suitcase", and the viewer has to imagine all of it. Shoppers judge a bag by its size, shape and inside, so with no picture the button is asking for trust instead of showing value. The weight also tips hard to the left with nothing on the right to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). Defect. If an image failed to load in this capture, check the live page. If it didn't, the fix is a large photo of the pack in the right columns. Best is one that shows a claim, such as the bag lying open flat or sitting under a seat, so picture and headline make one case. That one image fixes both the selling problem and the balance.
2. **There's no brand and no navigation on the screen.** There's no logo, no menu, no cart and no price. A new visitor can't tell whose shop this is or how much the pack costs before clicking "Shop the pack". Price is the thing a buyer acts on, and leaving it out makes the first click a gamble. Defect (common sense for a store page; Nielsen's heuristic 1, "Visibility of system status", covers knowing where you are). The fix is a standard header with the logo and a cart, plus the price near the button, for example "Shop the pack, £149".
3. **The headline could be on any bag maker's page.** "Travel lighter" is the generic travel-gear line, and the only specific word in it is the product name. The paragraph is much stronger: 22 litres, fits under an airline seat, opens flat like a suitcase, under a kilo. It compares the pack to things the viewer already knows, which is exactly how a viewer judges value for themselves. Taste, but it matters: the specific promise is sitting in 20 px grey type while a slogan holds the 76 px slot. The fix is to move the sharpest claim up into the headline, for example something built on "fits under the seat, packs like a suitcase", and keep the product name as the anchor.

**What's working.**
- **A clean type hierarchy.** I count four sizes with big steps between them: the headline at about 76 px, the paragraph at about 20 px, and the eyebrow and button text at about 15–16 px. The headline is heavy and tightly set, so it holds together as one block across two lines. Keep this scale when the image goes in.
- **One left edge.** The eyebrow, headline, paragraph and button all share one left edge at x=120, and the spacing groups them clearly. The headline block is plainly separate from the buttons.
- **A clear primary action.** There's one filled primary button, and the secondary action is a text link marked by an underline as well as its position. "Compare sizes" is a specific ask from the viewer's own world, and it's the right second choice for someone unsure about cabin limits.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether a product image or header failed to load in this capture (check the live page), the hover and focus states on the two actions, and how the left-heavy layout reflows on a phone.
