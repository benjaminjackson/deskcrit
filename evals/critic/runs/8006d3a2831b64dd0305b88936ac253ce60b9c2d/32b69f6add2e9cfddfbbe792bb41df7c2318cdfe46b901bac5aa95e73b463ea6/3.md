**First read.** My eye lands on the three big numbers across the top, 184,220, 3,912 and 2.12%, and reads them as a quick, calm summary. Then it drops to the large chart, finds three tidy rising lines, and stalls: nothing says which line is which channel, what the values are, or which weeks they cover. The page feels clean and trustworthy right up to the point where you try to learn something from the chart.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the overview screen of a web analytics product, where a marketer checks traffic and sign-ups over the last 12 weeks and sees which acquisition channel is driving them.

**Verdict.** The top half works, but the chart, which takes up about two-thirds of the screen, tells the viewer almost nothing. It has no channel names, no values and no dates. As it stands it's decoration, and the one question this page exists to answer, "which channel is doing the work?", can't be answered.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart has no labels at all** (defect). "Visits by channel" shows three lines with no series names, no y-axis values and no x-axis dates. The viewer can see that blue is above orange and orange above green, but not which channels those are, how many visits any line stands for, or which week a dip falls in. Someone who checks this weekly has to memorise the colour key from somewhere else, and a new user can't read it at all. Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2) and Few (*Show Me the Numbers*) say the reader should never have to guess what a mark measures. The empty band under the axis and the empty gutter to the left of the plot look like the places labels were meant to go, so if this is a rendering failure rather than the design, fix that first. Direction: name each channel at the right-hand end of its line, so there's no legend to look back and forth to (Tufte; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*). Put a few visit values on the y-axis and week dates on the x-axis.
2. **Colour is the only thing telling the series apart** (convention, WCAG 1.4.1). Even with a legend, a viewer with a colour-vision deficiency would struggle to match these three hues to a key. The blue and green in particular could become hard to tell apart. Labelling the lines directly (finding 1) fixes this too, because the name sits on the line and colour becomes a second cue.
3. **Without y values, you can't tell whether the gaps between the lines are honest** (convention: Tufte; Wilke and Few on scales). The lines are evenly spaced, so they look like three channels of similar size with similar growth. If the axis doesn't start at zero, orange might really be a third of blue, or nearly equal to it. Show the scale, and if it doesn't start at zero, make that visible.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Visits by channel" names the topic, not the finding. A title such as "Organic drove most of the 6.1% growth" (whatever is true) would tell the reader what to take away. This is taste and common sense, not a cited rule.
- "Last 12 weeks" in the top right is styled exactly like a static label. If it's the date-range picker, give it a visible control shape, such as a border and a chevron, so people know they can change the range.
- The chart's plot area starts at about x=121, while the card text and chart title start at about x=69–71. Once y-axis labels fill that gutter, check that the plot's left edge lines up with something on purpose.

**What's working.**
- The KPI cards each pair a big number with a plain-language comparison ("+6.1% vs previous 12 weeks", "+0.1 pts" for the rate). Each figure has context, and using "pts" rather than "%" for the conversion rate is exactly right. Keep it.
- The page is quiet and restrained: one neutral grey, white cards, colour kept for data and deltas, and a clear three-level type scale (heading, figure, label). Nothing competes with the data, which is why the empty chart stands out so much.
- A line chart is the right form for a 12-week trend, and the gridlines are faint enough to stay out of the way.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the chart shows values on hover. It's worth checking, but hover shouldn't be the only way to read the chart, so it doesn't change finding 1. Also whether "Last 12 weeks" opens a picker, and whether the labels in the chart simply failed to load in this capture. Check that in a live browser.
