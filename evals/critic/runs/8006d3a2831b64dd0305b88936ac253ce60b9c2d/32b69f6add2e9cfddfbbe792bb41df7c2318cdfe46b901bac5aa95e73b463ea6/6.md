**First read.** The eye goes to "Overview", then across the three big numbers, then down into the three coloured lines. It reads as a clean, generic SaaS analytics dashboard for a marketing or growth team, and the all-green deltas make it feel calm and positive. But the eye stops at the chart: three lines climb, and nothing on the chart says what they are.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard where a growth or marketing person checks traffic and sign-up performance over the last 12 weeks, and which channels drive it.

**Verdict.** The top half works, but the chart, which takes up two-thirds of the screen, tells the viewer nothing, and one headline number contradicts the other two. As it stands, someone can't use this to decide anything about channels, and anyone who checks the maths will stop trusting the cards.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart has no legend, no series labels, no axis values and no dates** (Visits by channel, 283–758 px). The title promises channels, but none is named. The viewer can see that blue is above orange, which is above green, and that all three rise. They can't tell which channel is which, how many visits any line stands for, which week a dip falls in, or whether the y-axis starts at zero. That means they can't judge whether the gaps between lines are 10% or 10×. The three series also differ only by colour, and orange against green is a pair that people with red-green colour blindness often confuse. This is a **defect**. Tufte and Few both say a reader should never have to guess what a mark measures, and WCAG 1.4.1 says meaning must not rest on colour alone. To fix it, put the channel name (and ideally the last value) at the right end of each line, add y-axis values with a unit and week labels along the bottom, and confirm the baseline. The empty band under the baseline (about 685–755 px) is where the dates belong.
2. **The conversion card contradicts the other two cards** (top-right card). Visits went up 6.1% and sign-ups went up 2.4%, so sign-ups grew more slowly than visits and conversion must have *fallen*. Working back, the previous period was about 3,820 / 173,630 ≈ 2.20%, against 2.12% now: roughly −0.08 pts. The card says "+0.1 pts" in "good" green. This is a **defect**, and the worst kind for a dashboard. The viewer is told a falling metric is improving, and a careful viewer who notices will doubt every other number on the page. Check the calculation (or the source of the other two deltas). Show the real sign, and colour it by whether the change is good or bad, not by default.
3. **The chart title states a topic, not a finding** ("Visits by channel"). Even once it's labelled, the viewer has to work out what the chart shows. Seeing that every channel grew, or that one channel gives most of the 6.1%, is exactly the work a dashboard should do for them. This is **taste** (no approved source): a title like "[Channel] drove most of the 6.1% rise" turns the chart into an answer.

**Smaller things.**
- The type scale has about seven sizes with small steps between them. "Overview" (~24) sits close to the KPI numbers (~26–28). The brand (~18), chart title (~17) and "Last 12 weeks" (~15) bunch together, and so do the labels (~14) and deltas (~13). Cut to about four sizes and use weight or colour for the rest (Refactoring UI).
- Green means "good" in the deltas and also stands for one channel in the chart. Pick series colours that don't reuse the status green.
- The empty band at the bottom of the chart card is leftover space; give it the date axis or tighten the card.
- "Last 12 weeks" gives no actual dates. Show the range (for example "1 Sep – 23 Nov") so a screenshot of this still makes sense next month.
- "Overview" is a heading that could go on any page. It's acceptable on a dashboard, but the space could name the property or product being measured.

**What's working.**
- Each KPI card has a label, a number and a comparison, and the comparison names its period ("vs previous 12 weeks"). That's exactly what Few asks of a headline number. Keep that pattern, just with correct deltas.
- The structure is quiet and disciplined. The three cards and the chart share one grid and one gutter, the gridlines are pale enough that the data lines are the strongest marks (good data-ink), and everything fits on one screen.
- Using a line chart for change over time is the right form for this data.
