**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers across the top (184,220 / 3,912 / 2.12%), reads them as a calm, competent analytics overview, then drops to the big chart, the largest thing on the page. There it stops: three coloured lines go gently up, and nothing says what any of them is, how big they are, or which weeks they cover. It feels clean and trustworthy until you try to read the chart.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a weekly marketing dashboard for a team checking traffic, sign-ups and conversion, and which channels drive them.

**Verdict.** The KPI row works. The chart, which takes up two-thirds of the screen, tells the viewer almost nothing. It has no series labels, no values and no dates, so "Visits by channel" can't be read by channel at all. That is the one thing in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart doesn't say which line is which channel.** *Defect.* In the "Visits by channel" panel, blue, orange and green lines have no legend and no labels. The viewer can see that one channel is biggest and one is bumpy, but not which is organic, paid or referral. Comparing channels is the whole point of the chart, so it fails. Colour is the only thing separating the series, so even a key would leave colour-blind viewers guessing between orange and green (WCAG 1.4.1). Fix: put the channel name, and ideally its latest value, at the right end of each line, where the eye already finishes (Tufte, *Visual Display*; Wilke).
2. **There's no scale and no time axis, so the viewer can't size or date anything.** *Defect.* The plot has faint gridlines but no numbers on the left and no week labels along the bottom. You can see the empty gutter on the left and the roughly 70 px band below the baseline where those labels should be. The viewer can't tell whether blue is 120k or 12k, how big the gaps between lines are, whether the bottom gridline is zero, or which week had the dip at about week 8. The trends read as "up-ish" and that's all. Fix: add values to the left axis with units (visits per week), week or date labels along the bottom, and start the axis at zero or mark clearly that it doesn't (Tufte, *Visual Display* ch. 2; Few).
3. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding.** *Taste, and common sense.* Above it, the KPI cards tell the viewer something ("+6.1% vs previous 12 weeks"). The chart only says what it holds. Once the lines are labelled, a title like "Organic drove most of the +6.1%; paid is volatile" would link the chart to the headline number above it. Then the viewer doesn't have to work out the story alone.

**Smaller things.**
- "Last 12 weeks" (top right) looks like plain text. If it's the date-range control, give it a dropdown chevron or a button outline so people know they can change it. If it's only a label, it's fine.

**What's working.**
- The KPI cards: one size step from label to number, a big bold figure, and a small green delta that always says what it's compared against ("vs previous 12 weeks", "+0.1 pts" for the rate). Each number has context and is quick to scan. Keep that pattern.
- The numbers add up (3,912 / 184,220 ≈ 2.12%), and "pts" rather than "%" for the change in conversion is exactly right.
- Layout discipline: one grid, matching card edges and gutters, a quiet grey page and white panels. Nothing competes with the data, so once the chart is labelled it'll read cleanly.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether hovering the chart shows values. Even if it does, the still view needs labels, because a dashboard is meant to be read at a glance.
