**First read.** My eye goes to the headline "Plans for every size of team", then across four big black prices, then down four matching blue buttons. It reads straight away as a pricing page for a collaboration tool. It feels clean and competent, and also like a hundred other pricing pages: the four cards carry the same weight, so the page doesn't suggest which plan to pick. When I compared the feature rows, I stopped at "3 editors" on Free and "1 editor" on Solo.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: the pricing section of a SaaS site for creative teams. Its job is to get a visitor to choose the right plan and click.

**Verdict.** The page is tidy and easy to read, but it doesn't help anyone choose. Every plan gets the same treatment, and the feature list contradicts itself in a way that makes the paid Solo plan look worse than Free. Fix the numbers first, then make one plan the obvious choice.

**What would move it most**
1. **The editor counts undercut the plan ladder** (defect). In the "editors" row, Free gives 3 editors and Solo, at $12, gives 1. A freelancer who has ever shared a file with someone sees that paying takes something away. Everyone else will assume the page has a mistake, and a mistake on a pricing page costs trust in every other number on it. If it's a typo, fix it. If it's deliberate (Free means 3 editors on 2 small projects, Solo means one person with lots of projects), the row has to say so, for example "Just you" on Solo. Otherwise the cheapest paid plan looks like a downgrade.
2. **Four equal primary buttons, and no recommended plan** (convention). All four cards share the same border, layout and solid blue button. A visitor has to compare 16 feature rows to work out which plan fits, with no help. Material Design and Apple HIG both say one primary action per view should dominate and the rest should step down. Here that means picking the plan you most want people to buy (probably Team at $39). Give it a stronger card, such as a darker border, a tint or a "Most popular" label, and keep the solid button only there. The others become outline buttons. The page then tells people where most of them should land.
3. **"$99 / month" next to "Talk to us" can be read two ways** (defect, because it's a price). Reading one: Company costs $99 and you have to call to buy it. Reading two: $99 is only a starting price and the real number comes from sales. Either way the visitor can't tell what they'd be committing to. Also, nothing on the page says whether prices are per team or per editor, and the $39 Team plan includes 15 editors, so that matters. Pick one meaning and say it. Write "From $99 / month" if it's a starting price, or keep $99 with a self-serve button and move "Talk to us" to a text link. Then add "per team" (or "per editor") under the price.
4. **The copy could be anyone's** (taste). "Plans for every size of team" and taglines like "For larger groups" would fit any product. The page never says what the product does, so the feature rows ("projects", "history", "Named contact") ask the visitor to already know. The subtitle is the best line on the page because it's specific: "Change or cancel at any time. Every plan includes unlimited viewers." Push the headline in that direction, toward what people get from the product rather than the plan structure. "Named contact" also needs a verb: a named contact who does what?

**Smaller things.**
- The gap between Team and Company looks about 37 px, against about 25 px between the other cards. The row's rhythm breaks and Company looks set apart by accident. Make all the gutters one size (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*, "Construction of the grid").
- About 70 px of empty card sits under every button, which looks like leftover space rather than a choice. Either end the card at the button's bottom padding, or use the space on purpose, for example for a "what's included" link.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is tight and clear: headline, plan name, a big price with a small grey "/ month", then feature rows. Each size has its own job and none collide. Keep it.
- The thin dividers between feature rows make it easy to compare across cards, and the four cards line up row for row. That's what makes the comparison quick. Keep it when you add emphasis to one card.
- The subtitle answers the two objections people actually have (can I cancel, and do the people I share with have to pay) right where the eye passes. It's the right content in the right place.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the buttons.
- How the four cards stack on a narrow screen. If one card gets emphasis, check it stays first or clearly marked when stacked.
