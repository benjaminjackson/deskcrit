**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then drops into three centered grey-black paragraphs that don't share the heading's left edge. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry: calm and tidy, but loose, like a draft whose alignment never got settled. Every line is centered, so you lose your place at the start of each one and keep going back to find it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling passengers how the ferry works (route, frequency, bicycles, winter changes) so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The page is easy to look at, but it's hard to read and hard to use: the body text is centered under a left-aligned heading, and the copy leaves out the times that passengers would actually plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are centered under a left-aligned heading** (defect and convention). The heading starts at the page margin (about x=120). Each paragraph line is centered on its own axis, so the left edges wander between about x=125 and x=157, and both sides of every paragraph are ragged. On four-line paragraphs, the reader has to search for the start of each new line. That is the main reason the text takes effort. The page also ends up with two alignment systems that nearly match: the first body lines sit a few pixels inside the heading's edge, so they look like a mistake, not a choice. Tschichold rejects centering by habit, and Bringhurst makes ragged setting depend on the text (2.1.3). Nothing in this content calls for centering. Fix: set the paragraphs flush left on the same edge as the heading, and keep centering for a few short lines (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, 2.1.3). That one change also gives the page a single grid.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts passengers act on** (defect). "Between early morning and late evening", "every forty minutes" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can't be used to plan a trip. A passenger can't tell from them whether they'll make the 7:10 or if a 17:30 boat exists in December. The page then sends them to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing", which only helps someone already at the landing. Times are what passengers remember and later hold the operator to, so vague wording here is a defect, not a style note. Fix: state the first and last departures from each end, in summer and in winter, and the date the winter timetable starts. Say where to find the current timetable online, not only on the noticeboard.
3. **The three topics have no labels** (taste). The page covers three separate subjects: the regular service, bicycles, and winter changes. A cyclist has to read the first paragraph to find out the second one is theirs. Short labels such as "Timetable", "Bringing a bicycle" and "Winter service" would let people scan straight to the part they need. On a page this short that's a modest gain, but it is the next thing I'd add after the first two.

**What's working.**
- The heading clearly leads: it's much larger and bold, and it names exactly what the page holds, so there's no doubt where to start.
- The body text is set well: a plain, readable sans at a comfortable size, about 60 to 65 characters per line, generous line spacing, and strong dark-on-white contrast. Keep all of this when you left-align it.
- The page is restrained: one typeface, no ornament, and empty space gathered in large calm areas. That suits a quiet practical notice.
