**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board" — the only bold, large thing on the page — and then hits a grey wall: three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, which read as one even, shouty block. It reads as an information page for ferry passengers. It looks calm and plain until the body starts, and from there it looks like a notice someone typed with caps lock on.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling first-time ferry passengers how the service works (route, frequency, bikes, winter changes) before they travel.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The structure is sound, but the body is set in all capitals, which makes the part people actually have to read the hardest part to read. And the copy leaves out the one thing a passenger plans around: the actual times.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set entirely in capitals** (defect). All three paragraphs, nine lines in all, are in caps. Capitals are all the same height, so words lose the shapes the eye uses to recognise them. Every line becomes a flat row of letters the reader has to decode letter by letter, and the paragraphs blur into one grey block, so scanning for "bicycles" or "winter" takes real effort. Caps also read as shouting, which works against the calm, helpful tone of "Before you board". Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says don't capitalise whole paragraphs and keep caps for headings shorter than one line, labels and captions. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) notes that text in capitals is particularly hard to read. The capitals also use the face's normal spacing, while the "PASSENGER NOTES" label is letterspaced. That makes the body look even more crowded, but letterspacing won't rescue it. Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for the short "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already does this correctly.
2. **The copy describes the timetable without giving it** (defect: this is information people act on). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone when a boat leaves. A reader can't tell whether early morning means 6:00 or 8:00, or whether a boat leaves at :00, :40 and :20 past. "In winter … one boat each hour" doesn't say which months count as winter. The page then points to printed timetables "kept in the shelter at each landing". So the passenger finds out the real times only once they're standing at the quay, which is too late to plan around. A passenger who reads "late evening" as 22:00 when the last boat is 20:00 is stranded. Fix: give the first and last departures from each landing, the minutes past the hour, and the dates for the winter timetable. Or link to the timetable from this page rather than only to the shelter.

**Smaller things.**
- The bicycle paragraph is the one with an instruction (wait until foot passengers have boarded, then lock to the rail). Once the text is in mixed case, think about leading with the action ("Wheel your bike on after foot passengers…"), so it talks to the rider rather than about "riders". This is taste: it makes the instruction land on the person who has to follow it.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy above the body is clean: a small, tracked grey label, then one bold headline, then text. That's three levels with clear steps between them and one obvious place to start. Keep it.
- The column's measure is comfortable (about 65–72 characters a line) and the space between paragraphs clearly separates the three topics. Once the text is in mixed case, this setting will read well as it stands.
- The short paragraphs, one topic each (route, bikes, winter), suit a page people skim before a trip.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone. The left-anchored column with empty space on the right is fine on desktop but should be checked at narrow widths.
