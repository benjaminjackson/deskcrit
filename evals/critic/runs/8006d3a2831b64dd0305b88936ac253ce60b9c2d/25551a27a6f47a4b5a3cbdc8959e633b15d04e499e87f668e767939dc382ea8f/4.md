**First read.** The eye goes to "Before you board", a bold dark headline in the top left with a small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it. Then it hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, and reading slows to a crawl. It reads as an information page for a local river ferry: calm, plain, and sensible until the body copy starts.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page telling ferry passengers what they need to know before a crossing: how often it runs, how long it takes, the bicycle rules and the winter timetable.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but the piece fails at its one job, getting a few facts into a passenger's head quickly. All of the body text is in capitals, and the copy never gives the times a passenger would act on.

**What would move it most**

1. **All of the body text is set in capitals.** *(Defect.)* All three paragraphs, eight lines of running text from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" down to "…AT EACH LANDING.", are in full caps. Capitals remove the word shapes readers use to recognise words, so every line has to be read letter by letter. Someone glancing at their phone on the quay will skim, lose their place, and miss "forty minutes" or "twelve minutes". The caps also take away the page's hierarchy. The small capitals label at the top is meant to mark a category, but the body now shouts in the same register at a larger size, so the label no longer stands apart. The body capitals also look set at default spacing, unlike the tracked label, which crowds them further. Butterick's *Practical Typography* ("All caps") is direct about it: don't capitalise whole paragraphs. Hochuli's *Detail in Typography* says text in capitals is especially hard to read. The fix is to set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case and keep capitals for the one-line label. Everything else about the text setting (size, line length of about 69 characters, line spacing, paragraph gaps) is already right, so this one change shows the work that's already there.

2. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on.** *(Defect, in the copy.)* "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone whether they can catch a 6:30 boat or get home at 22:00. "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" doesn't say when winter starts, and "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends the reader to the quay to find out what this page should have told them. These are the lines people plan around and later hold the operator to. A vague one gets read whichever way suits the reader, and the wrong reading means a missed last boat. Give the first and last sailing times, the dates of the winter timetable, and ideally the departure minutes ("on the hour and at :40…") or a link to the timetable. The bicycle paragraph would also read better spoken to the reader ("Wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded and lock it to the rail"), since it's an instruction to them.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is clear.** There are three sizes with big steps between them: the small tracked label, the bold headline and the body. The headline is set apart by weight and colour as well as size, so the starting point is never in doubt. Keep that.
- **The text column is well judged.** It shares one left edge at about x=120, lines run about 69 characters, and the line spacing is generous. The space between paragraphs is clearly larger than between lines, so each topic (schedule, bicycles, winter) reads as its own chunk.
- **There's no decoration.** No rules, boxes or ornaments, and the restraint suits a practical notice.
