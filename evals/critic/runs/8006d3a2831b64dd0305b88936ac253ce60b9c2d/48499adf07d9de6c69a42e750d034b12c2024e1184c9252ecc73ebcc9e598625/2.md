**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline, drops to the "Create your link" button, then slides into the row of four outlined cards. It reads as a scheduling SaaS homepage aimed at small service businesses: clean, competent, and almost interchangeable with any other booking tool. The right half of the hero is blank, so the top of the page feels lopsided and unfinished.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing homepage that turns small-practice owners (dentists, tutors, physios) into sign-ups for a booking-link tool.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and readable but doesn't sell: the hero shows nothing, and the copy could sit on any competitor's page, so the viewer never sees why this one is better. Monochrome blue on everything also flattens the hierarchy below the headline.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero has no picture of the product, and half the screen is empty** (defect). Everything above the cards sits in the left 650px, and the right 550px is blank. The viewer is asked to "create your link" without ever seeing what a guest sees when they open one. The page tips left, and the empty space looks left over rather than designed. Fill the right side with the real booking page, ideally one of the named customers' pages with real slots in the guest's own time zone. That one image also does the job of the "Time zones" and "Shared calendars" cards.
2. **The copy is generic, and the ask doesn't let the viewer judge the value** (defect). "Booking links that fill your calendar" and "keep every meeting in the right place" could be any scheduling product's tagline. Nothing says what's different, and "fill your calendar" promises demand a scheduling tool doesn't create. "Create your link" is a concrete ask, but it asks for trust. "See a live example" is the stronger move, so make it count: point to the customers' pages ("See how Harbor Dental takes bookings"), or set the product against what the viewer does now ("Stop the back-and-forth emails: guests pick a slot in 10 seconds"). Name one thing Slotwise does that the viewer's current tool doesn't.
3. **One blue does every job, so nothing below the headline stands out and links can't be told from labels** (defect, partly convention: accent restraint, Refactoring UI). The headline, nav links, card titles, 2px card borders, icons, badges, both buttons and the divider rule are all the same saturated blue. The card titles look as clickable as "Learn more", and the four heavy borders shout as loud as the CTA. Save blue for what you can act on: buttons and links. Set headings in near-black, and give the cards light grey borders, a tint, or just space.
4. **The customer strip is unlabelled and carries no proof** (defect). "Harbor Dental … Orchard Vets" sits in small grey text under a rule with no heading, so it reads like footer links rather than social proof. Label it ("Used by 2,000 clinics and coaches", if that's true) and use logos or one short quote with a number.

**Smaller things.**
- Card 1: the "New" badge forces "Shared calendars" onto two lines and sits at the card's right edge. Its body text and "Learn more" then fall about 30px below the other three cards. Put the badge after the title like the others, or above it, so all four cards line up.
- Badges mix two kinds of thing: "New" and "Beta" are statuses, "Auto" and "Email" are descriptions. Keep statuses only.
- The calendar icon is outlined while the clock, envelope and layout icons are filled. Use one style.
- There are two filled primary buttons, "Start free" and "Create your link", for what is probably one action. Use one label, or make the header button a lighter style.
- Four identical "Learn more" links: name the target ("How team routing works") so each link says where it goes, and screen-reader users hear something different for each.

**What's working.**
- The subhead is a real sentence about the user's action ("Share one link, let people pick a time…"). Keep that plain voice.
- The card copy lands on the viewer: "so nobody has to do the math", "cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful". Each one says what the user gets, not just what the feature does. Use this pattern for the headline.
- The type scale is clean: a big bold headline, an 18px subhead, and 15px body text, with clear steps, one family, and good line lengths.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the cards and links. Whether the cards stack cleanly on mobile, where card 1's wrapping title gets worse.
