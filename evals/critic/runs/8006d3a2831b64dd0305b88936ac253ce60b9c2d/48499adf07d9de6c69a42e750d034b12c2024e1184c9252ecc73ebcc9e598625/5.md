**First read.** I look at the big blue headline first, then the blue button, then down to a row of four blue-outlined cards. Then I notice the empty space on the right where a picture of the product should be. It reads as a booking/scheduling SaaS landing page: clean, competent, and interchangeable with a dozen others. Only when I reach the grey names at the bottom (dental, tutoring, physio, vets) do I realise who it's for.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the homepage hero for a scheduling tool, meant to get small appointment-based businesses (clinics, tutors, coaches) to create a booking link.

**Verdict.** The page is tidy and readable, but it doesn't sell. It never shows the product, it never tells its real audience the page is for them, and it puts one blue on everything, so nothing stands out. The biggest problem is the empty right half of the hero: the visitor is asked to create a booking link without ever seeing what their customers would see.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero has no picture, so the right half is a hole.** The hero runs from about y 120 to 380 with nothing to the right of x 650. The page tips left, and that gap is where the eye would check what it's buying. For a booking link, the product is the booking page your customer sees: a week of open slots, a time-zone line, a confirm button. Without it, "Booking links that fill your calendar" is a claim the viewer has to take on trust. *Defect*, unless an image is meant to be there and failed to load in this capture. Check that in a live browser first. Fix: put a realistic booking page there, ideally one from a business like the ones in the customer strip, so the headline and the picture make one argument ("this is what your patients will click").
2. **One blue does every job, so blue means nothing.** The nav links, headline, icons, card titles, badges, 2 px card borders, "Learn more" links, both buttons and the divider rule are all the same saturated blue. The "Create your link" button should be the one thing that says "act here", but it's just more of the colour that's everywhere. The four outlined cards also pull nearly as hard as the headline. *Convention*: keep saturated accent colour scarce so it can point at something (Refactoring UI). Fix: make the headline near-black and the card borders a light grey hairline, or drop the borders and let space separate the cards. Make the card titles dark too, and keep blue for things you can click, with the hero button as its strongest use.
3. **The copy could be anyone's, and the audience only shows up in an unlabelled footer.** "Booking links that fill your calendar" and "keep every meeting in the right place" could sit on any scheduling product. A dentist or tutor has no reason to think this one is for them. They only find out from the grey row of names at the bottom, which has no label, so it could just as easily be footer links or partner names. "Fill your calendar" also reads two ways: to a clinic it means more bookings, to anyone else a full calendar is a burden. The ask is decent: "See a live example" lets people judge for themselves. But nothing compares the product to what these businesses do now, such as phone tag, back-and-forth texts or a paper diary. *Taste, argued*: name the customer and the thing it replaces in the headline or subhead (for example, patients book themselves instead of phoning the front desk). Label the strip ("Used by clinics, tutors and coaches"), and if it's a customer strip, use logos or a one-line result.

**Smaller things.**
- "Shared calendars" wraps because its "New" badge is pushed flush right. Card 1's body and "Learn more" then sit about 30 px below the other three, breaking the row. Shorten the title, or put the badge inline like the others.
- The badges look identical but mean different things. "New" and "Beta" are status; "Auto" and "Email" describe features. "Email" also reads two ways: reminders go by email, or reminders are email *only* (no SMS), and someone choosing a reminder tool will hold you to that. Keep the pill for status only and put the feature fact in the body text.
- The header says "Start free" and the hero says "Create your link". If they lead to the same place, give them one label so the visitor isn't left wondering whether they're two different things.
- The icons mix styles: the calendar is an outline and the clock, mail and layout icons are filled. The intake-forms icon is a layout grid, not a form. Pick one style and a glyph that shows a form.
- The blue rule above the customer strip is a full-strength line doing a separator's job. Space or a pale grey would do.

**What's working.**
- The headline is clearly dominant and well set: big, bold and tightly leaded, with a sensible break. The subhead is a good reading length (about 65 characters). Keep that hierarchy when you take the colour out.
- The card body copy speaks to the user and says what each feature means for them ("so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful"). That's the voice the headline should have too.
- "See a live example" is the right secondary ask. It lets the visitor judge the product, not just take the claim on trust, and it's correctly styled lower than the main button.
