**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline, "Booking links that fill your calendar", then drops to the "Create your link" button. After that it moves across four identical blue-outlined cards and stops on a row of grey business names. It reads as a SaaS scheduling tool for small service businesses (dentists, tutors, physios, vets). It's clean, competent and blue, but it doesn't stand out: it looks like any booking tool's homepage, and I'd remember nothing about it after a minute.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing homepage that gets small service businesses to sign up and create their first booking link.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and it works, but it's generic. The hero makes its claim in words only and leaves the right half of the screen empty, so the visitor never sees the product or a reason to pick this one over the tool they already use.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero describes the product but never shows it.** The headline and subhead fill the left half (roughly x 64–650). The right half, about 560 px wide by 300 px tall, is empty. The page is heavy on the left with nothing to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). That gap is where the visitor should see a booking page: real slots, a guest's time zone, the thing they would send to their own clients. As it stands, the only proof is the claim "fill your calendar", which the visitor has to take on trust. Every booking tool says something like it. "See a live example" is the strongest ask on the page because it lets the visitor judge for themselves, but it's a small underlined link. *Defect.* Fix: put a real booking page in the right half of the hero, ideally one the visitor recognises as their own case (a physio's appointment page, say), so the comparison with what they use now happens at a glance. Then give the headline a point only this product can make. The four cards already have candidates: team routing and fewer no-shows.

2. **One blue does every job, so nothing signals what's clickable.** The same saturated blue is used for the headline, nav links, both buttons, all four card borders, card titles, badges, icons and the divider rule. Card titles like "Time zones" sit right above an underlined "Learn more" in the same blue and weight, so they read as links. The blue 2 px borders around every card are the loudest shapes below the hero and compete with the "Create your link" button. Things that only share a colour get grouped by accident (Arnheim, grouping by similarity). Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, says to drop heavy rules and boxes where space or a pale tone would do. *Defect.* Fix: set the headline and card titles in near-black, make the card borders pale grey or swap them for a light tint, and keep blue for things you can click. The primary button will stand out more as a result.

3. **The four cards don't follow one pattern, and the badges mean different things.** In "Shared calendars" the title wraps to two lines and the "New" pill moves to the right edge. That pushes the card's body text and "Learn more" about 30 px below the other three cards, so the row's baselines break (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: repeated elements follow one programme). The four pills also look identical but say different kinds of things. "New" and "Beta" are status: "Beta" is effectively a warning. "Auto" and "Email" are feature labels and tell the visitor almost nothing. *Defect.* Fix: keep the badge after the title in every card, shorten "Shared calendars" (to "Team calendars", say) or let the badge drop below it, and only badge status. Drop "Auto" and "Email", and give "Beta" a different look from "New".

**Smaller things.**
- Customer strip (y ≈ 770): five plain grey names with no label and no logos. A visitor can't tell whether these are customers, integrations or partners. Add a short lead-in such as "Used by clinics and tutors like" or use their marks.
- Icons in the cards: the calendar is drawn in outline but the clock, envelope and form are solid. Pick one style.
- The headline breaks as "…fill your / calendar", leaving one word alone on the second line. Break it at the phrase ("Booking links that / fill your calendar") or widen the box.

**What's working.**
- Both calls to action are well ranked: one filled primary button, with "See a live example" as an underlined link next to it. Keep that order and grow the example, don't demote it.
- The card body copy is specific and written for the visitor: "so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful". Each line ends on what the feature does for them. The headline should be written the same way.
- The type is restrained: one family, a clear jump from headline to subhead, and the subhead at a comfortable line length (about 60 characters).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Keyboard focus styles on the nav, buttons and the four "Learn more" links.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 4 yes, 4 no (headline and image make one idea; every element does a job; repeated elements look the same; each heading names its section)
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 8 yes, 5 no (exact alignment; baselines across columns; no accidental groups; space gathered; balance)
Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (clean breaks / no lone word)
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: 12 lines, 11 yes, 1 no (icons share one stroke style)
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 31 yes, 1 no (audience's own words: "Auto" and "Email" badges), 1 unsure (focus indicator)
