## Critique: Slotwise landing page hero (1280×800 still)

**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline, drops to the "Create your link" button, then gets pulled straight down into four equally loud blue-bordered cards. In about two seconds it reads as a scheduling tool like Calendly, for small service businesses. It feels clean and competent, and also like a hundred other SaaS heroes: half the hero is blank, and everything is the same blue.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the above-the-fold landing page for a booking-link SaaS. It should get small practices (dentists, tutors, physios, judging by the customer strip) to create a link or try the live example.

**Verdict.** It's clear and does its basic job, but it asks the viewer to take the product on trust. Nothing on the screen shows a booking page, and the right half of the hero is empty where that proof should be. A second problem: blue is on everything, so the one action that matters doesn't stand out from fifteen other blue things.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero shows no product, and its right half is empty.** *(Defect.)* From x≈660 to 1216, above the cards, there is nothing. The headline claims "fill your calendar", but the viewer never sees what a guest sees: the slot picker, the time-zone line, the confirmation. That gap costs you in two ways:
   - **Trust.** The claim stays a claim. Your best asset, "See a live example", is a small text link the viewer has to leave the page to use.
   - **Balance.** All the hero's weight sits on the left, so the page tips.

   If an image just failed to load in this capture, check it live. If not, the fix is to put a real booking page in that space, ideally with a customer's name on it (for example "Harbor Dental — pick a time"). Then the viewer can compare it to the booking flow they use today and judge the value themselves (Krug: self-evident over explained).

2. **Blue on everything means blue marks nothing.** *(Defect. Refactoring UI on accent restraint.)* All of these use the same saturated blue:
   - the headline
   - the nav links
   - both filled buttons
   - all four 2px card borders
   - the card titles and icons
   - the badges and the underlined links

   Two problems follow. The card titles look like links, so the eye reads every card as a click target. And the "Create your link" button has to compete with four heavy blue rectangles right below it. Keep the blue for things you click (the buttons and the links). Set the headline and card titles in a dark neutral. Swap the card borders for a light grey edge or a pale fill, or use space alone. The primary button will then be the strongest blue on the page by default.

3. **The badges mix two meanings in one style.** *(Defect.)* "New" and "Beta" are status flags. "Auto" and "Email" describe the feature. All four use the same blue pill, so the viewer reads "Auto" and "Email" as statuses and wonders what that means. One badge also breaks the layout:
   - "New" sits far right in card 1, which pushes "Shared calendars" onto two lines.
   - That drops card 1's body text and its "Learn more" a full line below the other three cards.

   Keep the pill for status only and put the descriptors into the body copy. Then set the badge inline after the title in every card, so all four cards share their baselines.

4. **The customer strip doesn't say what it is.** *(Defect, copy.)* Under the rule at y≈740, five names sit in small grey type with no label. A viewer can't tell whether these are customers, partner integrations or footer links. Add a short label in the viewer's terms, such as "Used by 2,000 practices like yours". Better still, use the real logos or a single quote with a number in it. That turns a list into proof.

**Smaller things**
- **Icons:** the calendar icon is an outline, but the clock, envelope and layout icons are filled. Pick one style. The layout glyph also doesn't suggest "intake form".
- **Links:** the four "Learn more" links can't be told apart out of context (WCAG 2.4.4). Use "How shared calendars work" and similar.
- **Buttons:** "Start free" in the nav and "Create your link" in the hero probably lead to the same place under two names. Pick one wording.
- **Headline (taste):** "Booking links that fill your calendar" could sit on any competitor's page. The subhead is more specific than the headline. Consider leading with the outcome for this audience, such as fewer no-shows or no back-and-forth emails.

**What's working**
- **The copy in the cards.** It talks to the viewer and lands each feature on them: "so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful". Keep this voice when you rework the badges.
- **The calls to action.** "Create your link" names one concrete thing to do. "See a live example" lets the viewer check the claim for themselves, which is the right kind of ask. It just needs more visual weight (see finding 1).
- **The grid.** The logo, the headline, the card row, the rule and the "Start free" button all share the same left and right edges (64 and 1216). The page feels orderly because of it.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the empty right half is a hero image that didn't load. Check in a live browser.
- How the four-card row reflows on narrow screens.
