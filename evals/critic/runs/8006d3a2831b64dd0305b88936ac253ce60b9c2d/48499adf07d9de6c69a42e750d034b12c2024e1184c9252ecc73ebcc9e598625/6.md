**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline, reads the subhead and goes to the "Create your link" button. Then it drops to four identical blue-outlined cards and scans them like a row of equals. The right half of the hero is blank, so the page looks like the main image failed to load. It reads as a clean, competent scheduling-tool landing page, but it never shows the product, and almost everything is the same blue.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the homepage hero of a booking-link tool, meant to get small service businesses (dentists, tutors, physios) to create a link.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and the copy is clear, but it asks people to sign up without showing them anything. The biggest problem is the empty right half of the hero, where a visitor should see the booking page their own clients would use.

**What would move it most**
1. **The product is never shown, and the hero is lopsided because of it.** The right half of the hero, from about x=680 to the edge, is empty above the cards. The visitor has to imagine what "a booking link" looks like to their clients. All the left-side text then tips the page, and the gap looks like a missing image rather than a deliberate pause. **Defect:** the viewer can't judge the value. **Fix:** put a real booking page there, in a believable customer's name (for example a Harbor Dental page with open slots in the guest's time zone). That shows the result and lets visitors compare it with how they take bookings now. "See a live example" already points this way, but it hides the best argument behind a click. Bring it into the hero.
2. **Blue everywhere means nothing stands out.** The headline, nav links, card titles, card borders, icons, pills, underlined links and both buttons are all the same saturated blue. Because everything is emphasised, the main button competes with eight other blue things, and the cards look as loud as the call to action. **Convention:** keep the accent for what you want clicked (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*). **Fix:** set the headline and card titles in near-black, make the card borders a light grey or remove them and let spacing do the work, and keep blue for the buttons and links.
3. **The pills mean two different things in the same style.** "New" and "Beta" are statuses, but "Auto" and "Email" describe how a feature works. Because they look identical, a reader takes them all as statuses and wonders whether "Auto" or "Email" is a pricing tier or a warning. "Beta" next to intake forms also makes people hesitate. **Defect.** **Fix:** keep pills only for status, and turn "Auto" and "Email" into words in the description, or drop them.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Shared calendars" title wraps to two lines because the "New" pill sits beside it. That pushes the first card's text and link about 30px below the other three. Put the pill above the title or shorten the title.
- Four links all say "Learn more", so none of them says where it goes. Use "How team scheduling works" and similar, or make each whole card the link.
- The customer names along the bottom have no heading. Without a line like "Booking for 2,000 clinics and studios, including…" they read as random text, and the 2px blue rule above them adds weight they don't earn.
- The header button "Start free" and the hero button "Create your link" are both filled blue but have different labels. If they do the same thing, give them the same label. If not, make the header one an outline button.
- The headline breaks as "fill your / calendar". It could break at "Booking links / that fill your calendar", or be set at a size that keeps it on one line.
- The headline "Booking links that fill your calendar" could belong to any competitor (taste). Say what is different here, for example fewer no-shows, as the reminders card suggests.

**What's working.**
- The subhead does its job: it's one sentence with real verbs ("share", "pick", "keep") that tells you how the product works.
- "See a live example" is the right second option. It lets visitors judge the product for themselves instead of taking the headline on trust. Keep it, and give it more room.
- The card descriptions end on what the visitor gets ("so nobody has to do the math", "cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful"). Keep that pattern.
