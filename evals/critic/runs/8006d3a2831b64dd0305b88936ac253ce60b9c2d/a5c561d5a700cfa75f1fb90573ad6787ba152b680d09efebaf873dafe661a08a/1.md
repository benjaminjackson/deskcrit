**First read.** The eye lands on the June bar, the tallest and darkest shape on the page, with its "$1150k" label. It then jumps up to the serif headline "Savings deposits rose every month", and across to "$4.82M" in the first card. This is a credit union's half-year report to its members. It feels calm, trustworthy and well ordered. The hatched bars make the chart buzz a little.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a public web page that tells members how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026, read once by people who aren't finance experts.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The layout is clean, the numbers add up, and every figure has its period and a comparison. The biggest problem is that the page's focal point, the June bar, is mostly a one-off bonus payroll. The only place that says so is a small grey footnote under the chart, so the piece suggests more growth than there really is.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June spike isn't explained where people look (defect).** June ($1150k) is 33% higher than May ($868k), while every other month rose by roughly 5–10%. That jump is what makes the chart look exciting. The reason, "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll", is only in the 12-ish px source line below the card. Most readers will see the bar and read the headline, and they'll think member saving sped up in June. The bonus also makes up part of the $4.82M total and probably part of the "Up 6.1%". Put the note on the June bar itself, for example "includes mid-year bonus payroll" next to its label, or split the bar into regular deposits plus a lighter bonus segment. That way the main point and its caveat are seen together. If June 2025 also had a bonus, say so, because then the year-on-year comparison is fair and readers should know that. (Common sense: a key point gets annotated on the chart, not in a footnote.)
2. **The hatched fill on the bars (convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 11; Tufte, chartjunk).** Each bar is filled with blue-and-white diagonal stripes. The pattern shows no data. At this size it shimmers, it makes the bar tops look slightly soft, and it competes with the bold value labels on top. Use one flat colour for all the bars. If you want June to stand out, or to show the bonus share from point 1, that second colour or tint then means something.
3. **The headline and the stat numbers are about the same size (convention: Bringhurst, sizes from a scale).** The serif headline (about 32 px) and the sans figures "$4.82M / $214 / 5,374" (about 34 px) are only a couple of pixels apart. So the right-hand column competes with the headline for the first look, and the page has two starting points. The smaller text crowds together the same way: the subtitle (~16), chart title (~15) and card labels (~14) sit almost on top of each other. Make the headline clearly the biggest, or bring the card figures down a clear step. Then pick one size for the subtitle, chart title and card labels, and separate them by weight or colour, not by a pixel of size.

**Smaller things.**
- Axis and bar labels mix units: "$1150k" and "$1200k" sit next to "$4.82M" in the card. Use $1.15M and $1.2M, or keep everything in k. A four-digit k number reads as a mistake.
- The gridlines and y-axis repeat values that are already printed on every bar. They're pale enough not to hurt, but you could remove them and lose nothing.
- "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025": "on" for "compared with" is British shorthand, and some US members will stumble over it. "Up $11 from June 2025" reads as a person speaking. The first card's "on Jan to Jun 2025" has the same problem.
- The headline "rose every month" is true, but the chart doesn't show whether that's normal for the season. A pale line for 2025 behind the bars would let members judge it for themselves (taste).

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding rather than the topic, and the chart title gives the unit ("US dollars per month"). Readers never have to guess what a number means. Keep both.
- Every big number in the cards comes with its period and a year-on-year change, and green is used only for "Up" next to the word itself. The figures also add up ($4.82M = the six bars; $214 × 5,374 ≈ June's total), which builds trust.
- The grid is disciplined: 48 px margins, the chart and cards aligned at the top, and a clear gutter between them. The space is gathered into calm open areas, so the page feels unhurried.
