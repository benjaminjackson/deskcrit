**Verdict.** It's clean, but it doesn't do the job well. The text runs about twice as wide as is comfortable to read. Worse, the copy never gives a time a passenger can act on: no first boat, no last boat, no departure times, and the winter rules are buried at the end of the third paragraph.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy never gives a time you can plan around.** In paragraph 1, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat leaves, and forty-minute gaps don't fall on the hour, so a passenger can't work out departures from it. In paragraph 3, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" changes from week to week, and "in winter" doesn't name the months. These are the lines a passenger acts on. Read the cautious way, "before dusk" means someone turns up at 4:30 in December and may find the last boat already gone. Read the hopeful way, they assume there's still time and get stranded on the wrong bank. *Defect.* Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing in clock times, the months the winter timetable runs, and either the winter last-boat time or a pointer to where it's posted. Keep the prose for the softer details, like the tide and the sandbank.
2. **The page is laid out as an essay, but passengers will scan it.** Departure times, the bicycle rule, and the winter changes all sit at the same weight in plain paragraphs under one heading. Someone checking "when's the last boat?" has to read all three paragraphs to find it, and it's at the very end. *Defect*, and common sense rather than a published rule: the piece is for someone who wants one fact fast. Fix: put the times first and make them easy to find, either as a short timetable block or under short subheads such as "Times", "Bicycles", "Winter". Let the explanations follow.
3. **The lines are far too long to read comfortably.** Each body line runs about 125 to 130 characters, from x≈80 to x≈1195. Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 for a single column, with 66 as the ideal (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). At this width your eye has a long way to travel back to the start of the next line and slips into the wrong one. You can see it in the wrap of paragraph 1 ("Each / crossing takes…"). *Convention, with a real cost here.* Fix: cap the text column at about 600 to 680 px at this type size, roughly 65 characters. The lower half of the screen is empty anyway, so there's room for the text to run longer down the page.

**What's working.**
- There's one face and two sizes, and the title is separated from the body by weight and size together. Because the type scale is that simple, the subheads you add later will have room to sit between the two without muddling anything.
- The body text is dark on white and a comfortable size. The gap between paragraphs is clearly bigger than the gap between lines, so the three topics already read as three separate groups. Keep that spacing when you narrow the column.
- The tone is plain and specific about the boat itself ("hold a wider course around the sandbank", "lock them to the rail along the side of the deck"). It sounds like a real ferry, not a template. Keep that voice; just add the numbers.
