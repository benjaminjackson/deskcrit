**First read.** I look at "Tell us about your project" first, then go right to the white card and down three fields to the blue "Send message" button. Within a second it's clear this is a design studio's contact page and that I'm meant to write to them. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also generic: nothing on it says anything about Fernwick in particular.

**What it's for.** My assumption is that this is the desktop contact page for a small studio, and its job is to get a prospective client to send a useful first enquiry.

**Verdict.** It works. A visitor knows where they are, what to do and when they'll hear back. The main gap is between the copy and the form. The text asks for a budget and a timeline, but the form only offers an empty message box, so the most useful facts are the ones people are most likely to leave out.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form doesn't help people give what the copy asks for.** The paragraph on the left says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form on the right has no field, prompt or hint for either. A visitor who has scrolled past or forgotten that sentence faces a blank "Message" box and writes "Hi, we need a website." You then spend your two working days asking for the budget. This is a defect, and the point is common sense rather than a cited rule. Fix: put the request where the typing happens. Either add two short optional fields (a budget choice with a few ranges, and a timeline field) or add hint text under "Message" that names them. With either, the left paragraph can just state the two-day reply promise.

**Smaller things.**
- **Nav doesn't mark the current page.** "Contact" in the top-right nav looks exactly like Work, Services and About, so nothing confirms where you are. Mark the current page with one clear cue, such as bolder weight, no underline, or a bar (Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist", NN/g).
- **Required fields aren't marked.** None of the three fields says whether it's required. Put an asterisk after each required label and explain it once, or add "(optional)" after the optional ones (Budiu, "Marking Required Fields in Forms", NN/g). This matters more if you add budget and timeline fields, which should be optional.
- **Field borders look pale.** The input outlines look like a light grey, close to 2.5:1 against white, which may miss the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for form fields. Measure them. If they fall short, darken the border one step.
- **Headline line break.** "Tell us about your / project" splits the noun phrase and leaves "project" alone on line two. Break it as "Tell us about / your project", or widen the column so it fits on one line. This one is taste.

**What's working.**
- **Clear hierarchy with a small type scale.** I count about four sizes: headline, wordmark, body and links, and labels and footer. Each has an obvious job, and the bold labels set the fields apart by weight, not by adding another size. Keep this.
- **A reassuring promise in plain words.** "We read every message and reply within two working days" tells visitors what happens after they press the button, which is the main worry on a contact page. It's specific, it's a commitment, and it can't be read two ways.
- **Simple form layout.** It's one column, every field has a visible label above it, and there's a single filled button with a label that says exactly what it does. The headline and the card's top edge line up, so the two columns look like they belong together.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus rings on the fields and button. Whether the email field rejects badly formed addresses. Error and success states after submitting.
