**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Tell us about your project", drops to the short paragraph, then crosses to the white form card and down to the blue "Send message" button. Within two seconds it reads as a design or dev studio's contact page, for prospective clients. It feels calm and competent, and also stock: apart from the wordmark, nothing on the page belongs to Fernwick in particular.

**What it's for.** My assumption: to get a prospective client to send an enquiry the studio can act on, ideally one that already includes budget and timeline.

**Verdict.** It does its basic job. The page is clear, the form is short, the primary action is plain, and the reply promise is real reassurance. The biggest gap is that the page asks for a budget range and a timeline in a paragraph far from the form, then gives one blank box, so most enquiries will arrive without either.

**What would move it most**
1. **The budget and timeline request isn't where people type.** "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them" sits in the left column, about 450 px from the Message box it's meant to shape. By the time someone is in the textarea, they're reading the label "Message" and nothing else. They write "Hi, we need a website", and the studio has to ask a follow-up question that the page already tried to ask. This is a **defect** for the page's job. Help belongs next to the step it concerns (Nielsen, heuristic 10). The direction: put that prompt directly under the Message label as hint text. Better still, give budget and timeline their own optional fields, such as a short select of budget bands and a "When do you want to start?" field. The viewer then picks from their own situation instead of having to remember to volunteer it. The fields are my common-sense call; no approved source covers them.
2. **The input borders look too faint to show where the fields are.** The 1 px borders on Your name, Email address, and Message look mid-grey on white, borderline or under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks of component edges. On the very light grey page they hold up for good eyes. For a low-vision visitor or a washed-out laptop screen, the fields fade into the white card, and the card is the only thing marking where to type. This is a **convention** with a real cost. Measure the border colour against white. If it's under 3:1, darken it one step; the rest of the style can stay as it is.

**Smaller things.**
- No field is marked required or optional. Mark them: if all three are required, say so once, or add "(optional)" to any that aren't (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- "Contact" in the top navigation looks exactly like Work, Services and About, so the page doesn't show where you are. Give the current item one cue, such as a bolder weight or a bar (Laubheimer, NN/g, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- The form card's right edge (about x 1068) lines up with nothing. The header and its links run to about x 1224, so the content block looks parked left of centre under a full-width bar. Either run the card out to the header's right margin, or narrow the whole content area to match it (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*; Arnheim on balance).
- The headline "Tell us about your project" could sit on any agency's contact page. That's **taste**, but the lede underneath is specific and human. A headline with the same specificity would make the page feel like Fernwick's own.

**What's working.**
- The lede: "We read every message and reply within two working days." It's a concrete promise with a number, it answers the visitor's real worry (will anyone read this?), and it's exactly the kind of line people hold you to. Keep it, and keep the commitment behind it.
- The form is honest and short: three fields, labels above them in bold, tight label-to-field spacing, one filled primary button with a verb-object label ("Send message"). There is nothing to trip over.
- The type is restrained. There are about four sizes (headline, wordmark, body/nav, labels) with clear steps between them, the headline is set tight, and every link is underlined, so nothing relies on colour alone.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the fields and button, and whether the email field uses an email input type. Check both in a live browser.
- What happens after "Send message": the confirmation and the error states. This matters here, because the reply promise only lands if the sender knows the message went through.
