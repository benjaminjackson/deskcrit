**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the serif headline "A restored hay barn for weddings of up to 120 guests", then drops to the paragraphs and the brown "Book a visit" button. It reads as a website for a small wedding barn, aimed at couples, and it feels warm and rustic. In version 1 the second glance trips: both body paragraphs are in the same brush script as the logo, so reading slows to deciphering. In version 2 the same text is in a plain serif and reads straight through.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a wedding venue, meant to get couples who are comparing venues to book a site visit.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job better. The only real change is the body face, script in version 1 and a reading serif in version 2, and it is the change that matters. The specific details that sell the barn (built in 1870, a new oak floor, a kitchen caterers can use from early morning, your own caterer, the cottage for two nights) are readable in version 2 and hard work in version 1. Both versions share one bigger problem: the picture panel, which takes nearly half the screen, shows no barn.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text face (this is what separates the versions).** In version 1 both paragraphs, about eight lines of selling copy, are set in a heavy brush script, and it looks cramped between lines. Script letters are harder to tell apart, so every line costs effort, and skimmers will skip the facts that make this barn different from the next one. It also adds a third unrelated face alongside the serif headline and the sans nav, so the page looks like a collage. Defect and convention: set paragraphs in a face made for reading, and keep script for a few words of display (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1); unrelated faces conflict (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 7–8). Version 2 makes exactly this fix: the script stays only in the logo, where it gives the page its personality.
2. **The picture is an empty gradient (both versions).** The right half of the hero is a tan-to-brown gradient box. For a venue, the photo is the strongest argument there is: couples choose barns with their eyes. As it stands, the headline promises a restored barn and the picture shows nothing, so the viewer has to imagine the place. If this is a placeholder or an image that didn't load, it's fine once the photo is in. Check that before shipping. If it is intended, it's a defect. Direction: one strong photo of the barn interior or the glass doors onto the orchard, so the picture and the headline make one case together.

**Smaller things.**
- Both versions: the headline breaks "120 / guests", which splits the number from its unit and leaves "guests" alone on line three. Reflow it so "120 guests" stays together, for example with a non-breaking space or a slightly wider measure.
- Version 1 only: "day." sits alone on the last line of the second paragraph (moot once you choose version 2).
- Copy: "Book a visit" is a good, concrete ask. It would be stronger if it named what the visit gives the viewer, for example "Book a walk-through of the barn".

**What's working.**
- Version 2's type plan: a script logo, a bold serif headline, a serif body in a clearly lighter weight, and a sans for the nav and button. Each face has one job, and the headline stands clearly above everything else.
- The copy is specific and concrete (1870, beam by beam, 120 guests, two nights, bring your own band), so it doesn't read like a template. Keep that voice.
- A calm two-column layout with one filled primary button and a restrained warm palette that fits the rustic mood.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the gradient panel is a photo that failed to load. Check in a live browser.
