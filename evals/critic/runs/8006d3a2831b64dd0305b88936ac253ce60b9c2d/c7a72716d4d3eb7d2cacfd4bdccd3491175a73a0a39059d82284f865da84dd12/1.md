**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks", drops to the orange "Start your free week" button, then catches on the grey "Keep your streak going" card. That card doesn't make sense for someone who hasn't started yet. The teal panel of chat bubbles on the right is noticed but adds little. It reads as a language-learning app's home page: calm, clean and competent, and fairly templated.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is the home page for adults who have never used Parlo, and its job is to get them to start the free week.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The promise is clear, the button is obvious and the three steps explain the method. Two things hold it back. A card meant for existing users sits right under the call to action, and the illustration adds nothing to the headline. The copy also leaves the key promise loose.

**What would move it most**
1. **The streak card is aimed at the wrong person and sits in the wrong place** (defect; the rule that every element should do a job is common sense here, not a cited source). "Keep your streak going" is a nudge for people already using the app, but here a first-time visitor sees it directly under the button. It's the third thing they read, and it talks about a streak they don't have. It also slows the path from button to steps. Its stat is incomplete: "finish the course twice as often" as whom? Learners who don't practise five days in a row? People using other apps? The idea underneath is good: practising daily is what gets results. Fix: remove the card, or rewrite it for a newcomer and move it below the steps as proof, for example "Learners who practise five days in a row are twice as likely to finish as those who don't."
2. **The headline promise is the thing people will hold you to, and it's vague** (defect, copy). "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks" doesn't say at what level, and "short daily lessons" doesn't say how short. A viewer will reasonably fill in "fluent chat, 5 minutes a day". If the real answer is "basic small talk, 15 minutes a day", that's a broken promise and a refund conversation later. "A tutor that listens" can also be read two ways: a human tutor or software. That changes both what people expect and whether the price seems fair. Fix: state the time per day and say what the tutor is, for example "15 minutes a day. An AI tutor listens and corrects every word."
3. **The illustration just says "conversation" again** (taste). The teal panel with two speech bubbles shows exactly what the headline already says, so the right half of the screen adds no information. It could show a lesson screen, a corrected sentence with per-word feedback, or a real exchange in Spanish. Any of these would let the viewer see the product and judge "a tutor that listens" for themselves rather than take it on trust. The same move would make the ask stronger: "Start your free week" asks for a sign-up on faith, while a visible sample lesson lets them judge first.

**Smaller things.**
- The illustration panel ends at about 1076 px, while the nav and the step row run to about 1224 px. That ~150 px shortfall looks like a mistake, not a choice, and leaves a dead strip on the right. Extend it to the right margin, or line it up with the middle step column. (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*; Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on placements that are neither clearly aligned nor clearly off.)
- The headline breaks as "conversation in / ten weeks", which leaves "in" hanging at the end of line 1. Break before "in" so "in ten weeks" stays together (common sense; Bringhurst covers this only for numbers and units).

**What's working.**
- The headline is a concrete result with a deadline, not a slogan, and it clearly dominates by size and weight. Keep that hierarchy.
- The orange button is the only warm, saturated colour on the page, so the single action is unmissable. Don't add a second orange element.
- "1. Listen / 2. Speak / 3. Use it" explains the method in a few seconds. The short bold labels with one plain line each are exactly the right density.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The button's hover and focus states, and how the two-column hero stacks on a phone, especially whether the button stays above the fold.

**Checklist.**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 6 yes, 2 no (image and headline make one idea; every element does a job)
- What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 11 yes, 2 no (one grid; aligned or clearly off)
- Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (headings break at phrase boundaries)
- Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
- Pictures: 12 lines, 12 yes
- Charts and data: skipped, no chart
- Interface: 33 lines, 31 yes, 2 unsure (hover/pressed/disabled states; focus indicator)
