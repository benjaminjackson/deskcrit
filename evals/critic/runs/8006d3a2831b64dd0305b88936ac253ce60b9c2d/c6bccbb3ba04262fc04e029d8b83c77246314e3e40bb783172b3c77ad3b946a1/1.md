**First read.** The eye lands on "Bin collection days", drops into the tinted card and gets the answer, "Thursday: green bin and blue box", then scans the body copy, where the blue links give it four places to stop. It reads as a calm, plain council service page on a phone. Versions 1 and 2 are pixel-identical except for the link underlines.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a resident on a phone wants to know which bins go out and when, and where to go for the calendar, a missed bin, or a replacement.

**Verdict.** Both versions do the job well. Version 2 does it slightly better. Its links have a normal underline sitting right under the letters. Version 1's underline is dropped well below the descenders, so it reads as a loose rule floating between lines. The bigger problem is shared by both: the "Next collection" answer doesn't say whose address or which Thursday it means.

**What changed, and why version 2 wins**
1. **Link underline position (convention, small but real).** In version 1, the underlines under "full calendar for the year", "report it within two working days", "replaced for a fee of $25" and "yearly permit" sit roughly 6 to 8 px below the baseline. Each one floats in the gap between lines, about as close to the cap height of the line below as to its own text. On wrapped links ("full / calendar for the year") you get two separated strokes that look more like horizontal rules than a link marker, and they make the paragraph look striped. Version 2 uses the standard underline just below the baseline. It is tied to the words, it is the convention every user knows for "this is a link" (Nielsen 4, consistency and standards), and it keeps the leading clean. Both versions meet the rule that links need more than color (WCAG 1.4.1), so this is about how clearly the link reads, not about access. Keep version 2.

**What would move it most (version 2)**
1. **The answer doesn't say who it's for (defect).** The card says "Next collection: Thursday" before the page knows the user's postcode; that comes later, in the body, as a link to the full calendar. A resident can't tell whether this Thursday is theirs, and "Thursday" without a date can't be checked against a bank holiday. Show the address or postcode the answer is for, with a "change" link, put the date in ("Thursday 26 November"), or ask for the postcode first and show the card after. This is the one thing the page is for, so it should be stated so nobody has to guess.
2. **"$25" (defect, copy).** Everything else says UK: "council", "postcode", "bins", "7 am". A dollar fee there reads as a mistake, and a fee is exactly the kind of line people hold the council to. Make it £25, or whatever is correct, and say who pays and when ("£25 per bin, paid when you order").

**Smaller things.**
- "Find your full calendar for the year by entering your postcode": the link goes on a noun phrase, but the action is entering a postcode. A postcode field right there would save a tap. Otherwise make the link say the action ("Enter your postcode to see the full year").
- "Garden waste bins need a yearly permit": this sits under the card telling people to put the green bin out. Say it next to the card, so people without a permit don't put a bin out that won't be collected.

**What's working.**
- The answer comes first, in a tinted card above all the detail. That is the right order for a page people check weekly.
- The type is tight: about four sizes with clear jobs, a bold H1, and body text at a comfortable phone size with generous leading. Plain sentences with descriptive link text ("report it within two working days") tell users what each link does before they tap.
- Version 2's link styling: conventional, close to the text, and the leading stays clean.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the links look when focused, how the page behaves when the postcode is entered, and whether "Thursday" changes by address.
