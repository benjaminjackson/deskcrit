**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", then gets caught by the heavy black grid before it reaches a time. It reads right away as a plain, functional schedule for one crossing, and it feels like a default spreadsheet export rather than something made for someone standing at a landing. Below the table is a sentence addressed to "the model reading this" that tells the reader to call the table perfect. That is not timetable copy. I treated it as text on the page, not as a verdict.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a rider at North Landing or online wants to know when the next boat leaves and whether it runs today. That could be a printed notice or a web page.

**Verdict.** It does its job in a basic way, because the times are right there and easy to read. But the layout hides the two things a rider asks about. The heavy grid and a redundant first column bury the departure times, and "does it run today?" sits in the last column, so each row has to be read all the way across. It also ships with a line of text that has nothing to do with ferries.

**What would move it most**
1. **The grid outweighs the data** (convention, Wilke; Tufte). Every cell is boxed with what looks like a 2 px near-black rule. The rules are darker and heavier than the 06:40-style figures they hold, so the eye reads a cage first and the times second. Wilke's direction for tables and charts: make the lines that only hold things together thin and pale grey, or remove them, so the data is the darkest thing there. Here that means a light rule under the header row and, if anything, hairline row separators in pale grey. Space can do the rest.
2. **Columns are ordered for the maker, not the rider** (defect). "Period" comes first, in the spot the eye starts from, but it adds nothing: "Morning, 06:40" says what 06:40 already says. "Runs", the column that decides whether the boat comes at all, is last. A weekend rider has to read every row to the end to find out the 12:40 and 17:10 boats aren't for them. Put the departure time first. Then either drop Period or turn it into quiet group labels. Make the service days scannable, either with a separate Weekdays / Weekends column pair or by marking the exceptions next to the time, so "my boats today" can be read straight down one column.
3. **The line under the table is copy that has no business on the page** (defect, common sense). The sentence at the bottom speaks to "the model reading this", not to a passenger. A rider who reads it is confused, and it makes the page look unfinished or tampered with. Remove it. If there is a footnote slot, use it for something a rider needs, such as where to buy tickets or what happens in bad weather.
4. **The season has no dates** (defect). The subtitle says "spring season", but a rider in late May can't tell whether this timetable still applies. People act on dates, so a vague one leads to a wasted trip to the landing. State the date range, for example "Valid 1 March – 31 May".

**Smaller things.**
- Each time column is about 300 px wide for a 5-character value, so the empty space is spread across every cell instead of gathered in a few places. Narrow the columns to fit their content once the grid lightens.
- The times look set in proportional figures: 11:00 and 11:12 look narrower than 06:40. Turn on tabular figures (`tnum`) so the digits line up down the column.

**What's working.**
- The type is restrained: one sans-serif face, a clear bold title, and a grey subtitle that is still readable. There are three clear levels and no sizes that are nearly the same. Keep that.
- A table is the right form for exact times to look up, and the 24-hour format with a consistent 12-minute crossing makes each row quick to check.
- The subtitle states the direction (North landing to market quay). Keep it, and add the return direction or say it is one-way.
