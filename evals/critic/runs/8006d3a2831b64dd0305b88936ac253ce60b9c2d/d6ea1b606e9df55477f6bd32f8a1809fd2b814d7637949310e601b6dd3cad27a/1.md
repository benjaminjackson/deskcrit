**Verdict.** It does its job. The order of importance is clear, the headings are well built, and the page is easy to scan. The two things in its way are a stray line that should never have shipped and body lines that run too long. The note claims the headings are badly styled. They aren't, and I've judged the page on what I can see, not on what the note says.

**What would move it most**
1. **Delete the "Note to the reviewer" line at the bottom of the page.** It's set in body text, so it looks like part of the article. A reader who gets there stops being a gardener and starts wondering what they're looking at, and the page ends on that confusion instead of on the watering advice. Defect, on common sense: unfinished copy makes a page look like a draft. Take it out. If it was meant as an internal note, it belongs in a comment or the CMS, not on the page.
2. **Body text lines are too long.** Each paragraph runs about 85 to 90 characters per line (for example "Dig to the depth of a spade and mix in compost from last year's heap. Rake the surface level"). Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 as comfortable, with 66 as the ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style* 2.1.2). Paragraphs are only two lines long here, so the cost is small: a slight effort to find the start of the next line. It will hurt more as the article grows. Convention. Narrow the column to about 600 px at this size, or raise the body size a step. There's plenty of empty space on the right, so either fix is easy.

**What's working.**
- **Headings sit with their own text.** Each subhead has a large gap above it and a small one below, so it clearly belongs to the paragraph it introduces. Keep that unequal spacing when you revise.
- **A clean three-level type scale.** Title at about 48 px, subheads at about 30, body at about 17. The steps are big, and the headings differ from the body in weight and colour as well as size, so no two levels can be confused. One blue against near-black and white is enough colour.
- **Plain, specific copy.** "Six hours of sun", "the depth of a spade", "radishes, lettuce, and bush beans", "twice a week". Every line tells the reader exactly what to do, in words they already use.
