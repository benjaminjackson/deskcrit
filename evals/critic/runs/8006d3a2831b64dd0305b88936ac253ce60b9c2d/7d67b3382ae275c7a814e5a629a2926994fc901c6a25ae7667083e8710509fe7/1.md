**First read.** The eye goes to the bold serif headline, "The canal path from Brindle Lock to the estuary". Next it takes in the byline, then drops into three paragraphs of route notes. It reads as a calm, well-set article on a small cycling site. In version 1 the body text is so pale that the page looks greyed out or half-loaded below the headline. In version 2 the body reads as solid text you are meant to read.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a long-form route guide for cyclists. They need to read the body comfortably, and pick out practical details like the tyre width, café hours and the lighting warning.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job better. The only change is the body text colour, and it fixes the one real defect: version 1's pale grey body text falls below readable contrast. The rest is a well-set page, and both versions share it.

**What would move it most**
1. **Body text contrast. This is what separates the two versions (defect, WCAG 1.4.3).** In version 1 the three body paragraphs are a light grey, roughly #80848c, which looks like about 3.8:1 on white. That is below the 4.5:1 that normal-size text needs. The readers here are cyclists planning a route, often on a phone outdoors, and pale body text costs them speed and comfort exactly where the useful facts are: "32 mm or more", "both close at four in winter", "no street lamps at all". It also flattens the page: the headline and byline are near-black while the body recedes, so the body looks secondary or disabled when it is the content. Version 2 darkens the body to a mid-dark grey, roughly #555, which looks like 7:1 or better. It still sits a step softer than the near-black headline, so the hierarchy survives. Keep version 2's value. Check it with a contrast tool rather than by eye.

**Smaller things**
- The apostrophe in "keeper's" (paragraph 1, line 2) looks straight. Use a curly apostrophe (Butterick, *Practical Typography*). Same in both versions.
- "brickworks." sits alone on the last line of paragraph 1. A one-word last line is a small break in the paragraph's shape. Rebalance the break, or let it go on a fluid layout. Same in both versions.

**What's working**
- The type plan is clear. A heavy serif headline, a light serif body and a small sans byline and wordmark make three levels at a glance, with no sizes fighting each other.
- The text column runs about 70 characters with generous leading, a comfortable measure for reading (Bringhurst).
- The copy is concrete and useful to the viewer: distances, surfaces, tyre widths, opening hours and a safety warning, all said plainly in "you" terms.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- How the column and line length behave at phone width. That matters here, because this kind of guide is likely read on a phone.
