**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline, "Shipments peak each third quarter", then drops straight into the row of blue bars and goes to the tallest one, Q3 2026 at 705. It reads as a clean, calm board-deck slide from an outdoor-gear maker. It is quiet and credible, maybe a little too even: every bar is the same blue, so nothing in the chart points at the claim the headline makes.

**What it's for.** Assumption: one slide in a board sales review, meant to show directors the seasonal shipment pattern and where Q4 2026 is heading. The header says "prepared for the October board meeting".

**Verdict.** It mostly works. It's legible, honest in scale and well built. But it draws a forecast exactly like the actuals, and its headline reports a pattern without telling the board what that pattern means for them. Fixing the forecast bar is the single biggest change.

**What would move it most**
1. **The Q4 2026 forecast looks like a result (defect).** The last bar (560) has the same solid blue and the same bold label as the seven actual quarters. The only signs that it's a forecast are a parenthesis at the end of the subtitle and the source line at the bottom. A director scanning the chart reads 560 as something already shipped, and October is when people start holding management to Q4 numbers. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says projections should look different from observed data. Fix: draw that bar as an outline, hatched or in a lighter tint, put "Forecast" on the bar or its axis label, and show a range if the September forecast has one.
2. **The headline states a fact and stops there (copy, taste).** "Peak each third quarter" is true, but the board has already lived through it. The point that matters to them is in the numbers and nowhere in the words. Every 2026 quarter is up on the same quarter of 2025: Q3 went from 610 to 705, about +16%, and the Q4 forecast of 560 is about +17% on 480. Also, two Q3 peaks are thin evidence for "each". Fix: end the headline on what the pattern means for the viewer, for example growth that holds across the seasonal cycle, or what the Q4 forecast implies. Then make the chart show that comparison: give the two Q3 bars the accent colour and set the rest back, or add year-on-year changes to the 2026 bars. Highlighting one point with a second colour is Few's approved fix ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").

**Smaller things.**
- The bottom ~120 px under the source line is empty, so the slide sits high and the chart looks squeezed. Make the plot taller or move the footer down to the bottom margin.

**What's working.**
- The scale is honest and the labels are clear. The value axis starts at zero, gridlines are hairline-pale, every bar has its value directly above it, and "Thousands of units" sits under the chart title. Nobody has to guess what a number means. Keep this.
- The type hierarchy is plain. The serif headline contrasts with the sans text, the chart title is set apart by weight rather than another size, and the small grey text for metadata stays quiet. It all reads at a glance.
- The source line names both the system and the forecast vintage ("September forecast"). That's the right habit for a board audience.
