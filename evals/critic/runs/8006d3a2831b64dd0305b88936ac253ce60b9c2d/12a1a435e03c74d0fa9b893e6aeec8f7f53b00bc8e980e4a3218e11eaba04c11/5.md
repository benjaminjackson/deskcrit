**First read.** I see three big numbers first: 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4. Right away I want to know what each one is in. Then the eye moves down to the orange bars and picks up the obvious pattern: orders build through the week, peak on Saturday and drop on Sunday. It reads as a calm, tidy admin dashboard for a small online bakery that sells one-off orders and subscriptions.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the home screen of a bakery owner's back office. It should tell them at a glance how the shop is doing and how much to bake for each delivery day.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and well built, but the numbers don't yet do their job. The three headline figures have no units, no period and nothing to compare against. The chart's weekday totals are also skewed by the calendar, so the owner can't tell whether things are good or bad, and they may plan baking around a trend that isn't real.

**What would move it most**

1. **The three headline figures can't be read on their own (defect).** In the top row, "3.8" has no %, "64.20" has no currency, and "Active subscribers 12.4" can't be a head count. Is it thousands? An average? A typo? None of the cards says what period it covers either; only the chart's source line mentions September 2026. None of them sits next to a target or last month's figure. So the owner sees three numbers and can't tell if 3.8% conversion is a good month or a bad one, and "12.4 subscribers" will be read at least two ways. These are the figures they'll act on and repeat to others, so the ambiguity matters. Fix: put the unit, the period and a comparison next to each number (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). For example: "3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400" (or "12.4k"), then a small line underneath like "▲ 0.4 pts vs August". Keep the arrow-and-words form so colour isn't the only thing showing the change.

2. **The chart compares days that don't occur equally often (defect, common sense; no approved source).** The source line says these are orders delivered in September 2026. That month starts on a Tuesday, so it has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of every other day. The bars are monthly totals, so Tuesday and Wednesday get an extra day's worth of orders. Reading the bars by eye (about 640, 710, 760 and 820 for Monday to Thursday), the steady climb from Monday to Wednesday goes away per delivery day. Monday is about 160 a day, Tuesday about 142, Wednesday about 152. Thursday (about 205), Friday (about 260) and Saturday (about 280) are where the real lift is. Anyone using this to staff the bakery or order flour would over-plan for Tuesday and Wednesday. Fix: plot average orders per delivery day, or pool several months so the weekday counts even out. Then say which you did in the axis label or source line.

3. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding (taste).** "Orders by delivery day" makes the owner work out the point. Once the numbers are averages, a title like "Friday and Saturday carry most of the week; Sunday is about a third of Saturday" gives them the answer in the time they'll actually spend looking.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing. The rest of the checklist passes.

**What's working.**
- **Tight structure.** One left edge at about 48 px runs through the wordmark, the page title, the cards and the chart panel. The gaps between cards match the gap above the chart. Keep this when you add units and comparison lines to the cards.
- **The chart is restrained.** It starts at zero, uses one colour for one series, has pale gridlines, puts the weekdays in their natural order, and has a source line. Those are the right calls, so only the data needs fixing, not the drawing.
- **The type scale is clear.** Big KPI numbers, a bold page title, medium labels and one small size for axes and the source. Every size has an obvious job.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the nav links show a visible focus state when you tab through them, and whether the period can be changed somewhere else in the app.
