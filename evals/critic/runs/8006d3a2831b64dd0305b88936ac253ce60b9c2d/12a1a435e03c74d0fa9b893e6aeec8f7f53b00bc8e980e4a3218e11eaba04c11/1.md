**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers: 3.8, 64.20, 12.4. Then it goes down to the orange bars, where Saturday's peak and Sunday's drop stand out. It reads as the owner's dashboard for a small online bakery. It feels calm, tidy and warm. But the first few seconds end in a question: 3.8 what? 12.4 what?

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the overview a bakery owner opens to see at a glance how the shop is doing and when orders land, so they can plan baking and delivery.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are well made, but the three headline numbers can't be read. They have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them with, so the part of the page that gets the most attention tells the owner almost nothing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three KPI cards have no unit, no period and no comparison (defect).** "3.8" could be 3.8% or 3.8 of something else. "64.20" has no currency. "Active subscribers 12.4" is a count shown with a decimal, so it could be 12.4 thousand, 12.4% or a data error. None of the cards says what time span it covers, and none shows a target, last period or trend. The owner has to remember what each number means, and even then can't tell whether 3.8 is good or bad. These are the biggest type on the page and the first thing anyone reads, so they set the value of the whole screen. Few lists the bare headline number as a common dashboard pitfall ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Fix: put the unit, the period and a comparison next to each number. For example "3.8%", "£64.20" and "12,400" (or whatever the true count is), a period line such as "September 2026", and a small change line such as "▲ 0.4 pts vs August". The comparison is what turns a number into a judgment the owner can act on.

2. **The chart title names the topic, not what it shows (taste, argued on its own terms).** "Orders by delivery day" makes the owner work out the point for themselves. The point here is clear and useful: Friday and Saturday carry the week, and Sunday is about a third of Saturday. A title like "Saturday is your busiest delivery day; Sunday is the quietest" tells the owner in one read what to plan around, and keeps the bars as the proof. If the takeaway changes from month to month, generate the sentence from the data instead of writing it once.

**Smaller things.**
- There is no visible control to change the period. The chart is fixed to September and the KPIs show no period at all. A date-range or period switch at the top right of "Shop overview" would let the owner compare months without leaving the page (Nielsen, heuristic 7).

**What's working.**
- The chart is plain and honest. It uses one colour for one series, the bars start at zero, the ticks are evenly spaced, the gridlines are pale, the days run Monday to Sunday, and a source line under the chart gives the system and the month. Keep all of that.
- The structure is clear. The title, a row of three equal cards and one wide chart all share the same outer edges and gutters, and the whole dashboard fits on one screen.
- The brand orange appears in only two places, the wordmark and the bars, so the data is the one strong colour on the page. The current tab ("Overview") is marked in bold.
