**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, then drops to the orange bars, where Saturday is tallest and Sunday falls off. It reads as a small bakery's admin dashboard. It looks calm, clean and well made. Then you look back at the numbers and can't say what any of them means: 3.8 what? 64.20 in what currency? 12.4 subscribers?

**What it's for.** Assumption: an owner-facing overview where the Crumbwell team checks at a glance how the shop is doing and plans around delivery days.

**Verdict.** The layout and chart are good, but the three numbers that lead the page can't be read. They have no unit, no time period and nothing to compare against. The most prominent part of the screen tells the owner nothing they can act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units, so they can't be read: defect.** In the top row of tiles, "3.8" could be 3.8% or 3.8 of something else. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" for *Active subscribers* can only mean 12,400 (or 12.4k), but nothing says so, and a reader could take it as a typo. These are the figures people repeat in meetings, so a second reading is a real risk: someone reporting "12 subscribers" or "3.8 conversion" gets it wrong. Fix: put the unit right on the number: "3.8%", "£64.20" (or whatever the currency is), "12,400". (Tufte, *Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2, on clear labelling.)
2. **Nothing to compare the numbers against: convention.** Even with units, none of the three tiles says whether the number is good or bad. There is no previous period, no target, no trend, and no stated period. The chart caption says September 2026, but the tiles don't say whether they cover the same month. The owner has to remember last month's figures to know whether to worry, which defeats the point of an overview. Fix: put the unit, the time period, and a comparison, such as the change from the last period or the target, next to each headline number (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). A small "+0.4 pts vs Aug" line under each figure is enough.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Orders by delivery day" names the topic but not the finding. A title like "Saturday is the busiest delivery day; Sunday has a third as many orders" puts the planning point into words. This is taste: no approved source covers it, but it saves the owner from working it out every visit.

**What's working.**
- **Clear structure.** Three tiles and one chart sit on a single grid with matching gutters and margins, and there are only four type sizes, each with a clear job (big number, page title, card title, label). Keep that restraint as you add the comparison lines.
- **A clean, honest chart.** The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, the days run in natural weekday order, there is no legend, and the caption gives the source and month (Tufte's data-ink principle). Don't sort the bars by value, because weekday order is what the owner plans in.
- **One brand colour, used with purpose.** The burnt orange is the logotype colour and the bar colour, and everything else is neutral, so the data is the only colour on the page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't check hover and focus states on the top nav, or how the tiles reflow on a narrow screen.
