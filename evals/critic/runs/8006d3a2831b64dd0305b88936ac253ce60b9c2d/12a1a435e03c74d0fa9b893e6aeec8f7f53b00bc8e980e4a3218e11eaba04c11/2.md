**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers across the top, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, then drops to the orange bars, where Saturday is tallest and Sunday falls away. It reads at once as a small bakery's shop dashboard, and it feels calm and tidy. The first second ends in a question, though: 3.8 what? 12.4 what?

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the overview page a bakery owner or staff member opens to see how the shop is doing and plan the delivery week.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are well made. But the three numbers that get the most space on the page are bare. They have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them with, so the viewer can't tell what they mean or whether they are good. Until that's fixed, the top half of the overview doesn't do its job.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline numbers are missing their units, and one of them can be read two ways** (top row, all three cards). "3.8" is probably a percentage. "64.20" is probably money, but there's no currency. "Active subscribers 12.4" is the worst: you can't have 12.4 subscribers, so the viewer has to guess whether it means 12,400 people, 12.4% of customers, or a rate. Someone who reads it the wrong way will plan stock or marketing on a figure 1,000 times off. This is a **defect**, and the convention is to label every figure so nobody has to guess what it measures (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few). The fix is to put the unit on the number itself: "3.8%", "£64.20", and "12,400" or "12.4k".
2. **Each number stands alone, with no time period and no comparison** (same cards). Even with units, "3.8%" tells the owner nothing unless they remember last month's figure. The page doesn't say whether it is up or down, or whether it beats a target, so the overview can't answer its basic question: is the shop doing better or worse? The chart's period appears only in the small grey source line ("September 2026"), and the cards give no period at all, so you can't tell whether they cover the same month. This is a **convention** that costs something here: Few lists a bare KPI as a common dashboard pitfall ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). The fix, per Few, is to put the period and a comparison next to each number, such as "+0.4 pts vs August" or a small trend line, and to state the period once near "Shop overview" so the cards and the chart clearly share it.

**Smaller things.**
- Chart title ("Orders by delivery day"): it names the topic but not the finding. Something like "Saturday is the busiest delivery day; Sunday is the quietest" would tell the owner what to plan around. This is **taste**, because no approved source states the rule; I'm arguing it because a busy owner may not stop to read the bars.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, there is one series in one colour, the days run in calendar order, and the source line is there. Keep all of that as you add to the page.
- The structure is clear. The cards line up exactly with the chart panel's edges, there's one accent colour (the logo orange reused for the bars), the bold "Overview" in the nav shows where you are, and three type sizes (page title, big figures, labels) each have an obvious job.
