**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the overview screen a shop owner or operator checks to see at a glance how the business is doing and where to look next.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are solid, but the screen fails at its main job. The three numbers it puts first can't be read without already knowing what they mean, so the owner can't tell whether things are good or bad. Fix the headline numbers and this is a good dashboard.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three headline numbers have no unit, no period and no comparison** (the top row of cards). This is a defect. Each one makes the viewer guess:
   - "3.8" is presumably a percent, but it could be read as a ratio.
   - "64.20" has no currency.
   - "12.4" active subscribers can't be a head count, so it must be thousands or some kind of average. The viewer has to work out which, and may get it wrong.
   - None of them says what period it covers. The chart below is pinned to September 2026, but the cards don't say whether they cover the same month, today or all time.
   - Nothing says whether any number is up or down, or on target or off it.

   So the most prominent things on the screen can't answer "how are we doing?" This is exactly the bare-number pitfall Few describes in "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design". The fix: put the unit, the time period and a comparison next to each headline number, such as the change from last period or the target. For example: "3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400" or "12.4k", "September 2026", and a small "+0.4 pts vs August" line under each. Make the period match the chart's, or say plainly that it's different.
2. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding** (the "Orders by delivery day" panel). This one is taste; my source for it isn't on the approved list. The real story is that Friday and Saturday carry the week and Sunday is less than a third of the peak. Right now the viewer has to work that out from the bars. A title like "Fridays and Saturdays bring in a third of all deliveries; Sundays are lightest" lands the point. If Sunday is low because you deliver less that day, a one-line note on the Sunday bar stops anyone from reading it as a problem.

**What's working.**
- The bar chart is honest and quiet: the axis starts at zero, the ticks are even, the gridlines are faint, the days are in their natural order, and there's one brand colour with no legend needed. Keep that restraint.
- The source line under the chart gives the system and the month. That's exactly the context the KPI cards are missing, so use it as the model for them.
- The type scale is tight and clear: page title, big numbers, card labels and small axis text are four sizes with a distinct job each, all on one even three-column grid.
