**First read.** My eye goes straight to the three big numbers across the top, then down to the orange bar chart, where Saturday's tall bar and Sunday's short one stand out. It reads as the overview page of the admin dashboard for a small bakery that delivers orders, maybe one that sells subscriptions. It feels calm and orderly, but generic. Then the questions start. 3.8 what? 64.20 in which currency? Is 12.4 subscribers in thousands, or a percentage? And for which period?

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the shop owner's first screen, where they see at a glance how the business is doing and whether anything needs attention.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and well built. It still fails at its main job, because none of the three headline numbers can be read on its own. They have no unit, no time period and no comparison, so the owner can't tell whether any of them is good or bad. That gap is the problem. The layout is not.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three headline numbers can't be read** (defect, convention). In the top row, "3.8", "64.20" and "12.4" are each shown with only a metric name. "3.8" is probably a percentage. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" for *Active subscribers* can be read two ways: 12.4 thousand, or a rate. An owner who reads it wrong comes away with a picture of the business that is off by orders of magnitude. None of the numbers says which period it covers, and nothing tells the viewer whether it went up or down. These are the biggest type on the page, but they give the viewer the least. Fix (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"): put the unit, the time period and a comparison next to each headline number. That means "3.8%", "£64.20" (or the right currency) and "12,400" or "12.4k", plus a small line such as "▲ 0.4 pts vs August" or a target. Once the numbers have a comparison, this becomes a page the owner can act on.

2. **The chart title names the topic but not the finding** (taste; no approved source states this rule, so I'm arguing it as common sense). "Orders by delivery day" leaves the owner to read the shape for themselves. The actionable fact is that Friday and Saturday carry the week and Sunday drops to about a third of Saturday. That bears directly on baking and staffing. Put that finding in the title, or mark the Saturday peak and the Sunday drop on the chart. If one of those bars is what matters, draw the rest in a muted tone so it stands out (Few's one-accent approach).

**Smaller things.**
- The page title "Shop overview" and the September 2026 date in the source line are far apart. If the KPIs also cover September, say so once near the top, so the whole page has one period that the viewer can see.

**What's working.**
- **The structure is strong.** It's a clear grid. The three cards and the chart panel share one set of edges and gaps, and everything fits on one screen. Keep this when you add units and comparisons. Put the extra information inside the existing cards rather than adding more boxes.
- **The chart is honest and plain.** It has one series in one colour, bars from zero, pale gridlines and weekdays in their natural order. There is no legend because none is needed, and the source and date are stated.
- **The colour use is restrained.** The brand orange appears only in the wordmark and the data. Everything else is neutral, so the data is what carries colour.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus states on the top navigation.
- Contrast of the small grey axis labels and source line. They look fine, but measure them against 4.5:1.
