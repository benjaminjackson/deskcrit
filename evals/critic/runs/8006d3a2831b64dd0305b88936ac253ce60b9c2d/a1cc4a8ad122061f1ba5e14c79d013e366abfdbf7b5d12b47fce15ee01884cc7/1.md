**First read.** The eye goes straight to the serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops to the white search field, then down to the eight topic cards in a 4×2 grid. Within two seconds it reads as a self-serve help center for a team workspace product. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also like every other help center.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing page of a product's help center. A customer with a problem should either search for it or pick the right topic quickly.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the layout sits on a sound grid and nothing is broken. What holds it back is that nothing on it knows what customers actually come here for. It's a correct template that makes everyone search or guess a category, including the many people who arrive with the same few problems.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing on the first screen points to the most common problems.** Below the search field there are only eight broad categories. Someone who can't sign in or whose sync broke has to decide whether that's "Account and sign-in" or "Troubleshooting", click in, and look around. A help center gets most of its traffic from a handful of issues, and the page shows none of them. Status is also hidden as a plain nav link, even though "is it down?" is often the first thing a visitor wants to know. Add a short row of the top 4 to 6 articles or recent issues, named the way users describe them ("Can't sign in with SSO", "Calendar not syncing"). Put it under the search field or above the cards. If there's a live incident, show it as a line in the hero. *Common sense: no approved source states this. It's the gap between a help center that is laid out well and one that actually saves people time.*

**Smaller things.**
- **Runts in the card descriptions:** six of the eight descriptions end with one or two words alone on the last line ("your team.", "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). That makes the grid look uneven. Rewrite them to fit one line or fill two, or widen the text box slightly.
- **The search field has no magnifier icon and no submit button:** it's a bare white bar. On the navy it still reads as a field, but the convention users scan for is missing (Jakob's law, Nielsen/NN/g). Add the icon on the left.
- **Copy is generic:** "How can we help?" / "Browse a topic or search the guides." could be on any product's help page. That's fine for a utility page, but one line in Harbor's own voice, or one that names what people come for, would make it feel like theirs. *Taste.*
- **"Orders and shipping" sits oddly** next to workspace, integrations and notifications. If Harbor really ships physical goods, keep it. If not, it's a leftover from a template, and visitors will wonder whether they're on the right help center.

**What's working.**
- **One clear entry point:** the serif headline is the only serif on the page, sitting on the one dark band, so the eye lands on the question and then the search field. Keep that pairing and the single dark band.
- **Consistent cards:** each card has one icon style (thin navy line icons with the same stroke), one bold title and one grey line of description. The 4×2 grid lines up exactly with the logo on the left and the nav on the right. That regularity is what makes eight options scannable.
- **Topic descriptions say what you can do there** ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Fix sync problems and error messages") rather than repeating the category name. That's what lets people pick a card without clicking around.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the cards and the search field. That matters here because the cards are the main navigation. Also, whether the grey card descriptions and the placeholder text reach 4.5:1 contrast: they look close. Check them with a contrast tool.
