**First read.** The eye goes to the serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, then drops straight to the white search bar, then into the eight topic cards. It's clearly a product's help centre. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, and it looks like a lot of other help centres.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the front page of a product help centre. People arrive with a specific problem and need to get to the right article fast, by searching or by picking a topic.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the layout is calm and the topic grid is easy to scan, and that common layout is what someone with a problem expects. Two things hold it back. The search bar, which is the main action on the page, has no magnifier icon and no submit button. And the topic list makes it unclear what Harbor actually is.

**What would move it most**
1. **The search bar doesn't look like a search bar.** It's the white field under the headline: a plain rounded rectangle with only the placeholder "Search for articles". People recognise search by a magnifier icon, and many expect a button to press. Without either, the field reads as a generic input, and there's no obvious way to submit except pressing Enter. This is the one thing every visitor with a problem is meant to use, so it should be the most obvious control on the page (Jakob's law, Nielsen/NN/g: use the patterns people already know). *Convention, broken at a cost.* Fix: put a magnifier inside the left of the field. Either add a filled "Search" button on the right edge or rely clearly on Enter. Consider a line of example searches under the field, such as "Reset password · Invite teammates · Track an order", so people can start without typing.
2. **The topics describe two different products.** "Set up your workspace and invite your team", "Integrations: connect calendars, chat tools" and "Notifications" describe team software. "Orders and shipping: track a delivery or start a return" describes a shop. A stranger can't build a picture of what Harbor is. Someone looking for the order they placed will wonder if they're on the right site, and a workspace admin will wonder why shipping is there (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*: the grouping and names should show what the product is). *Defect, if the copy is real; common sense.* Fix: if Harbor really sells physical goods and software, add a phrase to the subhead that says so ("…for your Harbor workspace and orders"). If not, the Orders card is leftover template copy and should be replaced.

**Smaller things.**
- Short last lines in the card descriptions: "data." in Privacy and security and "where." in Notifications sit alone. Reword or tighten them so each description ends on two or more words, or fits on one line (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- The placeholder grey in the search field looks close to the 4.5:1 limit. Measure it, and darken it a step if it falls short (WCAG 1.4.3).

**What's working.**
- **A clear order of importance.** The serif headline is the only serif on the page and the largest type. On the navy band it's unmistakably where to start, and the white field right below hands the eye straight to the action. Keep the single serif moment.
- **The topic grid.** Eight cards in an even 4×2 grid on the same edges as the logo and nav. Each card has one icon, a bold title and a one-line description of what's inside, such as "Track a delivery or start a return", so they scan fast. The descriptions name tasks, not categories. Keep that.
- **Restraint.** One navy, a neutral grey range, one icon style with matching strokes, and no decoration that isn't doing a job.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus ring on the search field and cards. Check that it's visible against both the navy band and the pale grey.
- Whether the whole card is clickable.
- What sits below the fold. Popular articles and a route to a person matter here, beyond the "Contact us" link in the nav.
