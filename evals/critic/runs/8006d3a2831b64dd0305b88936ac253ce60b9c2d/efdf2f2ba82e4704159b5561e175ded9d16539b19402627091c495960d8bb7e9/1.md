**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then hits a solid grey block of body text that gives it nowhere to go next. It reads as a plain, tidy web or help page for people using a river ferry. It feels calm and competent, but closer to a terms-and-conditions paragraph than to something a passenger can use on the ramp.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a practical information page for ferry passengers: how often the boat runs, how to board (on foot or with a bicycle), tickets, and winter changes. Most readers will scan it on a phone or screen to answer one question.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and readable, but the page fails at its job. Five different practical topics are packed into one 15-line paragraph, so a passenger looking for one answer has to read everything to find it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body is one unbroken block covering five topics** (defect). Lines 1–2 give the timetable, lines 3–4 the crossing time, lines 4–10 boarding and bicycles, lines 10–12 tickets and the season pass, and lines 12–15 winter hours and where timetables are posted. Someone standing at the landing wants one thing, like "when's the last boat in winter?" or "where do I take my bike?". They can't see where it is, so they read from the top, and that answer is in the second-last sentence. Nielsen ("How Users Read on the Web") is clear that people scan screen text and that long dense blocks are a defect. Fix: split the text into short sections under small subheads that name what a passenger asks, such as *Timetable*, *Boarding with a bicycle*, *Tickets*, *Winter*. Put the hard facts where the eye catches them: every 40 minutes, about 12 minutes per crossing, hourly in winter, last boat before dusk. The bicycle steps are a sequence (wait at gate → wheel to lower deck → lock to rail → bags stay on the bike → keep stairs clear), so they should be a numbered list.
2. **The facts a passenger acts on are vague** (defect, copy). "Between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines people plan around, and neither gives a time. "Before dusk" could mean the last boat leaves at sunset, or some time before it. Someone who reads it the first way and arrives at sunset is stranded on the wrong bank. Give the first and last departure times, or say plainly that the posted timetable is the authority and where to find it. Right now that pointer is the final sentence. "Every forty minutes" should also say from what time (for example, "from 6:40, every 40 minutes").

**Smaller things.**
- The title "Notes for ferry passengers" is generic. Something like "Using the river ferry: times, bikes and tickets" would tell a scanner what's here before they read a word. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- The type is right for reading. It's a plain sans at a comfortable size, near-black on white, with lines of about 60–70 characters and generous line spacing. Keep all of that when you split the text.
- The hierarchy is clear: two sizes with a big step, and the title is also bolder. When you add subheads, make them one weight step up from the body at body size or slightly larger, not a third size close to the title.
- The sentences already read like a person talking ("the crew waves them on"). That will survive the restructure.
