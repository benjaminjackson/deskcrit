**First read.** The eye hits three equal blocks of loud colour (orange, green, purple) and reads the three white headlines left to right: "Learn to swim", "Join the lanes", "Family hour". It reads as a local pool or leisure centre listing its sessions. It feels cheerful and confident. Then the eye drops through a large empty field of colour in each card and runs out of things to read, with nothing to do next. The grey line at the bottom ("Design review: approved, one offer clearly leads the page") reads as a note someone forgot to delete.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a section of a public pool's website. Its job is to get adults, regular swimmers and families each to spot their session and take the next step: book, check times, or turn up.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The page is clean, legible and friendly, but no offer leads and none leads anywhere. Three equal cards with no next step give the visitor a menu with no way to order from it.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing comes first, despite what the page says about itself.** The three cards are the same size, the same weight and equally saturated, and there's no page title above them. The eye bounces across all three and settles on none. The footer line claims "one offer clearly leads the page." That's content, not evidence, and on the screen it isn't true. If one offer is meant to lead (probably "Learn to swim", since it's the one that needs selling), make it clearly the first thing seen and set the others back by size (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Hofmann, *Graphic Design Manual*). For example, give it a wider card, or put it on a row of its own above the other two. If the three really are equal, then hierarchy has to come from a page headline that says what this is ("Swimming at [pool name]"). *Defect:* the visitor gets no starting point, and the page's own claim about what leads doesn't hold.
2. **Each card stops before the part the visitor acts on.** No card has a time slot, price, booking link or button. "Every morning from six" doesn't say six until when. "Tuesday evenings" gives no hour. "Sunday afternoons" gives no hour either. The visitor learns the sessions exist, but not whether they can go or how. Each card needs a specific next step from the visitor's own week, such as "Book a Tuesday 7pm class" or "See this week's lane times", next to the times and the price. Those are the facts people plan around and later hold you to. *Defect.*
3. **The lower half of every card is dead space.** About 55% of each card is empty colour. The space isn't gathered into a deliberate pause against something dense. It's leftover, so it reads as unfinished rather than calm (Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*, "Contrasts"). This is exactly where finding 2's times, price and ask belong. Either fill it with that content or shorten the cards so the empty area isn't stranded. *Convention.*
4. **Three vivid hues at equal weight cover most of the frame.** Orange, green and purple each fill an equal block, and there's no neutral role and no lead colour. That's a big part of why nothing in finding 1 stands out. Pick one lead colour for the offer that leads. Turn the others into light tints or neutrals, or shrink them to small accents (Itten, *The Art of Color*; Refactoring UI). Keeping a colour per programme is fine as a code, but at full strength across 70% of the screen it's shouting, not coding. *Convention.*

**Smaller things.**
- Footer line "Design review: approved, one offer clearly leads the page": this is an internal note, not copy for visitors. Remove it before it ships (common sense).
- The card row sits off-centre: the left margin looks about 56 px and the right about 96 px. Either centre the row or run it to a right margin that matches the left.
- Heading breaks: "Learn to / swim" and "Join the / lanes" leave "to" and "the" hanging at the line end. Break at the phrase instead, for example "Learn / to swim", or let the card width fix it.

**What's working.**
- The headings and the blurbs are clearly two levels: a big, tightly set bold heading and a plain regular body. The type plan is simple and correct, so keep it.
- The blurbs are good copy. Each names who it's for and something concrete ("a coach in the water beside you", "three speeds to choose from", "floats and toys for the children"), so each one sounds like a person and lets the right visitor recognise themselves. Keep that voice when you add the times and the ask.
- White text holds up on all three colours. It looks comfortably above 4.5:1, so whatever colour change you make, keep the backgrounds at least this dark.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards are clickable links. If they are, nothing shows it, and that would fold into finding 2.
