**First read.** Nothing catches the eye first. The page reads as one even grey block of left-aligned text, and you have to read it line by line to find out what it is: a help or FAQ page for collecting bakery orders. It feels plain and tidy, but flat. The headings blend into the body text, and the last line tells you the headings "stand out clearly", which is the opposite of what you see.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help page for customers who order bread online and collect it in the shop, and that they come to it to find one answer quickly.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The headings are set in the same size, weight and colour as the answers, so a page meant to be scanned can only be read top to bottom. Two of the answers that customers will act on can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings look the same as the answers (defect).** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are the same size, weight and dark grey as the paragraphs under them. The only cue is a slightly bigger gap above each one. A customer looking for "cancel" has to read the whole page instead of running down four headings, and that scanning is the only reason the page exists. With no page title either, nothing tells you where to start. Dair calls this a contrast too weak to see, and it is worse than no contrast: two things meant to differ have to differ obviously, in at least one of size, weight, form or colour (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). The fix is to make the headings obviously different from the text, for example semibold or bold plus one clear step up in size, not a pixel or two. Add a page title above them ("Collecting your order") so the page has one clear starting point (Tschichold, *The New Typography*).

2. **The closing line is a leftover, and it isn't true (defect).** "Each heading here stands out clearly, so the page is easy to scan" sits below the four answers in the same style as body text. To a customer it means nothing. It describes the page instead of helping them, and it reads like a designer's note that got published. It also tells them something they can see is false. Delete it. If the page needs an ending, use one that helps the customer, such as how to reach the bakery when their question isn't answered here.

3. **Two answers the customer will act on can be read two ways (defect, copy).**
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." A day ahead of what: the pickup slot, or the day you ordered? And does it mean 24 hours before, or any time the day before? Someone who reads it as "the day before" and cancels at 8pm for a 9am pickup may find they've lost the bread. Give a precise cut-off in the customer's terms, for example "You can cancel until 24 hours before your pickup time."
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name." Whose name, the collector's or yours? A friend who gives their own name when the order is under yours could be turned away. Say which: "…along with the name on the order."

   A smaller copy point in the same spirit: "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact and leaves the customer to work out what happens next. Finish it for them: what happens to the order after two hours, and are they still charged?

4. **The allergy answer only helps once you're already in the shop (defect, content).** "Every loaf has a card by the till" means someone with an allergy can only check ingredients after they've ordered and turned up. This is the one answer where a mistake can hurt someone. Point to where the ingredients are shown when ordering, or say plainly that they aren't and how to ask beforehand. Then "Ask the baker" becomes a fallback, not the only route.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: "Give the person your order number…" and "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead…" run about 95 characters on one line, which is past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the column to about 65 characters (roughly 600 px at this size).
- Short last line: "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Once the column changes width, reflow it or rephrase.

**What's working.**
- The spacing already groups things correctly. Each heading sits closer to its own answer (about 10 px) than to the answer above it (about 25 px), so the four question-and-answer pairs read as pairs. Keep that when you make the headings heavier.
- One plain sans serif in near-black on white, left-aligned with generous margins. For a help page that is calm and easy to read. Don't decorate it. The fix is contrast, not ornament.
- Every answer is short and opens with what to do ("Open your order…", "Give the person…"). Keep that order.
