**First read.** The eye lands on "Changing your pickup time" only because it comes first. After that it steps down four bold headings that all look the same, with a line or two of grey-black text under each. It reads as a plain, calm help page for a bakery's online pickup orders. It's tidy and quiet, and nothing on it says whose page it is or what page it is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the help or FAQ page a customer reads after ordering bread online for pickup, to change, hand over, check, or cancel that order.

**Verdict.** The layout is sound and easy to scan, but the words let it down where it matters most. The cancellation deadline and the collection rule can each be read two ways, and the allergy answer only helps someone who is already standing in the shop. The design can't fix that: the copy needs a pass.

**What would move it most**
1. **The answers people act on can be read two ways (defect).**
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead": a day ahead of the pickup slot, or any time on the day before? Someone cancelling at 8 pm for a 9 am pickup could read it either way. If the bakery means 24 hours, that customer finds out at the counter or on their card statement.
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name": whose name, the buyer's or the collector's? And a name is said, not shown, so does the collector need ID? If the friend gives the wrong name, they leave without the bread.
   - "Holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" doesn't say what happens next (is the bread sold on, and is the money refunded?).

   Deadlines and handover rules are what customers hold the business to, so each should state the exact cutoff and the exact proof: "Cancel at least 24 hours before your pickup time", "They'll need your order number and your name". This is common sense; no catalogue rule covers it.
2. **The allergy answer reaches the reader too late (defect, copy).** "Every loaf has a card by the till" only helps someone already in the shop, after they've ordered and paid. The person who most needs this section, someone with an allergy deciding what to order online, gets nothing they can use. The answer should point to where they can see ingredients before ordering (the product page, or a list linked here), and keep the till card and "ask the baker" as a backup.
3. **No page title, so nothing leads (convention).** The four headings are the same size, weight and colour, and nothing sits above them. A visitor who arrives from search or a link has to read the first answer to work out what page this is and whose bakery it is. The equal headings are right for parallel questions. What's missing is one clearly larger element above them, such as "Help with your pickup order", so the order of importance is plain (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show logical order by differences of size).

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the column is about 820 px wide, so the longest lines run about 95 characters, against a comfortable 45 to 75 (Bringhurst). It costs little while each answer is one or two lines. Narrow the column to about 640 px before the answers get longer.
- Short last lines: "anything." sits alone at the end of the allergies answer, and "after the chosen time." trails the first one. Rewording or a narrower measure will fix both (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- "Open your order and choose a new slot" doesn't say where to open it. Make "your order" a link, or name the page.
- Taste: the headings mix forms ("Changing…", "Collecting…", "Cancelling…", then "Allergies and ingredients"). Phrasing the third as a task too ("Checking what's in your bread") would make the list scan more evenly.

**What's working.**
- Spacing groups the content correctly. Each heading sits tight to its own answer, with a clearly bigger gap above it, so you can read each question and answer as one unit at a glance. Keep that ratio when you add a title.
- The type has a clean two-level scale, a bold heading over regular text with a big step between them, in one family, near-black on white. It's easy to read and has no extra sizes.
- "After that the bread has already been baked for you" gives the reason behind the rule in a human voice. That's the tone the other answers should match once they're made exact.
