**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers" and drops straight into three even paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for people using a small river ferry: quiet, tidy, and trustworthy. Within about ten seconds you notice that you've read about the ferry's times without learning a single one.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that helps passengers plan a crossing: when the boat runs, how long it takes, the bicycle rules and the winter changes.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and competent, and the layout needs nothing. The page still fails its job because a passenger can't plan a trip from it: every time it gives is vague, and two of the lines they'd act on can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **There are no real times, so nobody can plan a trip (defect, copy).** Paragraph 1 says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says "one boat each hour" in winter and that the last crossing leaves "before dusk". The reader still doesn't know when the first boat leaves, when the last one leaves, or which minute past the hour to arrive. "In winter" names no months, and "a little longer" when the tide turns gives no number. Times are what a passenger acts on and later holds the operator to, so vague wording here costs them a missed or wasted trip. To fix it, give the first and last departures from each landing as clock times, the winter dates, and a figure for the tide delay ("up to 20 minutes"). Once the times are real they will want to sit in a small timetable or a short list near the top, not inside the sentences.
2. **The direction of travel can be read two ways, and the wrong reading strands someone (defect, copy).**
   - "Runs from the north landing to the market quay every forty minutes" could mean a boat leaves *each* side every 40 minutes. It could also mean one boat makes a round trip every 40 minutes, so a boat leaves each side every 40 minutes but at different minutes past the hour.
   - "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" could mean only the last boat back from the market quay. It could also mean the last boat in either direction. A reader who takes the second meaning when the first is true, or the reverse, ends up on the wrong bank at nightfall.
   - "One boat each hour" has the same problem: one each way, or one in total?

   Name both landings and both directions in each of these lines.
3. **"At no extra charge" points at a fare the page never gives (defect, copy).** Paragraph 2 implies there is a ticket price, but the reader never learns what it is or how to pay. Add the fare and how to pay, even in one line, or link to it.

**What's working.**
- **Line length.** The body lines run about 65 to 72 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45 to 75. That comes from capping the column at about half the page instead of stretching it across 1280 px, and the open right side is what makes the page feel calm. Keep that.
- **A simple type scale.** There are two sizes, and the heading is set apart by both size and bold weight, so the page needs no extra levels. Any new subheads or timetable labels should come from weight, not new sizes.
- **Spacing and contrast.** Paragraphs are marked by space alone and the line spacing is comfortable, so the text reads easily. The dark grey text on white is clearly strong enough.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Passengers are likely to check this on a phone at the landing. Check that the column and any timetable you add reflow to a narrow screen without sideways scrolling.
