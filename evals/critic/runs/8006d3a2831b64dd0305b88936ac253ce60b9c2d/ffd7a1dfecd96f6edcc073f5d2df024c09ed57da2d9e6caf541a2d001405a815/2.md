**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline at top left, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three even paragraphs of prose. It reads as a plain information page for people using a small river ferry. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy. It also feels like an essay where a passenger expects a notice.

**What it's for.** My assumption: it tells a ferry passenger, often just before travelling, when boats run, how long the crossing takes, how bikes work and what changes in winter, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound. The page still falls short of its job, because the facts a passenger plans around are vague and buried in prose. Someone who wants to know "when is the last boat?" has to read everything, and then still doesn't get a time.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times you can act on aren't stated** (defect, copy). The first paragraph says boats run "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". The third says "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". These are exactly the lines a passenger will hold the operator to, and each can be read more than one way:
   - "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00, so the reader can't tell whether there's a boat for their commute.
   - "Every forty minutes" has no starting time, so a reader can't tell whether they've just missed one.
   - "Before dusk" changes by the week in winter, and "the quay" might mean only the market quay or both sides.

   The cost of the wrong reading is being stranded on the far bank. "In winter" has no dates either. The fix is to state the first and last departure from each landing as clock times, the winter dates, and which side the last boat leaves from. If the times really do change too often to print, the page has to say so plainly and point to where the current times are (it mentions printed timetables and the noticeboard only in the last sentence).
2. **Nothing helps a passenger find their answer** (taste, backed by Krug's "don't make me think"). The headline and the body are the only two levels on the page. Each paragraph covers one topic (schedule, bikes, winter), but nothing names those topics. A passenger looking for one fact has to read each paragraph to learn what it's about. The paragraphs are short, so this isn't a wall of text, but scanning is how people use a page like this. Give each paragraph a short heading in bold body size, or put the three or four key facts in a small block under the headline (frequency, crossing time, last boat, bikes free). The prose can then add the detail. The headline has the same weakness: "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category. Naming the route ("North landing ⇄ Market quay: times and notes") tells the passenger straight away that they're on the right page.

**Smaller things.**
- The bike paragraph tells riders what the crew does and what riders do, but never what happens to the rider. Say "you" ("Wheel your bike on after foot passengers…") so the instructions land on the reader instead of describing what other people do.

**What's working.**
- The typesetting is disciplined: one family, two sizes with a big step between them and a weight change on the headline, near-black on white, and lines of about 65–72 characters with comfortable spacing. This page is easy to read for long stretches. Keep it when adding headings, and use weight rather than new sizes for them.
- The paragraphs are separated by space alone, the left edge is crisp and the rag is gentle. There's nothing to fix in the setting itself.
- The text sits in one column at the left with the empty space gathered to the right and below. On a wide screen that's the right shape for reading, so don't stretch the column to fill the width.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone. Passengers checking the ferry at the landing will mostly be on one.
