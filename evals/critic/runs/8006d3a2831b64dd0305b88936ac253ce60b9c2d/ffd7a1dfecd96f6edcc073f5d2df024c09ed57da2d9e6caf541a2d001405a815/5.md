**First read.** My eye lands on the bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", then goes down three tidy paragraphs. It reads as a plain, calm notice page for a small river ferry. The type is clean, readable and confident. Then the practical question comes: "when is the next boat?" The page can't answer it.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about the piece, so I'm assuming this is a web page that tells people using the river ferry when it runs and what the rules are, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound, but the page fails at its main job. A passenger can't plan a trip from it, because every time on the page is vague and the timetable facts are split across two paragraphs. The words are what need work, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page gives no times a passenger can use** (defect, in the copy). Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says "one boat each hour" and that "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". The reader still doesn't know:
   - when the first and last boats leave;
   - at what minute past the hour the boats go;
   - when "winter" starts and ends;
   - what time "dusk" is on a given day.
   
   "Leaves the quay" can also be read two ways. Earlier the text names the north landing and the market quay, so "the quay" may mean the market quay only. That leaves the last boat from the north landing unknown, or a reader may assume it's the same time. Someone who guesses wrong misses the last boat and is stuck on the wrong bank. Paragraph 3 then sends the reader to printed timetables "in the shelter at each landing". That means the page tells people to travel to the ferry to find out when the ferry runs.
   
   The fix: put real clock times here, or at least the first and last departure from each side in summer and in winter. Name the winter dates. Name both landings wherever the text says "the quay". If the times really do change week to week, say where online to find this week's changes, not only on the noticeboard at the landing.

2. **The timetable is split by the bicycle rules, so readers have to piece it back together** (defect, minor in scale). Paragraph 1 covers the summer service and paragraph 3 the winter service, with bicycles in between. Someone checking times reads all three paragraphs and joins the first and last. Put the service times in one place, ideally first and in a form people can scan, such as a short list or a two-row summer/winter table. Put bicycles after that. Even short subheads like "Times" and "Bicycles" would let people go straight to the part they came for.

**What's working.**
- The text is set to be read. The body size is generous, lines run about 65 to 70 characters, and the space between lines is comfortable. Text is dark on white and set flush left, and paragraphs are separated by space alone. Keep all of this when you restructure.
- The hierarchy is clear: one bold headline clearly above one body size, with nothing competing. Two levels are enough for a page this short.
- The bicycle paragraph is the model for the rest of the page. It says who does what, and in what order: riders board after foot passengers, lock bikes to the side rail, and keep their bags on the bike. Write the timetable copy to that standard.
