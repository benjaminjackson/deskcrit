**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops straight into three paragraphs of the same weight. It reads as a plain information page for a local river ferry. It feels calm and trustworthy, but it reads like a letter: nothing pulls the eye to a time, a rule, or an answer.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page where passengers check how the ferry runs: when it goes, how long it takes, the rules for bikes, and the winter changes. Correct me if it's meant for something else.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is good and there's nothing to fix in the layout. The page falls short on the words. The facts a passenger needs to plan around are left vague, and they're buried in prose with nothing to scan for, so the page reads nicely but answers "when can I travel?" badly.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times you act on are vague or can be read two ways** (defect). These are the lines passengers will plan around and hold the operator to:
   - "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" (paragraph 1) gives no first or last departure.
   - "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" (paragraph 3) can mean the market quay or both landings. "Before dusk" could mean an hour before or five minutes before. A passenger who guesses wrong is stuck on the wrong bank in the dark.
   - "In winter" gives no dates, so a reader in late October can't tell which timetable applies.
   - The page then sends readers to a printed timetable "in the shelter at each landing". That only helps people who are already at the landing, not people reading this page to plan.

   Fix: give real times, or a link to the live timetable, and real dates. Say which landing the last boat leaves from and its departure time, or how long before sunset it goes. End that line on what it means for the reader, e.g. "if you miss it, you'll have to wait for the next day's first crossing." (Common sense; no catalog rule covers it.)
2. **There's nothing to scan for, and scanning is how people use this page** (taste, argued). People come here with one question, such as "can I bring my bike?" or "when's the last boat?". All three paragraphs look the same and the numbers are spelled out ("forty", "twelve"), so the reader has to read everything to find their answer. Krug's point applies: a reference page should be scannable rather than read straight through. Fix: open each paragraph with a short lead-in or subheading that names its topic: *Times*, *Bicycles*, *Winter*. Set the key numbers as digits, so "every 40 minutes, about 12 minutes per crossing" can be picked out at a glance. Keep the prose voice. It's friendly and it suits the page.

**What's working.**
- The type is set well and should stay that way. The body text is a generous size (about 19 px), lines run about 60 to 72 characters with comfortable spacing, and the right edge is clean. Paragraphs are separated by space only.
- The hierarchy is simple and honest: there are two sizes with a big step between them, and the heading differs in weight as well as size. Nothing competes with it.
- The open right side reads as a deliberate reading column, not leftover space. Don't widen the column to fill it.
