I followed critic.md. The image is 1280 × 800, small enough that I read it whole without crops. Every checklist line came out `yes`, so both ranked findings below are about the copy, which the checklist doesn't cover.

**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops straight into three even paragraphs of grey-black text. It reads as a calm, plain information page for a small local ferry. It feels quiet and trustworthy, but nothing tells you where the times are. You have to read the prose to find out when the boat goes.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or printed notice for people about to take the ferry. They need to know when it runs, how long it takes, and the rules for bikes.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound: one clear heading, a comfortable line length, good leading and strong contrast. The page falls short on its words. The facts a passenger plans around are vague and buried in prose, so a reader can finish the page and still not know when to turn up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable facts are vague exactly where passengers will act on them** (defect). "Between early morning and late evening" (paragraph 1), "every forty minutes" with no first departure, "one boat each hour" with no anchor time, and "before dusk" with no clock time (paragraph 3) all leave the reader guessing. "Before dusk" changes by about an hour across a winter, so someone who aims for "late afternoon" can miss the last boat. "In winter" has no dates either. "The last crossing leaves the quay" can be read two ways: the last boat in both directions, or only from the market quay. If the north landing's last boat leaves earlier, a passenger relying on the second reading gets stranded on the wrong bank. Fix: give clock times for the first and last departure from each landing, the season's start and end dates, and the minute past the hour for the winter boats. If times really do vary, name where to check them (the printed timetable, or a link if this is a web page).
2. **The facts are written as prose, so nobody can scan for them** (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: people scan pages rather than read them). Each paragraph covers one topic (the crossing, bicycles, winter), but none has a label. The numbers sit mid-sentence at the same weight as everything else, so a passenger checking "when's the last boat?" has to reread all three paragraphs. Fix: add short subheads that name the content ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter") and pull the departure times into a small table or list. Keep the prose for the rules.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading doesn't name the route. "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any ferry's page. "North landing – Market quay ferry" tells a stranger they're in the right place.
- "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes … a little longer when the tide turns." "A little longer" gives no number. Say "up to 20 minutes" so people can plan connections.
- If this is a web page, sending readers to "the noticeboard there" for the coming week's changes points them to a board they can only see once they've already arrived. Give an online source for changes, or say that none exists.

**What's working.**
- **The text setting.** The line length is about 60–70 characters, the leading is generous, there's clear space between paragraphs and the rag is clean. Keep this measure and spacing when you add subheads.
- **A single dominant heading in bold, with no extra decoration.** The page is honest and calm, which suits a public service notice. Don't add colour or graphics to "liven it up". Structure is what's missing, not decoration.
