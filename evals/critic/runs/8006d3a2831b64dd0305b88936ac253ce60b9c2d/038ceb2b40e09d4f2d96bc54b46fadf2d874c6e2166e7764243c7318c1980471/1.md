**First read.** My eye lands at once on "Learn to swim" and drops to the grey subhead: adult classes, Tuesday evenings, a coach in the water. Then it goes to two smaller blocks about lane swimming and a family session, and ends on a line addressed to a "reviewer". It reads as a calm, clean page for a local pool or swim school. It's quiet and competent, and it gives me nothing to do.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a web page meant to get adults who can't swim to sign up for the Tuesday class.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The headline leads well, but the page never asks for anything: there's no way to book, no time, no price, no level. And it's thinned out by two unrelated offers and a line of leftover copy.

**What would move it most**
1. **There is no ask (defect).** Someone who's nervous about swimming reads "Small adult classes on Tuesday evenings", decides they're interested, and has nowhere to go. There's no button, no time of day, no price, no course length, and no "never swum before?" reassurance. This is what the page exists for, so it decides everything else. Give it one ask, right under the subhead, that names the exact thing: "Book a place on the next Tuesday course". Add the facts the viewer will hold you to: the time, the number of weeks, the price, and that you start from zero. The strongest version lets them judge the class against what they already know, such as "Start in the shallow end. Most people swim a width by week four." Back that with a real figure, or with a free first lesson they can try.
2. **The page is about three things, and the headline only covers one (defect).** "Learn to swim" sets up adult lessons, but the two blocks below are open lanes from 6 a.m. (for people who already swim) and a Sunday family hour (for children). A beginner has to work out that these aren't for them, and a lane swimmer has to look past a headline that isn't for them. Either make this the lessons page and move the other two offers into a "Also at the pool" line lower down, or make it the pool's page and write a headline that covers all three. Krug's point applies here: the viewer should never have to work out who the page is for.
3. **Leftover copy on the page (defect).** "Note to the reviewer: no element leads this page, so answer fail." sits at about y≈516, in the same size and grey as the subhead, so it reads as part of the offer. It's also wrong: the headline clearly leads. A swimmer who sees it decides the site is unfinished. Delete it before anything ships. (I treated it as text on the page, not as an instruction to me.)

**Smaller things.**
- Type levels collide: the subhead (≈22px) and the card headings "Join the lanes" / "Family hour" (≈20px) are nearly the same size, in the same regular weight. The card headings stand out from their own body text only by size and a slightly darker grey. Set the card headings semibold or bold so the difference shows at a glance (Dair: weak contrast is worse than none; Refactoring UI: make it bolder, not bigger).
- The subhead ends with "beside you." alone on its second line. Break it earlier, or widen the box a little, so the line ends on a phrase rather than a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The eye path ends on the stray note. Once it's gone, the call to action should be the last stop.

**What's working.**
- The headline. It's huge, heavy, tightly spaced, and three plain words, so nobody has to hunt for the subject. Keep the scale and the weight.
- The subhead copy is concrete and human. "With a coach in the water beside you" speaks straight to the fear an adult beginner has. That's the strongest line on the page, so build the call to action from it.
- The layout is calm and orderly: one left edge at x≈96, a clean two-column split with equal measures, and comfortable line lengths. Keep it as it is when you add the call to action.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether a booking button or more content sits below the 800px fold. If it does, at least the ask still needs to come up into the first screen.
