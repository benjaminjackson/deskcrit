**First read.** The eye lands on "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left. From there it runs straight down a single column of evenly spaced text, an introduction and then sections headed Boarding, Tickets and Winter service. It reads as a plain, calm information page from a small ferry operator. It is easy to read, but it reads as one long column; the sections don't stand apart, so it feels more like a letter than something you can scan.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: a web page that tells someone about to take the river ferry when it runs, how to board, and how to pay, so they can turn up at the right time and place.

**Verdict.** The type is sound and the page is easy to read. It fails in two places. The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on: exact first and last sailing times, and prices. And the spacing doesn't separate the sections, so a passenger can't skim to the part they need.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on (defect).** The intro says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Someone planning a trip needs the first and last departure from each landing and the minutes past the hour. Without them they can't tell whether they'll make the last boat home, and "late evening" can mean 8 pm to one reader and 11 pm to another. The Tickets section names single, return and season tickets but gives no price and doesn't say whether the kiosk or the boat takes cards. The visible Winter service line ("thins to one boat each hour, and the last…") has the same problem. "A little longer when the tide turns against the boat" leaves the reader guessing how much time to allow. Fix: replace each vague phrase with the figure ("first boat 6:20, last boat 22:40 from the north landing"; "about 20 minutes against the tide") and add prices and accepted payment. If times change often, a small departure table would do more than any sentence.
2. **The spacing doesn't separate the sections, so you can't skim to "Tickets" (defect).** By eye, the gap above each heading (Boarding, Tickets, Winter service) is about the same as the gap between paragraphs inside a section and the gap around the bullet list. The headings are bold and sit close to their own text, which helps, but each section doesn't read as a block. The page is one even stack, and a passenger looking for tickets has to read heading by heading. Equal gaps everywhere erase the groups (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II, on grouping by proximity). Fix: roughly double the space above each section heading compared with the space between paragraphs, keeping the heading tight to its text. Bringhurst (§2.2.2) leads subheads with more space above than below.

**Smaller things.**
- The Tickets opening paragraph ends with "kiosk." alone on its last line. Rewrap or slightly reword so the last line carries more than one word (Bringhurst §2.4.2).
- "From the ramp on the upstream side" assumes the passenger knows which way the river flows. Name a landmark or a sign they will see instead. (Copy, common sense.)

**What's working.**
- **Type scale.** The page uses three clear levels: a roughly 40 px bold title, roughly 22 px bold headings and roughly 18 px body. They differ in both size and weight, so nothing collides. Keep that scale.
- **Measure and leading.** Body lines run about 55 to 68 characters with generous line spacing, inside Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range. That makes the paragraphs comfortable to read on this wide screen. Don't let the column stretch across the empty right half.
- **Restraint.** Dark grey on white, one sans-serif typeface, no boxes or decoration. That suits a public-service notice. The Boarding bullets are short, parallel instructions, and every section should be written that way.
