I've finished the checklist and checked the peak columns up close. Writing up the critique now.

**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, then drops straight into the dark blue block in the middle of the heatmap. In a couple of seconds it reads as "weekday office hours are busy, nights and weekends are quiet." Next it moves right to the three big numbers. It feels calm, sober and trustworthy, like a well-made internal report.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a support lead uses this report to see when tickets arrive so they can plan staffing by hour and day.

**Verdict.** The chart works. It's the right form, uses one ordered blue ramp, and is labelled and sourced properly. But one headline number contradicts it, and on a staffing report that's the line people act on. Fix that before anything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **"Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" disagrees with the heatmap.** The darkest cells (30+) are on Monday and Friday in the column that starts at the 12:00 label minus one, which is 11:00. On Tuesday to Thursday the 10:00 and 11:00 columns are the same shade (25–29). By the chart's own legend, 11:00 is at least as busy as 10:00 every weekday and busier on two days. A reader who compares the two now has to decide which one is wrong. They might staff for the wrong hour, or stop trusting the rest of the page. Two readings are possible: the stat is wrong, or the hour labels are off by one column. Either way it's a **defect**. Check the numbers behind both. If the labels are meant to mark hour boundaries, say so in the axis ("hour starting"), or centre the labels on the cells.
2. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** "When new tickets arrive" makes the viewer find the pattern themselves. The pattern is clear and worth saying out loud: weekdays peak late morning, there's a dip around 13:00, it picks up again mid-afternoon, and weekends stay light. A title like "Tickets peak late morning on weekdays; weekends stay quiet" tells the reader what to staff for and makes the chart the proof. This is **taste**: the catalog entry for it has no approved source, so I'm arguing it on common sense. It's a report someone opens to make a decision, and the decision should be in words.

**Smaller things**
- The "New tickets, September" card is easy to read as the whole of September, but the chart covers "1 to 28 September". Name the period on the card too, so the two don't look like they disagree.
- The title (~28px bold) and the stat numbers (~26px bold) are almost the same size, so they look like a near-miss. Keep the scale to clearly separate steps: make the title or the numbers a full step bigger or smaller.
- The legend swatches start at an arbitrary x (around 278 px) rather than lining up with a column of the heatmap. Start them on a column line, or right after the label with normal spacing.
- The middle shades (15–19, 20–24, 25–29) look close in lightness, which makes the cells around noon hard to tell apart. Widen the lightness steps in the middle of the ramp.
- There's no control to change the period or queue, even though the subtitle says "all queues, 1 to 28 September". A period picker and a queue filter beside the title would let a lead check their own queue without asking for a new report.

**What's working**
- The heatmap is the right chart for "which hour of which day." It uses a single-hue light-to-dark ramp with seven labelled bins, so the busy block stands out even in grayscale (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*).
- Every headline number has its period and a comparison next to it ("Up 4% on August", "Down 6 min on August"). That's exactly what makes a KPI readable (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").
- The source line says what the hours mean ("when a ticket was opened, not when it was answered") and which time zone they're in. It heads off the most likely misreading in one sentence. Keep it.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether hovering a cell shows its exact count. A staffing lead will want the number, not just the bin.
