**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big white headline on the electric-blue band, then down to "Try it free". After that it slides right into an empty blue field and finds nothing there. It reads as a landing page for a budgeting app aimed at individuals and couples. It feels clean, confident and competent, and also unfinished, as if the product shot never loaded.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home-page hero of a consumer budgeting app, meant to make a first-time visitor understand the product, trust it with their bank accounts, and start a free trial.

**Verdict.** The words and type are in good shape, but the piece never shows the product. The right 40% of the hero is empty blue, so the headline's promise ("see where every dollar goes") has nothing for the viewer to see. That empty space is the main thing holding the page back. The main button ("Try it free") is vague in a way the visitor may later hold against you.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero promises something visual and shows nothing.** About 500 px of solid blue sits to the right of the headline and buttons (roughly x 800–1280, y 62–462). The headline says "See where every dollar goes this month", and the viewer wants to see that: a month's spending broken into categories, a limit about to be hit, the heads-up alert. Right now they have to take it on trust, and they can't tell this product from any other budgeting app. Because there is no counterweight, all the weight piles onto the left and the right side reads as missing content rather than chosen space (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). If an image failed to load in this capture, this is fine once it loads. If the space is meant to be empty, it's a **defect**. Fix: fill that area with one real product view that finishes the headline's sentence, such as a category list with one bar near its limit and the alert showing. Don't just repeat the headline, and don't use a generic phone mockup. The point of the image is to show the claim is true.
2. **"Try it free" leaves the viewer to guess what they're agreeing to.** This is the one button that commits them to something. "Free" can mean free forever or a free trial, and it doesn't say whether a card is needed or whether they're about to connect their bank. Someone who reads "free forever" and then hits a paywall will feel misled. The ask also only invites them to try the product, when it could compare the product with something they already have. **Defect** (ambiguous wording at the point of commitment). Fix: say exactly what happens, such as "Start your 30-day free trial, no card needed" or the real terms, and consider an ask in the viewer's own terms, like "See last month's spending by category". That shows them something about their own money they don't know yet.
3. **The reason to trust the product sits at the bottom, at the same weight as two minor features.** A money app asks visitors to "Connect your accounts" in the second line of copy, yet "The app can see balances and history but can never move money" is the third of three equal columns below the fold line of the hero. A visitor deciding whether to connect their bank meets the worry at the subhead and the answer much later, if at all. **Taste**, argued on the job: this is the objection that stops sign-ups for this kind of product. Fix: put a short line of that reassurance right by the buttons, for example "Read-only: we can never move your money" under the buttons, and keep the column below.

**Smaller things.** None that matter beyond the above. The checklist found no other failures.

**What's working.**
- **The type is disciplined.** There are four clear sizes (headline about 56, subhead about 20, bold feature headings about 18, body about 15). The headline is tight in both line spacing and letter spacing, and steps between levels come from weight as well as size. Keep this scale when you add the image.
- **The subhead is good copy.** "Connect your accounts, set a limit for each category, and get a heads-up before you go over" says who does what and when, in verbs, and ends on what the viewer gets. The feature lines do the same ("you can fix any of them in one tap").
- **One grid runs the page.** There is a 64 px left margin, three feature columns whose right edge lines up with "Sign in", and a clear main button (filled) next to a second button (outlined). It's easy to scan and easy to extend.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right side of the hero is an image that hadn't loaded yet. Check in a live browser.
- Whether the page ends after the three features or continues below. The white band under them may just be the bottom of the viewport.
- Focus styles on the nav links and the two buttons, especially whether the outlined button's focus ring shows clearly against the blue.
