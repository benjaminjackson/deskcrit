**First read.** The eye goes to "Project notes" and then straight to five heavy black frames stacked down the left two-thirds of the page. The frames are the strongest thing on the page, stronger than any of the words. The cards read as stages of a design project in order (kickoff, research, sketches, review, handover), even though the title calls them "notes". It feels tidy but stiff, like a wireframe more than a finished page.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an internal page that shows a project team the stages of a design project and what each stage involves.

**Verdict.** It's clear and readable, but it's a wireframe, not a design. The near-black boxes carry more weight than the content. The piece also never says what the list is (a sequence of stages), so the one thing a team reads a plan for (what's next, and when) isn't there.

**What would move it most**
1. **The borders outweigh the content** (convention). Each of the five cards has a near-black outline about 3 px thick, wider than the strokes of the 16 px body text. So the page reads as a stack of compartments first and as a plan second. The gaps between the cards already separate the stages, so the frames add no information, and with five of them repeated they're most of the ink on the page. Lupton, *Thinking with Type*: boxes "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell"; Tufte's non-data ink makes the same point. Fix: remove the borders and let space separate the stages, or use a pale tint or a hairline rule. Then the stage names become the strongest marks under the title.
2. **The list is a sequence, but nothing says so** (defect, in the copy and the structure). The title "Project notes" names a loose category. The content is five stages that run in order, each ending in a handoff to the next. A reader can't tell that from the page, and can't see which stage the project is in, who owns each stage, or when it's due. This matters because the Kickoff card itself says to agree "the dates, and who signs off each stage", and the page shows neither. Fix: rename the page for what it holds (for example "Project stages" or "How this project runs") and make the order visible, with numbers or a connecting line. If this is a live plan, add an owner and a date to each stage and mark the current one. Then the page answers "where are we, and what's next?"

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- **Clear, small type scale.** Three sizes with big steps between them (title about 32 px bold, stage names about 20 px bold, body about 16 px regular), so each level is obvious at a glance. Keep it.
- **The copy is good.** Every line starts with a verb and names a concrete result ("Visit three sites and write up what the teams there need"). Keep that voice when adding owners and dates.
- **One reading column on a single left edge.** It suits a document page, and the open space on the right is gathered in one place rather than scattered.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards are clickable. The heavy borders make them look like buttons. If they don't do anything when clicked, that's one more reason to drop the frames. If they do, they need a visible sign that they open something.
