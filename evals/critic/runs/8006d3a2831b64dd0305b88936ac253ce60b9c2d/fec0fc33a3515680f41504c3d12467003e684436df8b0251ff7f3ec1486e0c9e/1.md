**First read.** The eye goes first to "Review your order," then across to the bold $1,385.00 total and the dark green "Continue to payment" button. It reads straight away as the last checkout step for a furniture shop, and it feels calm and trustworthy. The two versions are identical except for the amber delivery notice. Version 1 sets it in normal sentence case. Version 2 sets the same four sentences in all capitals.

**What it's for.** Assumed: let a buyer check what they're paying for, take in the delivery conditions, and go on to pay.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The only change in version 2 makes the most important warning on the page harder to read: the doorway warning is the one thing that can make this purchase go wrong. The rest of the page is solid in both versions.

**What would move it most**
1. **All-capital delivery notice (version 2 only).** In the amber box in the left card, four lines of running sentences are set entirely in capitals. They also use the normal letter spacing for lower case, so the capitals look cramped. Capitals take away the word shapes readers rely on, so the paragraph reads slower and looks like shouting. Buyers tend to skip text that reads like a warning label. Here that text includes "measure doorways and stairs first," which is the line that stops a $940 table from getting stuck in the hallway. Capitals are for headings shorter than one line and for labels, not whole paragraphs (Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps"; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*). Set it in upper and lower case as version 1 does. The amber tint and the left rule already mark it as important. *Convention, and a defect here.*

**Smaller things** (both versions)
- There's no sign of where the buyer is in checkout, such as "Step 3 of 4". Add a simple step indicator above "Review your order."
- There's no visible way back to change the order: no "Edit cart" link or back link. Add one next to the item list.
- The most important instruction, measuring doorways, is the last clause of a paragraph. Leading with it would make it land, for example "Measure your doorways and stairs first: our drivers can't take doors off their hinges." *Taste.*

**What's working**
- One clear primary action and a total that stands out from the rest, so the next step is obvious.
- Prices are right-aligned in one column with lined-up figures, so the items add up at a glance to the $1,320.00 in the summary.
- In version 1 the notice copy is specific and written for the buyer: it gives the weekday window, says a two-hour slot will be texted the evening before, and explains what the drivers can't do.
