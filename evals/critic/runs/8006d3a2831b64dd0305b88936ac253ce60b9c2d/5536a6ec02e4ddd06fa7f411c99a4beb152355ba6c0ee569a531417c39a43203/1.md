**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif title "Harbour Lights festival", then drops into three even columns of grey prose and has nowhere else to go. It reads as a local newsletter or council web page announcing a small-town festival. It feels warm, calm and well set, but it gives the reader no quick answers. You have to read all three paragraphs to learn when anything happens.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or printed notice telling local families what's on at the festival and when, so they can turn up, enter something or volunteer.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do that job well yet. The type is comfortable to read, but every fact a festival-goer acts on (days, times, places, deadlines, how to join in) is buried in running prose under a title that doesn't even give the date. The middle column also sits half a line lower than its neighbours, which looks like a mistake.

**What would move it most**
1. **The when and where are buried in the prose.** *Defect.* The three columns are really three events: the festival weekend, Saturday's lantern walk and Sunday's pie contest. None of them has a heading, and every time and place sits mid-sentence: "stalls open at ten", "leaves the town hall at dusk", "brought to the hall by one o'clock", "announced from the steps at four". Someone checking when to show up has to read all 30-odd lines and still rebuild the schedule in their head. People skim pages like this rather than read them, and unbroken blocks defeat skimming (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). The title has the same gap: it names the festival but not the date or place. The fix:
   - Put the dates and place next to or under the title.
   - Give each column a short heading naming its event and day.
   - Pull the day, time, place and any deadline out of each paragraph into a line or two the eye can find without reading. Keep the prose underneath as colour.
2. **The middle column is dropped about half a line.** *Defect (convention: Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "Construction of the grid").* The left and right columns start level with each other, at the same height as the tops of the dividing rules. "On Saturday night…" starts about 14 px lower. So across all three columns each line of the middle one sits level with the gaps between lines in its neighbours. It's off by just enough to look like a bug, not a choice, and it makes a carefully set page look careless. Fix: start all three columns on the same first baseline.
3. **The page asks for things without saying how to do them.** *Defect, about the copy.*
   - "Volunteers are still needed" gives no name, number, link or place to sign up, so a willing reader has nothing to act on.
   - The pie contest says entries must be "brought to the hall by one o'clock", but never says whether you need to register first or who to tell.
   - "The first weekend of September" has no year and no actual dates, and "at dusk" isn't a time.
   - The children's boat race is announced with no day, time or place, though parents are the readers most likely to act on it.

   These lines are the ones readers act on and hold the organisers to, so any gap there costs turnout. Fix: make each ask name one exact action ("Sign up to volunteer at the library desk by 30 August"), and give every event a real date and clock time.

**Smaller things.**
- The first column ends on "seagulls." alone on its last line. Rewrap it so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- The apostrophes in "children's" and "o'clock" are straight typewriter marks. Use curly ones. This is common sense, as no checked source covers it.

**What's working.**
- The text setting is comfortable. The serif body type is a sensible size, the lines run roughly 45–50 characters (right for several columns, per Bringhurst) and the line spacing is generous. Keep it when you add headings.
- One family does all the work: a bold weight for the title and regular for the text. It holds together, so don't add a second typeface when you bring in subheads; use weight and size within this family.
- The copy has a real local voice ("guard the cake tent against hungry seagulls", "the pie that makes the judges laugh"). That's what makes the festival feel like somewhere you'd want to be, so keep it as the prose under the practical facts.
