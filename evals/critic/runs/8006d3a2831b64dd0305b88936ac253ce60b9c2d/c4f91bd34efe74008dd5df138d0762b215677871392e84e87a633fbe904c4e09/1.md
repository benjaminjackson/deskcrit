**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big serif quote, reads it slowly over three lines, then drops to the bold attribution and finally the grey two-line paragraph. It reads as a calm, warm library newsletter lead with a human voice. The headline is a quote about what people call the room, though, so the news only arrives in the last and faintest line: the room is reopening on Monday.

**What it's for.** No brief was given, so I'm assuming this is the lead of a local library newsletter (email or web), telling regular patrons that the reading room is reopening and why they should come back.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid and calm: one clear focal point, a clean left axis, a type pairing with sensible levels. But the most important thing on the page isn't the news. A reader who stops at the headline, as most will, learns a bit of local colour and not that the room reopens Monday. Moving the news up the hierarchy is the fix that matters.

**What would move it most**
1. **The news sits in the weakest text on the page.** The headline (about 95 px serif, three lines) is a quote about the room's name. "Reopens on Monday with new lamps, new shelves" is in the smallest, lightest, greyest text, at the bottom. So the order of importance runs backwards: the colour gets the most emphasis and the fact readers need gets the least. A skimmer leaves without knowing anything has happened. It's a **defect** (the viewer misses the point of the piece; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: the page should make its point without the reader working for it). The fix: keep the quote as the voice, but get the news into the top two levels. Either put "Reopens Monday" in the eyebrow or a short deck, or promote the reopening sentence to the attribution's weight and size, so someone reading only the big type and the bold line comes away with the date.
2. **"Monday" is a date people will act on, and it's open to two readings.** In a newsletter that may be read days after it goes out, "Monday" could mean this coming Monday or the one after. A patron who picks the wrong one arrives to a closed room, or doesn't come. Dates and times are what readers hold the library to. **Defect.** Give the full date ("Monday 1 December"), and the opening hours if they've changed.
3. **The quote is hard to follow for a stranger.** "It's the 'old' reading room — nobody calls it anything else" relies on context the reader doesn't have. Why is "old" in quotes? Is the room no longer old, or does nobody use its new name? The volunteer knows the joke; the reader only has the words. Since this line is the headline, an unclear quote means the main message misfires. **Taste**, but it matters here. Either add the missing half in the deck ("…even after the refit") so the reader gets the point that it's renewed but still the same room, or choose a quote that lands without a setup.

**Smaller things.**
- The opening quote mark isn't hung: it sits on the 110 px margin and pushes "It's" right of "room" and "anything" underneath, so the headline's left edge looks notched at 95 px. Hang the " into the margin so the letters line up (Bringhurst on optical alignment; the entry's source is only partly confirmed, so treat it as common practice).
- The headline breaks "reading / room" across lines 1 and 2, which splits the name of the very thing the story is about. Rebreak so "reading room" stays on one line (taste).

**What's working.**
- The type plan: a large book serif for the human voice against a sans for the attribution and facts, with the attribution set apart by **weight** instead of yet another size. That makes four clear levels (eyebrow caps, display, bold attribution, grey body) with no sizes that collide. Keep it.
- One strong left axis at about 110 px carries the eyebrow, headline, attribution and body, with all the empty space gathered to the right and bottom. It reads as calm and deliberate, which fits a library.
- The warm cream background and the single rust accent on the letterspaced eyebrow give it a quiet, papery mood that matches the subject, and the accent stays small.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the three-line headline rewraps on a phone (where "reading room" lands, and whether the 95 px size is cut down enough).
