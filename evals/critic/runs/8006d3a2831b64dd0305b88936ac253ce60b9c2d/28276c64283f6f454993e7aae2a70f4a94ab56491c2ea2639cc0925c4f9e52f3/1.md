**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three plain paragraphs. It reads as a short information page from a small local transport service. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, and it's quick to scan. The type is clear. What you get from it is not: once you've read it, you still don't know when the boat leaves.

**What it's for.** This is my assumption: a web page that tells someone planning a crossing when the ferry runs, how long it takes and what the rules are, so they can turn up at the right time.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are competent and barely need touching. The page still fails at its job, because the copy leaves out every fact a passenger would act on: departure times, the last boat, and the fare. A passenger can read the whole page and still not know when to be at the landing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable is described, never given.** This is the defect that decides the page. Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening." Paragraph 3 says "one boat each hour" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk." None of those can be planned around. Read as a stranger, "early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00. "Before dusk" moves by an hour or more across the winter. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say from what time. There's also a direction problem: the page says boats run north landing → market quay, but the last winter crossing "leaves the quay." Is that the market quay? Is there a return boat at all? A passenger who guesses wrong misses the last boat home in the dark. The only fix the page offers is "printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing," which sends the reader to the dock to find out whether to go to the dock. Direction: put the first and last departure from each side, and the winter dates and last boat, as real clock times. A small two-column timetable is the natural form. Make it the second thing on the page, right under the title, so it leads instead of sitting in paragraph 3. Common sense, not a cited rule. The guidance here is that times and deadlines people act on must be unambiguous.

2. **The money line points at a fare it never states.** Defect, smaller. "Bicycles ride on the lower deck at no extra charge" tells you there's a charge, but not what it is or how to pay (on board? cash? card?). Add the fare and the payment method in one line.

3. **The first paragraph buries the useful fact in a run-on sentence.** Defect (copy). "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes" is useful. It's followed by a 40-word clause about tides, crew, a sandbank and the harbour wall that only means "allow a few extra minutes at low tide in the morning." Cut it to that, in the reader's terms. For example: "At low tide in the morning, allow about X minutes." It's shorter and gives an actual number.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 1 ends with "tide." alone on its last line, a runt (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.4.2). The copy edit above will probably fix it. If not, rebreak the paragraph or tighten its wording.

**What's working.**
- **The scale is clean.** There are two sizes, a heavy title and regular body, with a big jump between them. There is one clear place to start and no sizes that nearly collide. Keep it that way if a timetable is added: use weight or a table for the new level, not a third size that's only a few points different.
- **The text is set for reading.** Lines run about 65–72 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45–75. The line spacing is comfortable, the paragraphs are separated by space only, it's flush left with no indents, and the contrast is near-black on white. Nothing about the setting gets in the reader's way.
- **Each paragraph covers one topic** (schedule, bicycles, winter). That makes it easy to add a heading or a table per topic later without reorganising.
