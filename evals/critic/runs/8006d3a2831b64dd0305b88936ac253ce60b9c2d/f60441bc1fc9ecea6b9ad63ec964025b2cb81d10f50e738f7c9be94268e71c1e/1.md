**First read.** The eye goes to the three big numbers across the top (3.8%, €64.20, 12.4k), reads their green arrows, then drops to the orange chart, where Saturday's tall bar and Sunday's short one stand out. It reads at once as an owner's overview for a small online bakery that sells one-off orders and subscriptions. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and it's done in a familiar dashboard style.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is the home screen of the shop's admin tool. The owner glances at it to see how the business is doing and to plan baking and deliveries around the busy days.

**Verdict.** As a piece of layout this works: it's clear, well spaced, and every number has a comparison. What lets it down is the data. The chart's bars and the three cards' time periods quietly mislead the owner about the very things they'd act on: which days are busy, and whether the numbers describe the same orders.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart counts each weekday a different number of times** (defect). September 2026 starts on a Tuesday, so it has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of every other day. The bars are monthly totals, so Tuesday and Wednesday each get an extra day's orders. Reading the bars by eye and dividing by the number of times each day occurred gives about 160 orders per Monday, 142 per Tuesday, 152 per Wednesday, 205 per Thursday, 260 per Friday, 282 per Saturday and 95 per Sunday. So Tuesday is actually the quietest weekday, not Monday, and the smooth climb from Monday to Thursday that the chart shows doesn't exist. Someone planning bakes and drivers from this chart would staff the wrong days. Fix: plot average orders per delivery day, or orders over a whole number of weeks (the last 4 or 8). Then say that in the source line.
2. **Three different time periods on one screen** (defect, a copy problem). The first two cards say "Last 30 days", the subscriber card says "As of 30 September 2026", and the chart covers "orders delivered in September 2026". A reader can't tell whether the conversion rate and order value cover the same orders as the chart. If today isn't 30 September, they don't. "Last 30 days" is also relative to a date the page never shows. These numbers are what the owner will act on and quote, so this isn't a style point. Fix: use one period for the whole page and state it once under "Shop overview", for example "September 2026, compared with August". If the cards have to roll, show the dates ("1–30 Sep").
3. **The chart's title names the chart instead of saying what it shows** (taste, argued on common sense; the reference entry's sources aren't on the approved list, so I'm not citing them). "Orders by delivery day" makes the owner work out the point for themselves. The chart is the biggest and most colourful thing on the page, so it should do that work for them. Once item 1 is fixed, a title like "Saturday is your busiest delivery day; Sunday gets a third as many orders" tells them in one line.

**What's working.**
- **Units set smaller and lighter than the numbers** in "3.8%", "€64.20" and "12.4k". The figure reads first and the unit is still there. Keep this.
- **Every card has the same three tiers**: label, then number, then period, then the change against the previous period with an arrow, a sign and a colour. Gains don't depend on colour alone, and each number comes with a comparison.
- **The chart is plain.** It uses one brand-orange series, faint gridlines, a zero baseline, weekdays in their natural order and a source line. Nothing competes with the data. Keep that restraint when you change what the bars measure.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the nav links look on hover and keyboard focus, and how the three cards and the chart rearrange on a narrow (phone-width) screen.
