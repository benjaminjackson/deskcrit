**First read.** The eye goes to the centred headline, then across four bold prices ($0, $12, $39, $99), which are bigger and darker than the headline. Then it stops, because the four cards look the same and the page gives no hint which one to look at. This is the pricing page of a SaaS collaboration tool. It feels clean, calm and safe, and it doesn't push me toward anything.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a pricing page that helps a small team or freelancer pick a plan and click through to sign up.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and easy to scan, but it doesn't help anyone decide. The four plans have equal weight, and the tiers don't climb in a straight line, because Free gives more editors than Solo. On top of that, the price line can be read two ways. A visitor has to work out the choice alone and may get it wrong.

**What would move it most**
1. **No plan is the suggested one** (defect). The four cards, four large prices and four solid-blue buttons are all identical. A visitor who doesn't know which plan fits gets no default and has to compare four columns of four rows before acting. Making every plan a primary action leaves the page with none: one action per view should be dominant and the rest lower in emphasis (Material Design, "Buttons"), and the order of importance should be shown by contrast (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). Pick the plan you most want people to choose, probably Team, and make it the first thing seen. Give it a tint or a stronger border and a short label saying who it's for. Keep its button solid, make the other three outlined, and set the other cards back.
2. **The tiers don't climb in a straight line** (defect). In the first two cards, Free gives "3 editors" and Solo gives "1 editor". Someone working with one collaborator reads this and concludes Free is better than the $12 plan, or suspects a typo, and either way stops trusting the table. A first-time visitor should be able to form a correct picture of how the plans relate (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2). Every row should get better, or stay the same, from left to right. If Solo is deliberately single-seat, say so in its subtitle ("Just you, more projects"). As it stands, it looks like a mistake.
3. **"/ month" doesn't say per what** (defect; this is copy). Next to "15 editors", "$39 / month" can mean $39 for the whole team or $39 for each editor. That's $39 against $585, and the buyer will hold you to whichever they assumed. Company has a similar problem: it shows a firm "$99 / month" but the button says "Talk to us", so the visitor can't tell whether $99 is the real price or just a starting point. Name the unit ("/ month, whole team" or "per editor / month"). For Company, either make $99 something you can buy directly or write "From $99" so the button makes sense.

**Smaller things.**
- Every card has about 70 px of empty white below its button, against roughly 30 px of padding at the top. It reads as leftover space. End each card just below its button.
- The headline "Plans for every size of team" could sit on any SaaS site. Say what changes as you go up the tiers (editors and history), or what stays the same in every plan, so the line says something about this product.

**What's working.**
- The feature rows sit in the same order and on the same baselines in all four cards (projects, editors, history, support). That makes reading across the page work like a table without the clutter of one. Keep it.
- The type scale is tight: a large price, a medium plan name, body-size features, and small grey subtitles, with clear steps between them. The "/ month" is pushed back by size and grey colour, so the number leads.
- The colour is restrained: neutrals plus one blue. When you highlight the suggested plan (finding 1), that blue will actually stand out.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the buttons show a visible focus state.
