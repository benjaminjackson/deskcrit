**First read.** I look first at the bold headline "Landing stage rebuild". Then my eye drops to the wide flat figure, and the blue bar in it catches me because it looks like a button. Then I go across to the card on the right. It reads as a calm, tidy page from a ferry or harbour authority, with a sign-up box attached. It feels orderly and trustworthy but plain, and I have to read to the very last paragraph to find out why the rebuild matters to me.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a news page from a ferry operator telling passengers about rebuilt landings, with a sign-up for timetable change alerts as its call to action.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are clean and well set, but the sign-up can't do its job: it asks for your name and landing but has no email or phone field, so the note it promises has nowhere to go. After that, the page hides its one real piece of news (step-free boarding at every tide) under a noun-stack headline and a figure that shows nothing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form has no way to reach you.** *Defect.* The right-hand card offers "Get timetable changes" and says "We send one short note when the timetable changes". The only fields are "Your name" and "Home landing". A careful reader stops and asks "send it where?", and a careless one signs up and never hears anything. The name is the least necessary field here, and the contact field is the essential one. Fix: add an email field (or a phone number if the notes are texts), label it with the channel, and make "Your name" optional or drop it.
2. **The news is buried, and the headline doesn't carry it.** *Defect (copy).* "Landing stage rebuild" names a project but has no verb and nothing for the reader. The payoff, "The ramps now meet the deck at every tide, so prams and wheelchairs roll straight on", sits last, below the figure. That's the line a parent with a pram or a wheelchair user needs, and many readers stop before they reach it. Fix: put that consequence in the headline or the first sentence, spoken to the reader, for example "Step-free boarding at every tide: the landings are rebuilt". Then the benches and the kiosk can follow as extras.
3. **The figure doesn't show what the text claims, and its blue bar looks like a button.** *Defect.* The caption says it shows "the new north landing, seen from the water at low tide". What's there is three flat rectangles on a pale band. There's no visible ramp, and you can't tell which shape is the landing and which is the boat. The rounded blue bar on the right is the same blue and corner radius as the Sign up button, so it reads as a control, and the eye snags on it. If the image is a placeholder, it needs the real photo or drawing. If it's meant as a schematic, it has to show the ramp meeting the deck, because that's the whole story. Either way, take the action blue out of it.

**Smaller things.**
- "Home landing" is free text, although the landings are a fixed, known set. Make it a dropdown so a misspelled landing name can't break the alerts.
- "Home landing" is an operator's word, and nothing says what it does. Say "The landing you usually board at", and have the helper line say you'll only hear about changes there, if that's what it does.
- Neither field is marked required or optional. Once you add the contact field, mark the optional one.
- There's an empty band of about 200 px between the last paragraph and the footer note, so the footer floats loose. Either bring the footer up or let the article fill the space.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: four sizes with clear steps (headline, card heading, body, small text), and weight does the rest. Keep it.
- The body text is easy to read, with comfortable line length and line spacing, flush left, at a generous size.
- The helper line "You can stop the notes at any time" is honest and specific, and it lowers the cost of signing up. Keep that tone when you add the contact field.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus indicators on the two fields and the button.
- What happens after you press Sign up: confirmation, errors, and so on.
- How the card stacks on a phone. It should either sit below the key paragraph or be clearly reachable.
