**First read.** In both, the eye goes straight to the big serif headline, then down to the standfirst ("Now the last boat is for sale"), then the byline. It reads as the opening of a long-form magazine feature: quiet, literary, confident. In version 2 the headline is a dense black slab where the lines touch, and the eye snags on the overlaps before it reads the words.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the top of a long-read article on an editorial site, meant to pull a browsing reader into the story.

**Verdict.** Version 1 is better. Everything except the headline's line spacing is identical. Version 2 sets the headline so tight that letters from one line collide with the next, which breaks the one element the page depends on. Version 1 has no defect that matters at this level.

**What would move it most**
1. **Headline line spacing (version 2): defect.** In the three-line headline, descenders crash into the ascenders of the line below. The "p"s of "people" run into the "l" and "h" of "lighthouse", the "g" of "keeping" hits "lit", and the "g"s of "forgotten" and "lighthouse" run into "Fair Skerry". At a glance the lines merge into one black band, so the reader has to untangle the letters before they can read the words. That is the wrong first impression for a publication selling careful craft. Tight leading on display type is right; Bringhurst and Butterick both want large headings set tighter than text. The limit is that letters must not touch. Version 1's spacing, about 1.1× the type size, is already tight enough to read as one block. Go back to it, or tighten only until the descenders clear the next line's ascenders with a hairline to spare.
