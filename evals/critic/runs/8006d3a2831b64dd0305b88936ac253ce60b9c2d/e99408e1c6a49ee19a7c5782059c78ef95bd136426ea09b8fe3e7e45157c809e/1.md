**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", then gets caught by the heavy black grid, which is the darkest and largest thing on the page. It reads as a plain, competent bus-stop-style timetable for one route. It feels like a spreadsheet printout: clear and correct, but the cage of lines shouts louder than the times.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you didn't say: a passenger checking which crossing from North landing they can catch on a given day, on screen or as a printed notice at the landing.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The times are legible, in order, and the direction is stated. What stands in the way is that the frame is the loudest thing on the page, and the one thing a passenger has to check (whether this boat runs today) looks exactly like everything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **The grid outweighs the data.** Every cell has a near-black rule about 2 px thick, and those rules are as dark as the times and much longer. The page reads as 32 boxed compartments, and each departure is split into four separate boxes, so the eye moves cell to cell instead of along a row. Tufte calls this non-data ink, and Lupton's point applies directly: rules in a data table "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell" (*Thinking with Type*, p. 157). Fix: remove the vertical rules and the outer frame, and keep horizontal dividers thin and pale grey, or drop them for row spacing or a pale tint on alternate rows. The times should be the darkest marks on the page. *Convention, with a defect cost: it slows the scan along each row.*
2. **The exceptions are hidden in the "Runs" column.** "Weekdays" and "Weekends" are set exactly like "Daily", so a Saturday traveller has to read all seven rows to find that the 12:40 and 17:10 don't run. A weekday commuter also has to work out for themselves that their last boat is 17:10, because the 19:30 runs only at weekends. Missing a boat is the real cost here. Fix: make the exceptions stand out and leave "Daily" quiet. Use weight or a short marker on the restricted rows, or leave "Daily" blank and add a footnote. Better still, split the table into Weekdays and Weekends columns or tables so each reader sees only their own boats. *Defect.*
3. **The Period column repeats itself and doesn't group anything.** "Morning", "Midday" and "Afternoon" each appear twice. The gap between the two Morning rows is the same as the gap between Morning and Midday, so the repeated labels add reading without making groups. The column is also about 240 px wide for one word, which pushes the times to the right. Fix: either show each period once, with extra space or a subhead before each group, or drop the column. A clock time already says it's morning. *Taste, argued: the column costs width and reading time and tells the reader almost nothing.*
4. **"Spring season" gives no dates.** The subtitle is the only thing that says when this timetable applies, and dates are exactly what a passenger holds you to. "Spring" can mean March to May, or the operator's own season, which might start at Easter. Fix: give the date range, e.g. "1 March – 31 May". *Defect for a timetable.*

**Smaller things.**
- Times are set in proportional figures. "11:00" and "11:12" are visibly narrower than "06:40", so the minutes don't line up down the columns. Switch on tabular figures (Bringhurst 4.4.3).

**What's working.**
- Rows are in time order, and the column headers say what happens, "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay", rather than naming a category. A stranger can read the table without a legend.
- The type is restrained: one family, a bold title, a grey subtitle and regular body. Three clear levels with nothing competing. Keep that when you lighten the rules.
- The 24-hour times are consistently zero-padded (06:40, 07:20), which keeps them easy to scan.
