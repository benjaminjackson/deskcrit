**First read.** The eye goes to the bold headline, "How the river ferry runs in winter", then reads down three short blocks to a pale grey box. The page reads as a plain, calm notice for people who ride the ferry. It feels clear but it doesn't scan: the times, the ice warning and the fares are all in running sentences at the same weight, so you read every word to find the one fact you came for. The grey box is the only thing marked out, and it marks out bicycle and car fares, not the warning that might change your trip.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since nothing was given: a public web page telling winter ferry passengers when the ferry runs, what can disrupt it, and what vehicles pay.

**Verdict.** It's clean and readable, but it's laid out as prose for content people come to look up. The times are spelled out in sentences and the return trip is missing. The only highlight goes to fares instead of the ice warning. A rider still has to dig for the facts they'll act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable is buried in a sentence and only covers one direction** (first paragraph). "Every forty minutes… first crossing is at seven in the morning and the last at six in the evening" makes the reader turn words into clock times. It also only says when the ferry leaves the north landing. Someone on the south bank, who needs the last boat back, can't tell when it is. That's the fact a stranded passenger holds the ferry to. *Defect.* Fix: give the times as numerals in a pattern you can scan, for each landing. For example, "North landing: every 40 min, 07:00–18:00" and the same for the south landing, with "last boat back" stated outright.
2. **The emphasis is on the wrong block.** The tinted box is the only highlight, and it holds the fares. The ice paragraph is the one line that can change a trip and the one that tells the reader to do something ("Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue"), and it's plain body text. A rider skimming will see the fares and miss the warning. *Taste, argued:* a single highlight should go to the thing with a consequence. Fix: move the tint (or a stronger caution style) to the ice notice, or give each block a short label so neither has to rely on the tint.
3. **Lines run about 100–105 characters** (all body text, across the full 880 px column). On the way back to the next line the eye can lose its place, and here it's on dense, fact-heavy sentences. Bringhurst puts the comfortable range at 45 to 75 characters, 66 ideal. *Convention, and it costs something here.* Fix: narrow the text column to roughly 600–680 px at this size, and let the headline and box keep the wider width if you want.
4. **The fares don't say what anyone pays.** "Cars pay the standard fare" points to a number that isn't on the page. "Bicycles ride free on weekdays" leaves weekend cyclists to guess. *Defect.* These are amounts people will be held to at the ticket hut. Fix: state the car fare, or link to it, and say what bicycles pay at weekends.

**Smaller things.**
- Grey box padding is uneven, about 24 px above the text and about 45 px below it, so it looks like a stray empty line. Make the top and bottom padding match (common sense).
- With no section labels, the page only works because it's short. If more rules are added (dogs, foot passengers, holidays), add short subheads such as "Times", "Ice delays" and "Fares and vehicles".

**What's working.**
- There's one clear entry point. The headline is the only large, bold element, and it says exactly what the page covers.
- The copy is plain and human. "Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue" names one exact action, in a place the rider already knows. Keep that voice when you turn the times into numerals.
- The palette is quiet and the contrast is strong: near-black text on white and on a light grey. It suits a utility notice and is easy to read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column behaves on a phone. That matters here, since many riders will check this on the landing.
