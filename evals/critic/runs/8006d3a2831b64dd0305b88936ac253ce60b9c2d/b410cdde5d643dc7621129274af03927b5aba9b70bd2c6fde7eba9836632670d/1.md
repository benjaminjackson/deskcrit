**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the large dark-green headline, "Plan a week of dinners in ten minutes", then drops through two paragraphs and three feature lines to the green "Plan my first week" button. It reads straight away as a landing page for a meal-planning app, aimed at busy home cooks. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, if bare. The only change between the two is alignment: version 1 sets the hero column flush left, and version 2 centres it.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the top section of a consumer landing page, meant to get a stranger to understand the product and click "Plan my first week".

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The copy, scale and colour are the same in both, but version 2 centres paragraphs and a list that need a straight left edge, which makes them slower to read and gains nothing. Version 1's main remaining weakness is that it asserts everything and shows nothing: the empty right half is where proof of the product should go.

**What would move it most**
1. **Version 2's centring costs reading and buys nothing (convention). This is the difference between the versions.** Version 2 centres a 3-line and a 2-line paragraph of about 70 characters a line, so every line starts in a different place and the eye has to hunt for each start. The three feature lines ("Recipes tested in a home kitchen" …) stop reading as a list, because a list scans down its left edge and the centred one has none. The centre axis also has no link to the page frame: the logo and nav are pinned to the left and right edges, so the centred block floats between them. Version 1 lines the headline, paragraphs, list and button up with the "Weekplate" logo on one left edge, so the page reads top to bottom in one straight pass. Direction: keep version 1's setting. Set paragraphs of several lines flush left and keep centring for a few short lines (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.3). Strictly, the approved rule targets paragraphs of four or more lines, and these have three, so the cost here is moderate rather than severe. It is still pure cost, though, because nothing on the page shows a reason to centre.
2. **The page tells but never shows (defect, in both).** The right half of version 1, and the sides of version 2, are empty. The text promises seven dinners that share ingredients and a shopping list sorted by aisle, but the viewer has to take all of it on trust before clicking. Direction: put the result in that empty space, such as a sample week of seven dinners next to its aisle-sorted list. Viewers then judge the value against how they plan meals now, and version 1 already has the room for it.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline, both versions: "minutes" sits alone on the second line. Break it as "Plan a week of dinners / in ten minutes" so both lines carry weight and the promise ("in ten minutes") reads as one unit.
- Copy, both versions: "what you will not eat" and "any meal you do not fancy" are uncontracted and sound stiff. "Won't" and "don't" would sound like a person speaking (taste).
- Feature lines, both versions: they are set like body text, with no marker or extra weight, so they read as more paragraph rather than three quick reasons. This matters more in version 2, where they lose their left edge too.

**What's working.**
- A clear type scale: a big bold headline, one body size, and a strong button label, with no near-collisions between sizes. The headline owns the page.
- The ask is specific. "Plan my first week" names exactly what happens next, and it is the only filled button.
- The body copy lands on the viewer: "so less food goes to waste by Friday" and "sorted by the aisles of your usual shop" speak in the viewer's own terms.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover and focus states, and how the column reflows on a phone, where the alignment difference may disappear.
