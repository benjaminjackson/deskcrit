**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Trial to paid conversion by plan", then drops to the tallest purple bar, Growth at 4.0%, and then reads across the four value labels. Within about three seconds it reads as an internal analytics page for a SaaS team checking which plan converts best. It feels calm, competent and a bit generic. Nothing on it tells you what to think of the numbers.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a product or growth team uses this page to compare trial-to-paid conversion by plan for Q3 2025 and decide whether any plan needs attention.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, honest chart, and it gets the basics right. But it shows a gap of less than one percentage point as a clear ranking, with no sense of how firm the numbers are and nothing to compare them with. So the viewer can read the order of the bars but can't tell whether any of it is real or good.

**What would move it most**
1. **The differences look certain, and some of them aren't.** *Defect.* The four bars range from 3.1% to 4.0%, and the direct labels give each one equal authority. The table beside the chart shows how uneven the samples are: Enterprise is 38 paid out of 1,120 trials, while Starter is 286 out of 9,210. Using a standard 95% margin as a rough check, Enterprise could plausibly be anywhere from about 2.3% to 4.4%. That overlaps every other plan. Growth's lead over Starter probably holds up. Enterprise's place in the ranking doesn't. A viewer who acts on "Enterprise converts worse than Team" is acting on noise. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) asks for samples to be shown with ranges or a sample-size note. Fix: add error bars or a shaded range to each bar, or at least put "n = 1,120" under each plan label. If you keep the bars bare, soften the Enterprise label.
2. **The numbers have nothing to compare against.** *Convention.* "4.0%" alone doesn't tell the team whether that's good, bad or a change. There's no Q2 value, no target and no trend. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") flags metrics shown without a comparison. Fix: put the previous quarter next to each bar, as a ghost bar or a tick mark, or show the change in each label, e.g. "4.0% (+0.6 pp vs Q2)". Then the page answers "did it get better?", which is usually why someone opened the Conversion tab.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** *Taste.* "Trial to paid conversion by plan" describes the chart. It doesn't say what the chart shows, so every viewer has to reach their own conclusion, and given point 1 some will reach the wrong one. My view: once the uncertainty is visible, state the one finding the data supports, e.g. "Growth trials convert best; other plans' differences are within noise." The plain descriptive line can stay as the subtitle.

**Smaller things.**
- The subtitle ("became paid accounts within 30 days") and the Definition ("converted when the first invoice is paid… still open on 30 October") don't quite describe the same rule. A July trial that paid on day 45: does it count? Read as a stranger, you can't tell. Say once, in one sentence, which rule applies.
- The table gives Trials and Paid but no rate column, so anyone checking the chart against it has to do the division themselves. A Rate column, or an n under each bar label, would link the two.

**What's working.**
- The scale is honest: the bars start at 0% with even ticks and pale gridlines, so the gaps look as small as they are rather than blown up by a cropped axis. Keep that even if someone asks you to "make the differences pop."
- The labels sit on the data: values above each bar and plan names below, with no legend and one restrained purple. The page reads in seconds.
- The type is clear: a bold title, a grey subtitle, small grey supporting text, tabular figures right-aligned in the table, and the current nav tab marked by both weight and colour.
