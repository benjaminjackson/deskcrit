**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, drops to the subhead and the dark green button, and then crosses a wide gap to a small invoice card on the right. It reads as a calm, competent landing page for a freelancer invoicing tool. It's tidy and friendly, but it's the familiar SaaS layout, and nothing on it shows the product doing anything.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the homepage hero of an invoicing tool for freelancers and small studios. Its job is to get them to start a trial.

**Verdict.** It's well built and the words are good, but the hero asks you to take its main claim on trust. The only picture is a finished, static invoice that proves nothing about speed, auto-fill or chasing, so the page looks like every other invoicing tool.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invoice card doesn't back up the headline (defect).** The headline promises speed. The subhead promises that Tallyfold fills in clients, rates and tax and then chases late payments. The card on the right (about 340×200 px, floating in the right half) is just a completed invoice. It repeats the word "invoice" and shows none of the three things that make this product different, so the viewer has to believe "time it takes to pour coffee" with no evidence. The fix: make the picture show the claim. For example, show the invoice partly filled with the client and rates auto-completed, or the "Total due" next to a scheduled friendly reminder. Make it big enough to share the weight with the headline instead of sitting in a void.
2. **The feature cards are mostly empty (defect, unless something didn't load).** In the three cards across the bottom, the title and one or two lines sit in the top third, with about 130 px of blank white under each. The boxes look unfinished, the empty space reads as left over rather than planned, and the eye has nothing to stop on. If illustrations are supposed to go there, check in a live browser that they load. If not, shrink the cards to fit their text, or give each one a small proof: a sample reminder line, the two-tap pay sheet, the export file.
3. **The button asks the viewer to try the product, not to test the claim (taste/copy).** "Start free for 14 days" is clear but generic. The headline sets up a comparison the viewer can run against their own life, so let the button take that up, e.g. "Send your first invoice free". That way they judge the coffee claim against their current tool instead of just signing up for a trial period. If no card is needed, say so under the button.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line breaks: "in the / time" splits the phrase and leaves "coffee." alone as a runt. Rebreak it as "Send an invoice / in the time it takes / to pour coffee."
- Hero gap: the roughly 270 px of dead space between the text column and the invoice card should go once the visual grows (finding 1).

**What's working.**
- The copy is specific and human. "Tallyfold fills in your clients, rates, and tax, then chases late payments for you", "Reminders that sound human" and "Clients pay from the email with two taps" each say who does what. Keep that voice.
- The craft is careful. The text in the nav, hero and cards all starts on one 48 px left edge, the invoice card's right edge lines up with "Sign in", and the invoice amounts are right-aligned and add up ($640 + $45 + 8% tax = $739.80). Details like this build trust for a money product.
- The type scale is clean: a heavy display headline, one subhead size, one card-title size and one body size, with weight doing the finer distinctions.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button and nav, and how the hero reflows on mobile. Check whether the invoice card drops below the button or disappears.
