**What would move it most**
1. **The opening screen has no picture, though the byline promises photographs.** The whole first screen is text, and the byline says "Photographs by the residents". The story is about a physical place: pallets, bath tubs, a queue at a water tap. The reader is asked to commit to the text before seeing any of it, and the credit raises an expectation the screen doesn't meet. In a feature, the lead image and the headline should make one idea together. Here the headline is doing all the work alone. There are two possible readings. Either the photos sit further down, so the credit is a promise the first screen doesn't keep. Or an image failed to load, in which case it's a bug. If it's the design, put one resident's photo near the headline (the old bath tubs in use would land the "nothing built that could not be moved" line before the reader even reaches it). If it's a load failure, it doesn't apply. *Taste, argued: the "Visual and headline work together" entry has no approved source, but for a place-led feature, losing the place on the first screen is the biggest missed chance on the page.*
2. **The headline ends on one short word: "line".** "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" leaves a single short word on the second line. It also splits "bus line", the phrase that carries the image, and the eye catches it on an otherwise tidy page. Rebreak it at a phrase boundary ("The allotment at the end / of the bus line"), or narrow or widen the heading box so both lines carry weight. *Convention: Bringhurst, The Elements of Typographic Style §2.4.2 on short last-line words. Applying it to headline breaks is common sense.*
