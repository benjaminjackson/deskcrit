**First read.** The eye lands on the red-orange-yellow blaze in the middle of the grid, then drifts to the big "3,412" on the right. Right away it reads as an internal support dashboard: tickets bunch up in weekday working hours and drop off at night and at weekends. It feels tidy and competent, but loud. The rainbow makes a calm staffing report look like an alarm.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a reports screen that lets a support lead see when tickets arrive so they can plan staff cover by hour and day.

**Verdict.** The layout, labelling and side stats are well made. But the colour scale works against the data, and the one number a manager would plan around ("Busiest hour, weekdays 10:00") looks like it disagrees with the grid next to it. Those two problems decide whether the screen can be trusted.

**What would move it most**
1. **The "busiest hour" figure doesn't match the chart** (defect). On every weekday the 11:00 column is as hot as or hotter than the 10:00 column: Monday and Friday are red (30+) at 11:00 and only orange at 10:00, and Tuesday to Thursday are orange in both. The sidebar still says the busiest hour is 10:00. A manager building a rota will either staff for the wrong hour or stop trusting the page. Possible causes are an off-by-one in how hours are bucketed, the label marking where an hour bin starts versus its middle, or binning that hides the real ranking. Check the data. Then make the stat and the grid agree, and state what the hour means ("10:00–10:59").
2. **A rainbow scale on ordered data** (convention: Wilke, Few; Tufte on graphical integrity). The bins run in order from 0–4 to 30+, but the colours run purple-blue → cyan → teal → green → yellow → orange → red. That order isn't visible on its own: you can't tell that green (15–19) is more than teal without checking the legend. Yellow is the lightest colour, so in greyscale the busiest band looks lighter than the quiet one, and the value structure breaks. The quietest bin (0–4) is a deep, saturated violet-blue that fills about 60% of the grid, so the empty night hours are the heaviest-looking area on the page. Red, amber and green also read as status (bad, warning, fine), which these counts aren't. Use one hue in lightness steps (pale for 0–4, dark for 30+), or a sequential palette such as viridis. The dead hours then fade back and the peak stands out by itself.
3. **Colour is the only way to read a value, and some neighbouring bins are hard to tell apart** (defect for colour-blind viewers). The green 15–19, yellow 20–24 and orange 25–29 bins sit next to each other in the grid, and for a red-green colour-blind lead they merge. The cells have no numbers, so nothing else gives the value. A lightness ramp fixes most of this. Also put the count in each cell, or at least in the peak cells, or show it on hover. The cells are big enough to hold two digits.
4. **The title names the topic, not the finding** (taste, backed by common dataviz practice). "When new tickets arrive" makes the viewer work out the answer for themselves. The chart has a clear story, so say it: something like "Tickets peak late morning on weekdays; weekends stay under 15 an hour." This also forces item 1 to be checked.

**Smaller things.**
- The grey subtitle, source line and legend numbers look close to 4.5:1. Measure them, and darken them a step if they fall short.
- The source note is useful, but it sits 30 px under the legend in the same grey as the subtitle. It's fine where it is; just make sure it survives the colour change.

**What's working.**
- The subtitle and source line answer the questions a sceptical reader would ask: which queues, which dates, what time zone, and opened versus answered. Keep that care.
- The side stats each come with a comparison ("Up 4% on August", "Down 6 min on August"), so every number has a reference point.
- The grid itself: square cells, white gutters, hour labels every three hours and short day labels. It's spare, with almost no ink that isn't data, and the right form for day × hour data.
