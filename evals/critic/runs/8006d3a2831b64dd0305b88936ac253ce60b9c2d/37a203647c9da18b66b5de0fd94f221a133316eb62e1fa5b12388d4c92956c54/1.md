**First read.** My eye goes straight to the bold headline, "How the river ferry runs in winter". Then it reads down two paragraphs and a pale grey box. In about three seconds it reads as a plain, calm notice for people who ride the ferry. But the whole thing is pressed against the left edge of the frame, with nothing to its left. The first impression is that the layout is broken or the page didn't load properly.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or help article telling local riders when the winter ferry runs and what it costs to cross. It was not stated.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job: the headline is clear and the text is easy to read. Two things hold it back. Everything sits right against the left edge, which makes the page look broken. And the timetable is written as prose that a rider can read two ways, on exactly the facts they'll plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable can be read two ways and is hard to scan (defect).** "The first crossing is at seven in the morning and the last at six in the evening": is six the last departure from the north landing, or the last boat back? The text only gives departures "from the north landing every forty minutes", so someone on the south bank has no times at all. If they guess wrong, they're stranded on the far bank at dusk in winter. The times are also spelled out in words inside sentences, so you have to read them rather than glance at them. To fix it, say which landing each time is for, give the last return crossing, and set the times as numbers (07:00, 18:00, every 40 min) in a short list or table someone can check on the walk to the landing.
2. **The content touches the left edge of the screen (convention, and it costs something here).** The headline, both paragraphs and the grey box all start at x=0 with no margin. That makes the page look like a broken layout, not a designed one, and the box's left side presses against the frame. To fix it, put the column inside a proper text area with a clear margin on the left, ideally a centred or properly inset reading column (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "Margin proportions").
3. **Lines are too long (convention).** The paragraphs run about 95–100 characters per line across an ~880 px column. That's well past the comfortable 45–75 range, so the eye has to work to find the start of the next line, even with only two lines per paragraph. To fix it, narrow the column to about 600–680 px at this text size (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2).
4. **The fares box gives no figures (defect, copy).** "Cars pay the standard fare" doesn't say what the standard fare is, and "Bicycles ride free on weekdays" only hints that bikes pay at weekends without saying how much. This is the box meant to stand out, and it sends readers off to look for numbers elsewhere. To fix it, state the car fare and the weekend bike fare, or link to where they're listed.

**Smaller things.**
- The grey box has uneven padding: about 24 px above the text and about 48 px below it (probably a paragraph margin left inside the box). The box looks bottom-heavy and unfinished. Make the padding even on all four sides. This is common sense; the checklist names no source.

**What's working.**
- There's one clear headline in a heavy weight and two type sizes with a big step between them. The order of reading is obvious and the scale doesn't need more levels.
- The ice warning ends with an action the reader can take: "Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue." It tells the rider exactly what to do, where, and when. Keep that pattern.
- Putting vehicle rules in their own tinted box is the right instinct. It keeps them apart from the timetable. It just needs the numbers.
