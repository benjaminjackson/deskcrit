**First read.** The eye goes to "Changing your pickup time", then runs down the four bold headings. Within two seconds it reads as the help or FAQ section of a bakery's pre-order page. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. Then the eye drops to the last line, "Design review: failed, the headings are impossible to pick out." It is set in the same style as the answers, so it reads as part of the page, and it contradicts what you have just seen.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a customer help section on a bakery's order-and-collect site. It should let people find their question by scanning, then act on the answer without having to ask.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job. The headings are easy to pick out, so the review note at the bottom is wrong, and it is stray text that must not ship. The biggest thing in the way is the wording: the two answers with a deadline or a condition can each be read two ways. Those are the lines customers will hold the bakery to.

**What would move it most**
1. **Remove the "Design review: failed…" line.** It sits at the bottom of the column, about 75 px below the last answer, in the body style. A customer would read it as content: an odd remark about the page they are on, which makes the bakery look careless. Nothing on the page supports what it says. The headings are bold, about 30 px against body text of about 18 px, and darker. Defect (common sense: unfinished copy and an element that does no job). Delete it, and keep review notes out of the build.
2. **The deadline and the pickup condition are ambiguous.** These are defects, because customers act on these lines.
   - *"Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead."* A day ahead of what? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time on the day before. A customer who orders for 9am and cancels at 8pm the night before is fine under the first reading and refused under the second. State the cut-off as a time, for example "up to 24 hours before your pickup time". End on what it means for them: "after that, you can't cancel, because we've already baked your bread."
   - *"They will need to show it at the counter along with a name."* Whose name, yours or theirs? If the person collecting gives their own name and staff expect yours, they leave without the order. Say "your name" or "their name".
3. **Two answers leave the customer to fill a gap.** These are defects, but smaller ones.
   - "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact. Add what happens to the customer after that: is the order given away, and is it refunded?
   - "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps someone in the shop, not someone ordering online. Someone with an allergy can't check before they pay. Either point to the ingredients on the product page, or say plainly that ingredients are only listed in the shop.
   - "Open your order" doesn't say where. Name the place, for example the order confirmation email or the account page.

**Smaller things.**
- Single-line answers, such as the "Collecting for someone else" and "Cancelling an order" answers, run about 95 to 100 characters, wider than Bringhurst's 45 to 75. Narrow the text column to about 640 px.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the Allergies answer. Rewording will fix it, and so will the narrower column.

**What's working.**
- **The two-level type scale.** Bold headings at about 30 px, body text at about 18 px, and nothing in between. Each heading differs from its answer in size, weight and darkness at once (Dair's contrast), so scanning works. Keep it this simple.
- **The spacing.** Each heading sits about 8 px above its answer, and the sections are about 30 px apart. Each question clearly owns its answer.
- **The headings say exactly what is in each section** and use the customer's own words: "Collecting for someone else", not "Proxy pickup".
