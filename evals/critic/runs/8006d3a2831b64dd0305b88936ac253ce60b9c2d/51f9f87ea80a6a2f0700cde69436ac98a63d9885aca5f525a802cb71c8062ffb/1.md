**First read.** The eye lands on "Account settings", then drops down four identical white cards, each with a bold label, a grey line and a blue outlined button on the far right. Then it goes to the left nav, where **Billing** is highlighted, and that's where it trips: the page claims to be Billing, but the content is an overview of everything. It feels calm, tidy and competent, and somewhat generic.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a settings hub in a B2B SaaS app (Tallis Cloud) where a workspace member finds and jumps into the setting they came to change.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and well built, but the nav and the content tell two different stories. The highlight says you're on Billing, the heading says you're on an overview, and the cards and nav list different sections. The user can't trust the map, and in a settings area the map is what the page is for.

**What would move it most**
1. **The nav marks the wrong place (defect).** In the left nav, "Billing" has the selected tint and bold weight. The page is titled "Account settings" and shows Profile, Notifications, Billing and Sessions as equal cards. A user who clicked Billing expects billing detail and gets a menu. A user who arrived another way sees Billing marked as current and wonders whether they're in the right place. Either way they have to stop and work out where they are (Nielsen, heuristic 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist", guideline 5). Fix: make the marker and the heading agree. Either this is an overview with its own nav entry (or nothing highlighted), or it is the Billing page and shows billing content under a "Billing" heading. If the capture just caught the wrong state, check that first.
2. **Nav and cards list different sections (defect).** The cards include "Sessions", which has no nav entry. It most likely lives under "Security", but nothing says so. "Members", "Integrations" and "Security" are in the nav but have no card. A first-time user can't build a correct model of where things live. Someone hunting for active sessions scans the nav, doesn't find it, and has to guess (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). Fix: use one set of names and one order in both places. Either give every nav section a card, or present the cards as shortcuts ("Recent devices · in Security") so the link to the nav is visible.
3. **The billing line leaves the viewer to fill in the details (defect, copy).** "Your plan renews on 1 March. The invoice goes to the address on file." This is the line with money attached, and it names neither the plan nor the amount. It refers to an address the user has to remember or go and find. It also doesn't say whether "1 March" means this coming March, which matters near year end. And the only action offered is "View invoices", which does nothing about the two facts the line raises. Fix: say it to the user in full, e.g. "Your Team plan renews on 1 March 2027 for £240. We'll email the invoice to billing@…", and add "Change plan" or "Edit billing details" next to the invoice link (Nielsen, heuristic 6).

**Smaller things.**
- The cards are tall for one line of content each. The empty space inside them is bigger than the 16 px gap between them, so the page reads as four separate slabs rather than one list. This is taste: tighter rows with dividers in a single panel would read faster and make room for the missing sections.
- Button labels are a mix of specific and generic. "View invoices" says what happens. "Manage" and "Review" don't, though the description lines beside them carry most of it.

**What's working.**
- The alignment is disciplined. The logo and nav highlight share one left edge, the cards and the "Workspace" label share one right edge, and the content column starts on a single line. Keep this grid when adding rows.
- Most of the description copy speaks to the user and tells them why it matters: "Your name and photo appear on comments and in the member list", "You are signed in on three now." That's the voice the billing line should match.
- The four actions have equal weight, all in the same outlined style. That's the right choice for an overview where none of them is more important than the others.
