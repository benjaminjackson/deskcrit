**First read.** The eye goes to "Low-light houseplants" at top left, then drops straight to the row of four buttons in teal, purple, orange and blue, which are the loudest thing on the page. It reads as a small plant shop's category page: tidy, calm and a little generic. Then the buttons make you wonder whether the colours mean something. They don't.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a desktop category page for a plant shop. It's for someone with a dim room who wants to pick one plant and add it to the cart.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the headline is better than most. Two things stop it from selling. The four different button colours suggest a meaning that isn't there and break up an otherwise calm page. And the drawings don't show what you're buying.

**What would move it most**
1. **Four colours for one action (defect).** The Add to cart buttons in the bottom row of the cards are teal, purple, orange and blue. They do the same thing, so a shopper looks for a reason: is the orange one a sale, is the blue one different? There is no reason, and the question slows the choice. The orange button on the cast iron plant also comes forward more than the others, so that plant looks promoted when it isn't. The purple and blue don't belong with the green and terracotta of the plants either, which makes the page look pieced together. One kind of element should follow one rule (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*). On accent colours, Apple HIG and Material say to keep one accent for the main action. Fix: give all four buttons one colour. A deep leaf green taken from the illustrations would tie the actions to the brand.
2. **The pictures don't show the product (defect).** All four cards use the same pot-and-leaves drawing with the leaves rearranged. A snake plant, a ZZ plant, a cast iron plant and a pothos look very different. The pothos is sold as trailing to 6 ft but is drawn upright. Someone choosing a plant for a dim corner is choosing partly on looks, and here they can't, so they have to leave the page to find out. If these are placeholders for photos, the page isn't finished. If they're the final art, they need to show each plant's real shape: a tall spiky snake plant, glossy ZZ stems, a trailing pothos.
3. **"6 in pot" can be read two ways (defect, copy).** In the line under each name, "6 in pot" can mean a six-inch pot or six plants in one pot. This line sits right above a price, where the shopper is working out what they get for their money, so a wrong reading leads to a disappointed customer. Fix: write "6-inch pot · grows to 2 ft tall" so the line reads as a size.

**Smaller things.**
- The plant name (about 18 px bold) and the price (about 20 px bold) are almost the same size, so they look like a mistake rather than two levels. Make the price clearly different by size or weight, or set the name in medium weight.
- Nothing in the top navigation shows that you're in Houseplants. Mark the current section with weight or an underline (Nielsen 1, visibility of system status).

**What's working.**
- The subtitle "Four plants that do well more than six feet from a window" is excellent copy. It describes the shopper's own room in a way they can check, rather than using a vague label like "low light". Keep it, and copy its approach elsewhere.
- The grid is clean: four equal cards with equal gutters, and every text block aligned to the image edge. Each card reads in a clear order of name, specs, price, action.
- The muted grey-green tint behind each image and the terracotta pots set a quiet, natural mood. Fixing finding 1 would strengthen it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the buttons and nav links, and how the four-column row reflows on a phone.
