**First read.** The eye goes to the three blue bars first, then up to the title "Rainfall at the reservoir," then across to "Getting there." The chart has no numbers, labels, or dates, so after a few seconds you know rainfall is the topic and nothing else. Beside it, bus times and parking rules read like an explanation of the chart, but they aren't. It feels like a wireframe that was never filled in: clean and calm, but with nothing to read.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a visitor information page or panel for a reservoir. It is meant to show recent rainfall and tell people how to get there.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The only picture on the page is a chart with no values, categories, units, or dates, so nobody can learn anything about rainfall from it. Unrelated travel directions sit right beside it, so the page has no single message.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart says nothing (defect).** The three bars in the tinted panel have no value axis, no category labels, no units, and no time period. A viewer can see that the middle bar is tallest and that's all: not whether it's 20 mm or 200 mm, not whether the bars are months, years, or sites, and not whether this is a lot of rain. With no value axis you also can't check that the bars start at zero. Every chart needs labelled axes or direct labels, with units and a date (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). Fix: name each bar under its base (for example "Sep / Oct / Nov 2026"), put the value with its unit on or above each bar, and say what the period is. Then make the title state the finding, not just the topic ("November was the driest month since…"). That last point is my judgment, not a published rule: a visitor glancing at a panel takes away a sentence, not a bar height. If three bars are all the data there is, also consider whether three numbers in a sentence would say it better.
2. **The directions read as the chart's caption (defect).** "Getting there" sits on the chart's top line in the next column, at the same height. Grouping by position makes the two look like one unit (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II), so a reader looks to the text to explain the bars and finds bus times. The page title makes it worse because it claims the whole page for rainfall. Fix: decide which job leads. If the page is about the visit, give it a title that covers both and set the directions apart as their own section, with clear space or a rule between them, not sitting where a caption would go. If rainfall is the point, move the directions somewhere else.
3. **The frame competes with the data (convention).** The dark grey border, about 3 px, is the heaviest line on the page apart from the bars, and the pale blue tint inside it carries no information. Together they make the chart read as a boxed widget rather than a statement. Fix: remove the frame and tint so the bars are the darkest marks in the chart, and keep only a thin, pale baseline (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 23.2; Tufte, ch. 5).

**Smaller things.**
- The lower 40% of the frame and a strip on the right are empty. That space is left over rather than planned, so the page sits top-heavy. Once the chart has labels and a takeaway it will need more height, so let the content claim the space, or crop the frame to fit (Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*, "Contrasts"; Arnheim, ch. I).

**What's working.**
- The type is disciplined. There are three clear levels: a bold title of about 32 px, a bold section heading, and regular body text, all in one sans family. Every difference is carried by weight as well as size, which is a scale worth keeping.
- The directions are good copy. "The number twelve bus stops at the east gate every twenty minutes" names who does what, where, and how often, with no gaps for the reader to fill.
- There is one series in one strong blue on a neutral page, which is the right restraint for a chart this simple.

**Checklist.**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 5 yes, 3 no (purpose clear in one sentence; every element does a job; headings name their section)
- What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 10 yes, 3 no (only related elements look alike; white space gathered; balance)
- Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
- Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
- Pictures: skipped, no pictures besides the chart
- Charts and data: 16 lines, 10 yes, 6 no (data-ink; honest scales; enough time range shown; direct labels; axis values, units and labels; title states the finding)
- Interface: skipped, no controls
