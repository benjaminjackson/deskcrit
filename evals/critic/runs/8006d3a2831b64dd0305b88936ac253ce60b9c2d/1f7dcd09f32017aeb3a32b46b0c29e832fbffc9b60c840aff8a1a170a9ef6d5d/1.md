**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month", and then straight to the June bar on the right, which is almost twice as tall as January and much the tallest thing on the page. Next it goes to the big $4.82M on the right. It reads as a calm, trustworthy member report from a credit union, and it is well made. The problem is that the strongest thing in the chart is something the page only explains in the footnote.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in the credit union's public report that tells ordinary members how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026, so they read the result correctly and trust it.

**Verdict.** It's clean and mostly honest, but it leads members to the wrong conclusion. The June bar, the bar that jumps out, is mostly a one-off bonus payroll explained only in grey fine print. The chart also has nothing from last year, so "rose every month" looks like growth when it may just be the normal pattern of the year.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June bar has no explanation where people look.** **Defect.** June ($1150k) is 32% above May, while every other month rises by $40–80k. The only explanation is the last clause of the source line under the chart panel: "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll." Members see deposits speeding up, and most will never read the footnote. The headline makes it worse, because "rose every month" invites them to read June as part of the trend. Fix: put the explanation on the chart at the June bar, for example a short note beside it saying it includes the mid-year bonus payroll. Better still, split the June bar into regular deposits and bonus payroll in a lighter shade of the same blue, so the real trend shows in the bars themselves. (Highlighting the one point the reader must notice follows Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design". Annotating it is common sense.)
2. **The chart has nothing from last year, so the headline's claim can't be checked.** **Defect** (the source behind this entry is off the approved list, so this is my own argument). The bars nearly double from January to June. But the card on the right says the whole half-year is up only 6.1% on 2025. Those two facts fit together only if deposits climb every January to June anyway. So "rose every month" may describe the calendar, not anything the credit union did. A member who sees only the chart thinks deposits nearly doubled. Fix: add January to June 2025 as pale grey bars or a thin grey line behind the 2026 bars. Then the honest story (each month a bit above last year, about 6% overall) is visible. Rewrite the headline around that story, e.g. "Members saved 6% more than in the first half of 2025."

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Deposits into savings accounts, US dollars per month" repeats the line under the headline. Cut one, or let the chart title carry the unit only.
- Copy: the chart says "$1150k" and the card says "$4.82M" for the same kind of number. Use one style ($1.15M), so members don't have to convert between them.
- Copy: "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025" are clipped. Some readers will read "on" as "in". "Up 6.1% compared with Jan to Jun 2025" says it plainly.
- The total card compares the half-year, while the other two cards compare June only, and June includes the bonus. Say that on the cards, or compare all three over the same period.

**What's working.**
- The bar chart is honest where it counts: the axis starts at zero, there is one blue for one series, gridlines are pale, and every bar has its value written on it. The numbers check: the six bars add to $4.82M, and $214 × 5,374 accounts is about the $1.15M for June. Keep that rigour.
- Every card gives the number, its period, and a comparison with last year, in a clear order: label, figure, change. That is exactly what makes a figure mean something (Few).
- The type has three clear levels: serif headline, sans text, small tracked capitals above the headline. The hierarchy is calm and fits a credit union.
