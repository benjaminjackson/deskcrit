**First read.** The eye goes first to the wall of brush script, not the heading. The script is so heavy and loopy that it reads as one dark texture, and you have to switch into slow word-by-word reading to get anything out of it. Only then do you notice the plain bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers". It feels like a party invitation pasted under a transit notice.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web info page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and the rules for bikes and winter service, so they can plan a crossing quickly.

**Verdict.** This doesn't do its job. Practical information that people skim for times and rules is set entirely in a decorative brush script, which slows every sentence and fights the heading. Everything else about the page is fine, and the face is the single thing in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in a script face** (all three paragraphs, top left to mid page). Reading it costs effort the whole way through. Word shapes blur together because the letters join and loop: "twelve minutes", "forty minutes" and "lock them to the rail" all have to be worked out rather than glanced at. Someone checking whether they'll make the boat gives up or misreads. This breaks a published rule (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1: text faces don't carry ornament), and here breaking it costs the viewer: **defect**. The same choice causes three other problems:
   - **Two faces in conflict.** The heading is a neutral geometric sans and the body is a casual brush script. They differ in mood and era, not by deliberate contrast, so the page reads as two pieces stuck together.
   - **Wrong mood.** Festive handwriting on ferry rules signals "event" rather than "public service".
   - **Weak hierarchy.** The script is about as dark and heavy as the bold heading and looks only slightly smaller, so the heading barely wins the first glance.

   Fix: set the paragraphs in a plain text face. The sans already used for the heading, at regular weight, would close all four problems at once. If the piece wants warmth, keep a script for a few words of display at most (Bringhurst, 6.2.1).

2. **The copy leaves out the numbers passengers will act on** (paragraphs 1 and 3). "Between early morning and late evening", "a little longer", "one boat each hour" and "before dusk" give no actual times. In winter, "the last crossing leaves before dusk" can be read two ways: just before dusk, or well before it. Read the wrong way, someone gets stranded on the wrong bank. The page then sends people to "printed timetables… in the shelter", so the information is only available once you're already standing at the landing. **Defect.** Fix: state the first and last departures for summer and winter, and put the timetable (or a link to it) on this page. Then the ferry notes answer "can I get back tonight?" without a trip to the quay.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing separately. Once the face changes, the crowded look between the script's lines will go with it.

**What's working.**
- The structure is right. There's one heading, then three short paragraphs that each hold one topic (schedule, bikes, winter), separated by space alone. That chunking is exactly what skimmers need, so keep it.
- The measure is sensible, at about 70–75 characters per line, and the column is flush left with a calm rag and generous margins. Keep the column width when you swap the face.
- The heading says plainly what the page is.
