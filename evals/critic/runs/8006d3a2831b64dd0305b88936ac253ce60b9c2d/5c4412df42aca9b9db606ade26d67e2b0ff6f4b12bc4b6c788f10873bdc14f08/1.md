**First read.** My eye lands on the word "Pricing", then moves straight to the three prices: $9, $29, $79. All three cards are the same size, weight and colour, so it sweeps across them evenly and stops at the last feature line in each. There's nothing to click there, so the eye drifts back up to "Start free" in the top-right corner. It reads as a clean, competent and generic SaaS pricing page for a scheduling tool. It feels calm, but it's a dead end.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a pricing page for a calendar or booking product. Its job is to help a small-business or team buyer choose a plan and start on it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. You can read the prices, but the page never helps you pick one and gives you nowhere to act on a choice: no plan has a button, no plan is recommended, and the plans don't explain how they differ. It's a price list, not a pricing page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plans have no buttons, so choosing one leads nowhere.** All three cards end on a feature line and then empty space. The only action on the page is "Start free" in the header, and it isn't tied to any plan. A visitor who has decided "Team" can't act on that, and can't tell which plan "Start free" starts. Is it a trial of Solo? Of Business? A free tier that isn't listed? This is a **defect**: the control isn't next to the thing it acts on (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, natural mapping). It also breaks the **convention** buyers expect from every comparable pricing page, where each plan carries its own button. Fix: put a button at the foot of each card that names the plan and what happens ("Start Team free for 14 days"). Make the header button say the same thing, or point it at the recommended plan.
2. **No plan stands out, so the visitor has to do all the deciding.** The three cards are identical in size, border, weight and colour. The only thing that differs is the number. The eye has nowhere to settle and the page offers no default. This is a **defect** against "one element dominates" (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Hofmann, *Graphic Design Manual*). Three equal elements compete, and the one the business most wants chosen gets no help. Fix: make one plan clearly the first thing you see, probably Team since the page talks about teams. Give it the brand blue border or fill, a "Most popular" or "Best for teams of 2–10" label, and the only solid button. Set the other two back with outlined buttons.
3. **The copy leaves the viewer to fill the gaps on the things they'll hold you to.** Each card lists exactly three features, and they're different features, so you can't tell whether Team includes the Booking page and Email reminders, or whether Business includes Reports. Read literally, moving up a plan loses features. "/ month" doesn't say whether that's per user or per account, which turns $29 into anywhere from $29 to $290 for a ten-person team. "1 calendar" and "Up to 10 calendars" don't say what a calendar is (a person? a resource?). The subtitle, "Simple plans for teams of every size. Change or cancel whenever you like.", could sit on anyone's pricing page and tells the viewer nothing about Tidewell. That's a **defect**, because prices and limits are what buyers commit to. Fix: start the Team and Business lists with "Everything in Solo, plus" (and "Everything in Team, plus"). State the billing unit ("per month, whole team" or "per user / month"). Say what a calendar means in the viewer's terms ("1 person's calendar"). Then make the subtitle say something only Tidewell can, ideally one that compares against what the viewer uses now.

**Smaller things.**
- Lower third of the screen (about y 570–800): the page ends in a large empty band, so it looks top-heavy and unfinished. A per-card button, an FAQ, or a plan comparison table would fill that space with something useful.
- Subtitle under the H1: the second line is just "you like.", a runt (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 2.4.2). Widen the text box slightly or rebalance the break.
- H1 "Pricing": this is a label that repeats the active tab rather than a heading that makes a claim. Taste: a line that states the offer would earn the biggest type on the page.

**What's working.**
- The prices are clearly the second level: bold, large, with "/ month" set small and grey beside them. Keep that relationship when you add buttons, so buttons don't overpower the figures.
- The type scale is tight and disciplined: about five sizes (H1, subtitle, plan name, price, body), each clearly distinct, all in one sans family.
- The palette is restrained: neutrals plus one blue used only for the brand and the primary action. That leaves blue free to mark the recommended plan.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus and hover states on the nav and button. How the three cards stack on a narrow screen.
