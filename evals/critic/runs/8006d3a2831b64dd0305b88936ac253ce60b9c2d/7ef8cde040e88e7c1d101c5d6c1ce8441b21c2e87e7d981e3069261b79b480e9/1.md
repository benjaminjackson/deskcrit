It reads as a small, quiet web page for a community boat shed or sailing club, and it feels warm and unhurried. That calm tone is earned. But the right column, the only part that asks anything of the reader, is set smaller and reads like an afterthought.

**What it's for.** You didn't tell me, so I'm assuming this is a page for a volunteer-run boat shed. It sets the scene and is meant to bring new people along on Thursdays.

**Verdict.** It looks competent but it doesn't do the job yet. The invitation to volunteer is the smaller, quieter paragraph, and it doesn't tell a newcomer where to go, what time to arrive, or how to say they're coming. The mood works. The ask doesn't.

**What would move it most**
1. **The ask is the weakest thing on the page, by size and by content (defect).** The right column holds the only call to action: "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings…" It is set in what looks like a body size of about 17 px, against about 22 px for the scene-setting paragraph on the left. So the page tells the reader the description matters more than the invitation. The wording is warm, and "Nobody needs experience" removes the biggest worry early. But a stranger who wants to come is still left asking several things:
   - Which Thursday, and is it every week?
   - What time does it start?
   - Where is "the end of the lane", and which lane?
   - Do I just turn up, or do I tell someone first?

   "Thursday evenings" is a time the reader will act on, and it can be read two ways: "come whenever after work" or "there's a start time you'll miss". Someone who guesses wrong arrives to a locked shed or halfway through a session. Fix: give the invitation at least as much weight as the description. Add one line in the reader's own terms, for example "Come along any Thursday from 6:30 pm, [address]. Just turn up, or drop us a line at …". The kettle and the spare apron are good. Keep them as the closing reassurance.
2. **Two body sizes almost the same (convention, Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style* 3.1.1: "Don't compose without a scale").** The two paragraphs are the same face, the same weight and the same role, but about 22 px and 17 px. Seen side by side, the difference looks like an accident rather than a second level. It doesn't read as body text plus caption, just as two body texts that don't match. I count three sizes: headline, large body and small body. The last two collide. Fix: set both columns at one body size (about 18–20 px suits these line lengths). If the volunteer note should stand out, use weight, a short bold lead-in, or a rule or tint, not a size a few points off. That is the Refactoring UI point: make it bolder, not bigger.

**Smaller things.**
- **Baselines drift across the columns** (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*). The line spacing differs, roughly 34 px on the left and 28 px on the right, so the lines stop lining up after the first one. One body size and one line height fixes this as well.

**What's working.**
- **The headline sets the tone.** A heavy serif in near-black, clearly dominant, and its tracking and kerning look tidy. "Notes from" frames the page as a personal dispatch rather than a brochure. Keep that voice.
- **The left paragraph is specific and sensory.** The tide under the slipway, three clinker dinghies on trestles, the smell of tar and cut cedar. This shed could be no one else's, which is the opposite of template copy. The line length (about 50 characters) and line spacing are comfortable.
- **Restraint.** One family, black on white, a plain two-column grid and generous margins. Nothing is ornamental, and it suits a hand-made, volunteer subject.
