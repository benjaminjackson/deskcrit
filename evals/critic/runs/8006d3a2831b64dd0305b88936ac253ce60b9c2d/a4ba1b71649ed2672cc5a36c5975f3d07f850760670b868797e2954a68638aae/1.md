**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Shipments peak each third quarter", then drops to the tallest bar, Q3 2026 at 705. Next it notices the chunky 3D extrusions, which make the chart look like an old spreadsheet default. It reads as a board-deck slide from a sales review: tidy and calm, but the chart looks dated. What it doesn't show at all is that the last bar is a guess.

**What it's for.** As the header says, it's one slide in a 12-page sales review for the October board meeting. Its job is to show directors the seasonal pattern in units shipped, and where Q4 is heading.

**Verdict.** The slide is well organised and the headline states the finding, but the chart misleads on the one bar the board will act on. Q4 2026 is a forecast, and it's drawn exactly like seven quarters of real shipments. The 3D styling then makes every bar harder to read. Draw the chart flat and make the forecast look like a forecast, and this becomes a good slide.

**What would move it most**
1. **The Q4 2026 forecast looks like an actual result.** *Defect.* The rightmost bar (560) has the same solid blue, the same label weight and the same 3D treatment as every recorded quarter. The only warning is in parentheses at the end of the subtitle and in the source line, and a director glancing at the chart will read 560 as fact. That number is the one the board will plan against and later hold the team to, so if Q4 lands at 500, nobody will remember it was only a projection. A forecast has to look different from observed data, and ideally show a range (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Fix: draw Q4 2026 hollow, hatched or in a lighter tint, and label it at the bar ("Forecast: 560"). If the September forecast has a low and high case, add a range mark.
2. **The 3D extrusion makes values harder to read and adds nothing.** *Convention, and it costs something here.* Every bar has a lit top face and a dark side, about 15 px deep. The eye has to decide whether a bar ends at the front edge or the back edge, and the back edge sits higher than the gridline it should match. That's ink spent on a depth dimension with no data in it. It also gives a 2026 board deck the look of a 2005 spreadsheet, which costs some credibility. Fix (approved): draw the chart flat, with no depth, sides or tilt, so each value is read from one length (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*).
3. **The chart doesn't make the headline's comparison, and it misses the bigger story.** *Taste.* The headline says Q3 is the peak, but to check it the eye has to jump four bars from Q3 2025 to Q3 2026 with nothing between the two years to guide it. The thing a board cares about more goes unsaid: every 2026 quarter so far is above the same quarter of 2025 (455 vs 410, 590 vs 520, 705 vs 610), and Q3 is up about 16%. "Peak each third quarter" also rests on just two cycles. It's a fact about how the numbers move, and it never tells the board what that means for them. Fix: either put the two years side by side by quarter (paired bars, or a gap and a year label between Q4 2025 and Q1 2026), or rewrite the headline so it tells the board the meaning. For example, "Every quarter of 2026 beat 2025; Q3 peaked at 705k", with the seasonal point in the subtitle.

**Smaller things.**
- About 130 px of the slide sits empty below the source line, so the content looks pushed up and top-heavy. Make the chart taller to use that space, or centre the block vertically.

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding in words instead of naming the chart ("Units shipped by quarter" is kept for the chart label, where it belongs). Keep that split.
- Direct value labels on each bar, a zero baseline and very light gridlines. The board can read exact numbers without guessing against the axis.
- The frame is plain and consistent: the brand at top left, the meeting context at top right, and the source and page number in a footer on one shared margin. It fits a board pack.
