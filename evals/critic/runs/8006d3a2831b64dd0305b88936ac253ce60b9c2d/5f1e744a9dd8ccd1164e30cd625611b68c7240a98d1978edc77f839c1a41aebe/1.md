**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Help for ferry passengers", then splits evenly between the two columns. It reads as a plain, calm help page for a small local ferry, set in a neutral sans like a docs page. It's quiet, tidy and trustworthy, and a little bare: the lower third of the frame and most of the right column are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or phone help page that passengers read before travelling or at the landing, so they board smoothly and know what to do when there's a problem.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job and I'd change very little of it. The words are what let it down. The "If something goes wrong" section only tells you how to complain afterwards, and every practical answer sends you to a board or noticeboard you may not be standing in front of.

**What would move it most**
1. **"If something goes wrong" promises help and delivers a complaint form** (defect; WCAG 2.4.6 asks that a heading describe what its section holds). A passenger who has missed the last boat, been hurt, or lost a child or a bag reads this heading as "here's what to do now". What they get is three steps for writing a note and dropping it in a box. When the need is urgent, the page fails them at exactly the moment it says it will help. Do one of two things: rename the section for what it actually is, such as "To report a problem after your crossing", or add the urgent cases above the steps: who to tell on board and a phone number for the operator.
2. **The page sends readers to places they can't see** (defect, and common sense rather than a cited rule). "Check the timetable board at your landing" doesn't give a single time. It only says winter sailings run "once an hour" and the last boat leaves "before dusk", a time that shifts by weeks and that you can't plan around. The complaint goes "to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter", so someone reading at home can't send one. A help page you have to stand at the pier to use isn't much help. Put the real information here: the last winter departure from each landing, or a link to the timetable, and the postal and email address in full. Also say whether there is a reply and how long it takes, since that is what a person writing in will hold the operator to.

**Smaller things.**
- The two lists don't line up across the gutter. From the second item down, lines in the right column (for example the line that starts "3. Leave your note…") sit between lines of the left column. This is because the space between items isn't a whole multiple of the line spacing. Make that space a full line so rows match across the gap (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean: a heavy title, bold section heads, regular body text, and no stray sizes. You know where to start, and the two jobs ("before" and "if wrong") are clearly separate. Keep this three-level scale.
- The bullet-versus-number choice is right. The left column holds independent tips, so bullets fit. The right column is a sequence, so numbers fit. Wrapped lines hang under the first word, so both lists scan easily.
- The lines are a comfortable length (about 55 to 60 characters), with generous spacing and high-contrast near-black on white. The text reads easily for older passengers or on a phone in daylight.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the two columns stack in a sensible order on a phone ("Before you board" first) when the page narrows.
