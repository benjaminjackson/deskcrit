**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Planning a vegetable bed" at top left, then drops down through three smaller headings in a steady rhythm. It reads as a plain how-to article for a first-time gardener. It feels calm, orderly and unfussy, and a little austere for a garden subject. Within a couple of seconds you know what it is and how it's organised.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web article that walks a beginner through setting up their first vegetable bed, read top to bottom and skimmed later as a reminder.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the spacing groups each heading with its text, and nothing gets in the way. The biggest problem is the text column: it's too wide, so each line of body text is a long reach for the eye. After that, the first step has no heading, so someone skimming the headings misses it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text runs too wide.** Every paragraph is about 790 px across. At this size that's roughly 90–95 characters a line (for example, "Dig to the depth of a spade… Rake the surface level"). The eye has to travel a long way back to find the start of each next line, and it gets worse as paragraphs get longer. Bringhurst puts the comfortable range at 45–75 characters, with 66 as the ideal. To check, count the characters in one full line. Fix: narrow the text column to about 65 characters, around 600 px at this size. You have plenty of empty space on the right, so nothing else has to give. *Convention, and a real reading cost.*
2. **The first step reads as an intro.** The paragraph under the title ("Choose a spot that gets six hours of sun… Mark the corners with sticks and a length of string") is an instruction, the first thing you actually do. But it has no heading, so it reads as a lead-in. Someone scanning the headings sees soil, planting and watering, and skips choosing the site. Fix: give it a heading in the same style as the others, such as "Choosing the spot", and let the title stand alone or add a one-line intro. *Defect for anyone who skims, which is how this page gets used the second time.*
3. **Some numbers are missing their reference point.** "Six hours of sun" doesn't say per day, though a beginner will probably guess right. "Water deeply" doesn't say how much, so a new gardener has no idea what deep means. These are the lines a reader acts on, so each one should be complete: "six hours of direct sun a day", and a test for deep watering they can do, like "until the soil is wet a finger's length down". *Defect, copy.*

**Smaller things.**
- Headings differ from the body text only in size: same weight, same near-black. It works here because the size steps are big. Making the subheads a weight heavier would let them shrink a size and keep the page quieter (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8–9). *Convention, low cost.*
- "year's" in the soil paragraph uses a straight apostrophe. Use a curly one. *Common sense: it's a typographic detail, not a confirmed rule.*

**What's working.**
- The spacing inside sections is tight and the spacing between them is clear: each heading sits about 4–6 px above its text and about 30 px below the previous section. Each heading clearly belongs to the text under it. Keep this ratio when you narrow the column.
- The type scale is three clear sizes (title about 52 px, subheads about 32, body about 18) with nothing in between. Every size has an obvious job.
- The copy is concrete and sounds like a person talking: "things that forgive mistakes: radishes, lettuce, and bush beans", "twice a week, rather than a little every day". It's specific enough to act on, which is the point of a how-to.
