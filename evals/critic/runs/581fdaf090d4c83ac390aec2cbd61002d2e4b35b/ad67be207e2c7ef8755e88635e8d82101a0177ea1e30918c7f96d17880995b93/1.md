**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board", then drops into three short paragraphs. Within two seconds it reads as an information page for a small ferry service. It feels calm, plain, and trustworthy, and a bit generic. The right third and the bottom half of the frame are empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells first-time passengers how the ferry runs (frequency, crossing time, bikes, winter service) so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are sound. The words aren't. A passenger who comes here to plan a crossing still can't tell when the first or last boat leaves, or when the winter timetable starts. The page sends them to a printed timetable in a shelter, which they only reach once they're already standing at the landing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The service hours have no times in them** (first paragraph, lines 1–2). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" is the line a passenger acts on, and it can be read two ways. Is "early morning" 6:00 or 8:00? Is "late evening" 20:00 or 23:00? If someone guesses wrong, they miss the last boat home. This is a **defect**: it's a schedule claim people will hold the operator to. Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing ("First boat 6:20 from the north landing, last 22:40 from the market quay"). If departures follow a fixed pattern, say which minutes past the hour.
2. **"In winter" has no dates, and the timetable sits only in a shelter** (third paragraph). The viewer can't tell whether winter means November–February or follows the clocks changing. Saying printed timetables are "kept in the shelter at each landing" sends them to a place they reach only after they've set off. This is a **defect** for planning. Fix: name the months or dates of the winter service, and put the timetable itself, or a link to it, on this page. The shelter line can then become a backup.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines look like about 82–85 characters, which is above Bringhurst's 45–75 range (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). The generous leading stops it from hurting much. Narrowing the column to about 600 px would put it near 66 characters. This is a convention, and it costs little here.
- The bicycle paragraph doesn't say what riders need to bring. "Lock them to the rail" implies they need their own lock. Say so outright ("bring a lock"), because people only find out on the deck.

**What's working.**
- The three-level type scale works: a small, letterspaced grey eyebrow, a bold heading at about twice the body size, and comfortable body text. Every size has an obvious job, and the heading wins at a glance. Keep it.
- The content comes in short paragraphs, one topic each (timing, bikes, winter). It's easy to skim. If you add times, keep that structure rather than merging everything into one block.
- The restrained single-neutral palette and generous left margin suit a public-service notice. Nothing competes with the information.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't check reflow on a phone, which is where passengers at a landing will read this. Check that the column collapses cleanly at 320 px.
