**First read.** The eye goes straight to "ALL ABOARD THE MORNING FERRY". It's huge, black and widely spaced, and it fills the top half. It then drops to the two-line paragraph and stops. Above the headline, the small grey "CITY HARBOUR OPEN DAY" gets read last, if at all. It reads as the top section of an event web page: clean, confident and calm. Nothing on it tells me when this is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top section of a web page for a city harbour open day. Its job is to get local families to turn up and ride the free ferry, so they need to leave knowing when, where and what to do.

**Verdict.** The type is strong but the page doesn't do its job. It sells a ferry ride without saying what day it is, what time the boats run, or where the north landing is, and there's no next step. Everything the viewer would act on is missing, and the empty lower half is where it belongs.

**What would move it most**

1. **There's no date, time or next step, so the reader has nothing to plan around** (defect). The eyebrow names the event but not the day. The paragraph says "every half hour" but not from when to when. The headline's "morning ferry" can be read two ways: boats run only in the morning, or boats run all day and this is just a nice phrase. Someone who reads it the first way and turns up at 2 pm, or the second way and plans the afternoon, gets it wrong either way. "The north landing" assumes the reader already knows the harbour. The eye goes headline → paragraph → nothing, with no button, link or map below. The fix:
   - Put the date and the first and last sailings right under the headline, in the paragraph's size but bolder. Dates and times are what people remember and hold you to.
   - Say "north landing" in terms a stranger can find, such as a street or a landmark.
   - Add one specific ask, such as "See the sailing times" or "Get directions to the north landing", rather than a generic "Learn more".

2. **The headline is tracked so wide that it reads letter by letter** (convention). At this size the gaps between letters are almost as wide as the gaps between words. "ALL ABOARD THE" looks like a row of single letters more than three words, so the headline takes longer to read than five short words should. Bringhurst's guide for spacing capitals is 5–10% of the type size, and this looks well past that. Lupton (*Thinking with Type*, p. 80) notes that space between letters grows as type gets larger, so large type needs fine-tuning, not more space. Pull the tracking back to about 5% or less so each word holds together. The heavy capitals already make it feel like a big sign without the extra space.

3. **The empty space looks left over, not planned** (defect, unless the page clearly continues). All the content sits in the top-left 55% of the frame. The lower 40% and the right half are bare white with nothing to explain them. At 1280×800 that pushes whatever comes next off the screen and makes the layout look top-heavy. Moving the date, times and ask from finding 1 into that space fixes most of it. If the space is meant to frame the headline, the next content still needs to start inside the first screen, so people know there's more to scroll to.

**Smaller things.**
- The paragraph breaks "Bring a / hat and a thermos", which splits the phrase. Set the measure so "Bring a hat and a thermos." stays together on its own line.

**What's working.**
- There are three clearly separate levels of type: a small grey spaced-out label, the headline at roughly five times the paragraph size, and the paragraph. Each level is obvious at a glance and they share one left edge at about x=90. Keep that scale and that single edge when adding the new information.
- The paragraph's copy is concrete and sounds human. "Free rides every half hour" and "Bring a hat and a thermos" tell people what it costs, how often the boats run and what to pack. That beats generic event copy, so keep that voice when writing the date and the ask.
- The black-and-grey palette on white keeps the headline the only loud thing on the page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the headline is live text or a picture of text, and how it wraps on a phone. At this size and tracking, "MORNING" or "ABOARD" may overflow or break badly on a narrow screen, so check it at 320 px wide.
- Whether more content sits just below the 800 px fold, which would partly answer finding 3.
