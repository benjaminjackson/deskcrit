**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif title, "Winter timetable at the harbour", then drops into two even grey columns of prose. I start hunting for a departure time and don't find one: no table, no clock times, only sentences. The page then stops at "Fares and cards", a heading over a thin rule with nothing under it. It feels calm, bookish and literate, but unfinished, and it doesn't tell me when the boat goes.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a ferry operator's web page that tells winter passengers when boats run and how to travel with them.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The page is titled as a timetable, but it gives no departure times, and the facts a passenger needs are buried in polished paragraphs set at the same weight as notes about the paperback shelf. The type is good. The content isn't built for someone who wants the next boat.

**What would move it most**

1. **The "timetable" has no times in it (defect).** The title promises a schedule. The body says boats leave "every ninety minutes" without ever saying from when, and the ticket window stays open "until the last boat has left" without saying when that is. The office opens at "seven… an hour later than in summer", which is a reference to a time the reader doesn't have. Someone asking "can I make the 4 o'clock boat?" can't answer it from this page. They'd have to work out departures from a start time that isn't given (Nielsen, recognition rather than recall), and a heading that doesn't describe its content also fails WCAG 2.4.6. The fix is a real departure table at the top: first boat, every sailing or the 90-minute pattern written out as clock times, and the last boat. Times go in numerals, because "seven", "twenty" and "thirty-five" written as words don't stand out when someone scans for numbers.

2. **Everything is one weight of prose, so nothing can be scanned (defect).** Six paragraphs mix schedule facts (opening hours, frequency, crossing time), rules (bikes at the stern, prams by the ramp, dogs on a lead) and atmosphere (stove, paperbacks), all in the same size, weight and paragraph shape. A passenger on a phone at the pier has to read every sentence to find the one fact they came for (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). The fix is to group the content under a few plain headings, such as when boats run, travelling with bikes, prams and dogs, and the waiting room, with the hard facts pulled out into short lines or a list. Keep the friendly sentences, but under the facts, not instead of them.

3. **"Fares and cards" is an empty section (defect).** The heading and its rule sit at about y=585, with roughly 200 px of blank page below. A section that a passenger will act on, by paying or buying a card, reads as broken or unfinished. The opening paragraph already mentions season cards, so the reader is primed to look here and finds nothing. The rule is also split into two halves: the left half sits under the heading and the right half has no label, which reads like a second section heading that never rendered. Fill the section with fares and how to get a card, or remove it. If the content does exist and simply didn't render in this capture, see the note under "Couldn't judge from a still".

4. **Cancellations are only on a noticeboard at the gate (defect, copy).** "In rough weather the captain may skip a sailing, and notices are pinned to the board beside the gate before the first boat is due." Someone reading this page at home learns that a sailing might not run, and that they can only find out once they're standing at the harbour. That is the one moment a web page is most useful, and this one sends them away. The line should land on the reader: say where they can check from home (a status line on this page, a phone number, a feed). Otherwise it reads as an apology rather than help.

**Smaller things.**
- In the right column, "bags." sits alone on the last line of the bicycles paragraph. Rewrap or edit it so the line doesn't end on one short word (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The text setting is comfortable. The columns run about 55–60 characters to a line, the leading is generous, the reading serif is a good choice, and the baselines line up across the two columns. Keep this when you add structure.
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (title, section heading, body), each a clear step apart, with bold weight giving the headings contrast beyond size. Add the new section headings at the existing middle level, not as new sizes.
- The voice is warm and local ("the crew will always help with bags", "Dogs on a lead are welcome on every sailing"). That is a real asset. It just needs to sit beside the facts, not replace them.
