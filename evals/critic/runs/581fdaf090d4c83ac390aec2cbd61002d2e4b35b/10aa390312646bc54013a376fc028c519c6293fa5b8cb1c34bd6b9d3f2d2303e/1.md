**First read.** The eye goes to "The Wexley Gazette", runs along the double rule, and then splits three ways across headlines that all look the same. After a few seconds it has read one story and has nowhere else to go. The bottom 45% of the screen is empty white. It reads as a small-town paper's front page: calm, literate and tidy, but more like a list of notices than a front page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a local news site, where residents scan for what's happening in town and choose a story to read.

**Verdict.** It's clean and well set, but it doesn't work as a front page yet. Nothing tells the reader which story matters most, and the page stops halfway down the screen, so it feels unfinished rather than calm.

**What would move it most**
1. **No lead story, so the page has no focal point.** "Bridge to reopen in spring", "Market moves indoors" and "School wins garden prize" are set at the same size and weight, in equal columns, with nothing else on the page. The reader sees three items of equal rank and has to read all three headlines to decide which one counts. A front page exists to make that call for them. The masthead (~36px) is only a step above the headlines (~24px), so even the top of the page is a mild step, not a strong one. *Convention:* Tschichold asks that the order of importance be shown by differences of size, and Hofmann's contrast of "much against little" is missing here. Fix: pick the lead (the bridge, which affects the most readers) and make it plainly dominant. Give it a much larger headline, more width (two of the three columns, or full width above the other two) and, if you have one, a picture. Then set the other two back as secondary stories.
2. **The bottom half of the screen is leftover space.** All content ends at about y≈415 on an 800px screen. That empty space isn't framing anything. It just looks like the page forgot to finish, and all the weight sits in the top half. *Defect:* it makes the paper look thin, as if there's no news. If this really is the whole edition, fixing #1 (a bigger lead and a picture) will fill and anchor the page. If more stories exist, show them below as a lower tier of smaller items. It's fine if the page was captured before its content loaded, but check that in a live browser.
3. **Justified text in narrow columns opens holes between words.** The columns run about 40 characters. That's acceptable for multi-column setting, but too short to justify without hyphenation. The gaps are obvious in "months of repairs to its arches." and "Engineers found that two of the five" (left column), "the old corn exchange from next" and "The hall has" (middle), and "a regional prize for the vegetable" (right). They break the even grey that makes justified text look calm, and the eye stumbles across the gaps. *Convention:* Bringhurst warns that short justified lines lead to "white acne". Hochuli says that under about 50 characters even word spacing becomes "difficult, if not impossible". Fix: set the columns ragged right, or keep justification and turn on hyphenation.
4. **Dates the reader might act on are relative, and the page has no date.** "From next month" (market), "in April", "from March" and "this summer term" can only be pinned down if the reader knows when the issue came out, and the page shows no date or issue line. A resident reading this in late March can't tell whether "next month" means April or has already passed, so they may turn up at the square to an empty market. *Defect* (copy). Fix: add a dateline under the masthead, and write the market move as an actual date ("from Saturday 4 April").

**What's working.**
- The type is sound: a readable text serif at a comfortable size, generous leading, bold headlines in the same family, and baselines that line up across all three columns. Keep this setup when you build the hierarchy.
- The copy is plain and concrete. Each story answers who, what and when in the first sentence ("The old stone bridge over the Wex will reopen to cars in April"), and that's exactly the right voice for a local paper.
- The thin double rule under a centred masthead is a restrained newspaper cue. It sets the genre without any ornament.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the headlines are links and how they show hover and focus. How the three columns reflow on a narrow screen: justified text at mobile widths will get worse.
