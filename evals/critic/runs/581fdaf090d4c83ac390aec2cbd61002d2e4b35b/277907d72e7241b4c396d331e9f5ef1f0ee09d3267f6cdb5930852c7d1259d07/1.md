**First read.** The bold serif heading "Notes for the summer fete" lands first. Then the eye goes straight to the five underlined italic phrases and jumps from one to the next instead of reading the sentences. It looks like a calm, well-set reading page, but the underlines make it feel like a page full of links, and I wanted to click "arrive before ten".

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short web page or emailed note telling fete volunteers the practical rules (arrival time, cake labelling, raffle times, parking), which they need to remember and act on.

**Verdict.** The type is set well, but the emphasis works against the page. Five phrases are styled as hyperlinks, so readers either try to click them or learn to skip them, and with five equal highlights none stands out. Fix the emphasis and the page is nearly done.

**What would move it most**
1. **The emphasis looks like links.** Each of the five key phrases ("arrive before ten", "labelled with their ingredients", "three o'clock sharp", "collected by four", "on the field only") is both italic and underlined. On a screen, underlined text in a paragraph means "link", so readers will tap it and get nothing, or decide the page is broken. *Defect.* This also breaks a convention: emphasis changes one thing at a time (Bringhurst; Butterick, *Practical Typography*, says don't underline for emphasis at all). Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, explains why it costs something: the styling signals an action that isn't there. Fix: drop the underline and keep the italic, or use bold alone if these must be found by skimming. Keep underlines for real links.
2. **Five equal highlights means nothing is highlighted, and the rules are buried in prose.** Emphasis only works by contrast with what's around it. Here about a fifth of the words are marked at the same strength, so the eye scatters. The page is a set of separate instructions (when to arrive, what to do with cakes, when the raffle is drawn, the ticket deadline, where to park) written as two run-on paragraphs. A volunteer checking "when do tickets close?" on the morning has to re-read sentences. *Defect for this job.* Fix: give each rule its own line, as a short list or one sentence each, with the time or place leading, e.g. "10:00 Arrive, so stalls are ready when the gates open." Then the emphasis is hardly needed.
3. **The copy leaves the deadline open to two readings.** "All tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box" can mean winners must collect their prizes by four, or that sellers must hand in ticket stubs by four. Both are things someone will be held to. Read the wrong way, a winner turns up at 4:15 and loses their prize. *Defect (copy).* Fix: say who does what: "Winners: collect your prize by 4pm, or it goes back in the box." Also "before ten" and "by four" have no am/pm or day. Probably obvious on the day, but "10am" costs nothing.

**Smaller things.**
- Last line of the second paragraph: "only." sits alone, and the emphasized phrase "on the field / only" breaks across lines. Keep "the field only" together with a non-breaking space, or rewrite the line.
- "o'clock" seems to use a straight apostrophe. Check it in the source and use the curly one (’).

**What's working.**
- The type: one serif family, a heavy heading against regular text, true italics, comfortable line spacing, and a measure at the upper edge of the comfortable range. It reads as a calm, friendly note, which suits a village fete. Keep this when you restructure.
- The plain, single-column layout with a generous left margin. There's nothing to fight the content, so once the rules are made scannable the page needs nothing else.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the underlined phrases really are links, and if so where they go. If they are links, the issue becomes link text that doesn't say where it leads (WCAG 2.4.4), not false links.
