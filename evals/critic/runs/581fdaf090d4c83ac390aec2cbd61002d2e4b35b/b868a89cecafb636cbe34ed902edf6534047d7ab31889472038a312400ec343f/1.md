**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", top left, then drops into three plain paragraphs. It reads as a clean, calm, quiet page, a bit like a README. By the second paragraph you notice you've been promised a timetable and are reading an essay, with not one departure time on the page.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a public web page that tells passengers when the ferry leaves and how to use it. Most visitors arrive with one question: when is the next boat?

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The typesetting is competent, but the page called "timetable" has no timetable, so a passenger can't plan a crossing from it. Everything else is secondary to that.

**What would move it most**

1. **There is no timetable, and the times that are there can't be pinned down (defect).** The heading promises a schedule. The body gives "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "one boat each hour" in winter, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger standing at the north landing can't work out from any of that whether a boat leaves at 7:10 or 7:40, when the first and last boats go, or what time "dusk" means in December. Then the page sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", so the web page admits it isn't the timetable. The things people act on and later hold the operator to (times, the last boat, the season change) are exactly the things written so they can be read more than one way.
   - Why it matters: the page fails the self-evident, scannable test (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*) and makes people work out facts that should be on show (Nielsen, recognition rather than recall). "Early morning" and "dusk" are the operator's words, not the clock times a passenger plans with (Nielsen, match with the real world). The heading no longer describes the content under it (WCAG, Headings and labels).
   - The fix: put the departures first, as a table with columns for each direction and each season and real clock times, so readers can look up exact values. State the first and last sailings outright and say when winter hours start and end. Then move the prose below the table as notes, such as crossing time, bicycles and where changes are posted. If the times really do change week to week, say where today's times live and link to them; don't paper over it with "early morning".

2. **The prose hides the facts that are there (defect).** Even as text, the useful facts sit mid-sentence: twelve minutes per crossing, bikes free, bikes board last, winter hourly service. Someone scanning reads the opening words of each paragraph and gets "The river ferry runs…", "Bicycles ride…", "In winter…". The topics come through but the answers don't (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). The fix: once the table exists, turn the rest into short labelled points that lead with the answer, such as "Crossing time: about 12 minutes, longer against the tide" and "Bicycles: free; board after foot passengers; lock to the deck rail". The copy also stops at facts where it should speak to the rider. "The tide turns against the boat" should end on what it means for them, for example "allow up to N minutes".

**What's working.**
- The type itself is sound, and you should keep it when you add the table:
  - The lines run about 68 characters.
  - Line spacing is generous.
  - The text is near-black on white, so contrast is strong.
  - The paragraphs are flush left and marked only by space.
  - There are two sizes, with a clear step between heading and body, so the order of reading is never in doubt.
- The plain, quiet tone suits a public service page. Keep that restraint when the table goes in: rules only where they separate rows, and no colour coding.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page reflows on a phone, which is where most people at a ferry landing will read it. Once the table is in, check that it stays readable at 320 px wide without scrolling sideways.
