**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, and the coffee line pulls you through all three of its lines. From there it drops to the green button and then crosses to the white invoice card on the right. That card reads as a small screenshot of the product rather than a second focal point. Below the fold the three feature cards register as a row of boxes. Overall it feels calm, warm and trustworthy: a tidy invoicing tool for freelancers and small studios. It also feels a lot like every other SaaS landing page from the last few years.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: this is the homepage hero of an invoicing app for freelancers and small businesses, and its job is to get a first visit to start the free trial.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the type is clean, and there's one action. But the only picture of the product, the invoice card, shows nothing of the three things the copy promises: speed, auto-fill and chasing late payers. The page tells the viewer the product is fast and shows them nothing to back that up. The copy also contradicts itself about when the reminders happen.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invoice card doesn't prove the headline.** The card (right of the hero) is a finished, static invoice. The headline promises speed ("the time it takes to pour coffee"), and the subhead promises that the app fills in clients, rates and tax and then chases payment. The card shows none of that. A viewer sees an invoice they could make in a spreadsheet, so the claim rests on trust. It is also the quietest thing in the hero: thin grey border, 14 to 15 px grey text, about a third of the width. So it adds little weight to the argument. *Taste, argued on its own terms:* the image and the headline should be two halves of one idea. Make the card show the promise. Show the client, rates and the "Tax 8%" line visibly filled in for the user, or a "Sent · reminder scheduled" state, or the time it took. Then give it enough size and contrast to hold the right half of the hero.
2. **The copy contradicts itself about reminders.** The subhead says Tallyfold "chases late payments for you". The first card says "Three gentle nudges before an invoice is overdue". Before the due date and after it are different promises, and getting paid is exactly what this viewer will hold you to. One reader expects follow-ups once a client is late. Another expects only reminders before the due date, and then finds that nothing happens after it. *Defect.* Pick the true behaviour and say it the same way in both places, for example "nudges clients before the due date and keeps chasing after it, in your own words".
3. **The ask is generic.** "Start free for 14 days" names a trial, not a thing the viewer gets to do. The headline sets up one concrete task, sending an invoice, and the button doesn't pick it up. A label like "Send your first invoice free" lets the viewer test the coffee claim on their own work. That makes them the judge of the value, rather than being asked to trust it. *Taste, from the copy guidance:* tie the button to the headline's own task. If the trial terms matter, put them in a small line under the button ("14 days free, no card").

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count about seven sizes (roughly 52, 20, 19, 18, 16, 15 and 14 px). The logo (~20), subhead (~19) and card titles (~18) sit a pixel or two apart, which reads as sizes picked one at a time. Pull them onto three or four clear levels plus one small size (Bringhurst, 3.1.1).
- Headline breaks: "in the / time" splits the phrase, and "coffee." sits alone on the third line. Rebreak it as "Send an invoice / in the time it takes / to pour coffee." (common sense: break at phrase boundaries).
- Near-miss alignment: the invoice card's left edge (~x 820) sits about 4 px left of the third feature card (~x 824). Snap it to the same column line. Near-misses read as errors (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- The feature cards are about 150 px tall, but their text fills only the top 60 to 90 px. The empty bottom halves look like leftover space or a missing icon or image. Either size the cards to their content or give that space a job.
- "Reports for tax time" names a category. "Export a clean year of income in one file" is the real promise. Consider leading with it.

**What's working.**
- One dominant headline and one filled button in the brand's single deep green, with no competing actions. "Sign in" is correctly demoted to plain text. Keep that restraint when you add things.
- The coffee headline has a voice and a concrete picture, unlike most invoicing copy. Keep the idea and fix only the line breaks.
- The invoice details are real and add up: 640 + 45 + 8% tax = 739.80, with right-aligned figures. That kind of care earns trust from people who handle money.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the nav and button, the button's hover and pressed states, and how the two-column hero and the three cards reflow on a phone. On a phone, the invoice card will either stack under the button or get dropped, so check which.
