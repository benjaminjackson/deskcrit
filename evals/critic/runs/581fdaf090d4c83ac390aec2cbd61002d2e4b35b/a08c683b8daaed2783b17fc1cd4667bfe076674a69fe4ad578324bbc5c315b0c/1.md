**First read.** The eye lands on the big serif headline at top left, then drops to the four cards, pulled by the coral basket and the teal shirt. After the cards it stops: there is nothing to do there, so it goes back up to the small dark "Book a visit" button in the top-right corner. It reads as a local home-services landing page. It feels warm, tidy and friendly, and also like any other company's page.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: this is the landing page for a home-services company, and it should get a homeowner to pick a service, understand the price, and book.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and pleasant, but it doesn't close the sale. The subhead tells people to "pick a service", yet none of the cards can visibly be picked. The prices don't say what they buy. The only way to book is a small button in a corner, far from the services. The craft is fine; the funnel is broken.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cards don't look pickable, and the booking action is nowhere near them.** The subhead says "Pick a service, choose a day…", so the visitor expects to click a card. But the four cards have no button, arrow or link styling, so they look like static content. The only action is "Book a visit" in the header, about 600 px from the cards and the smallest thing on the page with any weight. A visitor who just decided on window cleaning has to guess whether clicking the card works, or go to the corner and start over without their choice carried along. *Defect* (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*: signifiers and natural mapping; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: make it obvious what's clickable). Fix: make each card an obvious action, either a button or link inside it ("Book window cleaning") or a whole-card link with a visible cue, and add one main call to action in the hero under the subhead. Keep the header button as the repeat.

2. **The prices can't be compared or trusted, because none has a unit.** "From $89", "From $189", "From $24" and "From $65" don't say what you get. Laundry at $24 could be per bag, per load, per pound or per week. Deep cleaning at $189 could be for a studio or a four-bedroom house. Prices are what a visitor remembers and later holds you to, so a figure that can be read two ways is a defect, not a style note. Someone who read "$24 a week" and gets billed "$24 a bag" feels misled. *Defect.* Fix: give each price its unit and baseline in the same line ("From $89 per visit, up to 10 windows", "$24 per bag, washed and folded").

3. **The copy is generic and contradicts itself.** "Help around the house, on your schedule" could sit on any company's page. It names no place, no proof and nothing that sets Fernway apart, so the visitor has no reason to pick it. It also breaks its own promise: the laundry card says "We collect on Monday and bring it back folded on Wednesday", which is your schedule, not theirs. "Vetted crew" asks for trust and gives no evidence: no reviews, no insurance note, no number of homes served. "Book a visit" is also the wrong verb for a laundry pickup. *Defect* for the contradiction, *taste* for the rest. Fix: anchor the headline in something specific and checkable (the area served, a same-week booking, a guarantee). Put one line of proof near the cards. Either drop "on your schedule" or make laundry pickup days choosable. Name the ask by what happens ("Pick a day", "See open times this week").

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "…on your / schedule", leaving one word alone on the second line. Widen the headline box so it sits on one line, or rebreak it at the comma ("Help around the house, / on your schedule").
- The illustrations don't share one line style: the window and basket have heavy navy outlines, while the shirt and plant are flat fills with none. Pick one treatment (*taste*, but it shows because the four sit side by side).

**What's working.**
- The grid is disciplined. The logo, headline and first card share one left edge, and the button's right edge lines up with the last card, which makes the page feel deliberate.
- The type pairing works by clear contrast: a heavy serif for the brand and headline against a plain sans serif for everything else, with card titles separated by weight rather than another size. Keep this when you revise.
- The palette is small and warm (cream, mint panels, teal, coral, mustard, dark slate) and fits domestic, friendly help without looking childish.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are already clickable, and their hover and focus states. If they are links, that makes finding 1 worse, not better: no one can tell they're links.
- The empty cream band below the cards, about 150 px. It's probably just where the screenshot ends, but check that the page continues below it in a live browser.
- How the four columns reflow on a phone.
