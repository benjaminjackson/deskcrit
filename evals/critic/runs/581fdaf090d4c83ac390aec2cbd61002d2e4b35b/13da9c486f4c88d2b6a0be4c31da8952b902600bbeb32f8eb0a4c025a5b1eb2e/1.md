**First read.** The bold serif headline "Notes from the boat shed" catches the eye first. The eye then drops into the left paragraph, crosses to the right one, and stops at "anyone who wants to help." It reads as a calm, literary page for a small community workshop, and the type is well made. Then the page just ends: the bottom 60% of the frame is empty, and the invitation leaves you with nowhere to go.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is a short web page or newsletter item for a volunteer-run boat shed. It sets the scene and then invites new volunteers to come along.

**Verdict.** The typography is good and the writing has real atmosphere, but the page doesn't do its job. It makes you want to help and then gives you nothing to act on: no time, no place, no contact, and no visual stop. The ask is the last sentence of an ordinary paragraph, so it ends the page as a dead end.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invitation has no details, so nobody can act on it** (defect, copy). The right column says "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings… for anyone who wants to help." Read it as a stranger: what time on Thursday? Which shed? "The end of the lane" means something only to people who already know the lane. Is it every Thursday? Should you just turn up, or tell someone first? A reader who's interested has to go and find all of that out, and most won't. The time and place are what a volunteer will act on and hold you to, so they must be exact. Fix: add one concrete line, such as "Thursdays, 6–9 pm, [the shed's address]. Just turn up, or message [name] first." Make it name one thing the reader can do this week.
2. **The ask has no visual weight, and the eye path ends on it** (defect). The invitation is set exactly like the scene-setting paragraph: same size, weight, and colour, in a parallel column. Nothing tells the reader that this paragraph is the point of the page. After its last line the eye runs into empty white. Fix: make the practical details a separate third level below the two paragraphs. Set the time and place as a short block, and keep the hierarchy to bold and spacing rather than a new size, so the page ends on what to do.
3. **The lower 60% of the frame is empty and unexplained, so the page looks unfinished** (defect, unless more content follows when you scroll). All the weight sits in the top 330 px, and below it there's about 470 px of plain white with no edge, footer, or image. It doesn't read as generous margin. It reads as a page that stopped, or one that hasn't loaded. That's fine if this is the top of a longer page. If this is all there is, use that space for the missing details (finding 1), or for a photograph of the shed and the dinghies. A photo would do what the copy already sets up with tar, cedar, and varnished clinker. The Negative space line in the checklist cites Tschichold, *The New Typography*, and Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*.

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale.** There are two sizes with a big step between them (headline about 58 px, body about 18 px), and bold against regular adds a second kind of contrast. Each level has an obvious job, which is what Bringhurst asks for. Keep it at two sizes, and do any new hierarchy with weight.
- **The text setting.** The measure is about 58 characters per line, the leading about 1.55, and the rag is even. The two columns share a top line and their baselines line up across the gutter. It reads easily and looks careful.
- **The copy's voice.** "There is always a kettle on, and a spare apron on the hook by the door" is a better invitation than any slogan, because it answers "will I fit in?" before the reader asks. Keep it, and add the facts after it.
