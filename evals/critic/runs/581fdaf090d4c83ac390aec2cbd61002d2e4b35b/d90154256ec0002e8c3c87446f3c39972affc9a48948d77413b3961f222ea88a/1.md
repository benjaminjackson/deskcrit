**First read.** The eye goes to the three bold headlines, "Learn to swim", "Join the lanes" and "Family hour", and then keeps moving between three equally loud blocks of orange, green and purple. None of them wins. It looks like a swimming pool's programmes page, and the bright colours make it feel friendly. Once the eye has read the three short paragraphs at the top of the cards, the page has nothing left to say: the bottom half of every card is empty colour, there's no button, and a grey sentence underneath reads like a reviewer's note that ended up on the page.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a section of a local pool's website where adults and families pick a programme and go on to book or find out more.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. It describes three activities attractively, but it has no next step, no lead and no pool name, so the visitor learns what exists and then has nowhere to go. The biggest problem is that it ends without an action.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no way to act.** None of the three cards has a button, a link, an arrow, a price, a time range or a booking route. Nothing even shows that the cards can be clicked. A visitor who wants Tuesday classes doesn't know what to tap, what it costs or how to sign up, so the page stops at the moment of interest. This is a defect. Krug (*Don't make me think*) asks that a user can tell "what they can do, and where to click" within seconds, and Norman's signifiers principle says the same. Fix: give each card one specific ask that names the real next step, such as "Book a Tuesday class" or "See lane times", styled clearly as a button or link, plus the facts people decide on: when it ends and what it costs. If the whole card is the link, it still needs a visible cue.
2. **No offer leads.** The three cards are the same size and weight, and the colours are equally saturated at about the same lightness. In greyscale they're the same mid-dark block three times. With nothing to land on, the eye goes back and forth and every offer gets a third of the attention. This breaks a convention, and here it costs something: Tschichold (*The New Typography*) asks that the order of importance be shown by differences of size, and Hofmann (*Graphic Design Manual*) gets tension from setting much against little. The grey line under the cards says "one offer clearly leads the page". That's text in the image, not evidence, and the page shows the opposite. Fix: decide which programme the pool most wants to fill and make it clearly first, for example a wider card, the only saturated one, or the only one with a filled button, and set the other two back. If all three really matter equally, at least hold the strong colour back for the actions so the row stops shouting.
3. **The empty bottom halves of the cards.** Below the text, roughly 45% of each card is flat colour: about 280 px of nothing, at the same height in all three. It isn't shaped space that gives anything room; it reads as leftover, as if content failed to load or the cards were sized before the copy was written. It also makes the text feel like it's floating at the top of a box that's too big. This breaks a convention (Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*: space should be gathered and set in tension against dense areas, not left over). Fix: that's where the times, price and button belong. Or, if there's nothing more to say, shorten the cards to fit the content.
4. **The grey sentence under the row.** "Design review: approved, one offer clearly leads the page" is an internal note shown to visitors. It means nothing to a swimmer, it makes the page look unfinished, and its claim is false. This is a defect. Fix: remove it. If something has to sit under the cards, make it something the visitor can use, such as the pool's address or "All timetables".
5. **Nothing says whose pool this is.** There's no name, place or mark in view, so the copy could belong to any leisure centre. That matters less if a header sits just above this crop (see below), but on its own the section can't be placed. This is a defect if the section ever stands alone, for example as a shared image or a landing block. Fix: make sure the pool's name and location are in view with this section.

**Smaller things.**
- The row isn't centred: the left margin is about 56 px and the right about 96 px, so the set leans left. Make the two margins equal, or check whether the screenshot cut off the right side.
- Three identical cards in a level row, all horizontals and verticals, feel static and samey (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Dynamics", as a prompt, not a rule). Once one card leads (finding 2), this mostly fixes itself.
- Copy: "on Tuesday evenings" and "Sunday afternoons" give no times, and "from six" gives a start but no end. These are the details people plan around. Add the hours.

**What's working.**
- The headlines are good copy and good type. "Learn to swim", "Join the lanes" and "Family hour" each name a real thing a real person wants, and they're set big, bold and tightly leaded so each two-line headline reads as one unit. Keep the voice and the treatment.
- The paragraphs are concrete and written to the reader: "a coach in the water beside you", "three speeds to choose from", "floats and toys for the children". They speak to the actual worry of each audience. Keep them and add the missing facts around them.
- White text on all three colours looks comfortably readable (roughly 5:1 or better by eye; measure it to confirm), with three clean type sizes, a short measure and even 24 px gutters. The basic system is sound; what's missing is a lead and an action.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are links, and whether they have hover and focus states. If they're clickable, they need a visible focus ring and a cue that they can be clicked at rest.
- How the row stacks on a phone (reflow at 320 px). With three tall cards that are half empty, that's a lot of scrolling past colour.
- Whether a header with the pool's name sits just above this crop.
