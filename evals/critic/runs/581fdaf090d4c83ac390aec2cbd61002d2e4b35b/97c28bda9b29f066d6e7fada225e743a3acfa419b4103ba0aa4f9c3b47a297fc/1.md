**First read.** The eye lands on "Harbour Lights festival", which is bold, dark and alone at top left, and then drops into a wall of grey text. Three paragraphs of the same size sit side by side with nothing to say what each one covers, so the eye just starts at the top left and reads. It feels like a parish newsletter or a council notice: calm and literate, and much quieter than a festival called "Harbour Lights".

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page announcing a local festival to residents. It needs to tell them what's on and when, and recruit volunteers.

**Verdict.** It reads well but doesn't work as an announcement. Every fact a visitor needs (the dates, the times, the events, how to help) is buried in running prose, and the facts a reader would plan around are missing.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts are locked inside paragraphs (defect).** The columns hold a lantern walk (Saturday, dusk), a pie contest (Sunday, entries by one o'clock, winners at four), a boat race, stalls at ten and a brass band at noon. None of it is pulled out. Each column is one solid block with no heading, so the lantern walk and the pie contest only show up once you read the first sentence of the middle and right columns. Someone who wants to know "when is the lantern walk?" has to read the page to find out. Nielsen (*How Users Read on the Web*) is exactly this: people skim, and unbroken blocks defeat them.
   - The three identical grey blocks also give the page no accent at all, so nothing tells you which event is the draw.
   - **Fix:** give each column a heading that names its event, and set the day, time and place on their own line under it, apart from the story. Keep the prose underneath for colour.

2. **The copy leaves out what people plan around (defect).**
   - "The first weekend of September" has no dates and no year.
   - The children's boat race has no day or time at all.
   - "By one o'clock" only makes sense if you've connected it to Sunday a sentence earlier.
   - "Volunteers are still needed" gives no way to volunteer: no name, no address, no link. The only ask on the page names no action, so nobody will act on it.

   **Fix:** put the dates (for example "Sat 6 – Sun 7 September") right under the headline. Give every event a day and time. End the volunteer line with one concrete step, such as "Email Sarah at … to take a shift on the cake tent."

3. **The look contradicts the subject (taste).** A festival named for lights on the harbour, with a lantern walk and paper boats on the river, is set entirely in dark grey on white, with no image and no colour. Nothing makes a local stop and think "I want to go to that". I'm arguing this as a designer, not as a rule. You don't need a redesign: one warm accent colour for the event headings and dates, or a single photo of last year's lanterns, would give it the mood the name promises.

**Smaller things**
- **Column rules:** the two grey vertical rules sit about 10 px left of the text in the middle and right columns but about 25 px right of the text before them. They crowd the column that follows. Drop them and let a wider, even gutter separate the columns, or centre them in the gutter.
- **"seagulls.":** the word sits alone on the last line of the left column, a runt (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Rewrap the last lines to pull another word down.
- **Apostrophes:** the ones in "o'clock" and "children's" look straight rather than curly. That's common sense, not a checked rule. Switch to typographic apostrophes.

**What's working**
- The type itself is comfortable: one serif family, with a bold headline that is clearly dominant. Keep the face and the weight contrast.
- The columns run about 45–50 characters with generous line spacing, and the lines sit on shared baselines across all three columns. That's a properly built three-column text grid; keep it when you add headings.
- The writing has a warm, specific voice ("the pie that makes the judges laugh", "guard the cake tent against hungry seagulls"). Keep it in the body copy once the facts have moved out.
