**First read.** The eye goes straight to the navy 38% wedge, the strongest thing on the page, and then up to the serif headline. Then it moves into the fourteen-row legend on the right and starts matching colours back and forth. The upper left of the pie is a fan of nine slivers labelled 4%, 3%, 3%, 3%, 3%, 2%, 2%, 2%, 2%, 1%, with no names. The page feels calm, well finished and trustworthy, but it gives you a lookup job and no answer.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a page in a company annual report, for shareholders and the board, showing what operating costs were spent on in fiscal 2026.

**Verdict.** It looks professional but doesn't make its point. A 14-slice pie with a matching 14-colour legend makes the reader decode colours to learn something the table already tells them. Nowhere does the page say what the split means. The biggest problem is the choice of chart.

**What would move it most**

1. **The pie has too many slices for what it has to show.** About 60% of the circle carries four readable slices. The rest is nine slivers of 1–4%, and four of them are identical at 3% and four at 2%. They can't be told apart, and their outside labels give a number with no name. To find out what the third 3% sliver is, you match an ochre against 14 swatches, and the eye path stalls there. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1) both limit pies to about five slices. This one sits at the bad end of the realistic range. Angle and area are also hard to compare: nobody can see that Marketing (9%) is roughly three times Insurance (3%) from the wedges. That last point is my own judgment, not a cited rule. *Convention, and here it costs real reading time.* Direction: keep the four largest (Salaries, Rent, Cloud hosting, Marketing) and group the other nine into one "Other operating costs, 25%" with its breakdown in the table. Or replace the pie with a sorted horizontal bar chart with the names on the bars. Both are Few's approved fixes. The bar chart also lets you drop the legend.

2. **Fourteen hues that encode nothing but which category is which.** Each slice gets its own saturated colour: red, pink, salmon and brown sit next to each other, and so do blue and indigo, and teal and pale teal. Nothing stands out because everything is equally loud, and a reader with red-green colour blindness loses the link between slices and legend. Clockwise order matching the legend order is the only other cue, and that is too weak. Few (pitfall 12) and Wilke (ch. 19.1) say to draw one series in one colour and keep a second colour for the point the reader must notice. WCAG 1.4.1 says colour can't be the only link between a mark and its name. *Defect for colour-blind readers, convention otherwise.* Direction: this follows from fix 1. Use one hue, or a neutral plus one accent for Salaries, and label each item with its name.

3. **The headline names a topic, not a finding, and the shares have nothing to measure against.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" promises an answer the page never gives. The finding is right there in the numbers: staff, premises and cloud hosting took two-thirds (66%) of costs, and salaries alone took 38%. Shareholders also can't tell what 38% is in money, because the total operating cost appears nowhere. They can't tell whether anything moved either, because there is no fiscal 2025. In an annual report, "salaries 38%" means little until you know whether it was 31% last year. *Taste and common sense (no approved source here).* Direction: state the finding in the headline. Put the total in the subhead ("…of £X.Xm total operating costs"). Add a prior-year column or a change figure to the table on the right, since that is the comparison a reader will actually use.

**Smaller things.**
- White "12%" on the orange slice looks like about 2.8:1, below even the 3:1 large-text minimum (WCAG 1.4.3). "16%" on teal is borderline at about 3.3:1. Darken those slices or use dark labels.
- The light slices (Postage salmon, Equipment repair pale teal) look under 3:1 against the cream background (WCAG 1.4.11). Fix 1 removes them.
- The legend's right edge stops around x=1060, while the header sets a right margin around 1216. That leaves a dead strip on the right and an empty band below the source line. Run the table to the margin, or size both columns from one grid.
- Type scale: about seven sizes (roughly 32, 18, 17, 15, 14, 12, 11). The 14/15 and 11/12 pairs don't read as different levels. Fold them into three or four sizes and use weight and colour for the rest (Refactoring UI).

**What's working.**
- The table on the right is well made: right-aligned figures, hairline rules, bold values, and sorted by size. It already works as an honest, accessible data table. Keep it, and make it the place where the detail lives.
- The source line states the rounding ("Shares are rounded to whole percent") and the period is exact ("twelve months to 30 June 2026"). That precision is what a financial reader needs. Keep it.
- The serif headline over sans body, the small tracked "4.2 COSTS" kicker and the quiet running header give a clear, restrained hierarchy that suits an annual report.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this page reflows to one column on a narrow screen without the pie and its outside labels shrinking until they can't be read.
