**First read.** Your eye goes straight to the bold headline, then drops through the italic standfirst into the body text. Nothing pulls it sideways. It reads as a long-form feature on a small, independent local magazine: calm, plain, and quietly confident. Two things snag in the first few seconds. The word "line" sits alone under the headline, and the byline promises "Photographs by the residents", but there are no photographs.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the opening screen of an online magazine feature, meant to draw a general reader into a long piece of reportage on desktop.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the text is easy to read, and the opening copy is genuinely good. The biggest problem is that a story credited to the residents' photographs opens with no picture at all, so the people and the place stay abstract right where the page should win the reader over.

**What would move it most**
1. **The promised photographs aren't there.** The credit line under the headline reads "Photographs by the residents", and the first screen is all text, with about 220 px of empty white at the bottom. The reader is told there are pictures and doesn't see one. On a story about a physical place (pallets, bath tubs, the queue at the water tap), the first image is what makes the reader care. If a photo should sit here and didn't load, the layout is fine and the capture is the problem. If the first photo really comes later, the opening screen is wasting its best asset. Fix: put a lead photograph above the fold, either across the text column under the standfirst or wider above the headline, so the credit pays off at once. *(Defect, assuming the image is meant to be there.)*
2. **The headline breaks badly: "bus / line".** "Line" sits alone on the second line. That splits the phrase "bus line" and leaves a one-word runt under the largest text on the page. It's the first thing anyone reads, and it looks unconsidered. Fix: break at a phrase boundary, e.g. "The allotment at the end / of the bus line". Use a balanced wrap, narrow the headline a little, or edit the line by hand. (Bringhurst §2.4 on line-break etiquette. The rule for headings specifically is common sense, not a checked source.) *(Defect in craft, at the most visible point on the page.)*
3. **The body lines are too long.** The text column is about 715 px wide at roughly 18 px, so body lines run about 85–90 characters ("It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to"). Bringhurst gives 45–75 as comfortable and about 66 as ideal. On a short opening screen it's fine. Over a long feature, readers will lose their place more often on the way back to the start of each line. Leading is generous, which helps. Fix: narrow the column to roughly 620–640 px, or step the body size up a little. Measure the actual character count per line in the browser. *(Convention, Bringhurst, The Elements of Typographic Style §2.1.2.)*

**Smaller things.**
- Taste: neutral greys and Inter throughout are clean, but they're the default look of any modern blog. Only the small green "Feature" label hints at the earthy subject. One warmer element, such as a serif for the headline or text, or a slightly warmer paper tone, would make "Groundwork" feel like its own publication. The photographs from point 1 may be enough on their own.

**What's working.**
- **The copy.** The standfirst does real work: "Ten years ago… a car park nobody used. Today forty households…" sets a before and after with concrete numbers, and "the queue for the shared water tap is the best place in the district to hear the news" lands the story on people. The body opens on a short, hooky "It started with a letter." Keep that voice.
- **A clear, quiet hierarchy.** There are four levels: green small label, large bold headline, grey byline, then italic standfirst over regular body. Each differs from the next by more than size (weight, colour, or italic), so nothing competes and the order of reading is obvious.
- **Restraint in the chrome.** A slim header, a single hairline, and one accent colour keep all the attention on the story, which is right for long-form reading.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the lead photograph is designed to appear here and simply failed to load. Check the live page: it decides whether point 1 is a design fault or a capture fault.
