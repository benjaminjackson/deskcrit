**First read.** The eye goes to the illustration first, pulled by the red and yellow against the pale blue. Then it moves right to the bold serif headline and down three short paragraphs. It reads as the intro to a web page for a small heritage lighthouse, and it feels calm, friendly, coastal and well made.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming it's the top section of a web page for a lighthouse that's open to visitors, meant to make people want to go and tell them how.

**Verdict.** As a piece of layout and type it works. It's quiet and well ordered, with almost nothing wrong in the craft. What holds it back is the job: it makes you want to visit and then gives you nothing to act on. The practical facts are vague and tucked into the story, and there's no next step.

**What would move it most**
1. **The visit has no when, how much, where, or next step. (Defect, if this page is meant to bring visitors.)** The words that matter to a visitor are "open to visitors on summer weekends" and "Tickets are sold at the cottage by the car park." They sit in the middle of paragraph 2 and in paragraph 3, set like the rest of the story. Both can be read more than one way:
   - "Summer weekends" could mean June to August or Easter to September. It gives no hours, and it doesn't say whether "a clear morning" means the tower opens in the morning.
   - The ticket line could mean you can only buy on the day, or just that this is one place to buy. A family that drives out on the wrong Saturday, or turns up after closing, will hold the page to it.
   - There's no price, no address, and nothing to click.

   Fix: keep the story, but pull the facts out into a short block under the text, set apart by weight or a small label rather than a new size. Give open dates, hours, ticket price, where to park, and one specific link such as "Plan your visit" or "Opening times and prices". If this is meant as editorial (a feature or guidebook entry) rather than a visitor page, this finding falls away, and the piece mostly does its job.

2. **The illustration is generic where the copy is specific. (Taste.)** The copy earns its charm with detail: ninety steps, brass plaques in the lamp room, the ferry across to the harbour wall, scones in the tea room. The picture is a stock flat-vector lighthouse with nothing of Gull Point in it. There's no headland (the place is called a Point), no estuary, no cottage, and no lit lamp even though the headline is "The light". The tower's base sits exactly on the horizon line, so it looks as if it stands in open sea. That clashes with "Point" and "mouth of the estuary", and the edges just touching look like an accident. The picture is the first thing anyone looks at, but it could be any lighthouse anywhere. Fix: give the illustration one or two details from this place, such as the point of land the tower stands on, the cottage, or the ferry. That way the picture backs up the copy instead of just showing a lighthouse.

**Smaller things.**
- At the end of paragraph 1, "room." sits alone on the last line, a one-word runt (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.4.2). Fix it by rewording, or by tying "lamp room" together with a non-breaking space.

**What's working.**
- **The grid is exact.** The top of the image lines up with the headline's cap height, the bottom lines up with the last baseline, and one clear gutter separates image and text. The two halves lock into one block. Keep that alignment when you add content.
- **The type scale is clean.** There are two sizes, and the headline gets its contrast from bold weight as well as size, all in one serif family. The text runs about 68 characters a line with generous leading, which is comfortable to read. If you add a facts block, set it apart with weight or colour, not a third size that's close to an existing one.
- **The copy speaks to the reader.** "Climb to the gallery on a clear morning and you can follow the ferry all the way across the bay" puts the reader at the top of the tower, which a list of features couldn't do. Keep that voice in the story part.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the two columns stack on a narrow screen. Check that the illustration doesn't push the headline below the first screen on a phone.
