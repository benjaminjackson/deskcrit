**First read.** Your eye goes straight to "Harbour Lights Festival", set very large in a cream italic serif. The amber date above it is the second thing you see, then the short paragraph. It reads as the landing page for a local summer evening festival. The night-navy background with the warm amber gives a calm, lantern-lit feel, and it looks well made. Then the eye reaches the end of the paragraph and has nowhere else to go.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of the web page for a free local festival. Its job is to get people to turn up: they need to know what it is, when it is, where it is, and what to do next.

**Verdict.** The type is confident and the mood suits the event, but as an invitation it doesn't work yet. It never says where the festival is, it gives no next step, and the date as written can't be right for 2026 or 2027. A stranger can't plan a visit from this page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page never says where, and offers nothing to do next.** *(Defect.)* The copy names "the quay" and "the old net shed", but there's no town, harbour or address anywhere. Only someone who already knows the place can use it, which is a Nielsen recognition-over-recall failure. There's also no link, button or line pointing to a map, a programme or travel details. So the eye goes date → title → paragraph and stops, and the bottom quarter of the frame (roughly y 610–800) is empty, as if a block is missing. People who can't place an event in their own diary and map don't go. Fix: put the place in the same amber line as the date (for example "Saturday 14 June, from noon · [Town] Harbour"). Then use the empty lower band for one specific action, such as "See the map and the programme" or "Find the net shed". Keep it visibly a link or button, and keep it smaller than the title.
2. **The date is probably wrong, and it's half a time.** *(Defect.)* 14 June is a Saturday in 2025 and 2031, not in 2026 (a Sunday) or 2027 (a Monday). A date is what people put in their calendar and hold you to. If this is for 2026, half your readers will turn up on the wrong day. If it's last year's page, it's advertising a past event. Check the date and add the year. "From noon" also gives a start with no end. Since the paragraph sells dusk, say until when (for example "noon till late" or "noon–11pm") so people know they can come in the evening.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break, *taste*: "Harbour / Lights Festival" splits the name "Harbour Lights" across the two lines. "Harbour Lights / Festival" keeps it whole. It's a small point at this size, but the name is the whole brand.

**What's working.**
- **The type contrast is clear.** The italic display serif, the plain sans for the paragraph and the letterspaced small caps for the date each do a different job. The hierarchy comes from size, form and colour together, not just size. Keep these three levels.
- **The colour matches the event.** The near-black navy reads as harbour at night, the amber reads as lantern light, and the cream text avoids harsh pure white on dark. Amber is used only on the date, so it stands out. Save it for the date/place line and the new action, and don't spread it further.
- **The layout is simple and well spaced.** Everything hangs from one left edge, with a generous margin. The paragraph runs about 60 characters a line, which is comfortable to read, and the line "Free entry for everyone" ends it with a real reason to come.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the large italic headline is live text or an image of text. If it's an image, screen readers and search lose the festival name.
- How the roughly 130 px headline reflows on a phone-width screen. Check that "Festival" doesn't overflow or break mid-word.
