**First read.** The eye lands on the big headline, "Plan the week with your whole team", then jumps right to the white card and the blue "Create account" button. Within two seconds it reads as a standard SaaS sign-up page: tidy, calm and competent. It's also forgettable. The left half stops a third of the way down, so the page reads as a form with a caption next to it rather than a pitch.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the sign-up page for a team weekly-planning tool. It should turn a visitor who is curious but undecided into a free account, probably someone who arrived from a marketing page or an invite.

**Verdict.** The form itself is well built, and it will work for people who have already decided to sign up. For everyone else the page doesn't sell. The left half has one generic line and an empty void where the reason to sign up should be, and the one concrete promise, "free … for up to five people", can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The left half gives no reason to fill in five fields.** Below the two-line subhead, about 420 px of the left column is empty grey, roughly half the page. The visitor sees what they're asked for (name, email, password, company) but nothing about what they get: no glimpse of a planned week, no example board, no proof that anyone uses this. The emptiness looks like space left over, not space someone shaped. The heavy white card on the right has nothing to balance it, so the page tilts and the left side feels unfinished (Ruder on printed and unprinted areas standing in tension; Tschichold and Müller-Brockmann on empty space that is chosen rather than leftover). The headline makes it worse. "Plan the week with your whole team" could sit on any of a dozen tools, so it can't tell the visitor what is different here. *Defect* (the page persuades less than it should); the headline point is *taste*. **Fix:** fill the lower left with something that shows the product doing its job. A real week board with a few teammates' names on it would work, because the visitor can compare it with how they plan their week now. Then sharpen the headline to say what this tool does that their current spreadsheet or standup doesn't.

2. **"Start a free workspace for up to five people" is a commitment line with two readings.** One reading is that it's free forever for teams of five or fewer. The other is a free trial, after which you pay. "You can invite the rest of the team later" adds a third question: is inviting a sixth person free, or does it trigger billing? People remember lines like this and hold the company to them. If the visitor reads "free forever" and later meets a paywall, the brand pays for it in trust. If they read it as a trial when it isn't, some won't sign up at all. *Defect.* **Fix:** say it outright and from the visitor's side, e.g. "Free for teams of up to 5, for as long as you like. No card needed. Adding a sixth person moves you to [plan]." Use whatever is actually true.

3. **The form doesn't say what's required, and the password rule disappears as soon as you type.** None of the five fields is marked required or optional. Company in particular looks optional for a 5-person free tier, yet it carries the same weight as Email. So the visitor has to fill everything in, or guess and maybe hit an error. The password rule, "At least 10 characters", exists only as placeholder text. It vanishes on the first keystroke, which is exactly when the person needs it, so they type an 8-character habit password and get bounced. *Convention* (Nielsen Norman Group: Budiu on marking required fields, Sherwin on placeholders): breaking it here costs failed submits and a longer form. **Fix:** put "(optional)" after any optional label, and consider cutting Company until after sign-up. Move the password rule into helper text below the field that stays visible while typing.

**Smaller things.**
- "Sign in" under the button is the same grey as "Already have an account?", with no underline and no colour. Returning users can't see it's a link. Give it the link blue or an underline.
- Placeholders that repeat their labels ("Enter first name", "Enter last name", "Company name") add noise and nothing else. Leave those empty, or use them only for a real example like the email one does.
- The input borders look pale grey-blue on white, probably below the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for on a component edge. Measure the border against white and darken it if it falls short.

**What's working.**
- The form's type and spacing are disciplined. There are about five sizes, each with a clear job, and labels are bold and sit tight to their fields. The gap between field groups is clearly bigger than the gap from label to field, so the form scans top to bottom without effort. Keep that rhythm when you add helper text.
- Putting first and last name side by side is the right exception to a single-column form: they're short and belong together. Every other field is full width.
- There is one blue, and it means "act". The filled "Create account" button is the only saturated block in the card, so there's no doubt where to click.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Error states and inline validation: what happens on a bad email or a short password.
- Focus rings on the inputs and the button, and whether they meet 3:1.
- Whether the password field has a show/hide toggle.
- How the two columns stack on a narrow screen. Check that the pitch on the left doesn't vanish or end up below the form.
