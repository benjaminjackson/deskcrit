**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", then moves into three even, tidy paragraphs. It reads as a calm, well-set information page. But when I look for the thing a ferry passenger wants (when is the next boat?), there's nothing to catch: no time, no number, nothing pulled out. I had to read all twelve lines to find out the page doesn't say.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web notice telling passengers when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and the rules for bikes. Most readers will glance at it before or while travelling.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and correct. The page still fails its job, because the facts a passenger acts on are either missing or vague, and the ones it does give are buried in running prose. The biggest problem is the copy, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times that matter are missing or can be read two ways** (defect). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last departure, so nobody can plan around it. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" (paragraph 3) is worse, and it's the line people will hold the operator to:
   - "the quay" could mean the market quay only, or both ends. A passenger on the north side who reads it the wrong way could be stranded.
   - "before dusk" changes by weeks across a winter and isn't a time anyone can check against a watch.
   - "In winter" has no dates, so a reader in early November can't tell which timetable applies.

   Fix: give clock times for the first and last boat from each landing, the winter start and end dates, and the winter last-departure times. If they change monthly, say so and give the range.
2. **Nothing can be skimmed** (defect). Passengers scan for numbers, but every figure is spelled out inside a sentence ("forty minutes", "twelve minutes", "one boat each hour"). Each fact sits in the middle of a line at the same weight as everything around it. With no digits, labels, or table, the page has to be read in full every time, which is the opposite of how people use a timetable (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Fix: put the hard facts in a block someone can take in at a glance, like a small table or a short list of times, frequency, crossing length and bike fare, set in figures. Keep the prose for the softer guidance, like boarding order and bags on bikes. There's plenty of room for it in the empty right column or straight under the headline.
3. **The headline doesn't say which ferry** (defect, mild). "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any operator's page. A passenger who lands here from a search can't confirm it's the right crossing until the first sentence. Fix: name the route in the headline, for example "North landing ⇄ Market quay ferry", and say what the page gives them.

**What's working.**
- The reading column is set well. Lines run about 65–72 characters, line spacing is about 1.5, and paragraphs are marked by space alone, so the prose is easy to read (Bringhurst). Keep these settings when you add the facts block.
- The hierarchy is just two levels made from size plus bold, in one family, so nothing competes with the headline (Refactoring UI). Any facts block can use weight or figures to stand out without adding a third size.
- The bike paragraph is good copy. It says who does what, in what order, and where ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). Use it as the model for the rest.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the column reflows cleanly at phone width matters most here, because many readers will be on the landing with a phone. Check it at 320 px.
