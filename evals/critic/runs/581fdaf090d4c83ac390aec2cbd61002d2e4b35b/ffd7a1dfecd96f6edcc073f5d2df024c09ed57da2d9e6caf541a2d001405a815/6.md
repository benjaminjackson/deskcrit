**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading at top left, then moves straight into three even paragraphs of plain sans text. It reads at once as a quiet information page for people taking a small river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and almost bare: the right 40% of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web or kiosk page that tells passengers how to take the ferry: when it runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike. They should be able to plan a trip from it.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound and the page reads easily, but it doesn't do its job. It never gives a passenger a time they can plan around, and it sends them to a printed timetable at the landing instead. What's missing is content and structure, not styling.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times are vague, so nobody can plan from this page.** *Defect.* The first paragraph says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening." The third says "one boat each hour" "in winter" and that the last crossing leaves "before dusk." A passenger will act on these lines, and each can be read more than one way:
   - "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00.
   - "Winter" could mean December to February, or the change of the clocks.
   - "Before dusk" moves by an hour across the season, and it's the line where a wrong guess leaves someone stranded on the wrong bank.

   The page then says the real timetable is printed in the shelter at each landing. That's the one place a person planning ahead can't see it. The fix is to put the actual first and last departures and the winter dates on this page, ideally as a small timetable or a short list: summer and winter, first and last boat, frequency. If the times really do vary, link to the current week's changes instead of pointing to the noticeboard. (This is my judgment of what a passenger needs, not a cited rule.)
2. **The facts people look for are buried in sentences.** *Taste, but it costs real speed here.* Someone checking the page on a phone at the landing wants three answers: when is the next boat, how long does it take, and can I bring my bike. Right now they have to read whole paragraphs to dig those out. The paragraphs are short, so this isn't a wall of text. Still, putting the key facts up front would let the page be scanned: small subheads such as "Times," "Bicycles" and "Winter," or a short list of times and the crossing length above the prose. The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" is a category label. Something that names the route ("North landing ↔ Market quay") would tell people at once that they're on the right page.
3. **The bike instructions leave out what a cyclist must do.** *Defect, smaller in scope.* "Lock them to the rail" doesn't say whether riders need to bring their own lock. A rider without one only finds out on board. "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" is written as a fact about the crew, not an instruction to the reader. "Keep your bags on your bike during the crossing" says it directly. Spell out what to bring and what to do, and address the reader as "you."

**Smaller things.**
- The empty right side of the frame is fine for a reading column. If the timetable gets added, it could go there, beside the prose.

**What's working.**
- The measure is about 70 characters per line, with leading of about 1.5. That's comfortable reading, right in the range Bringhurst gives, so keep it whatever else changes.
- The type scale is two sizes with a clear step, plus bold for the heading. Hierarchy needs nothing more on a page this size.
- Near-black text on white, set flush left, with paragraphs marked by space alone: plain, readable and correct.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Reflow on a phone. It matters if passengers check this page at the landing.
