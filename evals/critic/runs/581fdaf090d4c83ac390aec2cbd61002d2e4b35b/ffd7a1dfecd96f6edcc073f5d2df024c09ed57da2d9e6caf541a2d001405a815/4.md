**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, top left, then drops straight into three even, calm paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for people taking a small river ferry: quiet, tidy and trustworthy. Nothing on the page pulls attention, and the right half of the screen is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells ferry passengers how the service works: when it runs, how long it takes, the bicycle rules and winter changes. It's for people who arrive with one question, like "when's the last boat?", and want to find the answer quickly.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound and the page reads comfortably. Where it falls short is the copy and the structure. The facts a passenger came for are buried in prose and given in vague terms, so the page is pleasant to read but hard to use.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times aren't stated, so a passenger can't plan.** (Defect.) In the first paragraph the service runs "between early morning and late evening". In the third, the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk". No clock time appears anywhere. These are exactly the lines a passenger acts on, and each can be read two ways. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00. "Before dusk" could mean 15:30 or 16:45. If a reader guesses wrong, they miss the last boat. The page then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which only helps once they've already walked to the landing. Give first and last departures as times, for each direction and each season. If the times change, say where to find the current ones before the reader leaves home, not at the shelter.
2. **Three topics run together as prose with no labels.** (Defect. Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web": text people skim is broken up with headings, short paragraphs and lists.) The paragraphs are short, so this isn't a wall of text. But each covers a different subject: timetable, bicycles, winter. Only the order shows where one ends and the next starts. A cyclist has to read the first paragraph to learn it isn't about them. Add short subheads such as "When it runs", "Bringing a bike" and "Winter". Then pull the hard numbers (every 40 min, about 12 min crossing, hourly in winter) to the start of their sections, or into a short list, so a glance finds them.
3. **The headline names a category instead of saying something.** (Taste, argued.) "Notes for ferry passengers" could head any ferry's page. It tells the reader nothing they couldn't guess. A headline that names the route ("North landing ⇄ Market quay ferry") or gives the main fact ("A boat every 40 minutes, north landing to market quay") answers the reader's first question in the largest type on the page.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing. The checklist turned up no other failures.

**What's working.**
- The measure and leading are right for reading. Lines run about 65–72 characters with roughly 1.5 line spacing, so a reader moves through the text without losing their place (Bringhurst). Keep this column width when you add subheads.
- The type scale is disciplined. There are two sizes: a heavy headline at about twice the body size, and the body. Weight and size together make the headline clearly the top level. Any subheads you add should be a weight change at or near body size, not a third size squeezed in between (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).
- The near-black text on white is easy to read and calm. It suits a public-service notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't check how the column reflows at narrow widths or 400% zoom (WCAG reflow). That matters here, because many readers will check this page on a phone at the landing.
