**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three even grey paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page from a local ferry service. It feels calm and orderly, and a little like a manual. Nothing in the body stands out, so I had to read every sentence to find out when the boat goes.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or printed notice telling passengers when the ferry runs, how bikes work, and what changes in winter, for people who want to plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound and I found nothing in the layout to fix. The page fails its job in the words: it never gives a time anyone could plan around, so a passenger still has to go to the landing to read the timetable.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times you'd act on are vague (defect, in the copy).** Paragraphs 1 and 3 say "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening," "one boat each hour," and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk."
   - These are the facts a passenger plans around, and none of them is a real time. "Early morning" could be 6:00 or 8:00.
   - "Before dusk" moves by more than an hour across the winter, and "the quay" could be read as either landing. Someone could reasonably expect a boat back from the north landing and find the last one has already gone.
   - "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes… a little longer when the tide turns" has the same problem: "a little" gives no figure to allow for.
   - Fix: give the first and last departures from each landing in summer and winter, name the date the winter timetable starts, and say which landing "the quay" means.
   - The page then sends people to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing." That points them to a place they can only reach by going there. If this lives online, link the timetable or put it on the page.
2. **The key numbers are buried in prose (taste).** The frequency, the crossing time and the winter change are all inside sentences in the same weight as everything else. Someone checking the page a second time has to read it again from the top.
   - The paragraphs are short enough that this isn't a chunking failure. Still, once real times exist, I'd pull them into a small timetable or a two-line summary under the heading and leave the bicycle rules as prose.
   - I'm also counting the heading here. "Notes for…" could head any page and tells the reader nothing about what's in it.

**What's working.**
- **The reading column.** Lines run about 65–72 characters with generous leading and space between paragraphs, which makes the prose easy to read (Bringhurst's 45–75 range). Keep this column width when you add a timetable.
- **A two-step type scale.** One bold heading and one body size, with the heading set apart by both weight and size, so there's no doubt where to start.
- **The bicycle paragraph.** It's the one piece of copy that says who does what and when: wheel on after foot passengers, lock to the side rail, keep bags on the bike. Write the timetable copy to that standard.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, and whether the large empty area on the right is meant to hold content that didn't load.
