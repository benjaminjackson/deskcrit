**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline at top left, then drops into three grey paragraphs that look alike. It reads as a plain information page for people who use a river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and easy to read, but nothing in the text stands out, so finding a particular fact means reading every paragraph.

**What it's for.** You didn't tell me, so I'm assuming this is a web page that people about to take this ferry check to find when it runs and what the rules are, often on a phone at the landing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound, but the page doesn't do its job. A passenger can't get the one thing they came for: a time they can plan around. The schedule is written in vague words ("early morning", "late evening", "before dusk") and spread across the paragraphs, so the page explains how the ferry works without saying when to be there.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times can't be acted on (defect).** Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "one boat each hour". None of these is a time a passenger can catch, and dusk moves by about an hour across a winter. "Leaves the quay" can be read two ways. It could mean the market quay only, so the last boat from the north landing is unknown. Or it could mean either landing. A passenger who picks the wrong reading is stranded on the wrong bank. "In winter" doesn't say which months, and "one boat each hour" doesn't say at what minute past. These are the lines people remember and hold the operator to, so each one needs to be exact: first and last departure as clock times from each landing, the departure minutes, the months the winter timetable runs, and the landing named every time. Right now the page sends people to the "printed timetables… in the shelter", so the one place that should answer the question points them somewhere else.
2. **The schedule is split up and buried (defect, judged from the job rather than a rule).** Timetable facts open paragraph 1 and come back in paragraph 3, with the bicycle rules between them. Someone who wants the last boat has to read all three paragraphs and put the answer together themselves. Put all the timing first, in one place, with summer and winter side by side so the difference shows at a glance. A short two-row table would do it. After that, give each paragraph a short label ("Bicycles", "Changes to the timetable") so a passenger can skip to the part they need. The type and spacing can stay as they are.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not this ferry. Naming the route (north landing to market quay) tells a stranger at once that they're on the right page.
- "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" is fine. But "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" is the instruction cyclists actually act on, and it sits in the middle of a sentence. Make it its own short line.

**What's working.**
- The measure looks like about 70 characters a line, and the line spacing looks generous, around 1.5. Together they make the paragraphs comfortable to read (Bringhurst). Keep both when you add structure.
- The hierarchy is clear with just two levels: a bold, clearly larger headline over one body size, in one family, all dark grey on white. There are no stray sizes to clean up.
- Paragraphs are marked by space alone, and the gap is the same each time, so the page reads as calm and in order.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How it reflows on a phone, which is where most passengers will read it (check at 320 px wide).
