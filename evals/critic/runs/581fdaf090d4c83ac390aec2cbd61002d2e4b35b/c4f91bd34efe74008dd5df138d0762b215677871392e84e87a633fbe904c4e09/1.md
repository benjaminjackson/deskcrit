**First read.** The eye lands straight on the big serif quote and reads it as a magazine pull quote: warm, unhurried, a bit literary. Next it drops to the bold line under it, "said a volunteer…", and only last reaches the small grey sentence that says what is actually happening. The feel is right for a library: calm and human. But for the first few seconds you don't know what the news is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the lead of a library's newsletter (email or web), telling patrons the reading room is reopening and making them want to come back.

**Verdict.** It looks good, and the type is handled with care. But the order of information is upside down: the one fact a reader needs to act on (it reopens Monday) is the smallest, faintest text on the page, and "Monday" doesn't say which Monday. The quote only lands as a joke once you already know the room was renovated, so it should frame the news, not stand in for it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The news is at the bottom of the hierarchy, and its date is vague.** The page has three levels: the quote at about 96 px, the bold dark attribution at about 20 px, and the announcement in light grey at about 18 px. The eye path ends on the line that carries the message. A reader who skims the headline and attribution (most newsletter readers) comes away with a nice anecdote and no idea that anything is happening. On its own the quote is a puzzle: "the 'old' reading room" only makes sense once you know there's a new one. "Reopens on Monday" is the one thing readers will act on and hold the library to, and it can be read two ways: the coming Monday from whenever they open the email, or a Monday someone meant when it was written. Read late, it sends people on the wrong day. Direction: keep the quote as the hero, but put the news where the eye already goes. The usual moves are to make the kicker carry it ("The reading room reopens Monday, 1 December", with a real date and opening time), or to give the announcement line more weight than the attribution. *Defect.*
2. **The opening quotation mark pushes the headline's first line in.** At this size the "“" is about 40 px wide and sits on the left margin, so "It's" starts to the right of "room" and "anything". The strongest edge on the page wobbles in its first line. Let the opening mark hang in the margin so the letters line up on the 110 px edge that the kicker and text share. No approved source states this for display quotes, so this is common sense: at 96 px the offset is plainly visible, which isn't true at text size. *Convention (taste-backed).*

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks "reading / room", which splits the name of the thing the page is about. Rebreak it so "reading room" stays on one line, for example "“It's the 'old' / reading room—nobody / calls it anything else,”".
- The attribution sits about as close to the announcement below as to the quote above, so the spacing doesn't show what belongs together. Pull the attribution up toward the quote, or open the gap before the announcement.

**What's working.**
- The scale contrast is strong: one very large serif against small sans text gives the page a clear entry point and real tension. Keep that ratio.
- The pairing of serif display with sans text reads as plain contrast, not conflict. Typographic details are right: curly quotes, a closed em dash, a tracked small-caps kicker, and an announcement line about 60 characters long.
- The palette is restrained: cream ground, near-black, one rust accent used only on the kicker. It fits the subject and stays readable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the headline is live text or an image of text, and how the 96 px quote reflows on a phone, where the "reading / room" break and the line lengths will change.
