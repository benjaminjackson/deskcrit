**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Account settings", then runs down four identical white cards, each with a blue outlined button on the right. It reads as a calm, standard SaaS settings page, tidy and quiet. Then the left rail gets in the way: "Billing" is highlighted, yet the page is titled "Account settings" and shows Profile, Notifications, Billing and Sessions together. After a second you still aren't sure which page you're on.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the settings home for a workspace member in a B2B web app (Tallis Cloud). It should show what can be changed and send people to the right sub-page quickly.

**Verdict.** The visual craft is competent and almost nothing is wrong at the level of type or spacing. The page fails at the one thing a settings screen has to get right: telling you where you are and how settings are organised. The sidebar and the cards are two different, conflicting maps of the same thing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sidebar marks the wrong page and contradicts the cards** (defect; Nielsen heuristic 1, visibility of system status; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). The left rail highlights "Billing" with a fill and bold text. The content area has the heading "Account settings" and shows an overview where Billing is the third of four equal cards. Someone who clicked Billing expects billing: plan, payment method, invoices. Someone who arrived at "Account settings" sees a sidebar that tells them they're somewhere else. Either way they stop and check. This may be a capture of the wrong state, but if it ships like this it's a bug. The fix: the highlighted item and the page heading must name the same place. If this is an overview, it needs its own nav entry (or no highlight). If it's the Billing page, it should show billing content.
2. **Two navigation systems that disagree about how settings are organised** (defect; Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, conceptual model; Nielsen heuristic 4, consistency). The sidebar lists Profile, Notifications, Billing, Members, Integrations, Security. The cards list Profile, Notifications, Billing, Sessions. Three destinations can be reached twice on one screen, and three sidebar items have no card. "Sessions" has no sidebar entry. It probably lives under "Security", but nothing says so, so someone looking for "where do I sign out my old laptop" has to guess. Pick one model. Either the sidebar is the navigation and the content area shows one section, or the overview cards cover every sidebar section with the same names and the same order.
3. **The Billing card leaves out what people come to billing for** (defect, copy). "Your plan renews on 1 March. The invoice goes to the address on file." It gives no plan name, no amount, and no address, so the reader learns that something will be charged to somewhere, but not what or where. A billing date is a commitment people hold you to, so it needs the plan and the price ("Team plan, £240/month, renews 1 March 2027"). "The address on file" makes them go and check, so show the address. "View invoices" is the only action, but the copy is really asking "is this right?", and you can't answer that by looking at old invoices. It needs a way to change the plan or the billing address.

**Smaller things.**
- Sessions card copy: "You are signed in on three now" is a number without its noun. Say "three devices" and name the current one, so "Review" has a reason to be clicked.
- "Edit", "Manage" and "Review" are generic labels. Next to their card titles they're fine, but "Review devices" or "Change plan" would tell people what happens next.

**What's working.**
- The cards follow one strict pattern: bold title, one-sentence description in grey, outline button aligned right on a shared edge. That makes the list fast to scan. Keep it when you fix the structure.
- The Profile and Notifications descriptions say what the setting does to the user ("appear on comments and in the member list", "reach your inbox"), not just what the category is. That's the right voice for the other cards too.
- Buttons are kept to equal outline weight. There's no false primary action, which is correct for an index of destinations.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the "Billing" highlight is a stale state from the capture rather than the design (check this in a live browser first, because finding 1 depends on it). Also focus states on the buttons and nav items, and how the two columns reflow when the window is narrow.
