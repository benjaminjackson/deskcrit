**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top section of a local passenger ferry's landing page. Its job is to get a resident or visitor to plan a crossing and open the timetable.

**Verdict.** It's clean and readable, but it doesn't yet do its job. It promises a lovely crossing without saying where the boat goes, when the first and last boats leave, or what a ticket costs. The layout then leaves half the screen empty where those facts or a picture of the crossing should be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out the facts a traveller acts on** (defect; common sense). The page never names the bay, says what "the north landing" belongs to, or says where the boat lands. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives the interval but not the first and last sailings, and those are what someone planning a trip needs. There's no fare. A visitor can't tell whether this crossing works for them without clicking away, and a local can't check the last boat home at a glance. Put the hard facts in the paragraph or just under the headline: both ends by name ("Northport to Saltbay"), the real first and last times ("first boat 6:40, last 22:20, every 40 minutes") and the fare. That also gives "See the timetable" something specific to open, and you could make it name the route.

2. **All the weight sits in the left 60%, and the rest looks like leftover space** (convention and taste). The headline, paragraph, list and button stack in one column from x≈160 to x≈780. Nothing on the right or along the bottom balances them, so the composition tips left and the empty area reads as unfinished, not as chosen (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"; Tschichold, *The New Typography*, and Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*, on space that is shaped on purpose). My view as a designer: a calm twelve-minute crossing with an open deck is sold by showing it, and the headline has no visual to complete it. Either give the right side a photo of the boat on the bay, or a map of the route with both landings, or use that space for the times and fare from finding 1. If an image is supposed to load there, check it in a live browser. Nothing in this capture shows that one should.

3. **The feature list repeats the paragraph and looks like a stray second paragraph** (defect). "Bicycles travel free" repeats "Bring a bicycle", and "Seats on the open deck" repeats "the open deck", so two of the three lines tell the reader nothing new. The list is also set at about 18px against the paragraph's ~20px, in the same weight and colour, with no markers. The two sizes look like one level with a mistake in it, not two levels. I count five sizes in all (~14, ~18, ~18 bold, ~20, ~56), and 18 and 20 collide (Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style*, 3.1.1 "Don't compose without a scale"). Use the list for new facts the paragraph doesn't give: the fare, step-free boarding, pets, how to pay. Either set it at the paragraph's size, or step it down clearly with a marker or icon so it plainly reads as a list.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "twelve / calm minutes", which splits the number from "minutes" (Bringhurst 2.4.6). Break after "bay" instead: "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes".
- There's no logo or navigation. The brand is only 14px blue text, and visitors expect a logo at top left that links home (Jakob's law). This is fine if a site header sits above what was captured.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete promise with a number and a feeling ("twelve calm minutes"), and its size and weight make it the clear first read. Keep it.
- Blue appears only on the brand name and the button, so the one action stands out without competing.
- The paragraph reads comfortably: about 65 to 70 characters per line, generous line spacing, a clean left edge shared by every block, and strong contrast throughout.
