**First read.** The eye lands straight on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and the three short lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. Within two seconds it reads as the top of a local ferry operator's website. It feels clean, quiet and trustworthy. It also feels unfinished, because everything is packed into the top-left and the whole right half of the frame is blank.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about the piece, so I'm assuming it's the hero section of a ferry company's landing page. Its job is to get a local or a visitor to decide to take the boat and check when it leaves.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy works: one headline, one action, a clear path between them. But the page doesn't give the viewer the facts they decide on (when the boats run, what a ticket costs, where to board), and it leaves half the screen empty, so it looks like a draft rather than a finished page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy is pleasant but leaves out what the viewer acts on** (defect). The headline has a real number in it, which is good. The paragraph then goes vague exactly where the reader needs precision: "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no first or last boat. Someone planning a commute or a late return can't tell whether 06:30 or 22:00 is covered, and they'll hold you to whatever they assume. There's no fare anywhere. "North landing" only means something to people who already know the place. The three-line list mostly restates the paragraph: "Bicycles travel free" repeats "Bring a bicycle", and "Seats on the open deck" repeats "from the open deck". So the space where new facts could go is used to say things twice. Common sense says to show what the viewer needs next rather than make them go looking. Turn the list into the deciding facts: first and last sailing, the fare (and that bikes are free), and where the north landing is. Make the button point at a specific thing, such as the next departure time with today's timetable behind it, so the viewer can check it against their own plans instead of trusting "early morning".
2. **The right half of the frame is empty, and it looks like leftover space, not a choice** (defect, unless an image failed to load). All the weight sits in a column about 640 px wide at the top left, and nothing on the right balances it. The empty band at the bottom (below about y≈570) adds to the feeling that the content was dropped onto a canvas sized for something else (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). If a harbour photo or illustration is supposed to sit there, check it in a live browser, because the page depends on it. If nothing is planned, that's a missed chance: the headline promises "calm" and "watch the harbour slide past", and a picture of the bay from the deck would show it. Either fill the right column with that picture, aligned to the text block's top and bottom, or commit to a narrow column with the space shaped on purpose.

**Smaller things.**
- The paragraph (about 20 px) and the three list lines (about 18 px) are close enough in size to look like a mistake rather than two levels (Bringhurst, scale). I count five text sizes in total. Set the list at body size, or separate it by weight or by bullets, not by 2 px.
- The headline breaks as "…in twelve / calm minutes", which pulls the number away from "minutes". Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes", or narrow the measure so it breaks there.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete, checkable promise (twelve minutes) and adds a feeling (calm). Keep that pairing of number and mood when you rewrite the rest.
- There's exactly one action, a filled button in the only strong colour on the page, so nobody wonders what to do next.
- The type is easy to read: one sans family, dark text on white, the paragraph at about 60–70 characters per line with generous line spacing, and a bold headline that dominates without shouting.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half is a hero image that didn't load. Check in a live browser.
- The button's hover and focus states, and how the column reflows on a phone.
