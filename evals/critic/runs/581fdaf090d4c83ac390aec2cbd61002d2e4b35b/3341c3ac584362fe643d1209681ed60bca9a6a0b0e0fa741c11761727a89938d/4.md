**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold black headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then down the paragraph and the three short lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a small ferry operator's home page. It feels tidy, quiet and trustworthy, but also bare: all the content sits in a column in the left 60% of the frame, and the right side and the bottom are empty white.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the opening screen of a ferry company's website, meant to persuade locals and day-trippers that the crossing is quick and pleasant and to send them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The typography and order of reading are sound and the headline is good. But as a ferry page it doesn't yet do its job. It never shows the trip, and it leaves out the facts a rider acts on: where the boat goes, when the first and last boats run, and what it costs. Those gaps matter more than anything in the type.

**What would move it most**

1. **The page tells you about the crossing but never shows it, and leaves half the frame empty.** Everything is in the left column. The right 40% and the bottom quarter are blank, so the layout tips left and the empty space looks like a missing slot rather than a choice. An empty area of that size with no clear purpose is a fault (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). A lopsided layout needs a reason to be lopsided (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*). The bigger cost is persuasion. The copy sells a feeling ("calm", "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck"), and the viewer has to take it on trust because nothing shows the water, the boat or the route. Except for one blue accent, this could be the top of any software product's page.
   *Fix:* put the crossing in that space: a photo from the open deck, or a simple map of the bay with the two landings and "12 min" on the line between them. A map would also answer finding 2. If an image was meant to be there and didn't load in this capture, check it in a live browser; with the image in place, most of this finding goes away. (Defect for the job; the choice between photo and map is taste.)

2. **The copy leaves out what a rider needs before acting.** "Cross the bay" and "the north landing" never say where the boat goes. A stranger has to guess the other end of the trip. "From early morning until late evening" is vague in exactly the place people plan by: someone catching the last boat home needs a time, not "late evening". Price isn't mentioned at all, except that bicycles are free. "Every forty minutes" is concrete and good, and so is "twelve minutes".
   *Fix:* name both ends ("from the north landing to [town]"), give the first and last sailings as times, and state the fare. Consider making the button do some of the work. "See the timetable" is already a specific ask, but "Next boat 10:40 · see the full timetable" answers the question the viewer came with and makes the page feel live. (Defect: these are times and prices, which riders hold the operator to.)

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line break: "twelve / calm minutes" separates the number from its unit. Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" so the promise reads as one phrase (Bringhurst, section 2.4.6).
- Type scale: the paragraph looks about 20 px and the three feature lines about 18 px. They are the same face and weight, so they look like a mismatch rather than two levels (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1). Set the list the same size as the paragraph, or separate it by weight or colour instead.

**What's working.**
- There is one clear focal point and a straight path down to a single action. The headline is far larger and bolder than everything else, and the button is the only filled shape, so the order of reading never wavers. Keep that order when you add an image: the headline must still win.
- The headline is concrete and specific: a duration and a feeling, not "Welcome aboard". "Bicycles travel free" is the same: a fact the viewer can act on.
- The colour is restrained. One blue, used only for the brand name and the button, keeps the accent meaning "this is the action".

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover, focus and pressed states, and how the column reflows on a phone, where the image from finding 1 has to stack without pushing the button below the fold.
