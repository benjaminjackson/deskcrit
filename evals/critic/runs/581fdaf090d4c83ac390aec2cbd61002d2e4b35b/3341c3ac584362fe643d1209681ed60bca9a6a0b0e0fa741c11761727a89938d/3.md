**First read.** The eye lands straight on the bold headline "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes". It drops through the paragraph, skims three plain lines and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the hero section of a small local ferry's website. It feels tidy, quiet and trustworthy, but unfinished: everything is packed into the left 60% of the frame. The right half and the bottom are blank, and the blank looks like a missing picture rather than a choice.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a passenger ferry's landing page. Its job is to make day-trippers and locals want the crossing and trust it, and to send them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The basics are sound: one clear focal point, a clean reading order, one obvious action. But the piece is half-built. It sells a view it never shows, and it leaves out where the boat goes, when it runs and what it costs, which are the facts the visitor needs to plan the trip.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half is empty and nothing shows the crossing.** All the weight sits in a narrow left column, from the "Coastal Ferry Co." label down to the button. The right ~500 px and the bottom ~230 px of the 1280×800 frame are bare white. Visual weight needs a counterweight (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). Here nothing balances the heavy left side, so the layout tips. The space is too large and too unshaped to read as deliberate calm, so it reads as leftover, or as an image that didn't load. That's a **convention** break, and it costs you something specific. The copy sells a feeling ("calm minutes", "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck"), but the only proof is words. My view as a designer (**taste**): on a page like this the picture of the open deck *is* the pitch. Put a photo of the deck or the harbour in the right-hand columns, with its top and bottom lined up with the headline and the button. If an image is meant to sit there already, check in a live browser that it loads. If it does, this finding goes away.

2. **The copy leaves out what the visitor plans around.** Read as a stranger would:
   - "Cross the bay" doesn't say to where.
   - "The north landing" assumes you know where that is.
   - "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" is vague about exactly the thing people plan around: they need the first and last sailing ("7:10 am to 10:30 pm").
   - There is no fare anywhere.

   These are the times and prices people act on and later hold you to, so leaving them vague is a **defect**. It forces a click for basic facts that could sit right here (common sense: put the needed information in front of people). Also, "twelve calm minutes" has nothing to compare it with. The strongest version sets the crossing next to what the visitor already knows, such as the drive around the bay ("12 minutes across, or 40 by road"). Then they judge the saving themselves instead of taking "calm" on trust. The ask, "See the timetable", is good: concrete and honest about what happens next. Keep it, but answer the first-and-last-boat question before they have to click.

**Smaller things.**
- Type sizes: I count five (≈14, 54, 20, 18 and the 18 bold button). The paragraph (~20 px) and the three-line list (~18 px) are two text blocks a couple of pixels apart, which reads as a slip, not a level (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Set the list at the paragraph's size, or clearly smaller with a weight or colour change.
- The headline breaks as "Cross the bay in twelve / calm minutes", which splits the number from "minutes". Break after "bay" ("Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes") so the promise reads as one unit.
- There's no logo or navigation, only a small blue "Coastal Ferry Co." label. If this is the whole top of the page, visitors lose the usual home link and menu they expect at the top (Jakob's law, Nielsen). If it's a cropped section, ignore this.

**What's working.**
- The headline is specific and owned: "twelve calm minutes" makes a concrete promise a competitor couldn't copy word for word. Keep that voice when you add the facts.
- The hierarchy is clean. One dominant headline, a readable paragraph at a comfortable line length (~60–68 characters) and a single filled button mean the eye never has to choose.
- Restraint with colour: the blue appears only on the brand label and the button, so the action stands out without trying.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button, and how the layout reflows on a phone (especially once an image is added on the right).
