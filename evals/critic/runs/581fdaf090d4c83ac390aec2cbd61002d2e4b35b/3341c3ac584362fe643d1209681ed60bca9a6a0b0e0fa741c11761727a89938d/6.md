**First read.** The headline catches the eye first, which is right. From there it goes down the paragraph and the three short lines to the blue button. It reads as a ferry company's homepage and feels calm and orderly. It also feels unfinished, because everything sits in the left 60% and the right side is a blank white field.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming a homepage hero for a local ferry that should get people to take the crossing, residents and day-trippers alike.

**Verdict.** The headline is good, and the type hierarchy is clean, with one obvious action. But the page looks like a template with the picture missing, and it leaves out the facts a traveller needs to decide on a crossing: where the boat goes, when the next one leaves, and what it costs.

**What would move it most**
1. **The empty right side makes the page look unfinished and generic (defect).** The whole page is weighted to the left. The right 40% and the bottom third carry nothing, so the layout tips left and the blank space reads as a missing image, not a choice (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). Nothing on the page shows the bay, a boat or the route, so "calm" rests on the words alone. Inter type plus a blue button could belong to any company. If an image is meant to sit there and simply didn't load, check it in a live browser; the layout depends on it. Fill that space with something only this ferry has. The strongest option is a simple route map showing both landings and the twelve-minute line. It answers "to where?" and supports the headline at the same time. A good photo from the open deck would also work.
2. **The copy leaves out what a traveller acts on (defect).** "Cross the bay" never says where the boat arrives. The paragraph names only the "north landing". "From early morning until late evening" is a timetable fact a traveller relies on, and it can be read many ways: does a 6:30 commuter or someone coming back after dinner get a boat? There is no fare. The button, "See the timetable", is clear, but it sends the visitor off to hunt for the one thing they want, the next boat. Put the decision on the page: name both landings, give the first and last sailing times, show the next departure ("Next boat 10:40 from North Landing") and a single fare. Then let the timetable link serve people who want more. (This is common sense, not a sourced rule: show the next step instead of making people hunt for it.)

**Smaller things.**
- The three feature lines repeat the paragraph: bicycles and the open deck appear twice. Only "free" and "hot drinks" are new. Merge them into the paragraph, or make the list carry new facts such as fare, journey time and step-free access.
- The list is set about 2 px smaller than the paragraph, in the same weight and colour. That reads as a mistake, not a separate level (Bringhurst, 3.1.1; Dair, ch. 8). Set both at the same size, or make the list clearly different, for example with markers or a weight change.
- The headline breaks as "twelve / calm minutes", which separates the number from what it counts. Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" so the promise stays together on one line.

**What's working.**
- The headline is concrete and specific. "Twelve calm minutes" is a promise you can check, not a slogan. Keep it.
- The hierarchy is clear: one dominant headline, a readable paragraph about 60–70 characters wide with generous leading, and a single filled button with no competing actions.
- Everything shares one left edge at about 160 px, so the block reads as a single, tidy column.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see the button's hover and focus states or how the layout reflows on a phone. The reflow matters here, because a ferry page gets used on the quay.
