**First read.** My eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", then down three short paragraphs. It reads as a plain, calm information page from a small local service. It takes about ten seconds to read. When I finish, I still don't know when I can catch a boat.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a web page that tells ferry riders, possibly first-timers or cyclists, when the boat runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike. Correct me if it's for something else.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and the page reads easily, but it doesn't do its main job. A passenger can't plan a trip from it because none of the times are given. The writing is now the problem, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times are vague, so riders can't act on them** (defect). Paragraph 1 says "early morning and late evening", paragraph 3 says "one boat each hour" and "before dusk". These are the facts a rider plans around and then holds the operator to, and each can be read more than one way:
   - "Before dusk" could mean 4 pm or 5:30 pm. A rider who guesses late is stranded on the far bank.
   - "Each hour" could mean on the hour or at some other minute past.
   - "Every forty minutes" is useless without a first departure to count from.
   - "In winter" has no start or end date.
   
   Fix: give the real first and last departures from each landing, the minutes past the hour, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, written as numbers ("Last boat from the market quay: 16:20, November to February").
2. **The key facts are buried in sentences, so you have to read all of it to find one time** (defect). Someone standing at the landing wants one answer: when is the next boat, or the last one. Here that answer sits in the middle of paragraph 1 and the end of paragraph 3, written out in words ("forty", "twelve"), which the eye skims past.

   Fix: pull the schedule out of the prose into a short timetable or a few labelled lines (summer, winter, crossing time) that sit right under the headline. Keep the prose for the bicycle instructions, where the order of steps matters. In the list below the headline, put the time of the last boat first.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline names a category ("Notes") rather than what the reader gets. Something like "Ferry times and bikes: north landing to market quay" tells the reader they're on the right page before they start reading. This is taste.
- The bicycle paragraph says what riders do but not where they wait while foot passengers board. Add one clause so a first-timer doesn't block the ramp.

**What's working.**
- The reading column is comfortable: about 66–70 characters per line, line spacing around 1.5 and near-black text on white. Keep it when you add the timetable.
- The type scale is tight: one bold headline size and one body size. The hierarchy is obvious, and there's nothing to tidy.
- There's one topic per paragraph (schedule, bikes, winter), with space between paragraphs and no indents. That structure makes it easy to pull the schedule out into its own block.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I can't tell whether the column holds up on a phone. Most riders will open this at the landing, so check that the timetable you add doesn't need sideways scrolling at 320 px.
