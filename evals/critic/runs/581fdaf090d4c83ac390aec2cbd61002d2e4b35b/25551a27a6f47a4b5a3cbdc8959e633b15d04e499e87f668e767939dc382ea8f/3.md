**The page isn't readable as it stands, because all three paragraphs are set in capitals, and it leaves out the times a passenger needs.** The ranked list below starts with what would help it most.

**First read.** I see "Before you board" first. It's the only heavy, large thing on the page. Then the eye drops into three grey blocks of capitals, and reading slows to a crawl. It reads as a calm help page for passengers about a river ferry. The headline feels friendly, but the body feels like a printed warning notice.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a short web page for first-time or occasional ferry passengers. They want to know when the boat runs, how long the crossing takes, and what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** It fails at being read. Setting every paragraph in capitals turns twelve lines of plain information into hard work. Behind that, the copy holds back the one fact passengers come for, the actual times.

**What would move it most**
1. **All three paragraphs are in capitals** (defect, convention). Capitals remove word shapes, so the reader has to work through each line letter by letter, and paragraph after paragraph of them reads as shouting. Here the capitals cancel the friendly tone the headline sets up. Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps": "Caps are suitable for headings shorter than one line… Don't capitalize whole paragraphs." Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*: text in capitals "is particularly difficult to read." Fix: set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for the small "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already works because it's short, tracked, and grey. If the capitals come from a CSS `text-transform` on the body style, the fix is one line.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts people will act on** (defect, common sense). "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat goes. Someone catching the last boat home is exactly the person who gets stranded. "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" doesn't say which months. "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" tells someone reading at home to go to the pier to find out when to go to the pier. Fix: give the first and last departure times, the winter dates, and a link to the timetable (or the timetable itself) on this page. Then "every forty minutes" and "one boat each hour" become times the reader can plan around.
3. **The bicycle paragraph describes a procedure instead of telling the rider what to do** (taste). "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" is written about riders, not to them. Speak to the reader directly: "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike onto the lower deck and lock it to the side rail. There's no extra charge." The facts are the same, but now it reads as an instruction the rider can follow at the gangway.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline is a soft promise that the page doesn't keep. "Before you board" suggests a checklist, but the page is three loose notes. Either break them into short labelled points (Times, Bicycles, Winter) or keep the title and make each paragraph start with its topic.

**What's working.**
- **Clear structure.** There's one dark, bold heading, a small tracked grey label above it, and body text at a comfortable size. Three sizes with big steps between them mean every level is obvious at a glance.
- **Comfortable layout.** The column is about 70 characters wide, flush left, with generous line spacing and a clear gap between paragraphs. Keep that measure and spacing when you switch to lower case; it will read very well.
- **Restraint.** There are no boxes, rules, or decoration. That suits a plain information page.
