**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", then hits a grey wall: three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, which read as one dense, even texture with no word shapes to catch on. It reads as a passenger-information page for a small river ferry, aimed at riders planning a trip. The calm, plain layout feels trustworthy. The capitals make it feel like a notice someone is shouting.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page that tells first-time ferry passengers, cyclists included, when the boat runs and how boarding works, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the hierarchy is sound, but setting all the body text in capitals makes the one part people came to read the slowest part to read. After that, the copy is vague exactly where a passenger needs precision: the times.

**What would move it most**
1. **Body text set in all capitals (defect).** All three paragraphs, eleven lines of running text, are in capitals. Without ascenders and descenders, every word becomes the same rectangle. The reader has to spell their way through instead of recognising words, and when they skim for "bicycles" or "winter" nothing stands out from the texture. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says caps suit headings shorter than one line and that whole paragraphs should not be capitalised. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read". Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for the "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already does this well. This one change does more for the piece than anything else.
2. **The times a passenger acts on are vague (defect, copy).** "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone whether a 6:15 or a 22:30 crossing exists. Someone planning a commute or a last trip home will read it one way, find it means another, and miss the boat. The same goes for "In winter", with no months, and "one boat each hour", with no time on the hour. The page also sends readers to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", but someone reading online isn't at the shelter. Fix: give the first and last departure as clock times, name the months of the winter timetable, and link to the timetable from the page. Writing "every 40 minutes" and "about 12 minutes" as numerals will also make them easier to find when scanning.

**Smaller things.**
- Each paragraph opens with a topic (the ferry, bicycles, winter) that has no label. If the page grows, short subheads or bold lead-ins like "Bicycles" and "Winter" would let a cyclist jump straight to their part.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is tight: three sizes, each with a clear job. The heading is bold and much larger, and the eyebrow label is small, grey, letterspaced capitals. Each level differs from the next by more than size, which is what makes the hierarchy read at a glance.
- The text column stops at about 75 characters, at the edge of the comfortable range, with generous line spacing and clear gaps between paragraphs. Keep this measure when you switch to lower case. The lines will get shorter, which helps.
- The bicycle paragraph says who does what, in what order: wheel the bike on after foot passengers, then lock it to the rail. That is exactly what a first-time rider needs. Write the timetable lines to the same standard.
