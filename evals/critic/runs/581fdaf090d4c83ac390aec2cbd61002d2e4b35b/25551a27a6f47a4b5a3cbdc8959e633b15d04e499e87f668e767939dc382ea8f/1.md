**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold "Before you board", then hits a wall of capitals. The three paragraphs look like a block of grey that shouts, and you have to read them word by word, not skim them. It reads as a plain info page for ferry passengers, calm in layout but loud in its text.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers what they need to know before a trip (route, frequency, crossing time, bikes, winter service), so they can plan and turn up at the right time.

**Verdict.** The layout is fine, but the page fails at its job. All the body text is set in capitals, which makes it slow and tiring to read. The copy also leaves out the facts a passenger plans around: first and last sailing, the winter dates, and where to find the timetable without going to the landing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is all capitals** (all three paragraphs, rows 180–425). Capitals have no ascenders or descenders, so every word becomes a rectangle of the same height. Readers lose word shapes and spell their way through. Over seven lines that means slower reading, more skipping, and a tone that sounds like a warning notice rather than help. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") keeps capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) notes that text in capitals is particularly hard to read. Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for the "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow, which already uses them properly: short, small, and letterspaced. *Defect.*
2. **The copy is vague exactly where people need numbers.** "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone if they can catch a 06:30 or a 22:00 boat, and those are the times people plan a day around. "In winter" doesn't say which dates. "One boat each hour" doesn't say at what minute. "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends readers to the quay to find out what this page should have told them. Someone who misreads "late evening" gets stranded. Fix: give the first and last sailing times, the winter start and end dates, and a link to the full timetable. Make that link the one action on the page. *Defect.*
3. **The bicycle paragraph talks about riders, not to them** ("Riders wheel them on… and lock them to the rail"). The page title speaks to "you", but the body falls back into third person. A cyclist has to work out that this means "wait until everyone on foot has boarded, then bring your bike on and bring a lock." Fix: address the reader and name what they must bring or do ("Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike onto the lower deck. Bring a lock; you'll fasten it to the rail."). *Taste, though the lock is a real practical gap.*

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (small tracked grey eyebrow, large bold heading, body), each with an obvious job. Keep it when you change the body case.
- The heading sits close to the text it introduces, paragraphs are separated by space alone, and the left edge is crisp. The structure is right; only the body's case is wrong.
- The column is about 70 characters wide, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range, and the open right side keeps it from stretching. Keep that width; in lower case it will set a little shorter and read even better.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone at 320 px, where most passengers standing at a landing will read this.
