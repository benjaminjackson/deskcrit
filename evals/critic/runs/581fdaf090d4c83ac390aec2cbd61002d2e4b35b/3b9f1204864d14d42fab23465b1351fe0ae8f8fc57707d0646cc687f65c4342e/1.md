**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, drops to the row of four cards, and then slides along four large prices of equal weight without stopping. I read it as a standard SaaS pricing page for a collaboration or design tool. It is clean and calm, and it could belong to any product. Nothing says which plan is meant for me, and the second look turns up something odd: Free gives you 3 editors and the paid Solo plan gives you 1.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a pricing page that helps a visitor from a small team pick a plan and start, ideally on the plan the business most wants to sell (probably Team).

**Verdict.** The layout is tidy, but it doesn't help anyone choose. Four identical cards with four identical blue buttons leave all the comparing to the visitor, and the plan details contradict themselves in a way that makes the paid tiers look like a bad deal.

**What would move it most**
1. **The numbers argue against upgrading.** *(Defect.)* In the first two cards, Free gives "3 editors" and Solo, at $12, gives "1 editor". A visitor comparing them sees that paying takes away two seats, and stops trusting the rest of the table. The Company card also has a problem: it shows a fixed "$99 / month" and then asks you to "Talk to us". If the price is fixed, why talk? If it isn't, the $99 isn't real. Make every row only go up from left to right, or, if Free really allows 3 editors, say what Solo adds instead (for example "1 editor, unlimited viewers"). Then either give Company a Choose button, or replace $99 with "From $99" or "Custom". This is the line visitors will hold you to, so it can only have one reading.
2. **Nothing is recommended, so nothing gets chosen.** *(Convention: Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Material Design 3, "Buttons".)* The cards are the same size, weight, border and fill, and all four buttons are the same filled blue. The saturated colour is used up evenly, so it points nowhere, and the visitor has to read 16 rows to work out which plan fits. Pick the plan you want most people on (Team, if it's for "small studios") and make it clearly the first thing seen: a tinted or heavier card, a short label naming who it's for, and the only filled button. Turn the other three into outlined buttons.
3. **The copy is generic where it could be selling.** *(Taste.)* "Plans for every size of team" could sit on any pricing page, so it tells the visitor nothing about this product. The useful line is in the grey subhead: "Every plan includes unlimited viewers." That's a real difference from per-seat competitors, and it's set as fine print. Put the difference in the headline, ideally measured against what the visitor pays now (something like "Only editors pay. Everyone else views for free."). The taglines also only name categories ("For small studios", "For larger groups"). A team size ("Up to 15 people editing") would let a visitor place themselves without doing the maths.

**Smaller things.**
- The gap between the Team and Company cards (about 37 px) is wider than the other two gaps (about 24 px). It's just enough to look like a slip, not a choice. Make all three gaps equal.
- Each card has a band of empty space about 70 px tall under its button. It's leftover padding, not space doing a job. Either close it up or use it for a one-line note such as "No card needed" under Start free.

**What's working.**
- The prices are the strongest type on the page, with "/ month" set small and grey next to them. That's the right order: the number first, the unit second.
- Each card stacks its details in the same order (projects, editors, history, help) with hairline dividers, so you can compare across a row at a glance. Keep that order when you add a highlighted card.
- The type and colour are restrained: one sans family, a clear grey scale and one accent colour. That gives the recommended-plan treatment room to stand out once you add it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the four buttons, and how the row reflows on a phone (it should stack with the recommended plan first or clearly marked).
