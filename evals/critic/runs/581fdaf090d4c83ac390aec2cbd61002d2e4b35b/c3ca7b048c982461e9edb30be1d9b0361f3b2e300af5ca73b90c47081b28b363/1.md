**First read.** The eye lands on the big headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through a centred paragraph and a short list of features, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of the homepage for a small local ferry company. It feels calm, tidy and quiet. It also feels anonymous: apart from the words, nothing says ferry, bay or harbour.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: this is the landing view of a ferry operator's site, for locals and visitors deciding whether to take the boat and when.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound. There is one clear focal point and one clear action. But the page leaves out the facts a rider needs to decide, and it looks like it could belong to any product. The biggest problem is that you can't tell where the boat goes, what it costs, or when the first and last sailings are.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out the facts people decide on.** This is a defect, argued as common sense; no approved source covers it. "Cross the bay" and "the north landing" never say where you end up. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" can't be planned around: someone catching the first or last boat can't tell whether "early" means 6:00 or 8:30, and their evening depends on that. The page also gives no fare. The rider has to click through to learn the basics, and someone checking on a phone may leave instead. To fix it, name both ends of the crossing (for example "North Landing to …"), replace the vague span with real first and last sailing times, and show the fare. The ask itself is good: "See the timetable" is exactly what a rider wants next, so keep it.
2. **Nothing on the page shows the experience the copy is selling.** This is taste. The paragraph sells a feeling: "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck." But the page is a stock centred stack of type on white with no picture, mark or texture. Swap the brand name and it could be a page for a note-taking app. The cost is that nothing makes this crossing memorable or makes anyone want to go. Add one strong photograph of the deck or the bay as the visual half of the headline, so the calm is shown rather than claimed.
3. **The headline breaks the number away from its unit.** This is a convention: Bringhurst 2.4.6 says to keep short numerical expressions together. The first line reads "Cross the bay in twelve", so "twelve" is left hanging until "calm minutes" arrives. "Twelve minutes" is the hook of the whole page, and the break weakens it. Break the line as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes", or bind "twelve calm minutes" so it can't split.

**Smaller things.**
- The feature list ("Bicycles travel free", etc.) looks about 2 px smaller than the paragraph above it, in the same face, weight and colour. It reads as stray extra lines rather than a separate group (Dair: weak contrast is worse than none). Either set it at the body size, or set it apart clearly, for example in a row with small icons or in a bolder weight.
- "Hot drinks on board" sits right under "Bicycles travel free", so a reader may take it as free too. Say "for sale" or give the price.
- "Seats on the open deck" repeats the paragraph's "from the open deck". Use that line for a fact the paragraph doesn't give, such as step-free boarding or dogs allowed.

**What's working.**
- The headline wins by a big jump in size and weight, so the order of reading is never in doubt. Keep that size gap when you add an image.
- There is one filled button with a specific, honest label. "See the timetable" is the right next step for this audience, and nothing competes with it.
- The palette is restrained: dark text on white with one blue accent, plenty of space and strong contrast. That suits a calm crossing. Keep the blue for the action.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the "See the timetable" button. Check in a browser that keyboard focus is clearly visible, since it's the only action on the page.
