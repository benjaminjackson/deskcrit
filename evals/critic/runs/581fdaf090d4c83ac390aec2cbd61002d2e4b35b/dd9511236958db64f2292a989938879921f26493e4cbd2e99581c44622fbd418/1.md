**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for a lighthouse that people can visit, meant to make a reader want to go and to tell them how. Correct me if it's only a heritage note.

**Verdict.** It reads well and has a clear mood, but it doesn't yet do the job of getting someone there. The page tells you the tower is open and then gives no times, no price, no address and no next step. The biggest problem is that the visiting facts are buried in prose and are too vague to act on.

**What would move it most**

1. **The visit is hinted at, not offered (defect).** Paragraphs 2 and 3 hold everything a visitor needs, but in a form they can't act on.
   - "Open to visitors on summer weekends" doesn't say which months or what hours. Someone who turns up on the first weekend of June, or at 5 pm, may find it shut.
   - "Tickets are sold at the cottage" gives no price, and "by the car park" gives no place a reader can find on a map.
   - The page ends on scones, with no link, button or address, so the eye runs out at the bottom of the text block.

   The fix is to keep the story paragraphs and add a short, scannable visit block under them: the season dates, opening hours, ticket price, and an address or map link. That block becomes the end point of the page, so the eye finishes on what to do.

   The best line on the page is already there: "Climb to the gallery on a clear morning and you can follow the ferry all the way across the bay." It lets readers picture the payoff for themselves. Put the ask right next to it, something like "Climb it this summer: Sat–Sun, 10–4, £4", so the reward and the details sit together.

2. **The picture isn't anchored to the text (convention).**
   - The illustration's top edge (about y 230) sits about 85 px below the headline (about 145), and its bottom edge (about 573) hangs about 75 px below the last line of text (about 500).
   - It isn't centred on the text block either: the text centre is about y 322 and the picture centre is about 401.
   - Because it lines up with nothing, it reads as having slipped rather than as placed, and the whole spread looks a little unsettled. Müller-Brockmann asks that a picture take its top and bottom from the lines of text beside it. Arnheim notes that a placement that is neither clearly here nor clearly there makes the eye waver.
   - The fix is to align the picture's top with the cap height of the headline (the usual choice) and let the text run down beside it. Or, if you want it lower, move it down by a clearly deliberate amount.

**Smaller things.**
- At the end of paragraph 1, "room." sits alone on the last line. Rewrite slightly or adjust the measure so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The headline "The light at Gull Point" is paired with a lighthouse that gives off no light. Taste: a faint beam or a glow from the yellow lamp would make the picture and the headline one idea rather than a label and its picture.

**What's working.**
- The type is sound and restrained. There are two clear sizes, a bold serif headline over a readable serif body, a line length of about 68 to 75 characters, generous line spacing, and space between paragraphs with no indent. Keep this when you add the visit block: set it in the body size and use weight, not a third size.
