**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a help section on a small bakery's online-ordering site. Customers who have already ordered use it to answer one practical question quickly.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do the job yet. A scanning reader can't find their question, because the headings look the same as the answers. Two answers that people will act on (who can collect, and when you can cancel) can each be read two ways. The text is clean, but both the structure and the content need work.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headings don't read as headings.** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are in the same face, weight and near-black colour as the answers. They're also about a pixel *smaller* (roughly 17 px against 18 px). A reader who scans for their question has to read every line to find it, and the page loses its one job. The only thing that separates the levels is a size step too small to see, which looks like an accident rather than a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: weak contrast is worse than none). It also doesn't survive a squint or a greyscale view. Fix: make each heading obviously different from its text in at least one clear way, ideally more than size alone. Semibold at the current size, or a step up with weight, would do it, and you could also put the answers in a slightly lighter grey (Dair, ch. 8a–8g and 9). This is a defect.

2. **The answers people will act on can be read two ways.**
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name." Whose name: the person who ordered, or the person collecting? If the collector gives their own name and staff expect yours, they get turned away from the counter. Say it: "along with your name", or "the name on the order".
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." Ahead of what, and how far exactly? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot or any time the day before. Someone who cancels at 9 pm the evening before may find they've already been charged. Name the cut-off ("up to 24 hours before your pickup time") and say how to cancel. Right now the section never tells you.
   - Both are defects: they're the lines a customer will later hold the bakery to.

3. **Two answers send the reader somewhere they can't go from here.**
   - "Open your order" doesn't say where the order is, and nothing on the page links to it. So the most common task on a help page like this needs a search before it can start.
   - "Every loaf has a card by the till" only helps someone who is already standing in the shop. A customer with an allergy needs that information *before* ordering online, and "Ask the baker" gives no way to do that.
   - Fix: link "Open your order" to the order. Add an online ingredients list, or at least a phone number or email for allergy questions. This is a defect (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*: signifiers. Nielsen heuristic 6: recognition rather than recall).

4. **Lines run too long for how the page will be read.** The answers run about 90–95 characters per line, from x=160 to about 980 px, with half the width empty on the right. That's well over Bringhurst's 45–75 (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). With such short answers it costs less than it would in long body text, but it stretches each answer into one long line that's harder to take in at a glance. Cap the column at about 600–650 px. This is a convention, and here it's mildly costly.

**Smaller things.**
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer (a runt). Rewording or a narrower measure will fix it.
- The section has no title saying what it is ("Questions about your order"). Add one so that one element on the page is clearly dominant and the eye has somewhere to land (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). With the headings fixed this matters less, but the page still has no first thing to read.
- "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact. Finish it with what it means for the reader: what happens to their bread, and their money, if they come after that?

**What's working.**
- The answers are short, concrete and in plain speech ("the bread has already been baked for you" gives a reason, and a kind one). Keep that voice when you rewrite the ambiguous lines.
- The spacing already groups each heading with its answer: the gap above each heading is clearly bigger than the gap below it. Keep that rhythm once the headings get weight.
- It uses one family, one colour and no boxes or rules. That restraint suits a small bakery. Fix the hierarchy with weight and value, not decoration.
