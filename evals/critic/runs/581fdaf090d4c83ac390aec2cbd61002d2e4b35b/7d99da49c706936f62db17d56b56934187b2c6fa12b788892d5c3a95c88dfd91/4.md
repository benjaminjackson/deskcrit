I've finished the critique. Your note said nothing about the piece, so I assumed what it's for and say so below.

**First read.** The eye lands on the first line, "Changing your pickup time", and then reads straight down the page as one even block. All the text is the same dark grey sans serif at about the same size and weight. The four headings look like the first line of each paragraph, not like headings, so the page reads as eight paragraphs rather than four questions with answers. It feels calm, plain and a little anonymous. It reads as the help or FAQ section of a bakery's pre-order site.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section for customers who have ordered bread online and want a quick answer about pickup, collection, allergies or cancelling. Most will scan for their one question rather than read top to bottom.

**Verdict.** The copy is mostly clear and kind, but the page doesn't let a scanning customer find their question, because the headings look like body text. Two answers that customers will act on can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headings don't look like headings.** This affects "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order". Each is the same weight and colour as the answer below it, and looks about a pixel *smaller* (roughly 18 px against 19 px). Someone looking for "how do I cancel" can't skim the questions. They have to read the page line by line, and the four sections blur into one block of eight lines. The small size gap is worse than none, because it looks like a mistake rather than a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). It also means the page has no real type scale: two sizes almost the same, with no job for either. The fix is to make each heading different from its text in a way that is obvious at a glance. Use weight first: semibold headings at body size or one clear step larger. Optionally, also make the answers a little lighter in colour so the levels separate even when squinting (Dair, chs. 8a–8g and 9; Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*: bolder, not bigger). Pick one heading size and one body size, and don't let the heading be the smaller one. *Defect.*

2. **Two answers people will hold the bakery to can each be read two ways.** These are the cancellation rule and the pickup rule.
   - **"Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead."** A day ahead of what: the pickup time, or the baking? And does it mean 24 hours before, or any time on the day before? A customer who reads "the day before" and cancels at 9 pm on the eve of an 8 am pickup may be refused and charged. That's exactly the argument this line should prevent. State the cutoff as a time the customer can check: "You can cancel until [time] the day before your pickup." Make the consequence clear too: "After that we've already baked your bread, so we can't refund it", if that's the policy.
   - **"They will need to show it at the counter along with a name."** Whose name: yours or theirs? If the counter staff expect the account holder's name and the friend gives their own, the order isn't handed over. Say "your name" or "their own name".

   *Defect.*

3. **The lines are too long to read comfortably.** The text runs from about x=160 to x=980. The lines in "Collecting for someone else" and "Cancelling an order" come to about 95 characters, well past the comfortable 45–75 (Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). At this size the eye has to travel a long way back for each new line, and on a scanning page that adds up. Narrow the text column to about 600–650 px at this size; the right side of the page is empty anyway. *Convention.*

4. **"Open your order" names a place the reader can't see.** The fix for the most common question ("Open your order and choose a new slot from the list") sends the reader somewhere with no link and no path. Someone who arrived from search or a link in an email has to work out where their order lives. Make "Open your order" a link to the orders page, or name where it is ("Go to My orders"). *Defect* (Nielsen, heuristic 6; Norman, *Design of Everyday Things*, on signifiers).

**Smaller things**
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergy answer, a one-word runt (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). A narrower column or a small rewrite will move it.
- The allergy answer only helps someone already standing in the shop ("a card by the till"). A pre-order customer has to choose before they see that card. The answer is incomplete for them, not just the typography: say where to see ingredients when ordering, or how to ask before buying.
- "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact. Finish it for the reader: what happens to their order, and their money, after those two hours.

**What's working**
- The spacing groups things correctly. There's a small gap between each heading and its answer and a clearly larger gap between sections. Once the headings get weight, the four groups will be obvious. Keep these gaps as they are.
- The voice is plain and human. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" gives the reason behind the rule in words a customer accepts, which makes the policy easier to take. Keep that tone when you sharpen the wording.
- The type is restrained: one readable sans serif, flush left, dark on white, with strong contrast. Nothing here needs adding. It needs contrast between the levels, not more styling.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the headings are marked up as real headings for screen readers.
- How the text column reflows on a phone.
