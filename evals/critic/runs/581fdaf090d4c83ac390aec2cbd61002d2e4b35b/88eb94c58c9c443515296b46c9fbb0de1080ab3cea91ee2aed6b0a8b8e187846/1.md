**First read.** The eye goes to the bold sans headline "Notes for ferry passengers" and then into four serif paragraphs of the same weight, each with a deep first-line indent. It reads as a ferry operator's information page. It feels calm, tidy and a little bookish, like a page from a guidebook. After the headline nothing gets more weight than anything else, so the eye has no second place to land, and the right 40% of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page meant to tell ferry passengers, quickly, when the boat runs, when the last boat leaves, and what to do with a bike. Correct me if it's something else.

**Verdict.** The type is set with care and reads comfortably, but the page is set like an essay when it should work as a reference. A passenger checking "when's the last boat?" has to read every line, and when they find the answer it isn't a time.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts passengers need are buried in running prose. *Defect.*** All four paragraphs look the same, with no subheads, no list, and no times set apart. Someone on their phone at the landing wants one fact: the next or last departure. Here they have to read the first paragraph, then the third, then work out the winter rule themselves. Nielsen (How Users Read on the Web) and Krug (*Don't Make Me Think*) both make the same point: people scan pages like this rather than read them, and long uniform prose defeats scanning. **Fix:** pull the timetable out of the prose as its own block (first and last boat, how often it runs, summer and winter) and put it at the top, with short subheads for Bicycles, Winter service and Facilities under it. The empty right side of the page is the obvious place for an at-a-glance timetable box. That would also give the space a job, because at the moment it's just leftover.
2. **The times and rules can't be acted on, or can be read two ways. *Defect.*** Passengers will plan around these lines, so vagueness here counts as a defect, not a style point:
   - "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no clock time, so nobody can plan around it.
   - "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can be read two ways. Which quay, the market quay or the north landing? And dusk is 16:00 in December and much later in March. A passenger who guesses wrong gets stranded on the far bank.
   - "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends a web reader to a physical shelter for the very information the page should give them.
   - "Bicycles ride… at no extra charge" implies a fare that the page never states.

   **Fix:** give real clock times for first and last departures in each direction, name the landing each time, give winter start and end dates, and state the fare. Each line should tell the passenger what they can do and when, without them filling in a gap.

**Smaller things**
- Paragraphs are marked with both an indent and a space, and the indent is deep (about 2.5 em). The first paragraph after the headline is indented too. Choose one: keep the space and drop the indents, which suits a web page. If you keep indents, set the first paragraph flush left and cut the indent to about 1 em (Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.3.1–2.3.2).
- The last paragraph, about the new ramps and benches, is the least useful thing on the page, but it sits where a scanning reader lands last. Move accessibility information (wider ramps, covered waiting area) up under a Facilities subhead. Cut the history ("rebuilt last spring").

**What's working**
- The type itself: a sans headline against a serif text face is a clear contrast, not a clash. The text size is generous, the measure is about 60 to 70 characters, and the leading is comfortable. Keep all of this when you restructure; the problem is the structure, not the setting.
- The scale is restrained: just two sizes with a big jump between them. When you add subheads, make them a bolder weight of the text size or the headline face at a modest size. Don't add a third size that sits close to an existing one.
- The colour is near-black on white, with strong contrast and no decoration. That's right for a practical notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Reflow: whether the column and any new timetable block hold up at phone width. That matters here, because most readers will be on a phone at the landing.
