**First read.** The serif headline lands first, "Shipments peak each third quarter". The eye then drops to the row of eight identical blue bars and settles on the tallest one, Q3 2026 at 705. It reads as a calm, well-made board slide. It tells you about seasonality, but it doesn't tell you anything has changed. Nothing on the chart marks the forecast. You only learn that the last bar is a guess from a bracket in the subtitle and the source line.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is one page of a board deck (page 3 of 12), read on screen or projected. Its job is to tell directors how shipments are trending and what to expect for the quarter now under way.

**Verdict.** The craft is clean and the chart is honest about scale. But the slide draws the Q4 2026 forecast exactly like the actual quarters, and its headline tells the board the seasonal pattern they probably already know. It misses the real news: every 2026 quarter is up on 2025.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast looks like a fact.** *Defect.* The Q4 2026 bar (560) has the same solid blue, the same weight and the same bold label as the seven measured quarters. The only signals are "(Q4 2026 is the forecast)" in the subtitle and "from the September forecast" in the footer, and both are small grey text far from the bar. A director who looks only at the chart sees 560 as a result. It's also the biggest year-on-year jump on the page (+17% on Q4 2025's 480), so it's the number most likely to be repeated later as if it had happened. Projections should look different from observed data and should carry their uncertainty (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). To fix it, draw that bar as an outline or a pale tint, put "Forecast" on the bar or its axis label, and show a range if the forecast has one.
2. **The headline gives the board the familiar pattern, not the news.** *Copy, and taste in the framing.* "Shipments peak each third quarter" describes the seasonal shape, which a board for an outdoor-gear maker almost certainly knows. The story in the numbers is that every 2026 quarter beats the same quarter of 2025: 455 vs 410, 590 vs 520, 705 vs 610, and 560 vs 480 forecast, which is roughly 11% to 17% up and speeding up. The way the chart is laid out hides that. To see it, a director has to compare bars four positions apart and do the arithmetic. The headline also stops at a fact and never says what it means for the reader. "Each third quarter" rests on only two cycles, so even the seasonal claim is thinner than "each" suggests. Fix: lead with the growth, for example "Every quarter of 2026 is shipping more than the same quarter of 2025". If year-on-year is the point, pair the bars by quarter (2025 next to 2026) or label each 2026 bar with its change.
3. **Nothing in the chart backs up the headline.** *Convention.* The headline is about Q3, but all eight bars are the same saturated blue at the same value. Nothing marks the two Q3 bars, so the chart doesn't show the claim. The viewer has to find it. Keep one colour for the series and a second colour for the point the reader must notice (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Concretely, mute the context bars to a grey-blue and keep full colour for whichever comparison the final headline makes: the Q3 bars, or the 2026 bars if you take finding 2.

**Smaller things.**
- Blank band at the bottom: everything ends around y≈680, leaving a strip of about 120 px under the footer, so the page sits top-heavy. Either give the plot that height or check whether the slide frame is really this ratio.

**What's working.**
- The value axis starts at zero and the gridlines are pale, so bar lengths are honest and the data is the darkest ink in the chart. Keep this when you restyle the forecast bar.
- The source line, units ("Thousands of units") and meeting context are all there and quiet. A director never has to guess what a number measures or where it came from.
- The type pairing works: a heavy serif headline over a plain sans for everything else gives one clear entry point and two or three distinct levels below it, with no near-duplicate sizes.
