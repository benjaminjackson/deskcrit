**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" and drops straight into three paragraphs of dark grey text. It reads as a plain information page for people taking a river ferry. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. The one thing that catches on a first look is the right edge of the text. The last line of the first paragraph sticks out well past the lines above it, and the second paragraph breaks one line early, as if someone pressed Return by hand.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a public web page that tells passengers when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and what the rules are, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and readable. The layout does its job, but the words don't: the facts a passenger plans around (first boat, last boat, winter hours) are vague or buried in long sentences. The hand-broken lines are the one visible typographic flaw.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule is vague and buried in prose.** *Defect.* A passenger comes for three things: when the first and last boats leave, how often they run, and what changes in winter. The page gives "between early morning and late evening", "every forty minutes" with no time to count from, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk", which changes daily and can't be planned around. These are the lines a passenger will act on and later hold you to. "Before dusk" can be read as "a fixed early-evening departure" or as "whenever it gets dark". Read it the wrong way and someone is stranded on the wrong bank. The first paragraph also spends half its length on one long sentence about the tide and the sandbank (about 60 words, from "Each crossing" to "low tide"), which matters less to the reader than the times do. Direction: put real times first, as a short timetable or list (first boat, last boat, frequency, summer and winter). Shorten the tide detail to one line that says what it means for the passenger, such as "allow up to N extra minutes at low tide". Keep the paragraphs for the bike rule and where to find printed timetables.

2. **Line breaks look set by hand, so the right edge is lumpy.** *Defect, and a convention.* In the first paragraph, the line "sandbank by the old harbour wall on most mornings at low tide." runs about 40–50 px past every other line. In the second paragraph, "boarded," ends a line even though "and" clearly fits after it. When lines end where someone chose to break them, not where the text runs out of room, the edge stops looking gently uneven and starts looking like a mistake (Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style* 2.1.3; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, p. 35, against irregular rhythms and holes at line ends). On the web it gets worse: at any other window width, the forced breaks leave short lines scattered through the paragraphs. Direction: remove the manual breaks, give the text column a fixed maximum width (it already sits near a good 55–65 characters), and let the text wrap on its own.

**What's working.**
- There are two type levels, and they're clearly different: a large bold heading over regular text in the same sans-serif family. You never wonder what is the title and what is the body.
- The measure and line spacing are comfortable, at roughly 55–65 characters a line with generous leading. The space between paragraphs is clearly larger than the space between lines, so each topic reads as its own block.
- The dark text on white has strong contrast, and the single left-aligned column with an open right side suits a short notice. Keep this restraint when you add the timetable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the column rewraps cleanly on a phone-width screen. Check this once the manual breaks are removed.
