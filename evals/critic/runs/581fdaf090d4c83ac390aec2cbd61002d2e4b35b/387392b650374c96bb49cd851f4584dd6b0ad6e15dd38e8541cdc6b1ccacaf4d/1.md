**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is an internal report page that shows a growth or marketing team where Q3 sign-ups came from and what the team will change because of it.

**Verdict.** This is a sound, honest chart, and the page mostly works. The main problem is that the story sits in a side column the eye reaches last, while the chart shows a plain ranking. Neither the Q2 comparison nor the budget decision is backed by anything you can see.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart shows the ranking; the takeaway is somewhere else.** The title "New sign-ups by channel" names the topic. All six bars are the same blue, so nothing tells the reader which bars matter. The two points the page exists to make (organic search grew to a third of sign-ups; events are being cut) only appear in the right-hand note, which the eye reaches last. Someone who skims the chart and leaves takes away "organic is biggest", which they could have guessed. Fix: make the headline the finding, e.g. "Organic search brought a third of Q3 sign-ups, up from a quarter". Then mark the bars the note talks about: keep Organic search in the strong blue, mute the rest, and flag Events. The chart and the text should tell one story. *Taste / common sense: no approved source covers this, but the cost to a skimming reader is real.*
2. **The page's main claim can't be checked on the page.** "Up from a quarter in Q2" is the news, but the chart has no Q2 data. The reader can't see whether organic grew in absolute terms or other channels shrank, or whether referral and paid social moved at all. They have to trust the sentence. Fix: put Q2 on the chart, with a thin marker or ghost bar per channel, or a "Q2: 1,1xx" label under each value, so growth is something you see. *Defect: it slows trust in the one claim the page leads with.*
3. **The budget decision rests on evidence the page doesn't show, and the copy leaves gaps.** "The events budget moves to referrals in Q4" is the line people will hold the team to. But the chart shows sign-up volume, not cost per sign-up, and 260 accounts from a cheap channel could be a good result. The sentence before it groups podcast ads with events, then cuts only events, so a stranger asks what happens to podcasts. "Fewer than 700" is also vaguer than the real figure, 670, about 12% of the total. Fix: give the reason in numbers ("events cost $X per sign-up against $Y for referrals"), say what happens to podcast ads, and use the exact figure with its share. *Defect: a decision line that can be read as weakly supported invites pushback.*
