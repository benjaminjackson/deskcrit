**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline at top left, drops into three equal grey columns of serif text, and reads them left to right. It reads as a plain information page about a local ferry: calm and tidy. It also feels unfinished. The text fills the top-left three-quarters, and the right edge and the bottom 40% of the frame are empty.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming a web info page that helps passengers plan a crossing: when boats run, whether they can bring a bike, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** It's clean but it doesn't do the job well. The headline promises one thing and the columns deliver three unlabelled others, and the facts a passenger will act on are vague and can be read two ways. The type and the grid are competent. The structure and the words are where it falls down.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline describes a different page, and the three columns have no labels.** *Defect.* "The river ferry through the seasons" promises a tour of spring, summer, autumn and winter. The body gives route and crossing time (column 1), bicycles (column 2), and the winter timetable plus the noticeboard (column 3). Only winter is mentioned. Someone who came to find out whether they can bring a bike, or when the last boat leaves, can't see which column answers them and has to read all three. A heading should describe the topic of what's under it (WCAG 2.2, SC 2.4.6). Fix: retitle to what the page actually covers, or write the seasonal content the title promises. Give each column a short subhead such as "Times", "Bicycles" and "Winter and changes", set as a level between the headline and the body (for example bold sans at body size), so the page can be skimmed.

2. **The timetable can be read two ways, and these are the facts people act on.** *Defect.* Column 1 says boats run "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" with no season attached. Column 3 says winter drops to one boat an hour, with the last crossing "before dusk". Reading one: forty minutes all year, as column 1 implies. Reading two: forty minutes except in winter. A winter passenger who reads only column 1 turns up expecting a boat every forty minutes and a late-evening service, and finds the last boat gone. "Early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk" are not times anyone can plan around, and dusk moves by hours through the year. Fix: say which months each timetable covers, and give first and last departure times as numbers. Pull them out of the prose so they can be found at a glance.

3. **The columns are too narrow, and they leave dead space that looks like a missing column.** *Convention and defect.* Lines run about 25–30 characters, well under the 40–50 Bringhurst gives for text set in several columns (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). The eye jumps back every four or five words, so phrases like "the sandbank near the old / harbour wall" get chopped up. Meanwhile a strip about 270 px wide on the right sits empty. So does everything below roughly y=490. Neither space has a job: it reads as a fourth column that never arrived, not as room left on purpose. Empty space should be shaped and given a job (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Fix: use the full width. Either set two columns at about 45 characters, or set one reading column at about 65 characters with the timetable facts from finding 2 in a side column. The empty area then becomes the place where the important facts go.

**Smaller things.**
- Column 2 ends with "stairs." alone on its last line. Rewrap or edit so the paragraph doesn't end on one short word.
- "A little longer when the tide turns" (column 1) gives no figure. Say how many minutes, as the calm-weather crossing does.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing is clear: a bold sans headline over a regular serif body, so they differ in both weight and structure (Dair, *Design with Type*). There are only two sizes, so nothing looks like a mistake. Keep this pairing, and add the subheads as weight changes, not new sizes.
- The grid holds. The gutters look equal at about 60 px, all three columns share a top line, and the lines of text sit on the same baselines across the columns. Keep that discipline when you change the column count.
- The prose itself is plain and concrete (north landing, market quay, lock bikes to the rail along the side of the deck), and the text has strong dark-on-white contrast.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Reflow: whether the three columns collapse into one readable column on a phone (WCAG 1.4.10). This matters for a page passengers will check on the way to the landing.
