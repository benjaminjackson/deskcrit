**First read.** The eye goes to the headline, drops to the row of four big prices, and then scans left and right across them without stopping anywhere. It reads as a clean, competent SaaS pricing page in the Stripe or Linear style: calm, trustworthy and neutral. It also reads as anonymous. The four cards are identical, so the page tells me nothing about which plan is for me.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a pricing page for a collaborative design or creative tool. It should help a visitor (a freelancer, a small studio or a larger company) pick a plan and click through.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and readable, but it doesn't help anyone choose. Two things get in the way. First, nothing is recommended: four equal cards with four equal buttons. Second, the plan list contradicts itself, because the Free plan gives more editors than the paid Solo plan.

**What would move it most**

1. **The plan ladder is broken: Free gives 3 editors, Solo gives 1.** This is in the second feature row of the first two cards. A visitor comparing plans sees that paying $12 gets them fewer editors than paying nothing. That's the first thing a careful buyer will notice. It makes the whole table look untrustworthy, and it sends small teams straight to Free. **Defect** (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*: the conceptual model. The tiers should read as a clear step up). If Solo is meant as one paid seat, make that explicit ("Just you") and give Free fewer editors, or cap Free at 1. Each row should only ever go up from left to right.

2. **No plan is recommended, and all four buttons are the same filled blue.** "Start free", "Choose Solo", "Choose Team" and "Talk to us" have identical weight, and so do the cards. The eye has nowhere to land, and the visitor has to work out which plan fits them alone. **Convention plus defect:** Material Design 3 asks for one high-emphasis action per view, and Krug's *Don't Make Me Think* applies to making people do this kind of comparison themselves. Pick the plan you most want to sell (probably Team) and make it the single break in the pattern: a "Most popular" or "For studios" tag, a coloured border or tint, and the only filled button. Give the other three outlined buttons. "Talk to us" in particular is a different kind of action from the other three and shouldn't look the same.

3. **"/ month" doesn't say what is charged.** This is next to every price. Is $39 per team or per editor? For Company, "$99 / month" alongside "100 editors" can easily be read as per seat. Price is the line people hold you to, so a figure that can be read two ways is a **defect**. If the price is per team, say "/ month, whole team". If it's per seat, the page needs to say so and give a total.

**Smaller things.**
- There's about 70 px of empty space under each button inside the cards, plus a blank band below the row. The cards look stretched to a fixed height. Close the bottom padding to match the top, or use the space for a "Compare all features" link.
- The visitor is likely to ask next about annual billing and the full feature list, and neither is offered. Add a monthly/annual toggle and a compare link under the cards.
- The copy is generic. "Plans for every size of team" could sit on any SaaS page, and the taglines ("For trying it out", "For larger groups") name sizes, not jobs. Name a situation the visitor will recognise, such as "For one designer with client reviews" or "For a studio running several projects at once". The subhead's "unlimited viewers" is the most distinctive promise here and deserves more weight than a grey line.

**What's working.**
- **The prices.** They're the largest, heaviest type on the page, with a light grey "/ month" beside them. That's the right thing to dominate each card, and it's done with weight and colour rather than size alone. Keep it.
- **A strict program for the four cards.** The order is always name, tagline, price, four features, button, and the feature rows line up across all four columns, so you can compare each row straight across. That makes the fix in point 2 easy: one deliberate break in a regular pattern will read clearly.
- **Restrained colour.** Blue appears only on things you can click, so once one button is set apart, its emphasis will be clear.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the buttons, and how the four columns reflow on a phone. Check whether the recommended plan stays first or visible once the cards stack.
