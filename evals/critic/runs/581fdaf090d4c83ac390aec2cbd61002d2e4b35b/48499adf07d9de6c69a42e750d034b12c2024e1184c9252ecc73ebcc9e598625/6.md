**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline, drops to the "Create your link" button, and then slides into a row of four blue-outlined boxes that all carry the same weight. It reads as a scheduling tool for small appointment-based businesses: the names along the bottom are a dental practice, a tutoring service, a physio and a vet. It feels clean, calm and finished. It also feels like it could be any booking product, and the empty right half of the hero makes it look like a picture failed to load.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of a booking-link product. Its job is to get a small-practice owner to create a link, or at least open the live example.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and competent, but it doesn't make the case. The page never shows the product. Its words could sit on any rival's page. And one blue is used on nearly everything, so nothing besides the headline stands out. Showing the booking page and writing to the practices it already names would change the most.

**What would move it most**

1. **The product is missing, and half the hero is empty.** The area right of the headline (roughly x 680–1216, y 100–400) is blank. All the weight sits on the left, and the right side gives the eye nowhere to go. A large empty area with no apparent purpose reads as leftover space, or as an image that failed to load. If an image did fail to load in this capture, this finding becomes a check in the browser. If not, it's the biggest gap on the page. A booking tool's best proof is the page the customer's own clients will see: a week of open slots with one being picked. Right now the visitor has to click "See a live example" to see anything at all. That's taste plus common sense, but it costs the page its proof. Fix: put the booking page itself there, as a real screen with real-looking times. That one move balances the hero and does what the copy can't.

2. **The copy could be on anyone's page, and it talks about "meetings" to people who book appointments.** "Booking links that fill your calendar" makes a promise a link can't keep: a link removes back-and-forth, it doesn't bring in demand. And every competitor could say the same line. The subhead's "keep every meeting in the right place" leaves the reader to guess what the right place is. The customer row, though, shows who this page is for: Harbor Dental, Northfield Tutoring, Kestrel Physio, Orchard Vets. Those people talk about appointments, patients, clients and no-shows, not meetings. The ask is half right. "See a live example" lets the visitor judge for themselves, which is the stronger kind of ask. "Create your link" is specific, but neither one sets the product next to what the visitor does now. Fix: write the headline about the thing these practices already suffer. For example, clients book their own appointment slot instead of phoning the front desk, and reminders cut no-shows. Point the example link at a page in their own trade ("See a dental practice's booking page"). Also give the bottom row a label such as "Used by". Without one, five grey names under a rule read like footer links.

3. **One blue does every job, so the main button doesn't stand out.** The same saturated blue is used for the headline, the nav links, all four 2px card borders, the card titles, the icons, the badges, the rule above the customers, the "Learn more" links and both buttons. The eye has nowhere to go after the headline. "Create your link" is the same colour as the text right above it, so only its fill separates it. Apple's HIG and Material both reserve the accent colour for the main action. Lupton warns that borders which carry no information compete with the content. There's a second cost: blue-outlined boxes with blue titles look like clickable tiles, but only the small "Learn more" link inside each one looks like a link. Fix: keep the blue for the buttons and links only. Set the headline and card titles in a near-black. Replace the card borders with a pale tint or with space, and give the icons a quiet neutral. If the whole card is clickable, make that the rule and drop the separate "Learn more" links.

**Smaller things.**
- There are two filled buttons with different labels: "Start free" in the header and "Create your link" in the hero. If they do the same thing, give them the same label. If they don't, make the header one an outline button (Material Design 3, "Buttons").
- In the first card, the "New" badge forces "Shared / calendars" onto two lines. That pushes its body text and link about 30px below the other three cards and breaks the row's shared lines (Müller-Brockmann). Put badges above the title or after it on its own line, or shorten the title.
- The headline breaks as "fill your / calendar", which splits "your calendar". Break after "fill" so the phrase stays together.
- The badges mix two kinds of information in one style. "New" and "Beta" are statuses, "Auto" and "Email" describe features. Keep badges for status only and move the descriptors into the text.
- There are four identical "Learn more" links, so out of context they don't say where they go (WCAG 2.4.4). Name the destination ("How reminders work") or label them for screen readers.
- The icons are mixed: the calendar is outlined while the clock, envelope and layout glyph are solid. The intake-forms icon is a page-layout symbol, not a form. Use one icon style and pick a glyph that means "form".

**What's working.**
- The page has a clear left edge. The headline, subhead, buttons, cards and customer row all start on the same line at x 64. Keep that column when you add the product image.
- The card text is written for the reader: "so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful". Each one ends on what it means for the user. That's the tone the headline needs.
- There's real contrast in size between the 46px-ish headline and everything else. The secondary text levels are separated by weight and colour rather than by sizes a point apart.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the whole card is clickable, and its hover state. This decides whether the boxes are an honest signal that they can be clicked or a misleading one.
- Whether the empty right half of the hero is an image that didn't load. Check in a live browser.
