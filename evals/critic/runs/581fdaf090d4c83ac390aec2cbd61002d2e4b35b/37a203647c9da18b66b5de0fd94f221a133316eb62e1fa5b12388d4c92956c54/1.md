**First read.** The bold heading at top left takes the eye first. From there it runs straight down three blocks of text and stops on the pale grey box. This is clearly a notice from a local ferry service, and it feels calm and plain. The composition doesn't look finished, though. The text column is pressed flat against the left edge of the frame, and a band about 400 px wide on the right, plus the whole bottom half, is empty. It looks like a page that has lost its container, not one that was laid out on purpose.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web information page that tells winter passengers when the ferry runs, what can stop it, and what each vehicle pays. The reader is probably on a phone or planning a trip. They want to know whether they can catch a boat and what it will cost.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The look is tidy, but the copy leaves out or blurs the facts a passenger acts on: times written as words, one direction of travel only, a fare with no amount. The layout has no left margin and lines about 100 characters long. The type and colour are sound. The problems are in the words and the frame.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts people act on are vague, and they're written so they're hard to scan.** *(Defect.)* This page exists to give a timetable, and the timetable lives in the first paragraph as prose: "every forty minutes… the first crossing is at seven in the morning and the last at six in the evening." A passenger checking a time has to read the whole sentence, and it still leaves gaps:
   - It only covers departures from the north landing. Someone on the south bank gets no times at all.
   - "The last at six" can mean the last boat leaves the north landing at 18:00 or the last crossing arrives at 18:00. If they guess wrong, they're stranded on the far bank.
   - "Every forty minutes" from 07:00 gives 07:00, 07:40, 08:20… and does not land on 18:00. So either the last boat is 17:40 or 18:20, or the interval changes somewhere. The reader can't tell which.
   - "Cars pay the standard fare" names a price without giving it. "Bicycles ride free on weekdays" leaves weekends open. "Vans over two metres… use the cargo deck" doesn't say whether vans pay more.

   Fix: give the times as numbers in a small table or list, both directions, with first and last departure from each landing. Put the actual fares next to each vehicle type. Prices and times are what readers remember and later hold the operator to, so any of these lines that can be read two ways counts as a defect, not a style point.

2. **The emphasis is on the wrong paragraph.** *(Taste, argued from the job.)* The tinted box in the lower third highlights bicycles, cars and vans, which is useful but routine. The line that actually changes what a passenger does is in plain text: "the crew may hold the boat… Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue." In winter, that delay warning is what a reader most needs to see. As it stands, the eye skips past it to land on fares. Move the box treatment to the ice warning. If you can, also say how long a hold usually lasts, or whether the delay appears anywhere they can check before leaving home. "Check the board at the ticket hut" only helps someone already standing at the landing.

3. **No margin, and lines that are too long.** *(Convention plus defect.)* The heading, both paragraphs and the box all start at x = 0, with no space between them and the edge of the frame. Text and boxes should sit inside a type area with a margin around it (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "Margin proportions"). The column is about 880 px wide, so the body runs about 100 characters per line. The first paragraph's opening line is "From November to March … The first crossing is at", which is well past the 45–75 range (Bringhurst, 2.1.2). On long lines the eye loses its place on the way back to the next line, and that's in exactly the paragraph people will reread for times. The same fault explains the lopsided composition: everything is piled on the left and the rest of the frame is empty space nobody chose. Fix: give the content a real left margin and narrow the column to about 600–680 px at this text size. Then either centre the column in the page or keep it to a left-hand column with something useful in the space beside it. If the missing margin comes from a broken container in the capture and not from the design, check that in a live browser.

**Smaller things.**
- Grey box, lower third: the space below the text (about 50 px) is nearly twice the space above it (about 27 px). The box looks like it's waiting for a missing line. Make the padding equal.

**What's working.**
- The headline says exactly what the page is: "How the river ferry runs in winter" is specific, plain and useful. Keep that voice.
- The type is restrained and clear. There's one sans-serif family in two sizes and the heading is set in bold. The bold heading is clearly above the body text without any extra decoration. Dark text on white and on the pale grey tint reads easily.
- The chunks are short: three short blocks of two lines each, with even spacing between them. Keep this when you add the timetable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone. Most people will read a ferry timetable on one, so check it at 320 px.
