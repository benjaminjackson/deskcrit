**First read.** The eye lands on the big four-line headline, "Quayside ferry crossings by daylight, spring through early summer", and reads all of it, because it is the only thing on the page with any weight. Then it drops to the short paragraph, reaches "weather." and stops. Nothing comes after it. It reads as the calm, competent top of a small transport or tourism page, but the small grey label above the headline says "Spring timetable" and there is no timetable. You are left asking: so when does it go?

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: the top of a web page for a local river ferry. It should tell someone planning a crossing when and how often the ferry runs, so they can turn up at the right landing at the right time.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do that yet. The type is set well, but the page puts its loudest voice on a description of the service and gives no time the viewer can act on. Missing content is the single biggest problem; the layout is not.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page promises a timetable and doesn't give one.** The label "Spring timetable" sits above a headline and paragraph with no first crossing, no last crossing, no dates and no departure times. A rider learns the ferry exists and runs "by daylight". That's not enough to plan around, because daylight moves by a couple of hours between spring and early summer. The eye has nowhere to go after the paragraph, and the label's promise is broken. *Defect* (the honesty point is common sense: a label that names content which isn't there misleads). Fix: show the actual departures, or at least first and last crossing from each landing and the date range. Give them a clear place directly below the intro. If the full table is lower on the page, the top still needs one concrete fact and a visible way down to the rest.

2. **The few facts that are there are worded so they can be read two ways.** "Runs from the north landing to the market quay every forty minutes" has two possible readings:
   - A boat leaves the north landing every 40 minutes, and the trip back is unstated.
   - A boat leaves each landing every 40 minutes.

   Someone waiting at the market quay can't tell which applies. If they guess wrong, they could wait up to 40 extra minutes or miss the boat. "Spring through early summer" has no dates, so nobody can tell whether the timetable covers their trip in June. "In calm weather" points to a rough-weather case it never explains. These are the details people act on and hold the operator to. *Defect.* Fix: name both directions ("Boats leave both landings every 40 minutes"), give start and end dates, and say what happens in bad weather or where to check.

3. **The headline spends the page's best space on a topic, not the fact people came for.** At about 80 px over four lines, the headline takes up a third of the screen to say what the page is about. The one useful number, every 40 minutes, sits in 20 px body text below it. *Taste, and I'd argue it firmly:* someone checking a ferry wants frequency and hours at a glance. Let the headline carry the key fact, for example how often it runs and between which landings, and move the season into the label or the paragraph. That would also shorten the headline from four lines to two, which fixes the "summer" problem below.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks "early / summer" across lines and leaves "summer" alone on the last line. Shorten the headline or adjust its width so the break falls between phrases.
- The paragraph ends with "weather." alone on its last line. Adjust the width or the wording so at least two words finish the paragraph (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- **The type scale.** There are three clear sizes, each with an obvious job: the small grey bold label (~15 px), body text (~20 px) and a heavy headline (~80 px). The steps are large, and the label is separated by weight and colour as well as size. Keep this scale when you add the times, and resist adding a fourth size close to one of these.
- **The text block is comfortable to read.** The paragraph runs about 66–68 characters per line with generous line spacing, and it shares a firm left edge with the headline and label at x≈120.
- **The headline is set tight and holds together.** Line spacing and letter spacing are tightened without any lines colliding, so it reads as one confident block.
