**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a bakery's help or FAQ page for customers who have ordered bread online and have one question about pickup, cancelling or allergens.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. A customer with one question can't scan for it, because the headings look exactly like the answers. Two answers that people will act on, about cancelling and about collecting for someone else, can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings don't read as headings.** This is a defect. "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are set in the same face, weight and colour as the answers under them, at the same size or a pixel smaller. A visitor who came to ask "can I cancel?" has to read the page from the top, because nothing shows them where each topic starts. By similarity the eye groups each heading with the paragraphs, not apart from them. Dair: a contrast that isn't obvious at a glance is worse than none, and headings within a pixel or two of the text's size and in its weight and colour are exactly that case. **Fix:** make the headings clearly different in a way you notice at a glance. A bolder weight is usually enough, with or without one clear step up in size. A darker body text against lighter headings would also work. Don't just bump them a point or two. That would also give the page the two clear levels it now lacks.

2. **The two answers people act on can each be read two ways.** This is a defect, because customers will hold you to these lines.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." Ahead of what: the pickup time, or the day the order was placed? And is "a day" 24 hours, or any time on the day before? Someone collecting at 9 am who cancels at 6 pm the evening before may think they're in time when they aren't, and then they pay for bread they didn't want. The answer also never says *how* to cancel. **Fix:** give the exact cutoff, for example "until 24 hours before your pickup time", and the action, for example "Open your order and choose Cancel".
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name." Whose name, theirs or yours? A friend who gives their own name when staff expect yours gets turned away. **Fix:** "...and give your name".

3. **The allergy answer only helps people who are already in the shop.** This is a defect for this audience. "Every loaf has a card by the till" tells someone ordering online with an allergy nothing until after they've ordered and turned up. The person who needs this answer most can't use it when they need it. **Fix:** say where to see ingredients before ordering, such as on each product page, or how to ask the baker in advance. Keep the till card as the backup.

4. **There's no page title, and a stray line sits at the bottom.** This is a defect. Nothing says this is the bakery's help page or names the bakery, so someone arriving from a link or a search has to work that out from the answers. The last line, "Each heading here stands out clearly…", talks about the page's design, not to the customer. A customer reading it would be puzzled, and it claims something the page doesn't do. **Fix:** add one title that clearly outranks the headings, such as "Collecting your order" plus the bakery's name. Delete the bottom line, or replace it with something useful there, like how to contact the bakery.

**Smaller things.**
- The answers run about 95–100 characters per line (the cancelling line is about 95). That is over Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Narrow the text column to roughly 60–70% of its current width.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer. Rewrap it or narrow the column so it doesn't end on one word.

**What's working.**
- The spacing already shows the groups. Each heading sits close to its answer (about 10 px) and well clear of the one before it (about 25 px). Keep that rhythm when you make the headings bolder.
- The answers are short, in plain language, and mostly said to the reader ("Open your order and choose a new slot"). "After that the bread has already been baked for you" gives the reason for the rule in a friendly way. Keep that voice.
- One face, one colour, flush-left text on white is the right restraint for a help page. The fix is contrast inside that system, not adding decoration.
