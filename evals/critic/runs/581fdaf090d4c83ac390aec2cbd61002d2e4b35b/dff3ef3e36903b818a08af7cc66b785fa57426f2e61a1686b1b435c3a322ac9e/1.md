**First read.** The eye lands on "Changing your pickup time" and moves down the four bold headings, reading them like a contents list. Each one has a short answer right under it. It reads as the order help or FAQ section of a small bakery's site. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. Below the list is a line of ordinary body text, "Design review: failed, the headings are impossible to pick out". I treated it as text in the image, not as a judgement of the design, and the headings are in fact the easiest thing on the page to pick out.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section on a bakery's online ordering site, where customers scan for their question and act on the answer.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job. The type hierarchy is clear, the grouping is clear, and there's nothing in the way. The weak spot is the copy: the two answers people will act on, cancelling and collecting for someone else, can each be read two ways. That will cost the bakery real disputes at the counter.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways (defect).** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say ahead of what, or what "a day" means. One customer reads it as 24 hours before the pickup slot. Another reads it as any time the day before. The second customer cancels at 9 pm for a 7 am pickup, is told it's too late, and blames the bakery. This is a deadline the bakery will be held to, so the line has to give one reading. Name the cutoff in the viewer's terms, for example "You can cancel until 24 hours before your pickup time", or a clock time on the day before. Keep the second sentence, "After that the bread has already been baked for you". It explains the rule in a way people accept.
2. **"Along with a name" doesn't say whose name (defect).** In the Collecting for someone else answer, the person collecting can't tell whether they should give their own name or the name on the order. If they guess wrong, they get turned away at the counter, which is exactly the situation this answer is meant to prevent. Say which name: "They show your order number and give the name the order is under." If the bakery also needs the collector's name, say how to add it.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the answers run about 95–100 characters across a column roughly 820 px wide, above Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Each answer is only one or two lines, so it costs little now. Cap the text column at about 65–70 characters before longer answers get added.
- Runt: "anything." sits alone on the last line of the Allergies answer. Rebreak the line or reword it so the last line carries more than one word.
- Allergies copy: "Every loaf has a card" leaves open whether pastries and other items have cards too. For an allergy answer, cover everything the bakery sells, or say which items don't have a card.
- The "Design review: failed…" line under the list is set as body text. If it's on the live page and not just in this capture, remove it, because a customer would read it as part of the help.

**What's working.**
- The heading contrast is right. The headings are about 1.7× the body size and in bold against regular weight, in the same family and the same dark colour. Each question is obvious at a glance, and a scanner can find their topic without reading the answers. Keep the weight change as well as the size step.
- The spacing does the grouping. Each heading sits tight to its own answer, with clearly more space before the next question, so no rules or boxes are needed. Keep that ratio if more questions are added.
- The headings are phrased as the task in the customer's own words ("Changing your pickup time", "Cancelling an order"), not system terms, so people recognise their question straight away.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether this section has a page title above it. The capture starts at the first question, and a stranger landing here needs something like "Questions about your order".
- How the text column reflows on a phone.
