**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold sans headline, then drops into three serif columns. Before you read a word, the columns look moth-eaten: wide gaps between words and white streaks running down through every column. It reads as a short informational article about a local ferry. It feels calm and orderly from a distance and rough up close.

**What it's for.** You didn't say, so I'm assuming this is a short web or print article giving ferry passengers practical facts: how often it runs, bikes, winter service.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but the text setting is broken. Full justification on columns about 30 characters wide tears holes in every paragraph, and the writing leaves out the times a passenger needs.

**What would move it most**
1. **Justified text on columns that are too narrow (defect).** All three columns are justified at roughly 28–32 characters a line. To fill each line, the browser stretches the word spaces. Look at "In   winter   the   timetable", "minutes   between   early", "bicycle   during   the" and "weather,   and   a   little". The gaps line up into white channels down each column, so the eye trips on every second line and the page looks careless. Bringhurst calls short justified lines "white acne". Hochuli says that under about 50 characters even word spacing "becomes difficult, if not impossible", and gives 40–50 characters for text set in columns. The simplest fix is to set the text flush left with a ragged right edge, which removes the holes at once. If you want justified text, use two wider columns of about 45–50 characters with hyphenation turned on. There is room for this, because the fourth column is empty.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on (defect).** "Between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "one boat each hour" are the lines a reader would plan a trip around, and none of them gives a time. "Before dusk" can be read two ways: the last boat leaves some time before dusk, or it leaves at dusk. Someone who reads the second way misses the boat. Give the first and last departure times, the winter last sailing as a clock time (or "about 30 minutes before sunset"), and which months count as winter. Point people to the printed timetables in the shelters, which the third column already mentions.
3. **The headline promises more than the text gives (taste and copy).** "Through the seasons" suggests a tour of the year, but the body covers the normal timetable, bikes, and winter only. A reader looking for summer or holiday changes won't find them. Either write the seasons the headline promises, or rename it for what's there, for example "Taking the river ferry: times, bikes and winter service".

**Smaller things.**
- Columns have no labels. Each covers a separate topic (timing, bicycles, winter), and a short bold label at the top of each would let a passenger jump straight to their question.

**What's working.**
- Strong contrast between headline and text. The bold sans headline against the serif body changes size, weight and structure at once, so there is no doubt what to read first. Keep it.
- A clean grid. The three columns have equal gaps, share a top line, and their lines sit on the same baselines across the gutters. That gives the page its calm order, so keep the grid while you fix the setting.
- Dark text on white with generous line spacing reads easily once the word spacing is fixed.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the three columns reflow on a narrow phone screen. Justified text at an even narrower width would get worse, so check the setting at mobile widths.
