**First read.** The eye goes to "Landing stage rebuild", then down to the flat blue-grey picture, then over to the boxed "Get timetable changes" form on the right. It reads as a news item on a small ferry operator's site with a newsletter sign-up beside it. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and a bit bare.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a ferry operator's update page that tells passengers about the rebuilt landing and gets them to sign up for timetable change notices.

**Verdict.** The form can't do the one thing it promises. It asks for a name and a "home landing" but not an email or phone number, so the "short note" has nowhere to go. That gap matters more than everything else on the page put together. Second, the article doesn't show or lead with what the rebuild means for passengers.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sign-up has no way to reach anyone.** This is a defect. The card on the right says "We send one short note when the timetable changes", but its only fields are "Your name" and "Home landing". A passenger who presses Sign up has given nothing the operator can send to. Careful readers will see the gap and not trust the form. Everyone else will think they've signed up and never hear anything. The page doesn't let a first-time user work out how this works (Norman, *Design of Everyday Things*, conceptual model; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). The fix: add the channel the note goes by, such as an email field (or a phone field if notes go by text), and name that channel in the helper line ("We email you once when…"). The name field is the one to drop if the form needs to stay short, because the note doesn't need it.
2. **"Home landing" is a blank box when it should be a choice.** This is a defect (Nielsen heuristics 5 and 6; Norman, constraints). It makes passengers remember and spell the operator's name for their landing. Typos and nicknames ("north pier", "N. landing") will send them the wrong timetable or none, and the label doesn't say why the form wants it. The fix: a short list of the landings to pick from, with a hint like "We only send changes for this landing". Since this article is about the north landing, it could be preselected.
3. **The picture doesn't show the rebuild, and the best news is buried.** This is a defect for the article. The figure is a few flat blocks: sky, a grey slab, a lighter grey box and a blue rounded boat. The caption calls it "The new north landing", but there's no visible ramp, cover, benches or kiosk, so the picture shows none of the claims in the text. The one line that changes a passenger's day, "prams and wheelchairs roll straight on" at every tide, comes after the figure, in the last paragraph. The headline "Landing stage rebuild" is a label: it doesn't say which landing, or what's different for the reader. The fix: lead with the consequence, for example a headline that names the north landing and says step-free boarding at any tide. Then either show the ramp meeting the deck or drop the figure. Finally, give the form one line that ties it to the article, so the eye path from story to sign-up has a reason.

**Smaller things.**
- The boat in the figure is the same saturated blue, with the same rounded shape, as the Sign up button. One colour then does two jobs, and the picture pulls attention away from the only action on the page. Give the boat a muted tone and keep that blue for the action.
- "Sign up" is generic. Say what happens instead, e.g. "Email me timetable changes", which also fixes the missing-channel problem in the copy.
- "Figure 1." reads like a report, not a passenger update (taste). A plain caption is enough.

**What's working.**
- The body text is set well: a comfortable line length (about 65 characters), generous leading, and a clear four-step type scale (headline, card heading, body, small text). Keep it.
- The form is laid out correctly: labels persist above the fields, input borders are dark and clear, there's one filled primary button, and the helper text promises that notes can be stopped at any time. That honest, low-pressure framing is right for this audience.
- The page is quiet and calm. Nothing fights the headline.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Error and validation states on the form, and how the two columns reflow on a phone, matter here because many passengers will sign up standing at a landing.
