**First read.** The eye lands on the big serif headline, "The finest bread in the valley", reads it, drops to the two-line paragraph, and stops. Nothing comes after that. The bottom 40% of the screen and the right third are empty cream. It reads as a calm, traditional, well-set bakery homepage, but one with nothing in it beyond a sentence and a tagline.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: the first screen of a local bakery's website. It's for nearby people deciding whether to visit or order, and it should tell them what's sold and how to get it.

**Verdict.** The type is handled with real care, but as a homepage hero it doesn't do its job. It gives the visitor no next step, no hours, address or order route, and no picture of the product. The one claim it makes is one every bakery makes.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page ends where the visitor's questions start.** After "…on Mill Street." there's no button, link, opening hours or menu. There's also no header or navigation, so a first-time visitor can't tell what else the site holds. Someone who has just been told about sourdough baked every morning wants to know when they can get it and where. The page makes them go and look for that. Defect: it breaks the convention that a site shows its navigation at the top (Nielsen, Jakob's law) and that a view has one primary action (Material Design, Apple HIG). Krug's test also fails: you can't tell what you can do here. Direction: add one dominant action that names an exact thing to do, like "See today's loaves" or "Order for pickup tomorrow", not "Learn more". Put hours and the Mill Street address close by, and add a normal top bar with the site's sections.
2. **The empty lower half reads as leftover, not as breathing room.** Below the paragraph, from y≈510 to the bottom edge, nothing sits on the page. It looks as if a block failed to load. White space that isn't shaped around anything makes the page feel unfinished (Tschichold; Müller-Brockmann). Convention. Direction: use that space for the action and visit details from point 1, or for a photograph. Then the headline keeps its room, and the space it sits in has a reason.
3. **The copy could be on any bakery's page, and there's no bread to see.** "The finest bread in the valley" is a superlative the visitor can't check and has seen a hundred times, so it tells them nothing about Hartley's. The specific, believable facts are in smaller type: since 1962, a wood-fired oven, baked every morning, Mill Street. For a food business, the missing photo of the loaves is the bigger loss. The visitor can't judge the bread for themselves and has to take "finest" on trust. Taste, argued: lead with what only Hartley's can say, for example the oven or the 60 years. Then let a real photo of today's bread do the persuading.
4. **The headline breaks badly and leaves "valley" alone on line 2.** At this size the break is the most visible typographic choice on the screen. "in the / valley" separates the article from its noun, and one short word on the last line reads as a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2, for paragraphs; the same logic applies here). Convention. Direction: break as "The finest bread / in the valley", or narrow the headline's measure so the lines are closer in length.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing. A classical old-style serif for the display line against a plain sans for the eyebrow and body is a clear contrast, not a conflict. It feels like an established, traditional baker. There's even a proper "fi" ligature in "finest". Keep it.
- The type scale is disciplined. There are three sizes, eyebrow, headline and body, each obviously its own level. Size, weight and colour work together, with the brown caps for the eyebrow.
- The eyebrow, "HARTLEY'S BAKERY · SINCE 1962", is correctly letterspaced, and the brown is used once, with restraint. It's the right place for the brand and the heritage cue.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the empty lower half is the design or an image or section that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check in a live browser. If a hero photo belongs there, findings 2 and part of 3 change. Reflow at narrow widths also can't be judged from a still: the headline will rewrap, so check that the breaks hold.
