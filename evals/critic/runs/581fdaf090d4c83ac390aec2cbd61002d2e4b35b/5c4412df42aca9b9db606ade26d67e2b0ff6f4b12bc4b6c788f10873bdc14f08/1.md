**First read.** The eye lands on "Pricing", drops to the three prices, and then reads across three identical cards with nothing to tell them apart. It stops at the bottom of the feature lists. Nothing there to click, so it goes back up to "Start free" in the top-right corner. It's clean, calm and polite, and it could be almost any SaaS pricing page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the pricing page for Tidewell, a scheduling or booking tool. It's for small teams who have already clicked "Pricing" and want to pick a plan and sign up.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The page shows prices but gives no way to act on them: no plan has a button, and none is recommended. It also leaves out what the price covers, so the viewer can't commit with confidence.

**What would move it most**

1. **No plan can be chosen (defect).** Each card ends at its feature list and leads nowhere. The only action on the page is "Start free", at the far top-right of the nav. It's about 300 px and a whole layout band away from the plans it's meant to start, and it doesn't say which plan you get. "Free" doesn't appear on any card, so the viewer can't tell whether it's a trial, a free tier, or how long it lasts. Pricing pages almost always put a button in each plan, so people look for one. Here they find none and have to guess that the nav button is the way in (Nielsen: users expect sites to work like the others they know; Norman: the control should sit next to what it controls; Krug: where to click should be obvious within seconds). **Fix:** put a button at the foot of each card that names what happens, such as "Start Team free for 14 days". Add one line under the price, or under the subtitle, that says what "free" means and whether a card is needed.

2. **Nothing tells the viewer which plan is for them (defect).** The three cards match in size, weight, colour and border, so all three compete to be seen first. The viewer has to compare three lists line by line and decide alone (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance through differences of size). Most pricing pages solve this by pointing at the plan most buyers should take. **Fix:** make one plan clearly the first thing seen. Team is the likely pick, given "teams of every size". Give it the accent border or a tinted card and a filled button, and set the other two back with outlined buttons. A short label in the viewer's terms helps, such as "Most teams of 3–10 pick this", rather than "Popular".

3. **The terms on the prices can be read two ways (defect, on the numbers people will act on).**
   - "$29 / month" could mean $29 for the whole team or $29 per person. A team of 8 reading it the second way expects $232, or the other way round, and someone ends up surprised at checkout.
   - "Up to 10 calendars" doesn't say whether a calendar is a person or a room.
   - The lists don't say whether higher plans include lower ones. As written, Business lists "Unlimited calendars, Single sign-on, Priority support" and not "Shared availability" or "Reports". A buyer who needs SSO and reports can fairly conclude that no plan gives both.

   The viewer has to fill in the meaning, and some will fill it in wrong (Nielsen: use the user's own words, and don't make them carry facts from one card to the next). **Fix:** state the unit and the billing period ("per team, billed monthly" or "per user / month"), say what a calendar is ("1 calendar (1 person)"), and start the Team and Business lists with "Everything in Solo, plus:" and "Everything in Team, plus:".

4. **The words could sit on anyone's page (copy, taste).** "Pricing" repeats the nav item that's already underlined, at the same size as the prices. "Simple plans for teams of every size" could belong to any product, and it doesn't even fit Solo. Nowhere does the page say what Tidewell does. A visitor who arrives here straight from a search sees "calendars" and "booking page" and has to piece the product together. **Fix:** use the headline to tie the price to the job, such as "Let clients book you, from $9 a month". The subtitle can then carry a concrete promise in the viewer's terms, such as what they stop doing by hand.

**Smaller things.**
- The subtitle leaves "you like." alone on a second centred line. Shorten the sentence or break it at "Change or cancel whenever you like" so it holds together.

**What's working.**
- All three cards are built the same way: name, price, then three features on matching rows. This lets the eye compare across at a glance, so keep it when you add buttons and "Everything in…" lines.
- Inside each card, the price is big and bold and "/ month" is small and grey. That's the right order: number first, terms second. The fix in point 3 adds words to that grey line; it doesn't make it louder.
- The nav marks the current page with a coloured underline on "Pricing", so the viewer knows where they are.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The bottom 230 px are empty. If the page really ends there, that's where an FAQ belongs (billing, cancelling, what "free" means). If more content sits below the fold, check that the plan buttons come before it.
- Hover and focus states on the buttons you add.
