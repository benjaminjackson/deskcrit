Then it reaches one more sentence under the button, "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge.", and stops there, puzzled. Before that last line it reads as a quiet, tidy landing page for a small local ferry. The last line makes it feel unfinished.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a ferry operator's web page, meant to get a local or visitor to choose the boat and check when it sails.

**Verdict.** The skeleton is sound: one clear headline, one clear button, a calm palette. But the page ends on a sentence that has nothing to do with ferries and isn't even true, and it leaves out the facts a rider acts on. Remove the stray line and add real times, the destination and the fare, and this works.

**What would move it most**
1. **A stray design note sits under the button (defect).** "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge" is the last thing on the page and the last thing the eye reaches, right after the button. A rider can't make sense of it. It describes the page, not the ferry, and it's false: every block on the page is centred, and nothing is aligned left. It reads like a leftover note to the designer or placeholder text. It pulls attention away from the button at the very moment the viewer should be acting, and it makes the whole operator look careless. Delete it. If left alignment was the intent, the layout doesn't deliver it (see 3). Don't keep the line as a claim. (Nielsen's "aesthetic and minimalist design": every element should support the user's task.)
2. **The copy leaves out what a rider plans around (defect).** The headline is good: "twelve calm minutes" is a specific, checkable promise. But "Cross the bay" never says where to. The paragraph names only the departure point, "the north landing". "From early morning until late evening" gives no first or last boat, so a commuter or someone catching a train home can't plan without clicking through. Every fare is missing except the bicycle's ("Bicycles travel free"), which raises the obvious question of what a person pays. These are the lines people act on and later hold the operator to, so vagueness here costs trips. Name both landings. Give the first and last sailings ("First boat 6:20, last 22:40, every 40 minutes"). Put the adult fare next to the free-bike line. The button label is already a specific ask, which is right. It would get stronger with the next departure beside it ("Next boat 10:40, see the full timetable"), so the viewer checks the value against their own plans.
3. **Everything is centred, including the paragraph and the list (convention, with a real cost).** Centring the short headline and the button suits the calm mood. The three-line paragraph is a different matter: its lines run long (about 75 characters), and with both edges ragged each line starts somewhere new, so the eye has to hunt for the start of every line. The three features ("Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board") have no bullets and no shared left edge. They read as three loose captions, not a scannable list of benefits. Tschichold rejects setting everything on a central axis by habit, and Bringhurst (2.1.3) treats ragged setting as conditional. Keep the headline and button centred if you like. Put the paragraph and the list on one shared left edge, or cut the paragraph to two short lines so centring holds up.

**Smaller things.**
- Type sizes: the paragraph (about 20 px) and the feature lines (about 18 px) differ by only a couple of pixels, with the same weight and colour, so the list looks accidentally smaller, not like a separate level. Set them at the same size, or separate them clearly by weight or colour (Bringhurst 3.1.1; Dair on weak contrast).
- Headline break: "twelve / calm minutes" separates the number from what it counts. Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" (Bringhurst 2.4.6).
- (Taste) A page selling the view from an open deck shows no picture of the bay. One photo of the harbour would do more than the paragraph's "watch the harbour slide past".

**What's working.**
- The headline is a concrete promise ("twelve calm minutes"), set far larger than anything else, so there's no doubt where to look first.
- One filled button with a specific label ("See the timetable"), and blue kept for the brand name and the action only. The next step is never in question.
- Restrained palette and generous space that match the calm the copy promises. Keep this mood when you add the facts.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button, and how the centred column reflows on a phone.
