**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big dark-green headline, "Plan a week of dinners in ten minutes", then down through two short paragraphs to the green "Plan my first week" button. Within two seconds it reads as a calm, plain landing page for a meal-planning service aimed at busy households. Both versions read the same way. The difference is how easily you get through the two paragraphs between the headline and the button.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the top section of a landing page that has to tell a home cook what Weekplate does and get them to press "Plan my first week".

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. Only the alignment changed: V1 sets the whole section flush left and V2 centres it. Centring makes the two explanatory paragraphs harder to read, and those paragraphs are what turn the headline's promise into a reason to click. The gap is small, because the copy and the order of things are good in both. Version 1 is a sound section with nothing major in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The centring in V2 costs reading ease where the page is making its case** (convention, and a mild defect). In V2 the 3-line "Tell Weekplate who you cook for…" paragraph and the 2-line "Swap any meal…" paragraph are centred. Each line starts at a different place, so the eye has to hunt for the start of every new line, in exactly the text that explains the product. Centring suits a few short lines, such as the headline, the three feature lines or the button. It doesn't suit paragraphs of several lines (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* 2.1.3). At two and three lines the cost is modest, and it would grow with any extra copy. V2 also centres everything on one axis by default, so the section feels stiff and formal rather than chosen. V1 keeps one clean left edge from logo to headline to body to button: you read down it in one line and land on the button. If V2's centred balance is wanted, centre only the headline and button, and keep the paragraphs as one flush-left block.

For the better version (V1), nothing else reaches the ranked list.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break, both versions: "ten / minutes" splits the number from its unit and leaves "minutes" alone on line two. Break after "dinners" ("Plan a week of dinners / in ten minutes"), or widen the measure so it breaks there.
- The three feature lines ("Recipes tested in a home kitchen" and the two below it), both versions: they have no marker and are set like body text, so they read as three stray short sentences, not a list of benefits. Give them a tick or a bullet, or a shared label, and tighten the gap between them against the space above and below.
- V1: the right half of the screen and the bottom quarter are empty. That is acceptable, but it's the obvious place for one picture of a planned week or the shopping list, which would show the "ten minutes" claim instead of asking people to trust it (taste).

**What's working.**
- The copy is concrete and speaks to the reader. The body says who does what ("Tell Weekplate who you cook for, what you will not eat…") and lands on what it means for them ("less food goes to waste by Friday"; a list "sorted by the aisles of your usual shop"). Keep that voice.
- The button names one exact thing to do, "Plan my first week", rather than "Get started". It is the only filled element on the page, so nothing competes with it.
- The type scale is tight: one big bold headline, one body size, a slightly smaller size for the list and nav, and a bold button label. Every level is clearly different, using a single family and colour from dark green to grey.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover and focus states, and how the left-aligned column reflows on a phone.
