**First read.** The eye goes straight to the serif headline, "Shipments peak each third quarter", then drops into a row of eight identical blue bars and wanders across them with no place to land. It reads as a board-deck slide from an outdoor-gear maker showing quarterly shipments. It feels clean and sober until you reach the chart, where the extruded 3D bars look like a spreadsheet's default chart pasted under editorial type.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one slide in a 12-page board sales review. It has to get one seasonal or growth point across to directors in a few seconds, and it must not mislead them about what is actual and what is forecast.

**Verdict.** The framing is good: a headline that states a finding, a dek, units, and a source. But the chart doesn't show the headline's claim or mark the forecast. A director sees eight equal bars and has to hunt for the two Q3s the headline is about, and could take the Q4 2026 forecast for a result.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast bar is drawn exactly like the actuals.** Q4 2026 (560) is the same solid blue as the seven real quarters. The only sign it's a projection is a parenthesis in the dek and a clause in the source line. A director looking at the chart, or quoting "560" later, will treat it as a result, and on a board slide that number becomes something management is held to. Projections must look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Draw it as an outline or a lighter tint, label it "Q4 2026 (forecast)" at the bar, and add a range if the September forecast has one. *Defect.*
2. **The chart doesn't show what the headline says.** The headline is about third quarters, but Q3 2025 and Q3 2026 look the same as every other bar. Nothing on the chart carries the eye from the claim to its evidence. Keep one colour for the series and a second colour for the point the reader must notice (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Put the two Q3 bars in the accent and the rest in a muted blue or grey, so the seasonal rhythm shows at a glance. *Defect.*
3. **3D bars.** Every bar has a lit top face and a dark side face. That extra depth carries no data, and the reader can't tell whether to read the value at the front edge or the back edge against the gridlines. The back edge of the 610 bar sits near 630. The look also clashes with the restrained type above it. Draw the bars flat so each value is one length (Wilke, ch. 26.1; Few, pitfall 7). The value labels already give exact numbers, so you lose nothing. *Convention, and it costs something here.*
4. **The headline undersells the story and overstates the pattern.** "Peak each third quarter" rests on two Q3s, which is a thin basis for "each". It also skips what a board will care about more: Q3 2026 is up about 16% on Q3 2025 (705 vs 610), and every 2026 quarter beats the same quarter of 2025. Lead with the growth and keep the seasonality as support, for example "Q3 shipments up 16% on last year; Q3 remains the peak". Use the exact figure your finance team signs off on. *Taste (common sense: the board acts on growth, not on the shape of the season).*

**Smaller things.**
- Empty band of about 120 px below the footer: the slide sits high and the space looks left over. Give the chart that height or centre the block vertically.
- Dek: "to Q4 2026 (Q4 2026 is the forecast)" names Q4 2026 twice. Try "…eight quarters from Q1 2025; Q4 2026 is forecast." This becomes unnecessary once the bar itself is marked.

**What's working.**
- The page structure: the brand label at top left, the meeting context at top right, a headline that states a claim, units under the chart title, and a source line that says where the forecast came from. Keep all of it.
- Direct value labels on the bars and pale gridlines. The numbers can be read without the axis, and the grid stays quiet.
- The type pairing: a firm serif headline against a plain sans for everything else gives a clear contrast with only a few sizes.
