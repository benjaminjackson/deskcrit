**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading at top left, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three grey-black paragraphs that run the full width of the screen. It reads as a plain info page for a small river ferry: calm and tidy, but you have to read it like an essay. The bottom half of the frame is empty white.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes and the rules for bicycles, which people check quickly, often on the way to the landing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The facts a passenger came for (how often it runs, the first and last boats, the winter changes, where to find the timetable) are buried in long, wide paragraphs with nothing to scan. Most of those facts are also too vague to plan a trip around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts are buried in prose, so nothing can be scanned. Defect.** All three paragraphs mix timetable facts ("every forty minutes", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour" in winter) with background ("the crew must hold a wider course around the sandbank"). Someone asking "when's the next boat?" has to read every line to find the answer. Nielsen ("How Users Read on the Web") says text that people skim needs headings, short paragraphs and lists. Fix: pull the timetable out into its own block that can be scanned, such as a short list or small table with frequency, first and last sailing, crossing time and the winter timetable. Give the bicycle rules and the "where to find changes" note short subheads of their own. Keep the sandbank explanation as a secondary note.
2. **The lines are about twice as long as they should be. Convention, and it costs something here.** Each line runs the full content width, roughly 130 to 140 characters, against Bringhurst's 45 to 75 (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). At that length the eye loses its place going back to the start of the next line, which matters most for readers who are scanning. Fix: limit the text column to about 65 to 75 characters, which is roughly half its current width at this type size. The heading can stay where it is.
3. **The copy is vague exactly where passengers will act on it. Defect.** "Between early morning and late evening", "before dusk" and "every forty minutes" with no departure times give the reader nothing to plan with, and "before dusk" changes through the winter. A passenger who reads "late evening" as 10 pm may miss the last boat. These are the lines people later hold the operator to. Fix: give actual first and last departure times for each season, saying which landing they leave from. If the exact times change, say where to find today's times. Right now that is only "the shelter at each landing", so add a link or URL if one exists.

**Smaller things.**
- The page doesn't say what foot passengers pay, or say outright that they ride free. "Bicycles ride … at no extra charge" suggests there is a fare, so say what it is, or where to see it.

**What's working.**
- The type is a calm, clear system with two sizes. The heading is set apart by weight as well as size, it sits close to the text, and the space between paragraphs groups the text cleanly. Keep that restraint when you add structure: subheads can use the bold weight at body size rather than introducing a third size.
- The text is dark on white at a comfortable reading size and line spacing, so contrast and legibility are fine.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the page reflows on a phone, where passengers standing at the landing will most likely read it. Narrow screens would fix the line length on their own, so check whether this layout is mainly for desktop.
