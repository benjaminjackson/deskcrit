**First read.** In both, the eye lands on the big serif headline "Small-batch ice cream, made on Mill Street", drops to the three script subheads, and then has to read. In version 1 that reading is easy. In version 2 the three paragraphs become a wall of brush lettering, and the eye skims the texture instead of reading the words. It feels like a warm, homemade, local shop.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a local ice-cream shop. It should convince a nearby visitor that the shop is worth a trip and tell them when to come.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better, clearly. Version 2 sets every paragraph in the logo's brush script, so the shop's whole case (local milk, market fruit, tub returns) becomes slow to read. Neither version tells the visitor where the shop is or gives them a next step, and that is the biggest gap left in version 1.

**What changed, and why it matters**
- **Version 2 sets the body copy in a decorative script. This is a defect.** All three paragraphs under "Our milk / Our fruit / Our tubs" are in a heavy brush script. Joined letters, tight leading and similar word shapes slow reading to a crawl, and readers with dyslexia or low vision get hit hardest. These paragraphs are the whole argument for the shop, so the cost lands on the content that matters most. Bringhurst (6.2.1): a text face is "unlikely to be carrying excess ornament". The approved fix is to set paragraphs in a face made for reading and keep script for a few words of display. Version 1 does exactly that.
- **Version 2 also removes the contrast between the levels.** In v1 the script subheads stand out because the body is a plain sans. In v2 subhead and body are the same face, and they differ only by colour and a few pixels of size. The three columns read as one undivided mass, and the subheads lose their job as entry points. Dair's point applies: weak contrast is worse than none.
- **Version 2's faces have no logic.** The script does logo, subheads and body, yet the hours note just below stays in sans. Nothing explains why one block of text is plain and the other is not.

**What would move version 1 most**
1. **The page never says where Mill Street is or what to do next. This is a defect.** The eye path ends at the hours band and then hits roughly 270 px of empty cream. A local visitor convinced by the copy still has to go looking for the address. Fix direction: put the street address and a map or directions link into the hours band, so "when" and "where" sit together. If there is one thing to push, make it a single clear ask, such as "Today's flavours". Avoid a generic "Visit us".
2. **There is no picture of the ice cream. This is taste.** The page sells something you eat entirely in words. One strong photo of a scoop, or the board of seasonal flavours, would back up "small-batch" and "when a fruit is out of season, its flavour leaves the board". The empty lower third is the obvious place for it.

**Smaller things.**
- Short last lines: "same day." in column 1 and "the board." in column 2. Rewrap or adjust the measure so no column ends on a scrap.
- The empty lower third looks leftover rather than planned. Fill it (see finding 2) or tighten the page.
- The nav shows no current page. That is fine on a home page; just confirm it is marked on the other pages.

**What's working.**
- The type roles in version 1: a strong serif for the headline, script only for the logo and three short subheads, and a plain sans for reading. Keep that split.
- The copy is specific: "eleven miles up the valley", "Monday and Thursday mornings", "fifty pence off your next scoop". It sounds like a real shop and not a template, and it gives a concrete reason to bring a tub back.
- The hours line gives an unambiguous commitment and explains the January closure.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the three columns reflow on a phone, and the focus states on the nav links.
