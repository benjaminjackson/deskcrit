**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into a block of heavy brush script and slows right down. The script reads like a café chalkboard or a party invitation, not a transport notice. Every line takes effort, and the facts a passenger needs (how often the ferry runs, the last boat, bikes) don't jump out. The right 40% of the frame is empty, which is fine for a single reading column.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or printed notice giving ferry passengers the practical facts: frequency, crossing time, bike rules and winter changes. Readers will skim it, often while they're on the move.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The content is short and sensibly organised, but all three paragraphs are set in a decorative script. That turns quick lookup information into slow decoding, and the vague copy hides the times readers need to plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in a brush script** (all three paragraphs, from y≈140 to 520). This is a **defect.** Script letterforms join, slant and vary, so readers can't recognise word shapes at a glance. Every sentence has to be decoded, which is the opposite of what a notice people skim needs. It's worst for dyslexic and low-vision readers and for anyone reading on a phone at the landing. It also clashes with the heading: a plain grotesque sits over a party-invitation script. Those two faces look like they were pulled from two different pieces, and the playful one is on the part people actually have to read. The fix: set paragraphs in a face made for reading, and keep script and other decorative faces for a few words of display (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1). Here, the simplest route is to set the body in the same family as the heading. If you want some warmth, put it in a small accent such as the heading or a pictogram, never in the text.
2. **The facts people will act on are vague and buried in prose.** This is a **defect** (copy). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing time. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" in winter moves every week, so a passenger can't plan around it, and they'll remember the gap if they miss the boat. "A little longer when the tide turns" doesn't tell them how much longer. State the real times, such as "first boat 06:20, last 21:40; winter: hourly, last boat 16:00", and pull them out of the paragraphs onto their own lines or into a small table. That lets a skimming reader find them without reading a sentence (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: make it self-evident and scannable).

**Smaller things.**
- Copy: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" is easy to miss in the middle of a paragraph, but it's an instruction. If the rules are pulled out, make it a short imperative ("Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on") so it speaks to the rider.

**What's working.**
- **The structure is right:** three short paragraphs, one topic each (the service, bikes, winter), with clear space between them. Keep that division when you reset the type.
- **The heading is clear and plain.** A neutral bold sans, set well above the text size and sitting close to the copy it introduces. It's the right model for the rest of the page.
- **The measure and margins are comfortable:** about 70 characters a line, a generous left margin, and dark text on white with strong contrast.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the column reflows at narrow widths. Phones matter here, because passengers are likely to read this at the landing.
