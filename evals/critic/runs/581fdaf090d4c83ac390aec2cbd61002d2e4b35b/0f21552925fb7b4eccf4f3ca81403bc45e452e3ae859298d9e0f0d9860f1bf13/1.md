**First read.** The eye lands on the big headline, drops to the dark green "Get started" button, then hits "Start now" right beside it and stops: two buttons that seem to say the same thing. Everything sits in the top-left. The right half and the bottom third are empty cream. It reads as a calm, tidy group-trip planner, but it shows nothing of the product, so it could be anyone's.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a consumer landing page for a group-trip planner, and its job is to get people who are organising a trip with friends to sign up.

**Verdict.** The type is well done, but the hero doesn't do its job yet. Its one decision point is two buttons for the same action, and nothing on the screen shows the product it promises. The duplicate buttons are the biggest problem, because they sit exactly where the viewer is meant to act.

**What would move it most**
1. **Two buttons for one action** *(defect)*. "Get started" (filled) and "Start now" (outlined) sit side by side, and their labels mean the same thing. A viewer who reaches the end of the page's eye path has to stop and work out how the two differ. Some will guess there's a catch, such as "one of these is a trial". The duplicate also weakens the filled button, which should be the only thing to do here (Nielsen heuristic 8). Keep one button and give the second a different job. For a group product, the natural second button is something like "See a sample trip" or "How it works". Also make the main button say what happens, for example "Plan a trip" or "Start a trip with your group". "Get started" could be on any site and doesn't say what the click leads to.
2. **The promise has nothing showing it** *(defect for this job, plus taste on the layout)*. "Plan the whole trip in one place" asks the viewer to take it on trust. Nothing shows flights, stays and day plans sitting together, or a group agreeing. The right half of the screen, about 560 px wide and the full height, is the obvious place for that. A real itinerary screen with three people's votes on a hotel would prove the claim and give the viewer something to compare with their current tools. Without it, the page is all weight top-left against blank space, which looks unfinished, not calm. The empty band under the buttons comes from the same gap. My view as a designer: fill the right half with the product, and the balance problem goes away too.
3. **The copy stops at a description** *(defect)*. The headline is a category claim that every planning app makes. The sub-line has the one point only this product makes, "without a dozen chat threads", but it's at the end of the second line, in grey. That's the viewer's own pain and the comparison they already accept: their group chat. Bring it forward. The headline or the sub-line should set Wayfold against the group chat and tell the viewer what they get, for example "Stop planning the trip in a group chat. Everyone sees the flights, the stays and the plan, and votes in one place." Then it lands on the viewer instead of describing the software.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks "in / one place" across lines, which splits the phrase. Break after "trip" so "in one place" stays together.

**What's working.**
- **Type scale:** about four sizes (headline around 52, sub-line 20, buttons 17, nav 15), with big steps between them. Each size has an obvious job. Keep it as you add content.
- **Restraint:** one warm neutral, one deep green, near-black type, and the accent kept for the action and the logo. It feels calm and trustworthy, which suits a planning tool.
- **The sub-line:** about 60 characters a line, comfortable leading, and the one specific promise on the page. Keep it as text, just move it up.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the two buttons and the nav links, and how the hero reflows on a phone, where the right-hand product visual will have to stack.
