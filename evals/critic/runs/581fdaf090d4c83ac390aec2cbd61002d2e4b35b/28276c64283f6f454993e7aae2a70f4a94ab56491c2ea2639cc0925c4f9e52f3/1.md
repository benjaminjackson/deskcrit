**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then goes into three paragraphs of steady, easy prose. The right half of the screen is empty. It reads as a quiet notice page for a river ferry. It looks calm and tidy, and it gives a passenger almost nothing they can plan with.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and the rules for bikes and winter, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and the page reads easily, but it fails at its job. A passenger who comes here to find out when the boat leaves gets "early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk", and is told the real timetable is on paper at the landing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy has no times, and times are what the reader will act on.** In paragraph 1, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat leaves. In paragraph 3, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" has no clock time, and dusk moves by more than an hour across a winter. "One boat each hour" doesn't say on the hour or at half past. The last line, "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing", sends the reader to a place they're probably not standing yet. The cost is concrete: someone reads "before dusk", aims for 4:30 in December, and is stranded on the wrong bank. A passenger holds the operator to these lines, so anything that can be read two ways is a defect.
   - *Fix:* give first and last departures from each landing, summer and winter, as clock times. The usual way is a small two-column timetable (north landing / market quay) or at least "First boat 6:40, last boat 21:20". Name the dates or month when the winter timetable starts. Put the timetable on this page instead of pointing to the shelter. (Defect, common sense: this page exists to answer this question.)
2. **The one fact people scan for is buried in the longest sentence.** Paragraph 1 is one sentence of about 60 words. It runs from the route, through the frequency and crossing time, to the tide, the sandbank and the harbour wall, and the conditional detail takes most of it. A passenger skimming for "how often" and "how long" has to read about the sandbank to get there.
   - *Fix:* lead with the facts, one short sentence each: route, frequency, crossing time. Then put the low-tide delay in a plain sentence of its own that tells the reader what it means for them: "At low tide most mornings, the crossing can take X minutes longer, so allow extra time." The current wording describes the crew's course, not your wait. (Defect.)
   - *Optional, taste:* a short bold label before each paragraph ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter") would let a returning reader jump straight to the bike rule. The page is short enough that this is optional.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 1 ends with "tide." alone on its last line (a runt, Bringhurst 2.4.2). It will likely go away once the sentence is rewritten. If not, rewrap or nudge the measure so the last line carries at least two words.
- Paragraph 2, "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded", is clear, but "no extra charge" begs the question of what the fare is. If fares exist, state them here.

**What's working.**
- The text column is the right width (about 65 characters a line) and has generous, even line spacing. The body text is dark and a comfortable size. Keep the column width and line spacing as they are when the timetable goes in.
- The hierarchy is plain: one bold heading clearly bigger than one body size, with nothing else competing. Two sizes are enough. Don't add more when you add a timetable; use weight for its labels.
- The tone is calm and human. "Bicycles ride on the lower deck" is the right voice, so keep it while you add the numbers.
