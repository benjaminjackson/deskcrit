**First read.** Your eye goes to the headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan", then to the four purple bars. The tallest one, Growth at 4.0%, holds it for a moment. The table on the right reads as backup. It looks like a calm, tidy analytics page you can trust. But after a few seconds you're still asking "so what?" The page shows four numbers and leaves you to decide what they mean.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal analytics screen for product, growth or finance people deciding where to put effort across pricing plans. Correct me if it's for something else.

**Verdict.** It's well made and honest: the bars start at zero, every value is labelled, the source and definition are there, and the table's numbers match the percentages. What holds it back is that it never says what the data means. On top of that it presents gaps of 0.2 to 0.9 points between plans as if they were all real, when for Enterprise most of that gap could be chance.

**What would move it most**
1. **The gaps look certain, but only some of them are.** *Defect.* Enterprise's 3.4% comes from 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. At that size the rate could easily sit anywhere from about 2.3% to 4.5%. Team, at 3.6%, has a similar spread of about ±0.5 points. So the ordering Growth > Team > Enterprise isn't settled. The only gap clearly bigger than chance is Starter (3.1%) against Growth (4.0%). The chart draws every bar the same solid way with a confident bold label, so someone could, for example, fund an Enterprise fix on noise. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says to show uncertainty with the estimate. The fix: add a range to each bar (thin error bars or a pale band) or a note on sample size. At the least, show the number of paid accounts under each plan name so the thin Enterprise bar can be seen as thin.
2. **The headline names the topic, not the finding.** *Taste, but I'd argue it hard for a decision screen.* "Trial to paid conversion by plan" could title this chart for any quarter with any result. The reader has to work out the point alone, and different readers will land on different points. Write a title that says the finding and stays within what the data supports, e.g. "Growth trials convert best; Starter trails by about 1 point". Then make that one bar stand out and set the others in a muted tint. Today all four bars are the same full purple, so nothing tells the eye which comparison matters. This is the "one idea" the page is missing.
3. **The definition can be read two ways.** *Defect, because it sets what counts as a conversion.* The subtitle says "became paid accounts within 30 days". The Definition says "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." Is the window 30 days from each trial's start, or one fixed cutoff on 30 October? Take a trial that started on 1 July and paid on 20 August. Under the first reading it isn't converted. Under the second it is. Someone comparing this with another report will get different numbers depending on which they assume. Choose one rule and state it in a single sentence that names who did what by when, e.g. "A trial counts as converted if its first invoice is paid within 30 days of the trial starting."

**Smaller things.**
- The table is titled "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" but also shows paid accounts and leaves out the rate. Retitle it, e.g. "Trials and paid accounts", and add a Rate column so readers can check the bars without doing the division.
- The y-axis runs to 5.0%, above the highest value, and repeats what the direct labels already say. You could drop the axis labels and keep only the zero line, but this is a judgment call.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest. The bars start at zero, each bar has its value in bold just above it, and the plans are in tier order, which suits a reader who knows the product. Every percentage matches its row in the table (286/9,210 = 3.1%, 185/5,140 = 3.6%, 118/2,950 = 4.0%, 38/1,120 = 3.4%).
- The type is restrained: about four sizes, with clear steps between levels made by weight and grey rather than extra sizes. Table numbers are right-aligned with light rules between rows. Keep this when you add annotations.
- The source line and a definition sit on the page itself. That's the right instinct; the definition only needs its wording fixed.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the top tabs. Whether the two columns stack cleanly at narrow widths, with the table and definition kept next to the chart they explain.
