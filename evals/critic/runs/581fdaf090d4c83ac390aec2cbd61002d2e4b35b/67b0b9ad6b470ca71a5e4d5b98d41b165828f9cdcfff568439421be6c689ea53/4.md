**First read.** My eye goes straight to the four purple bars and lands on the tallest one, Growth at 4.0%. It then drifts up to the title and across to the table on the right. It reads as a calm, competent product-analytics report, cleanly typeset in the usual SaaS-dashboard style. After a few seconds I know what is being measured, but not what I'm supposed to conclude from it.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is a page in an internal analytics tool where a growth or pricing team checks how well each plan's trials convert, so they can decide where to act.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid and the chart is honest. As an analysis, though, it stops halfway. It shows four rates that differ by less than a point, gives no reference point or caveat, and states no conclusion. The reader can't tell whether any of the differences is real, good or bad, or worth acting on.

**What would move it most**

1. **The page shows data but never says what it means.** The title, "Trial to paid conversion by plan", names a topic. The reader has to rank four bars between 3.1% and 4.0% by eye and decide for themselves which gap matters. The most useful fact on the page sits in the table instead of the chart: Starter converts worst (3.1%) but produces the most paid accounts, 286 against Growth's 118. Nobody reading the chart sees that. There is also nothing to compare against, such as Q2, a target or the all-plan average. So "3.6%" can't be read as good or bad. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") flags metrics shown with no comparison. *Convention* for the missing comparison. Stating the takeaway is *taste*, but I'd argue it hard: a chart that leaves the conclusion to the reader gets four different conclusions. **Fix:** put the finding in the title, or in a line just under it, in the reader's terms, for example "Growth trials convert best; Starter still brings in the most paid accounts". Show the previous quarter next to each bar (a paler paired bar or a tick mark) or as a reference line.

2. **The bars suggest a ranking the sample may not support.** Enterprise's 3.4% comes from 38 conversions. My rough arithmetic gives it a margin of about ±1 point. That covers Starter, Team and nearly Growth, so its place in the order could be noise. Starter and Team, with thousands of trials each, are far more solid. In the chart, though, every bar looks equally certain. That certainty is the risk: someone will "fix Enterprise onboarding" based on a gap that might not exist. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) asks for uncertainty to be shown. *Defect.* **Fix:** add interval whiskers to the bars, or at least print the n under each plan label ("38 of 1,120"). Then the reader can see how much weight each bar can carry.

3. **The definition contradicts the subtitle, and it's the line people hold the number to.** The subtitle says trials count if they became paid accounts "within 30 days". The definition says trials "still open on 30 October are counted as not converted". That reads as a single calendar cut-off, not a 30-day window per trial. Take a trial started on 1 July that pays on day 45. The subtitle excludes it, while the definition seems to include it. These are two different metrics, and anyone recomputing the number will get a different answer. *Defect* in the copy. **Fix:** pick one rule and say it the same way in both places, for example "Paid within 30 days of starting; trials started in late September were followed until 30 October."

**Smaller things.**
- The rates are in the chart and the counts they come from are in the table on the right, so the reader has to match plans across the page by eye. Add a "Rate" column to the table, or print the counts under the bar labels.
- The right column stops at about y=415 and leaves an empty block roughly 320 px tall below the Definition. It looks left over rather than designed. Either move the table and definition down to sit level with the chart, or put the comparison data from finding 1 there.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are hairline-pale, each bar has its value printed directly above it, every bar is one colour, and the plans run in tier order instead of being sorted by value. Keep all of that when adding context.
- The source line, the stated period and the explicit definition show the right instinct: the page explains where its numbers come from. It only needs the two definitions to agree (finding 3).
- The table is set well: tabular figures aligned right, light rules, and quiet bold headers.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I couldn't check how the two-column layout behaves at narrow widths or high zoom. The table should drop below the chart, not squeeze beside it.
