**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan", then drops to the tallest bar (Growth, 4.0%) and reads the four bars from left to right. It reads as a clean, calm analytics report page for a product or growth team. It feels competent and quiet, maybe too quiet: after five seconds I know the topic, but nothing on the page tells me what I'm supposed to conclude.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal analytics dashboard where a product, pricing or growth lead sees how each plan's trials convert to paid and decides whether to act.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid, but the page doesn't do its job yet. It shows four rates as if their differences were real and important, says nothing about which difference matters, and Enterprise, one of the four bars, rests on too few sales to support the conclusion a reader will draw from it.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart invites a conclusion the data can't support.** *Defect.* The bars show 3.1 / 3.6 / 4.0 / 3.4%, and a reader will take away "Growth converts best, Enterprise falls off." But the table on the right shows Enterprise is only 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. As a rough 95% interval, that bar is about ±1.1 points, against about ±0.35 for Starter. Growth is about ±0.7. The 0.6-point gap between Growth and Enterprise is well inside the noise. Only Growth against Starter is borderline real. The chart draws all four bars with the same certainty, and the sample sizes sit in a separate table that most people won't check against the bars. Someone could reprice or rework Enterprise onboarding because of a dip that may not exist. Fix: show the uncertainty on the chart itself, either with interval whiskers on each bar or with "n = 1,120" under each plan name. If the page states a finding, say how sure it is (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16).

2. **The headline names a topic, not a finding, and nothing on the chart is singled out.** *Taste, argued: no approved source states this rule.* "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells the reader what the chart is, which the axis already says. All four bars are the same saturated purple, so the page makes no point and the reader has to build one, probably the wrong one from finding 1. Fix: once you know what's actually true, put it in the title, for example "Growth trials convert best; the other plans are within noise of each other". Keep the purple for the bar that title is about and draw the others in a muted tint (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": one colour for the series, a second for the point the reader must notice).

3. **There's nothing to compare the rates against, so 3–4% means neither good nor bad.** *Convention (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2).* The page gives one quarter with no previous quarter, no overall rate and no target. A pricing lead can't tell whether Team at 3.6% is an improvement, a decline or normal, so they can't decide anything from it. Fix: add a comparison next to each rate, such as the Q2 value as a tick or ghost bar, or a change label, plus an "all plans" reference line.

4. **The metric's definition can be read two ways.** *Defect: this is the number people will hold the team to.* The subtitle says a trial counts if it became paid "within 30 days". The Definition says a trial counts "when the first invoice is paid", and that trials "still open on 30 October" don't count. Reading one: each trial has its own 30-day window. Reading two: there's one cutoff on 30 October, so a July trial had about three months to convert and a late-September trial had 30 days. Under reading two, the rates mix different windows, and any plan whose trials skew early in the quarter looks better. Fix: write one sentence that says both rules in the viewer's terms. For example: "A trial counts as converted if its first invoice was paid within 30 days of the trial starting. We waited until 30 October so every Q3 trial had its full 30 days."

**Smaller things.**
- The lower right quadrant, below the Definition and beside the bottom half of the chart, is a large empty area left over by the layout, so the page tips to the left. Moving the Definition and source under the table so the right column ends level with the chart's base, or using that space for the comparison in finding 3, would close it (Tschichold; Müller-Brockmann).
- The table title "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" undersells the table, which also holds paid counts. A conversion-rate column would also put the numbers behind each bar in one place.

**What's working.**
- The chart follows the basics. The axis starts at zero, the bars are labelled directly, the gridlines are light enough that the bars are the darkest marks, the plans run in tier order rather than alphabetically, and there's a source line with exact dates. Keep all of that when you add intervals and comparisons.
- The table on the right is well set. The numbers are tabular and right-aligned under right-aligned headers, the rules are light, and Trials and Paid can be scanned at a glance. It's the right tool for looking up exact values next to a chart that shows the pattern.
- The type is restrained: one family, with levels separated by weight and grey rather than by more sizes. The active "Conversion" tab is clear without being loud.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indicators and hover states on the top navigation links, and the size of their clickable area. Whether the chart and table stack sensibly at narrow widths or 400% zoom.
