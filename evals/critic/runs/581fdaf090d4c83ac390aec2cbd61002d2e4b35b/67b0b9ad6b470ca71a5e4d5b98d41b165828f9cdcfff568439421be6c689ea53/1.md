**First read.** The eye lands on the purple bars, then the 4.0% over Growth, then the title. In two seconds it reads as a clean, quiet analytics page that compares conversion across four plans. It feels trustworthy: there's a source line, a definition and a table of raw counts, which is more than most dashboards give you. Then it stops. Nothing tells me whether any of this is good, bad or new.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal analytics screen where product, growth or finance people compare trial-to-paid conversion by plan and decide whether to act.

**Verdict.** It's well made and honest in its drawing, but it stops at showing numbers and never says what they mean. It also presents gaps of a few tenths of a point as a firm ranking, when the sample sizes beside it don't support that. The biggest problem is that a reader can't tell from this page whether anything needs doing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page gives no conclusion and no point of reference.** The title "Trial to paid conversion by plan" names the subject, not the finding. The bars are all the same purple, so none of them is singled out. There is no last quarter, target or average to measure 3.1%–4.0% against. The reader has to work out the conclusion, and with no comparison they can't. Is Growth at 4.0% a win or the usual figure? Did Starter drop? Put the finding in the title, in plain words. Put a comparison next to each rate: the change from Q2, or a target line. Keep the single colour, and use a second colour only for the bar the title is about. The comparison is a convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", says a metric needs a period and a comparison. The finding-as-title is my judgment rather than a cited rule: a chart people use to make decisions should say what it found.
2. **Gaps that are probably noise look like a ranking.** The labels are printed to one decimal (3.6% Team, 3.4% Enterprise), so the bars read as a firm order. But the table shows Enterprise rests on 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. A rough margin of error for that rate is about ±1 point, which spans the whole 3.1–4.0% range. Growth vs Enterprise and Team vs Enterprise are within noise. Starter vs Growth is about the only gap that looks real. A reader will conclude "Enterprise converts worse than Team" and may act on it. This is a defect. Add a range to each bar (error bars, or a shaded interval), or at least a note on how big a gap has to be to count. Then say in the copy which differences hold up. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16, covers showing uncertainty.
3. **The definition can be read two ways.** The subtitle says trials that "became paid accounts **within 30 days**". The definition says "Trials still open **on 30 October** are counted as not converted." Reading A: each trial gets its own 30-day window. Reading B: anything paid by 30 October counts, so a July trial that paid in September is counted as converted. The two readings give different numbers, and anyone checking this against another report will pick one and may get it wrong. The source line ("1 July to 30 September 2025") also covers only trial starts, though the payments run to the end of October. This is a defect, because people will hold the team to these numbers. Write one rule that says who does what by when, for example: "A trial counts as converted if its first invoice is paid within 30 days of the trial starting." Then give both date ranges in the source line.

**What's working.**
- **The chart doesn't exaggerate.** The bars start at zero, each bar carries its own value, the gridlines are pale and there is one colour. The numbers check out against the table: 286/9,210 = 3.1%, 118/2,950 = 4.0%, 38/1,120 = 3.4%. Keep all of that.
- **The raw counts and the definition sit right beside the chart.** That is what makes finding 2 visible at all. Keep the table, and make its numbers part of how the chart is read.
- **The layout is tidy.** The type hierarchy is clear, the table's numbers are right-aligned, and the active "Conversion" tab is clearly marked. Nothing needs restructuring.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the two columns stack properly on a narrow screen, and whether the nav links show focus and hover states.
