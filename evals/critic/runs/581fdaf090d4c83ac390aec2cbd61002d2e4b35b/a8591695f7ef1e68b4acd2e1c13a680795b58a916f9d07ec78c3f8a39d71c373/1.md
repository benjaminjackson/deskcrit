**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big three-line serif headline, drops to the byline, then slides into the opening paragraph without catching on anything. It reads as a long-form feature from a quality news site or magazine: quiet, literary, confident. The headline holds a whole story (people doing something odd, and a reward) and makes you want the next line.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a long-form environment feature on the web, and its job is to pull a reader past the headline into nine minutes of reading.

**Verdict.** This works. The hierarchy is clean, the type is well judged and the copy is strong. Nothing is broken. The only thing that would noticeably help is giving the opening something to look at besides type.

**What would move it most**
1. **The opening has no picture (taste, and it may not be the design).** Nothing sits above the fold on the right, or under the lede, in the empty lower 40% of the frame. That's a lot of unused space at the moment a reader decides whether to stay. A text-only opening is a legitimate editorial choice, and here it looks calm rather than broken. But this story ends on something you can picture: a hand's depth of water over a meadow, and lapwings. One lead photograph would show the reader what "came back" means before they've read a word of it, and give the empty space a job. Check first that an image wasn't meant to load there. If none was planned, consider a full-column photo under the byline or after the lede, set to the same left edge at about x=180.

**Smaller things.**
- Straight apostrophe in "hand's" in the first paragraph. Use a typographic (curly) apostrophe, and check the CMS converts quotes everywhere. This is common sense rather than a sourced rule, but in a serif this refined, a straight mark looks like a typewriter character in a typeset page.
- The headline breaks "and the / birds" between its second and third lines, which strands "the" away from its noun. Breaking before "and" ("…flood on purpose, / and the birds that came back") puts each clause on its own line, and the turn of the story lands on the last line (taste). Use a non-breaking space or `text-wrap: balance`, then check the result.

**What's working.**
- **The type system.** I count three sizes (about 14, 19 and 52 px), and each has an obvious job. Serif for the story and small grey sans for metadata ("Environment", byline, "9 min read") contrast clearly without clashing. Keep it this tight as more elements are added.
- **The reading column.** Body lines run about 67 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range. The leading is generous, and every element shares one left edge. The headline runs wider than the body column, and that reads as a deliberate break.
- **The copy.** The headline says who does what and what came of it, with no jargon. The lede gives a place, a month, a measurement and a sound. The second paragraph sets up the stakes ("paid to keep them wet") in two plain sentences. Readers don't have to fill any gaps.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The empty lower band may be the end of a short test page or a capture taken mid-load. Check in a live browser whether an image, pull quote or more text should appear there.
- I couldn't see whether "Environment" and "Tom Harrow" are links, or what hover and focus states they have. If they are links, they need a visible focus state.
