**First read.** The eye lands on the blue bars first. They are the largest and most saturated thing on the page. Then it looks for what they mean and finds nothing: no labels, no numbers, no axis. So it moves right to "Getting there", which turns out to be about buses and parking. After a few seconds this reads as a page about a reservoir with a rainfall chart nobody can read, sitting next to directions that seem to have wandered in. It feels clean and calm but empty, and the bottom 40% of the frame is blank.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a section of a visitor or information page for a reservoir site, for the general public, and that the chart is meant to show how much rain fell over some periods.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The chart, which is the focal point and the only thing the headline promises, carries no information. The bars have no categories, no values, no unit and no time period, so a visitor learns nothing about rainfall. Everything else is secondary to that.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart says nothing (defect).** The three bars in the left panel have no category labels, no value axis or numbers, no unit (mm? inches?) and no period (months? years? this week against average?). A reader can see that the middle bar is tallest and nothing else. They can't tell what it is, how much it is, or whether it's normal. They also can't tell whether the unsorted order is time order or random. Every chart needs its axes or direct labels with units, and a date or source where relevant, so the reader never has to guess what a number measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). The fix is to name each bar directly underneath it (for example "Sept / Oct / Nov") and put the value with its unit on or above each bar. Then add a one-line caption with the period and source. The meaning of the chart also lives only in the picture, so a short text statement next to it helps every reader, including screen-reader users (WCAG text alternatives).
2. **The headline names a topic, not a finding, and half the page doesn't belong under it (defect, plus taste on the title).** "Rainfall at the reservoir" heads both columns. It sits the same distance above the chart and the "Getting there" block, and the two blocks start on the same line with similar weight. So they read as one section, and the transport paragraph reads as if it were about rainfall. The page is trying to carry two ideas, conditions at the site and how to get there, under one title. The fix is to give the chart its own heading and give "Getting there" its own clearly separate place: below the chart, or as a sibling section with a heading at the same level as the rainfall one, with more space between the two groups than inside either. My taste: make the chart title say what happened ("October was the wettest month this autumn", or whatever the data shows), so that someone who only glances still gets the point.
3. **The frame and tint compete with the data (convention).** The chart sits in a dark-grey 2–3 px border on a pale blue fill. The frame is the darkest line in the chart, darker than the baseline, and the tint adds a panel that carries no data. Together they make the chart look like a placeholder card rather than a graphic. Remove the frame and the fill so the bars are the strongest marks, with a thin pale baseline (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 23.2; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). The space between the columns already does the separating.

**Smaller things.**
- The bottom ~40% of the frame and everything below the "Getting there" paragraph are leftover space, not shaped space, which makes the page top-heavy. Once the content is regrouped, the layout should fill the frame on purpose, or the frame should be cropped to the content.
- The "Getting there" copy is clear and specific. Its one weak spot is "Parking is free after four in the afternoon": it doesn't say whether parking is paid before four or what it costs, and visitors will act on that line. State the daytime rule too.

**What's working.**
- The type is well handled. There are three clear levels (title, bold subhead, body), and the right column runs about 40 characters a line with comfortable leading. The "Getting there" heading sits tight to its paragraph, so it reads as one block. Keep this scale.
- The palette is restrained: one strong blue for the data and near-black text on white. Once the chart has labels, the bars will clearly be the thing to look at.
- The directions name exact things a visitor can act on (bus number twelve, east gate, every twenty minutes, racks beside the glasshouse) rather than "easy to reach by public transport".

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the two columns reflow on a narrow screen. Check that the chart and the directions stack under their own headings rather than under the one rainfall title.
