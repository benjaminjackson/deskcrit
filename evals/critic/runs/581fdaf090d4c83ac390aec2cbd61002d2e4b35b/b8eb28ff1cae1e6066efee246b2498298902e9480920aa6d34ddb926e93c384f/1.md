**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif title, "Winter timetable at the harbour", then drops into the left column expecting times. There are none: it finds a paragraph about when the office opens and has to read prose to dig out facts. The page looks calm, well set and literary. It reads like a page from a guidebook, not a timetable. Your eye then goes to "Fares and cards", finds no fares, and ends on two grey lines with nothing under them.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page for ferry passengers that tells them when boats run this winter and what it costs, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The typesetting is good, but the page is called a timetable and has no timetable, and its one subheading promises fares that never appear. Someone who wants to know when the next boat leaves can't find out here.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable is missing. The useful facts are buried in prose.** *Defect.* The title promises sailing times. What the reader gets is "every ninety minutes", "opens at seven", "until the last boat has left" and "twenty minutes… closer to thirty-five", all in the middle of sentences, with no first sailing, no last sailing and no list of departures. A passenger has to read three paragraphs and still can't plan. The two grey rules at y≈585, with empty space above and below, look like the frame of a table that never loaded. If a table belongs there, it's missing from this capture and the page is broken. If not, the rules are decoration. Either way the reader has to work it out from the text, which is exactly what Nielsen ("How Users Read on the Web") and Krug (*Don't Make Me Think*) warn against: people scan, they don't read. Fix: put a real departure table straight under the title (first boat, every 90 min, last boat, with clock times), and pull the office hours and crossing times out as short labelled facts. Leave the prose for the incidental details.
2. **The headings don't match what's under them.** *Defect (WCAG 2.4.6 Headings and labels).* "Fares and cards" sits over a paragraph about wind and crossing times, one about bicycles and wheelchairs, and one about dogs. There are no prices, and the only mention of cards (season cards) is in the *other* column. The left column has no heading at all. A reader looking for prices goes straight to that heading, finds nothing, and may decide fares aren't published. Fix: give both columns headings that name what they cover (something like "Opening hours and sailings" and "On board"), and either add the fares or drop the word from the heading.
3. **Cancellations are only posted at the gate.** *Defect, from the copy.* "Notices are pinned to the board beside the gate before the first boat is due" means you only find out a sailing is cancelled once you've reached the harbour. That's the one thing a winter page most needs to tell people before they leave home. Fix: say on this page where cancellations are published (this page, a text alert, a phone line), and say it to the reader: "check here before you set out".

**Smaller things.**
- The two rules under the columns separate nothing from nothing and sit far from both the text and the edge of the page. Remove them, or let them close a real section (Tufte's non-data ink; Lupton, *Thinking with Type*).
- Only the right column has a subhead, so the two columns don't match. Give both columns the same structure (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*).
- The subhead pushes the right column's lines down by about half a line, so lines don't sit level across the gap (left first line ≈145, right ≈184 vs left ≈172). Snap both to one baseline grid, or give the left column a matching heading (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- Runts: "bags." alone on the last line of the bicycles paragraph, and "thirty-five." nearly so. Re-break or edit a word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The text setting is genuinely good: a measure of about 60 characters, generous leading, paragraphs separated by space rather than indents, and a dark, sturdy serif that reads easily. Keep all of that when you add the table.
- The scale is clean: three sizes (title, subhead, body), each clearly its own level, with the title in a heavier weight as well as a bigger size, so it's plainly the first thing you see.
- The tone ("the crew will always help with bags", the shelf of paperbacks) is warm and specific. Keep it as the supporting voice under the hard facts.
