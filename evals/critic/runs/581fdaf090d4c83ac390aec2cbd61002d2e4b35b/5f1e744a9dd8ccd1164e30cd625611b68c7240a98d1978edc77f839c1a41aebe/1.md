**First read.** The eye lands on "Help for ferry passengers", drops to the two bold subheads, then reads down the left list. It reads as a plain help page for a small local ferry, written for passengers. It feels calm and trustworthy, like a well-kept council notice. Nothing is fighting for attention.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page passengers read before a trip or at the landing, so they can plan the crossing and know how to report a problem.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job and there's little to fix in it. The page fails on content. On both halves it sends the reader somewhere else for the one fact they came for: the sailing times on the left, the address on the right. Right now it's a tidy page that can't answer "when is the last boat?" or "where do I send this?"

**What would move it most**
1. **The complaint steps lead nowhere you can act on (defect).** Step 3, top right, says to "send it to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter". A person reading this at home or on their phone after a bad crossing now has to go back to the landing to find out where to write. That's the moment they're least likely to bother, so you lose the report. Step 2 has the same gap: "the number printed on the back of your ticket" assumes there's only one number and that the reader can tell which it is. Fix: print the actual postal address and an email on this page. Name the ticket number the way it appears on the ticket ("the 6-digit ticket number, top right on the back"). Add one line on what happens next, such as who replies and roughly when, so leaving a note feels worth doing.
2. **The timetable line has no times in it (defect).** The first bullet on the left, "winter sailings run once an hour and the last boat leaves before dusk", is the line people plan around, and it can be read more than one way. "Before dusk" could mean 3:30 or 5:00 depending on the month. "Once an hour" doesn't say on the hour or at what minute. If someone reads it the generous way, they're stranded on the wrong side of the water. "Check the timetable board at your landing" again sends them to the place they're trying to plan a trip to. Fix: give the actual last-sailing time, or a range by month, or link to the timetable. Say what changes in summer. Keep "check the board" only as a backup for day-to-day changes.
3. **"If something goes wrong" covers too much (defect).** The right column treats a lost bag, a refused ticket and an injury on the gangway as the same thing: write a note and put it in a box. Someone with an urgent problem reads three steps that end in a letter. Fix: add one line above the steps that sends urgent problems somewhere immediate ("On board or at the landing? Tell the crew. Injured or in danger? Call 999"). Then tighten the heading to say what the three steps are actually for, e.g. "To report a problem after your crossing".

**Smaller things.**
- Baselines drift across the two columns. After the first item, lines on the left sit level with the gaps between lines on the right (left item 1 runs 3 lines, right items run 2). It costs almost nothing on a help page. If you want the two columns to read as one block, make the gap between items equal to one full line of leading (Müller-Brockmann).

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is clean.** I count three sizes (title, subhead, body), with weight doing the extra work, and every size has an obvious job. Keep it that tight when you add content.
- **The list types match the content.** Bullets for unordered advice, numbers for steps you do in order. A reader can tell at a glance which list is a procedure.
- **The text reads well.** Lines run about 55–60 characters, the line spacing is generous, the text is near-black on white, and the gap between items is clearly bigger than the gap between lines. It's easy to read on a cold quay.
