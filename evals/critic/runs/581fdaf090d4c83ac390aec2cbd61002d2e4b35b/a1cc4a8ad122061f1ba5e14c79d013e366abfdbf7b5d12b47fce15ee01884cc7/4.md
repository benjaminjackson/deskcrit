**First read.** The eye lands on the serif "How can we help?" in the navy band, drops to the white search field under it, then moves down to the grid of eight topic cards. Within a second it reads as the help-centre home page for a software product. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, and also very familiar: it is the standard help-centre layout, done neatly.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: this is the landing page of a product's help centre, for existing customers who arrive with a problem and need to reach the right article fast, by searching or by picking a topic.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The order of importance is clear, the cards are easy to scan, and the copy is specific. The biggest weakness is the search field: it is the page's main action, but nothing on it says "search" and there is no visible way to submit.

**What would move it most**
1. **The search field has no search icon and no submit button (convention, with a real cost).** It is a plain white rounded box in the hero. Its only cue is the grey placeholder "Search for articles", and that disappears as soon as someone starts typing. A search box with a magnifier is one of the most familiar patterns on the web (Jakob's law, NN/g). Without one, the field reads as an empty text input, and once someone has typed a query, nothing on screen tells them they can submit it. Norman's point about signifiers applies: the cue that tells people where to act and how is missing. Fix: put a magnifier icon inside the field on the left, and add a submit control on the right (either an icon button or a "Search" button in the same navy). The page has no other primary action, so this one can stand out.

**Smaller things.**
- **Three names for the same content.** The nav says "Guides", the subtitle says "search the guides", and the placeholder says "Search for articles". A visitor can fairly wonder whether guides and articles are different collections, and whether search covers both. Pick one word and use it everywhere (Nielsen 4, consistency).
- **The topics don't add up to one product.** "Set up your workspace and invite your team" and "Integrations" describe a team software tool, but "Orders and shipping / Track a delivery or start a return" suggests a shop. A first-time visitor can't tell what Harbor sells. If it really is both, make the card text say what is being ordered and shipped (Norman, conceptual model).
- **Short last lines in the card descriptions.** "data.", "where.", "messages.", "receipts.", "recovery." and "your team." each sit alone on a second line. Six of the eight cards wrap like this, which makes the grid look ragged. Either tighten the text so each description fits on one line or wraps evenly, or turn on balanced wrapping (`text-wrap: balance` or `pretty`) (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- **Icons and text don't share a left edge.** Each card's icon starts about 5 px to the right of the title and description below it. Line the icon's visible left edge up with the text (taste and common sense: an offset this size looks like a mistake, not a choice).

**What's working.**
- **The hero does one thing.** The headline, one line of support and the search field stand alone on navy, so the first step is obvious. Pairing a serif display face with a sans for everything else is a clear contrast rather than a clash. Keep that pairing and keep the hero this sparse.
- **The card descriptions are specific and start with verbs:** "Track a delivery or start a return", "Choose which alerts you get and where". A visitor can find their own problem in them without guessing what a category name covers. Keep this writing standard as topics are added.
- **The grid is calm and consistent.** It has four even columns, gaps of about 21 px both ways, one stroke weight across all the icons, and pale borders that separate the cards without boxing them in heavily. It scans fast and holds up when there are many cards.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The focus ring on the search field and the cards: this page is likely to be used from the keyboard, so check it in a browser. Hover state on the cards. How the four-column grid reflows on a phone. Whether popular articles or a contact block appear below the fold; if not, add one, because many visitors to a help home page are looking for the most common answers.
