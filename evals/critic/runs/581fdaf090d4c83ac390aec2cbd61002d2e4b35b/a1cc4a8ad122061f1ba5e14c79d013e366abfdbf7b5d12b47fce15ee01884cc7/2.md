**First read.** The eye goes straight to the serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops to the white search field, then out to the eight topic cards below. In under three seconds it reads as a product help center for customers who are stuck. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. It also feels like every other help center: nothing tells you what Harbor is or what usually goes wrong with it.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the home page of a product's help center, meant to get a frustrated customer to the right article quickly, by search or by topic.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the grid is clean and the topics are well described. Its weakness is that it's a pure template. It waits for visitors to sort their own problem into a category or think up search terms, and gives no shortcut to the few answers most of them came for.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing anticipates the common questions (taste, common sense).** Below the hero there are only eight equal category cards, so a visitor with an urgent problem has to decide whether "my sync broke" counts as Integrations, Notifications or Troubleshooting. A help center's traffic is usually dominated by a handful of questions. Put five or six top articles, or a known-issue line linked to Status, where the visitor sees them before the cards or alongside them, so most people land on their answer in one click and skip the classifying.
2. **The search field doesn't look like search (convention, Jakob's law).** It's the page's main action, but it's a plain white rectangle with a placeholder. There's no magnifier and no button, so it reads more like a form field than a search, and there's no visible way to submit with a mouse. Add a magnifier icon inside the field and a submit button or an attached icon button. Consider a placeholder that shows a real query ("e.g. reset two-step sign-in") so visitors know they can type a problem, not just a keyword.
3. **The copy could be on anyone's help center (defect, mild).** "How can we help?" and "Browse a topic or search the guides." only describe what the page already shows. Nothing tells the visitor what Harbor is, and the topics add to the confusion: "Set up your workspace" and "Integrations" sound like SaaS, while "Orders and shipping" and "start a return" sound like a shop. A first-time visitor can't tell which of their problems this place covers. Use the subline to name the product and what people come here to fix, and check that the topic set matches one clear picture of the product.

**Smaller things.**
- Card descriptions: four of eight end with a lone word ("receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). Rewrite them slightly shorter or longer, or use balanced wrapping, so each two-line description breaks evenly.
- Type scale: I count about six sizes: headline ~34, logo ~20, "Browse by topic" ~18, card titles and subline ~16, nav ~15, descriptions ~14. The 18 and 16 bold headings and the 14/15/16 text sizes sit too close to read as separate levels. Narrow to headline, section, card title and body, and let weight and colour do the rest.

**What's working.**
- The hero hierarchy. One serif headline against an otherwise all-sans page gives the eye one obvious place to land, and the search sits right under it. Keep the serif for that single moment.
- The cards follow one strict program: the same icon style and stroke, bold title, one-line plain-language scope, equal gutters, and edges on the same 64 px margins as the header. That consistency is why eight options don't feel busy.
- The descriptions are concrete ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Passwords, two-step sign-in, and recovery"). They name what you can do, not just the category. Keep that voice when you write the top-articles list.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the cards and the search field. The cards have no chevron or other "go" cue, so the hover state has to show that they're clickable.
- How the 4-column grid reflows on narrow screens.
