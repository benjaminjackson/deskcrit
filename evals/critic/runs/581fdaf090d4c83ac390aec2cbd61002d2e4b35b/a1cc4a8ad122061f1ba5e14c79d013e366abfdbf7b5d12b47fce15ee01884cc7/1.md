**First read.** The eye goes straight to the white serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops to the white search bar, then moves into the eight topic cards below. It reads as the help centre for a team-workspace product. It feels calm and trustworthy, and quite like many other help centres.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the home page of a product's help centre, and its job is to get a customer with a problem to the right article quickly, by searching or by picking a topic.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the grid is clean and the topic copy is concrete. The biggest weakness is the search bar: it is the page's main action, yet it is just a white box with no magnifier, no button and no label. The page also gives the visitor nothing to start from beyond the eight topics.

**What would move it most**
1. **The search bar doesn't look like a search bar.** It sits in the centre of the hero and is the one thing most visitors came to use. It has no magnifier icon, no submit button, and its only label is the placeholder "Search for articles", which disappears as soon as someone types. People who scan for the familiar magnifier won't find it, and nothing shows that pressing Enter is the way to submit. Help-centre search is a strong convention for a reason: it shows at a glance where to act and how (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, on signifiers; Nielsen's Jakob's law). A placeholder used as the only label is a known fault (Nielsen Norman Group, "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful"). Convention, with a real cost. Fix: put a magnifier inside the left edge of the field and a "Search" button on the right, filled in the brand navy or an accent. "How can we help?" already works as the visible label, so connect it to the field in code instead of adding a second label.

2. **Nothing helps the visitor who already knows their problem.** Below the search there are only eight broad categories. Someone who can't sign in, or whose sync has broken, has to choose between typing a query and guessing which card holds the answer. Most help-centre traffic goes to a small set of articles. A short "Popular articles" list of five or six specific titles, such as "Reset your password" or "Fix calendar sync errors", under the search or under the grid, would send many visitors to their answer in one click. This is common sense, not a cited rule. The rule it comes from (anticipation) has no approved source here. The same goes for the end of the grid: give a visible next step, such as "Still stuck? Contact support", for people whose problem none of the eight cards covers. Right now that path exists only as a small nav link at the top right.

**Smaller things.**
- **Muddled type scale:** I count about six sizes: roughly 36 (hero), 20 (logo), 18 ("Browse by topic"), 16 (card titles, subline, search), 15 (nav) and 14 (card descriptions). Sizes 14, 15, 16, 18 and 20 are too close to read as separate levels. Keep the hero size, one heading size, one text size and one small size, and separate levels with weight and colour instead (Bringhurst; Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*).
- **"Browse by topic" barely differs from the card titles:** it is only about 2px larger and the same weight and colour, so it doesn't read as a level above them. Make the gap obvious, through a clearly larger size or a different colour or form (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8).
- **Single words left alone in the card descriptions:** "data." (Privacy and security) and "where." (Notifications) each sit alone on a second line, and "receipts." and "recovery." nearly do. Rewrite the lines or adjust the card width so each description ends on a fuller line (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- **Copy, Privacy and security:** "How we protect and store your data" is about the company. Every other card names something the reader can do. Rephrase it as a task, for example "Manage two-step sign-in, data export, and who can see your data."
- **Copy, Orders and shipping:** this topic sits oddly beside workspaces, integrations and sync. If the product really ships physical goods, fine. If not, it reads as template filler and makes the visitor doubt they're in the right help centre.

**What's working.**
- **The hero:** the navy band with one serif question and one field makes the entry point plain. The serif headline against the sans text is a clear, deliberate contrast, not a clash. Keep that pairing.
- **The topic cards:** all eight follow one strict pattern (thin line icon, bold title, one-line task description) on an even 4×2 grid with equal gaps. The icons share one stroke weight and one level of detail, so the grid reads as a single calm set.
- **The descriptions:** most use verbs and name specific tasks ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Fix sync problems and error messages"). That lets people recognise their problem without opening the card.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Hover and focus states:** I couldn't see them on the cards or the search field. Both need a visible focus ring, because the whole page works by keyboard and click on these items.
- **Reflow:** I couldn't see how the 4-column grid collapses on narrow screens.
