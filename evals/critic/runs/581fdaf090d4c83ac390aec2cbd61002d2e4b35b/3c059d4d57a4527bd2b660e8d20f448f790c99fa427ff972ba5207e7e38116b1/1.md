**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Plan the week with your whole team" on the left, then crosses to the white card and stops on the blue "Create account" button. Within two seconds it reads as the sign-up page for a team planning tool. It feels calm, competent and anonymous: this could be the sign-up page for any SaaS product released in the last five years.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a desktop sign-up page that should get a team lead to start a free workspace now.

**Verdict.** The layout and hierarchy work. The form and the pitch are what let it down. The fields are named only by placeholders, which disappear as soon as you type, and the left half asks for a sign-up without showing the product or explaining the free limit in a way you can rely on.

**What would move it most**

1. **The fields have no labels, only placeholders (defect).** All five inputs in the card are named only by their grey placeholder text. Once someone types in the third field, "At least 10 characters" disappears, and the page never actually says that field is the password. The password rule disappears at the moment it's needed. Someone who goes back to check a field, or is filling it from autofill, can't tell what each box was for. Two of the placeholders are formats rather than names ("name@company.com", "At least 10 characters"), so even before anyone types, the viewer has to work out what is being asked. Nielsen's sixth heuristic says labels belong with the fields and should not be placeholders that vanish, and NN/g's "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful" makes the same point. WCAG 3.3.2 wants a label or instruction that stays visible. Fix: put a short label above each field ("First name", "Work email", "Password", "Company name"). Move "At least 10 characters" under the password field as hint text that stays visible, and add a show-password toggle.

2. **The pitch doesn't show the product, and the free offer reads two ways (defect on the offer line, taste on the empty space).** The headline promises "your whole team", and the next sentence caps the free plan at five people and says "You can invite the rest of the team later." That line can be read two ways:
   - "Later" means it's free whenever you get round to it.
   - Anyone past five pays.

   This is the line a buyer will hold you to. If a team of eight signs up, meets a paywall and feels misled, that costs you more than the sign-up gained. Below this copy, the left column is empty: there's no screenshot of a planned week and nothing a team lead can compare with their current spreadsheet or whiteboard. So the only thing pushing them to "Create account" is the headline, and the headline could belong to any planning tool. Fix: say the limit plainly, for example "Free for up to five people. Larger teams start at $X per person." Use the empty left space for one real image of a planned week, so the viewer can compare it with how they plan now and judge the value on terms they already trust.

**Smaller things**
- Input borders: the pale grey-blue borders on white look to be well under 3:1, and the border is the only thing that marks a field (WCAG 1.4.11). Darken them until they measure at least 3:1.
- "Sign in" link: it has the same colour and weight as "Already have an account?" and no underline, so it doesn't look clickable (Norman, signifiers; WCAG 1.4.1). Underline it or set it in the link blue.
- Required fields: nothing says which fields are required, and "Company name" in particular could be optional. Mark the required fields with asterisks or add "optional" after the optional ones (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- Left column space (taste): the space under the copy is left over rather than shaped. The text block floats high and the lower 60% of the column is empty. It's tied to finding 2, and a visual there would fix both.

**What's working**
- The hierarchy is clear: there is one dominant headline, one filled primary button and one quiet secondary link. Keep this order when you add labels and a visual.
- The type is restrained, with one family and about five sizes in clear steps. The headline is set tight and holds together as one unit.
- The blue is used sparingly, only for the logotype and the primary button, so the button is the only saturated thing in the card.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Focus rings on the fields and the button.
- What errors look like when someone enters a bad email or a password that's too short.
- How the two columns stack on a narrow screen.
