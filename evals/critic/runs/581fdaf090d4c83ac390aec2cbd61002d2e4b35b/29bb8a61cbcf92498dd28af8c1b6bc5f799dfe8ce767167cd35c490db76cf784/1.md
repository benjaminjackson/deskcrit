**First read.** Your eye goes straight to the big black headline, "Travel lighter with the Wayfarer pack". It drops to the grey line under it and stops on the black "Shop the pack" button. It reads as the top section of a shop's product page for a travel backpack. It feels calm and confident, but also unfinished, because you never see the bag and most of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a desktop product page (1280 × 800). Its job is to make someone who travels light want this bag and click through to buy it.

**Verdict.** The type and order are solid, but it doesn't do its job. It sells a physical bag without showing it. That leaves the right third and the bottom third of the screen blank, and the whole sale rests on words.

**What would move it most**

1. **There's no bag, and the empty space shows it was meant to be there.** The text block covers x 120 to 850 and y 120 to 495. The right side (about x 850 to 1280) and everything below the buttons are empty white. This is a bag, and the copy makes physical claims: it fits under a seat, it opens flat like a suitcase, it weighs less than a kilo. The viewer has to picture all of that and take it on trust. Nothing on screen shows them the size, the material or the look, and those are what people buy a bag on. The emptiness also tips the layout. All the weight sits top-left and nothing on the right holds it up (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). The open area doesn't frame anything, so it reads as space left over, not space you chose (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). If a product image was meant to sit on the right and didn't load in this capture, that's the first thing to check. If there was never one, this is the main problem. **Defect.** Fix: put a large picture of the pack on the right, edged to the same top and bottom lines as the text. Ideally show the one claim words can't carry, such as the bag opened flat or packed beside an airline seat. Then the picture and the subhead make one point together, instead of the headline working alone.

2. **The headline could be on anyone's bag page, and the strong copy is in the small grey line.** "Travel lighter" is what every travel-bag brand says, so it gives the viewer no reason to stop here. The real case is in the subhead: 22 litres, fits under the seat, opens flat, under a kilo. Those are concrete, comparable claims, and they're set at about 20 px in mid-grey. A traveller already compares against things they know, like the airline's size limit and their current bag. The page should put this bag next to those. **Defect** (generic copy costs attention). Fix: put the most distinctive claim into the headline (for example the under-the-seat or under-a-kilo point, said to "you"). Or bring one of those facts up to a size the eye hits second.

**Smaller things.**
- No price anywhere. "Shop the pack" asks for a click without saying what it costs. A price or a "from £…" near the button would let people decide before they click.
- The subhead breaks "opens flat like a / suitcase". Rebreak it so "like a suitcase" stays together, or let the line run slightly wider.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean, with four clear levels: blue eyebrow, roughly 80 px headline, roughly 20 px subhead, button label. The headline is tightly leaded and tightly spaced, so its two lines hold together as one shape. Keep that.
- The primary and secondary actions are clearly ranked. "Shop the pack" is a filled, dark button. "Compare sizes" is an underlined text link. There's one obvious next step and a quieter alternative.
- The subhead's content is specific and believable, with numbers and comparisons a traveller can check. It just needs more prominence.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether a product image or the rest of the page failed to load in the capture. Check in a live browser.
- How this section reflows on a phone, and whether the buttons show a visible focus state.
