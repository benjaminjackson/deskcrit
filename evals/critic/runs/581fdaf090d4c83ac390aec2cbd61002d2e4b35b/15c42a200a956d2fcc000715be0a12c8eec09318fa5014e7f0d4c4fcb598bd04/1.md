**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "River ferry timetable", drops to the grey subtitle, and then runs down the table's left column. It reads as a calm, tidy web timetable for people taking the ferry: light rules, plenty of air, nothing loud. Then the sentence at the bottom says the "heavy black lines around every cell make this timetable a mess", and nothing on the page matches that.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming a rider uses this to find when the next boat leaves north landing, whether it runs on their day, and when it arrives.

**Verdict.** The table itself is well made: clean, quiet, and easy to scan row by row. Two things get in the way of the job. A sentence below the table describes a different table from the one on screen. And the days a boat runs, which decide whether there is a boat at all, are the least visible thing on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sentence under the table contradicts the table.** "The heavy black lines around every cell make this timetable a mess" sits below the last row at body size, where a note to riders would go. There are no heavy black lines. The table uses thin pale grey horizontal rules and no cell borders. A rider reads it, looks for the problem, and starts wondering whether they are looking at the right or current version. Notes in that spot normally carry the fine print a rider acts on, so it borrows that authority and wastes it. This is a defect, and it's also common sense: a page shouldn't make claims the image doesn't support. Delete it, or use that spot for what riders need: the journey time (12 minutes on every sailing), the season's actual dates, and where to buy tickets.

2. **"Does a boat run today?" is hard to answer.** "Weekdays" and "Weekends" are set exactly like "Daily", in the same weight and colour, and they sit in the far-right column about 850 px from the departure time. On a Saturday the 12:40 and the 17:10 don't run. On a weekday the 19:30 doesn't run. Nothing flags either case, so a rider has to read every row to the end and match it back across the page, and a quick glance will miss it. The subtitle's "spring season" has the same gap: there are no dates, so a rider can't tell whether this table applies today. This is a defect, because this is the part they act on, and getting it wrong means waiting at the landing for a boat that never comes. Make the exceptions stand out from "Daily" (bolder, or a marker with a short note), or put the day next to the time, or split the table into weekday and weekend versions. Put real dates on the season.

3. **The table is stretched to the page width and starts with the least useful column.** The first column is "Period", which only repeats what the times already say (06:40 is plainly morning). So the left edge, where every scan starts, carries no information. "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are a pair, yet about 300 px of empty space separates them, the same gap as between unrelated columns. The trip reads as two separate numbers instead of one journey. This breaks a convention: space should show the groups (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II, on grouping by proximity). Putting departure time first and dropping Period is my taste. Start with the departure time, keep departure and arrival close together, and size the table to its content instead of the page. If the period labels help, use them as quiet group dividers rather than a column that repeats on every row.

**Smaller things.**
- Header labels "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are clipped. "Leaves north landing" and "Arrives market quay" read without a gap to fill, and there is room for them.

**What's working.**
- The rules. Thin pale horizontal lines with a slightly firmer one under the header guide the eye along each row without boxing in the cells. This is exactly what Lupton recommends for tables in *Thinking with Type* (p. 157). Keep it.
- A clear hierarchy from a small type scale. The bold title, the grey subtitle, bold column heads and regular cells give four distinct levels using only three sizes, with weight and colour doing the rest.
- Generous row height and a steady rhythm. The seven rows are easy to track across, and the page has room to breathe.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Reflow: how this four-column table behaves at phone width, where the stretched layout will need rethinking anyway.
- Whether the return direction (market quay to north landing) is published somewhere else. Riders will look for it here.
