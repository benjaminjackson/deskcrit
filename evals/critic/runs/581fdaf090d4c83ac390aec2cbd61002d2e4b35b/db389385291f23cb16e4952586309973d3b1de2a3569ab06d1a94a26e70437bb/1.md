**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline. From there it drops to the grey line under it and then to the blue button, a clean path with no detours. It reads as the top of a landing page for a team-planning tool. It feels calm and competent, but it could belong to any company: there is no product, no picture and no proof, and about half the screen (the right side and the bottom third) is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a marketing site for Weekplan, aimed at team leads or engineering managers, and its job is to get them to start a trial. Tell me if that's wrong.

**Verdict.** The type and hierarchy are solid, but the page doesn't do its job yet. It promises "one honest picture of the next five days" and then shows nothing, so the viewer has to take the whole value on trust before they'll click a generic "Start a free trial."

**What would move it most**
1. **The promise has no picture.** The subhead says Weekplan turns tickets, meetings and leave into "one honest picture of the next five days." The empty right half and bottom third are exactly where that picture should be. Right now a first-time visitor can't tell what the product looks like, what it connects to, or how it differs from their calendar or Jira board, so they're asked to sign up on words alone. The empty area also reads as space left over rather than space with a purpose, and with all the weight in the top-left nothing balances it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). Fix: put a real week in that space, one team's five days with tickets, meetings and someone's time off visibly fitted together, so the visitor sees the "honest picture" for themselves. That one move fixes the concept, the balance and the empty space together. *Defect*: visitors who can't picture the product don't start the trial.
2. **The ask is generic and asks for trust.** "Start a free trial" could sit on any SaaS page. It doesn't tell the visitor what they'll get or let them check the value against something they already trust. Fix: point the ask at their own week, for example "See your team's next week" or "Connect Jira and see this week," so the first thing they get is a comparison with the plan they already have in their head or their tool. If the trial needs a calendar or tracker connection, say which ones near the button. *Defect* (copy).
3. **The headline breaks in the wrong place, and its spacing is loose.** The break comes after "your," so line 1 ends on a word that belongs to line 2, and "Plan the week your" reads as an unfinished phrase before "team actually works" saves it. The headline also looks set at text tracking: at about 80 px the letters and the gaps between words look wide, which softens a headline that should feel tight and confident (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 80; Bringhurst, 2.1.1: at larger sizes word spacing can be tightened too). Fix: break as "Plan the week / your team actually works" (or bring the measure in until the break falls there), and tighten the tracking a little, checking the word gaps by eye. *Convention*: it costs some reading fluency and polish in the most important line on the page.

**Smaller things.**
- "Leave" in the subhead can be read as time off or as leaving, and a stranger only sorts it out on a second read. "Time off" says it in one.
- Nothing near the button answers the next question (price, which tools it connects to, how long setup takes). One short line of reassurance under the button would cover it.

**What's working.**
- **One clear focal point and a clean eye path.** Headline, then subhead, then button, at three sizes that are clearly different, with weight and grey doing the finer work. Keep that order whatever you add.
- **The headline's idea.** "The week your team actually works" names a real gap between the plan and what actually happens. That's a sharp claim that deserves a visual that proves it.
- **Restraint.** One neutral palette, one blue saved for the single action, and a comfortable subhead width (about 60 characters).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button and nav links, and how the hero reflows on a narrow screen. Also whether more content starts just below the fold: if it does, the empty bottom band matters less, but the missing product picture still matters.
