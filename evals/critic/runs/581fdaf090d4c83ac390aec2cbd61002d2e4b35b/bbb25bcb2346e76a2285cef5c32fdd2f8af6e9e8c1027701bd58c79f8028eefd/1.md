Version 2 is better. The only change is the heading style, and it fixes a real hierarchy problem. Both versions share two bigger problems: the picture area is empty, and there is no ask.

**First read.** In version 2 the eye goes to "Maps of the inland sea" in heavy sans, then jumps to the large tan block on the right, finds nothing there, and comes back down the left column. In version 1 the regular-weight serif headline and the tan block carry about the same weight, so the eye hesitates between them. Both read as a calm, scholarly museum exhibition page. Both stop at the opening times with nothing to do next.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an exhibition landing page for a museum website. It should make a casual visitor want to see the maps and help them plan a visit.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job better. Its bold sans headline and subheads give the page clear levels, and version 1's headings don't. In both versions the biggest problem is the blank picture: a page selling forty old maps shows no map at all.

**What changed and why it matters**
- In version 1, "What you will see" and "Opening times" use the same serif, weight and colour as the body text. They are only about 24 px against 18 px, so they look like slightly large first lines rather than signposts. The headline is light enough that the empty tan block competes with it for first look. Version 2 sets all three headings in bold sans. Weight and type style now both separate the levels, the headline clearly wins the page, and the bold sans matches the "Calder Museum" wordmark. This is the fix Dair describes (*Design with Type*, ch. 8–9): when the size step is small, change weight or type style too. Convention, and here it fixes a real defect.

**What would move it most (version 2)**
1. **The picture is an empty tan gradient** (right column, about 420×560 px). It's the largest thing on the page and it carries nothing. The headline promises maps, and a viewer deciding whether to visit gets no idea how beautiful or strange these charts are, which is the main reason to come. If this is an image that didn't load, check it in a live browser. If it's the design, it's a defect. Fix: show a real chart, cropped close enough that the hand colouring and lettering can be seen at this size, and add a one-line caption saying which chart it is. Common sense: no approved source covers this.
2. **No ask.** The eye path ends at "gallery is on the ground floor." with nothing to do next. "Tickets" is in the nav, but the opening times just told the viewer entry is free, so it's unclear what they would book. Defect: the most interested viewers leave without a next step. Fix: put one primary action straight after the opening times that names a concrete thing to do, such as "Plan your visit: directions and today's hours" or "See all forty charts online". Don't use a generic "Learn more".

**Smaller things**
- The image's right edge (about x 1220) doesn't line up with the end of the nav (about x 1184), so the right margin is narrower than the 96 px left margin. Take both from one grid.
- The image's top and bottom don't line up with any line of text. Align its top to the headline's cap height and let the grid set its height.
- The nav doesn't show that the viewer is in Exhibitions. Mark the current section.
- "until 2 March" has no year. Visitors plan around dates, so add the year.

**What's working**
- The copy is specific and well voiced. "A brass sounding lead found in the harbour mud in 1958" makes you want to see it, and the dates, hours and "Entry is free" answer a visitor's questions plainly. Keep it.
- In version 2 the bold sans headings over serif body make a clear two-face system with an obvious job for each, and the headings echo the wordmark.
- The text column has a comfortable measure (about 70 characters) and generous leading, and each heading sits tight to its own paragraph, so the three blocks read as clear groups.
