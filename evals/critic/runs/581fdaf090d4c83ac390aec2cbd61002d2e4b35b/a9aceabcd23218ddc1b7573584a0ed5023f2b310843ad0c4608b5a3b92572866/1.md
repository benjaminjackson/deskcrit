**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Changing your pickup time", then drops down the left edge through four bold headings evenly spaced. Within two seconds it reads as a help or FAQ page for a small bakery's click-and-collect orders. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, and a little bare: there's no title, no brand and no way to do anything from the page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help page for customers who have already ordered bread online, answering the four questions they're most likely to have before or at pickup.

**Verdict.** The layout mostly does its job: it's easy to scan, the headings are well grouped and every answer is short. What lets it down is the copy where customers will act on it. The cancellation deadline and the "collecting for someone else" rule can each be read two ways. Allergy information is only available at the till, after the customer has already paid. These are content defects, not styling ones, and they matter more than anything typographic here.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways.** In "Cancelling an order", the line "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say ahead of what, or how long "a day" is. It could mean 24 hours before the chosen pickup slot, or any time before the end of the previous day. A customer who reads it the generous way and cancels at 9 pm for a 10 am pickup finds the bread is already "baked for you". They will hold the bakery to the reading they chose. It also never says how to cancel. *Defect.* Fix: state an exact cutoff against the pickup time, e.g. "You can cancel until 24 hours before your pickup time", and add the action: "Open your order and choose Cancel".
2. **Allergy information arrives too late.** In "Allergies and ingredients", the only source is "a card by the till listing what is in it". Someone ordering online with an allergy can't see the ingredients until they're standing in the shop holding a loaf they've already paid for. "Every loaf" also leaves out anything that isn't a loaf. Telling customers to ask the baker only works if they're already there. *Defect*: it shuts out the readers who most need this answer. Fix: say where ingredients and allergens appear before ordering (on each product page) and keep the till card as the backup. If that doesn't exist yet, it's a product gap the page is currently hiding.
3. **"Along with a name" doesn't say whose name.** In "Collecting for someone else", "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name" could mean the friend's name or the name on the order. The friend gets to the counter, gives their own name, and is turned away. *Defect.* Fix: say whose name: "the order number and the name the order is under".
4. **The answers send people somewhere the page doesn't link to.** "Open your order" and cancelling both need the customer to find their order, but the page offers no link, button or hint of where "your order" lives. Everyone who arrives here mid-task has to go looking (Nielsen, recognition rather than recall). *Defect.* Fix: make "Open your order" a link to the customer's orders, and give the cancellation answer the same link.
5. **No page title.** The four headings are the biggest things on the page, so nothing says what page this is or whose bakery it is. Someone who arrives from search, or from a link in an email, gets the answers with no context. *Defect*, though it's mild on a page this short. Fix: add one title above the headings that names the page, such as "Questions about your order", so the page has a clear starting point and the four headings read as its sections.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the paragraphs run to about 95 characters ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…"), well past Bringhurst's 45–75. The text is short, so it costs little, but capping the text column at about 65–70 characters (roughly 600–640 px here) would tighten the page and suit the open right side.
- Runt: "anything." sits alone on the last line of the Allergies answer. Shortening the measure will probably fix it, or rewording the line will.

**What's working.**
- Spacing makes the groups obvious. Each heading sits tight on its answer and well clear of the one above, so the page splits into four clean question-and-answer pairs with no rules or boxes. Keep that ratio of gaps.
- Two type sizes, with bold weight on the headings, are enough. The headings are found at a glance and nothing competes with them. Don't add a third size for emphasis.
- The answers are short and in plain words ("After that the bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule as well as stating it). Keep that voice when you add the missing specifics.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the page reflows cleanly on a phone, which is where customers standing at the counter will read it.
- Focus states, once "Open your order" becomes a link.
