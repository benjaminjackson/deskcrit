**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the big headline "Bring your bike in for a service". It drops to the paragraph and the price list, then crosses to the button in the right panel. It reads straight away as a booking page for a local bike repair shop, and it feels calm, tidy and trustworthy. The two versions look identical except for the button colour: teal in version 1, a strong blue in version 2.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a bike shop's service page that should get a local rider to choose a drop-off day and book.

**Verdict.** They are the same. The only change is the button's colour. Both colours are clearly the strongest thing in the panel, both pass contrast with white text (about 5:1 on the teal, about 6.7:1 on the blue), and neither is used anywhere else. So the viewer finds and reads the action equally easily in both. Taste would lean slightly toward the teal: it feels more like a shop's own colour, while the blue looks like a default. That doesn't change how well either page works. The page itself is solid. What gets in the way is the booking panel: the dates don't look like things you can pick, and the booking never asks which service you want.

**What would move it most**
1. **The booking doesn't ask what work you want.** The three prices on the left are only a list, and the panel on the right asks only for a day. A rider who clicks "Book drop-off" hasn't told the shop whether it's a $25 safety check or a $160 rebuild. They can't tell whether that gets asked later or whether they've just booked something vague. The page splits into two halves that don't connect, when it should be one task (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, on the conceptual model; Nielsen heuristic 5, error prevention). *Defect.* Fix: make the services selectable, in the panel or as the first step, so the booking always names a service and a day. Or say plainly that the shop will quote when you drop the bike off.
2. **The date rows look like text fields, not choices.** "Tuesday 28 October", "Wednesday 29 October" and "Friday 31 October" are white boxes with thin borders, the usual look of an input field. They have no radio circle, no tick and no selected state, and none is chosen by default. A viewer can't tell whether to click one, type into one, or whether the button books all three (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*: affordances and signifiers; Nielsen heuristic 1, visibility of system status). *Defect.* Fix: give them a clear selectable look, such as radio marks or a segmented control, and show one as selected or make the selected state unmistakable. Then the button can repeat the choice, for example "Book drop-off for Tuesday 28 October".

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks after "a" and leaves "service" alone on the second line. Break it as "Bring your bike in / for a service" so it reads as one phrase.
- Copy: "collect it the same evening" doesn't say by what time, and "the quote" mentions a quote the page never gives. Add the pickup time, and say where the quote comes from (presumably the prices above, or one made at drop-off).

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean. There is one big headline, a short promise underneath, a scannable price list with right-aligned prices, and a single filled button. Keep the type scale this tight: four sizes, each with a clear job.
- The promise "We call you before doing any work that costs more than the quote" speaks straight to the rider's real fear of a surprise bill. It's the best line on the page.
- The two-column split puts the information on the left and the action on the right, inside a tinted panel. That grouping is right and is worth keeping.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date rows have hover and selected states, the button's focus ring, and how the two columns stack on a phone.
