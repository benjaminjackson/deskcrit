**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", drops to the line about replying within two working days, then crosses to the white form card and stops on the blue "Send message" button. It reads as the enquiry page of a small design studio. It is tidy and calm, but it could be any studio's page: nothing here says what Fernwick makes or for whom.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you gave no brief: a desktop contact page meant to get a prospective client to send an enquiry the studio can actually respond to.

**Verdict.** It works as a basic form: it's clear, readable and has one obvious action. It falls short on its real job, which is getting a useful brief in. The page asks for a budget and a timeline, then gives you one small box to type everything into. The layout also stops short of its own right margin, which makes the page look unfinished.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form doesn't collect what the copy asks for.** *(Defect.)* The intro says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form below has only Name, Email and a Message box about five lines tall. The visitor has to remember that request while writing, and many won't, so the studio's first reply becomes "what's your budget?" That costs the extra round trip the two-day promise is meant to avoid. The page should put what the visitor needs next in front of them rather than make them recall it. That is common sense here; the related source is unapproved, so I'm not citing it. Fix direction: add two optional fields, Budget range and Timeline. Ranges to choose from work better than free text, because a nervous client can pick one without naming a number. Make the Message box taller, and give its label a prompt that says what to write, such as "What are you making, and who is it for?" The copy above has the same root problem. "Tell us about your project" could sit on anyone's page, so the visitor learns nothing about why to pick Fernwick. One line saying what kind of work the studio takes on would let the right clients recognise themselves (taste, but it's the cheapest change on the page).

2. **The layout sits off its own grid and leaves dead space.** *(Convention: Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "Construction of the grid".)* The header runs from the left margin (about x=56) to the right margin (about x=1224, under "Contact"). The form card stops at about x=1068. That leaves a strip about 156 px wide on the right that lines up with nothing, and the lower half of the page is empty down to the footer. The card's edge doesn't line up with the nav, so it reads as placed by eye. The empty space reads as leftover rather than chosen, and the page feels left-heavy and slightly unfinished. A visitor won't name this, but it adds to the "template" feel. Fix direction: put the card's right edge on the same line as the nav's right edge, or narrow the whole page (header included) to one shared container so every block uses the same column lines.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Contact" looks the same as Work, Services and About, so nothing shows which page you're on. Mark the current page with one clear cue, such as bolder weight or a bar under it (Nielsen Norman Group, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Form: no field is marked required or optional. Once budget and timeline are added, put "(optional)" after those labels, or asterisks on the required ones plus a one-line key (NN/g, Budiu, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- Input borders: the grey field outlines look lighter than 3:1 against white, so the fields could fade for low-vision users. Measure the border colour against white and darken it to at least 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11).
- Headline: "Tell us about your / project" leaves "project" alone on line two and splits "your project". Break it as "Tell us about / your project", or widen the column so it fits on one line.

**What's working.**
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a specific promise that someone can hold you to. It lowers the risk of writing in. Keep it close to the form.
- The form itself is well built: labels are bold and sit above their fields, each label is clearly closer to its own field than to the one above, it's one column, and the single filled button is the only strong action on the page.
- The type is restrained, with about four sizes that each have a clear job. The links in the nav and footer are underlined, so they don't depend on colour alone.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the fields and button, and what the button looks like on hover and press.
- Error messages for an empty or invalid email, and the confirmation after sending: does it repeat the two-day promise?
- Whether the two columns stack properly on narrow screens.
