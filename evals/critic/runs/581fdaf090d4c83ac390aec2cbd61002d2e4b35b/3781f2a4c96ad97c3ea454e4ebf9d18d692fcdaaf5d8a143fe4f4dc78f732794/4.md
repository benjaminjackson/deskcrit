**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", the only large dark type on the page, then moves right to the white card and down the three fields to the blue "Send message" button. Within a second it's clear this is a contact page for a small design or dev studio. It feels calm, tidy and safe, and a little anonymous: apart from the name in the top-left corner, nothing here could only belong to Fernwick.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a contact page where a possible client sends an enquiry the studio can quote on. It needs enough detail to answer usefully, without putting the client off.

**Verdict.** The page works. It's clear and readable, nothing is broken, and it has one obvious action. What holds it back is that the studio's one real request (send a budget and a timeline) sits in the left column, a long way from the box where people type. So the enquiries that arrive will mostly lack the details the studio needs.

**What would move it most**
1. **The guidance is a long way from the field it's about** (defect). "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them" is grey body text in the left column, about 100 px left of the form and above it. By the time someone clicks into the Message box (541–1035 px across, 362–481 px down), that sentence is behind them and out of sight. Most people will write "Hi, we're looking for a new website, can we chat?" Then the studio's first reply is a list of questions, which uses up the two-day reply time the page promises. Nielsen's heuristic 10 says help should sit next to the field it applies to. Put the request directly under the Message label as helper text. Or go further and add two optional fields, Budget (a select of ranges) and Timeline. That is common sense rather than a cited rule: if you ask for something specific, give it its own slot, and the person won't have to remember to include it. If you do this, the left column can drop the sentence and keep only the reply promise.
2. **The headline could be on any agency's site** (taste, on the copy). "Tell us about your project" is the line everyone reads, and it says nothing about Fernwick or about which projects it wants. A visitor can't tell whether their job fits here, so the page gives them no reason to write now rather than keep looking around. The line under it is much better: "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a concrete promise someone can hold you to. Bring the headline up to that level by naming the work you take on, in the visitor's own terms. For example, which kind of project, at which stage, gets a proposal back from you.

**Smaller things.**
- No field is marked required or optional (Your name, Email address, Message). If you add Budget and Timeline, put "(optional)" after their labels, or mark the required fields with an asterisk and explain it once (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group).
- The input borders are pale grey on a white card, and they're the only thing showing where each field is. They look under 3:1 against the white. Measure them, and darken them to at least 3:1 if they fall short (WCAG 1.4.11).
- The card's right edge (about 1068 px) lines up with nothing else on the page. The nav ends at about 1224 px, which leaves a dead strip of grey to the right of the card. Extend the card to the right content edge, or set both columns on the same grid so the empty space looks chosen (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- The headline breaks as "Tell us about your / project", leaving one word alone on line two and splitting "your project". Give the left column a bit more width or rewrite the line so it breaks at a phrase.

**What's working.**
- There's one primary action and only one. The filled blue "Send message" is the only filled element on the page, and the plain underlined links in the nav and footer don't compete with it. Keep it that way when you add fields.
- The labels sit above the fields, in bold, close to their own field and clearly separated from the one before. The form reads in one quick pass from top to bottom.
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is the strongest line on the page. It's specific, it promises something, and it answers the visitor's real worry (will anyone reply?). Keep it, and keep it near the top.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Error and success states: whether a missing email or a bad address is flagged in words next to the field, and whether sending shows a clear confirmation with what happens next.
- The focus ring on the inputs and button. It matters more here because the resting borders are already pale.
