**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big white headline, "Three days on building quieter, slower software". It then picks up the yellow date line above it and the yellow "Get tickets" button below. Within two seconds it reads as a conference landing page for software designers and engineers. The tone is calm and confident, which fits the "quieter, slower" pitch. In version 1 the headline looks jammed: the two lines run into each other and read as one dense slab. In version 2 they read cleanly.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero of a conference site, and its job is to tell practitioners what the event is, when and where it happens, and get them to buy a ticket.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job better. The only real change is the headline's line spacing. In version 1 the letters of the first line crash into the second, which damages the one element the whole page rests on. Version 2 fixes that and gives the headline a little more room above the supporting text. Apart from that, both versions are the same, and both are solid.

**What would move it most**
1. **Headline line spacing (fixed in version 2). Defect.** In version 1 the line spacing is below the type size. The descenders of "days" and "building" (the y and g) touch the ascenders of "quieter" and "software" (the l, t and f). Two lines become one tangled block, and at a glance letters merge: "building" and "software" lose their bottoms and tops. Bringhurst allows tight leading on a display line only "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide" (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, p. 37). Version 2 opens the spacing until nothing touches, and keeps it tighter than the body text, so the headline still holds together. Keep version 2's spacing. If anything, check the y and g against line 2 at narrower widths, where the headline may wrap to three lines.

For version 2, nothing else rises to the ranked list.

**Smaller things.**
- "Get tickets" is clear but gives no price or "from £X". For a paid event, a price next to the button would let the viewer judge the cost before clicking. This is taste, and it's optional for a hero.
- In both versions the right half and lower third of the screen are empty. That suits the "quieter" message, but nothing hints that there's more below the fold. Make sure the next section starts close enough to peek in at common laptop heights.

**What's working.**
- The headline copy is specific and ownable. "Quieter, slower software" tells this audience what makes the event different, rather than "the future of software".
- Yellow is used for only two things: the date/venue line and the button. Together they carry the facts and the action, so the eye reads what, when, where and how to buy in one pass.
- The type scale is tight: one very large bold headline, a 20 px deck in a softer colour, and small labels. Each level does one job.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the headline wraps to three lines on narrower screens, and whether the tighter leading in version 1 would collide even worse there. Also the button's hover and focus states.
