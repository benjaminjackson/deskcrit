**First read.** The bold sans title "Notes for ferry passengers" catches the eye first. The eye then drops into four calm serif paragraphs and settles into reading. It reads as a plain, trustworthy information page from a small local ferry service. It feels quiet and well-mannered, more like a printed leaflet than a website.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a web page that tells people who will take, or are about to take, this river ferry when it runs, how to bring a bike, and what to expect at the landing.

**Verdict.** The typography is careful and close to faultless. The page still fails its job, because it never gives a passenger a time they can act on, and the facts they come for are buried in prose. The layout isn't what's in the way. The words are.

**What would move it most**

1. **The page gives no times (defect).** Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger plans around the first and last boat, and this page gives neither. "Before dusk" moves by hours across a winter. "The quay" could mean the market quay or both ends, so a reader on the north side can't tell whether the last boat back leaves earlier. Paragraph 3 then sends readers to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing", which they only see once they're already standing at the water. Someone who plans from this page can easily miss the last boat. Fix: put the actual first and last departure times from each landing, for summer and for winter, on the page. Say which landing each last departure leaves from. The shelter line then becomes a backup, not the only source.

2. **The facts are written as a story, not set out for scanning (defect).** Passengers skim. They want to know when the boat goes, what it costs, whether they can bring a bike, and when the last boat leaves. Here every answer sits inside a sentence, with equal weight, in an order that mixes the timetable (paragraphs 1 and 3) with bikes (2) and building news (4). The winter change, which is what decides whether someone gets stranded, is third, after the bike rules. Nielsen's finding that web readers scan instead of reading applies. Fix: lead with the timetable, then group the rest. Short bold lead-ins ("Timetable", "Bicycles", "Winter", "At the landing") or one small table of times would let the eye find its answer without losing the quiet tone. The current type gives you room for one more level, set in the sans at body size and bold.

3. **The copy talks about passengers instead of to them, and leaves gaps (defect at the price, taste elsewhere).** "Bicycles ride… at no extra charge" suggests there is a fare, but the page never gives it. A reader can't tell what they'll pay or how to pay. "Riders wheel them on… The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" is a rule written as a description. Saying it to the reader would make it clear and friendlier: "Wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded, lock it to the side rail, and keep your bags on it." Paragraph 4 is news about the rebuild, and it never says what that means for the reader: step-free access with wider ramps, and somewhere dry to wait. Fix: add the fare and how to pay. Write the instructions with "you". End the rebuild paragraph on what it gives the passenger.

**What's working.**
- The reading column: about 68 characters a line, generous line spacing, and space between paragraphs with no indent. Bringhurst's comfortable measure, done properly. Keep the width when you add structure.
- The clear contrast between a bold sans for the title and a book serif for the text (Dair's contrast of structure and weight). It gives two plain levels with no fuss, and it leaves the bold sans free as a third level for section lead-ins.
- Dark text on white with nothing else on the page. It's quiet and legible, and it fits a public-service notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone. Most passengers will read this at a landing on a phone, so check that the text keeps its size and margins at 320 px.
