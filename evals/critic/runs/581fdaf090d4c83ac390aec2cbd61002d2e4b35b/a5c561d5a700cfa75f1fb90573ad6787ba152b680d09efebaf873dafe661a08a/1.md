**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Savings deposits rose every month", drops into the chart, and goes straight to the tall June bar at $1150k. Then it crosses to "$4.82M" at top right. It reads as a calm, trustworthy member report from a small US credit union, and it is easy to take in. The striped bars shimmer slightly the whole time you look at them.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a public report page that tells members their savings deposits are growing. The reader is an ordinary member, not an analyst.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the numbers add up. But the most striking thing on the page, June's jump, is mostly a one-off bonus payroll, and that is only explained in a small footnote below the chart. The page lets the reader believe something the data doesn't support. The hatched bar fill is the second problem.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June bar tells the wrong story, and the only fix is in the footnote.** June is the biggest change on the page: about $282k more than May, against rises of $40–80k in earlier months. The one thing members will remember is that deposits took off in June. The explanation, "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll", is a grey line in small type under the chart card, which most readers won't reach. The "$4.82M, Up 6.1%" card and the "rose every month" headline also include the bonus, and nothing says so. *Defect:* it misleads the member about the trend. Mark the bonus on the June bar itself, for example by showing the bonus as a lighter part of the bar with a direct label. Or give a June figure without the bonus. Then say in the subtitle whether "rose every month" still holds without it. If it does, the headline gets stronger. If it doesn't, the headline has to change.
2. **The diagonal hatching on the bars is decoration that fights the data.** All six bars are filled with tight blue-and-white stripes. At this size they shimmer, they make the bars look lighter than the dark labels above them, and the top edge of each bar, which is where you read its value, gets ragged. The stripes don't encode anything: there is one series and no second category to tell apart. *Convention:* this is moiré hatching, the classic chartjunk (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 11). Use plain flat blue. If you take fix 1, a pattern or lighter tint could then mark the bonus part of June, so it carries meaning.

**Smaller things.**
- Number formats don't match. The chart says "$1150k" and the axis "$1200k", while the card says "$4.82M". Use "$1.15M" and "$1.2M" for anything over a million, and add the thousands comma if you keep "k".
- "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025": "up … on" is a British phrasing, and a US member could read it as an amount added on that date. "Up $11 from June 2025" reads one way only.

**What's working.**
- The headline states a finding, not a topic, and the subtitle says exactly what is being counted ("New money paid into member savings accounts, by month"). Keep that pairing.
- The three cards each pair a number with what it measures and a year-on-year comparison, and they check out: 5,374 × $214 ≈ June's $1.15M, and the six bars add up to $4.82M. That consistency builds trust. Keep it when you split out the bonus.
- The hierarchy is restrained and easy to read: one serif display face for the brand and headline, one sans for everything else, a zero-based axis, pale gridlines, values labelled on the bars, and a stated source. Don't add anything to it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the chart and the card column reflow at narrow widths, and whether the nav links show a visible focus state.
