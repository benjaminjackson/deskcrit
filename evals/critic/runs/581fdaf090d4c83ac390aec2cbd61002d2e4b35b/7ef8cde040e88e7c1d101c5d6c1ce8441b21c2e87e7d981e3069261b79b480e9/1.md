**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold serif headline, "Notes from the boat shed", drops into the larger left column, then crosses to the right column. That column is set smaller and reads like a footnote. After it there is nothing: the lower half of the frame is empty white. It feels quiet and literary, and a little unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a web page for a community boat shed, and its real job is to get a stranger to turn up and volunteer. The right column is the only part that asks for anything.

**Verdict.** The writing is good and the type is clean, but the layout works against the page's purpose. The invitation is the smallest, quietest text on the page, and it doesn't give the reader enough to act on. Fix those two things and this becomes a good page.

**What would move it most**

1. **The two columns look like a mismatch, and the less important one wins.** Both columns are ordinary text in the same typeface and weight, with no label on either. But the left looks about 22 px and the right about 17 px, with tighter line spacing. The difference is too big to read as the same level and too small to read as a deliberate one, so the right column looks like an afterthought or a mistake. Because the line spacing differs, the lines of the two columns drift apart from the second line down, so the two blocks don't form shared rows across the gap.
   - The cost: the reader treats the volunteer paragraph, the only thing they can act on, as a minor note.
   - *Convention* (Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style* §3.1.1: choose sizes from a small scale; Dair, *Design with Type* ch. 8: a weak contrast is worse than none; Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*: the lines of side-by-side columns sit on the same baselines).
   - Fix: pick one of two directions.
     - If the columns are equal partners, set them at one size and line spacing so their lines align.
     - If the invitation is meant to stand apart, make it plainly different: a short bold lead-in such as "Come and help", a tint panel, or a rule. Don't just make it smaller. If anything, it deserves more weight than the description.

2. **The invitation doesn't tell anyone how to come.** "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings" gives no time, no place and no next step. A stranger can't tell whether to just turn up, which lane or town this is, or who to contact. The path ends there, because nothing on the page comes after it.
   - The headline adds to the problem. "Notes from the boat shed" frames the page as a journal, so nothing at the top tells a reader that they are being invited.
   - *Defect*: the only action the page asks for can't be taken from the page.
   - Fix: say exactly what to do, for example "Thursdays, 6–9pm, at [shed address]: just turn up." Add a contact line, and if possible make the headline or a subhead carry the invitation. Keep "Nobody needs experience" and the kettle and spare apron: they answer the reader's worry about not knowing how to do the work, in the reader's own terms.

**Smaller things.**
- Lower half of the frame: the content fills only the top ~45%, so the page looks top-heavy and the empty area reads as leftover rather than chosen. That's fine if this is the top of a longer page. If this is the whole piece, give the space a job: put the details from finding 2 there, or move the block down and add a photo of the shed.

**What's working.**
- The headline is in the same serif family as the text, set bold and much larger, so it is clearly first without a second typeface. Keep that one-family approach.
- The left column's writing (tide under the slipway, clinker dinghies on trestles, tar and cut cedar) is specific and sensory. Nobody else could have written it, and it makes the place real.
- The text column widths look comfortable, roughly 50–60 characters, and the margins are generous. Keep that width whatever you do to the sizes.
