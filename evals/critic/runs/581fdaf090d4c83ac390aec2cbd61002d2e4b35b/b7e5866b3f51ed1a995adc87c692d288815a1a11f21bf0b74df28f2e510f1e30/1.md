**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into what looks like a smudged grey block. The lines in each paragraph sit on top of each other, so descenders from one line cut into the capitals and ascenders of the next. It reads as a broken page, not a finished notice, and it feels careless.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a plain web or kiosk notice telling ferry passengers about times, bikes and winter service, read once and acted on.

**Verdict.** It fails its job. You cannot read the body text without effort because the line spacing is less than the type size. That one fault outweighs everything else on the page. Once it is fixed, the next problem is that the copy gives vague times where passengers need exact ones.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text lines collide.** This happens in all three paragraphs, from y≈140 to 360. Line spacing looks like about 15 px on type that looks about 19 px. The bottoms of "forty minutes", "takes about twelve" and "on after foot passengers" run into the line below. The reader has to pick each line out of the pile, loses their place when returning to the start of the next line, and rereads. On a page that is nothing but three paragraphs, this is the whole experience. It is a **defect**, not a style choice. Bringhurst allows tight or even negative leading only "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide" (*Elements of Typographic Style*, p. 37). Here they collide. Bringhurst and Hochuli also both say that the longer the line, the more space it needs between lines, and this line runs about 65–70 characters. **Fix direction:** set the body line spacing to about 1.4–1.5 times the type size (roughly 26–28 px here). Then check that the paragraph gaps stay clearly larger than the gaps between lines, so the three blocks still read as three. This looks like a CSS line-height bug rather than a choice, so check whether a global `line-height` is being overridden.
2. **The copy is vague exactly where passengers act on it.** This is a **defect**, about the words. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "a little longer when the tide turns" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines someone will plan a trip around, and none of them gives a time. "Before dusk" can mean 15:50 or 16:30 depending on the date and on how the reader judges dusk. Someone who reads it the generous way misses the last boat. **Fix direction:** give the first and last departures as clock times. Give the winter last sailing as a time, or as "about X minutes before sunset, see the noticeboard for this week's time". Make "a little longer" a number ("up to 20 minutes").

**Smaller things.**
- The heading spacing works now only because the body is so tight. Once the leading opens up, check that the heading still sits closer to the first paragraph than to the top margin.

**What's working.**
- **One face and a clean type scale.** It uses two sizes (heading and body) and a bold weight for the heading, so it is obvious at a glance what is title and what is text. Keep the scale at that.
- **Readable line length.** Lines run about 65–70 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Don't widen the column to fill the empty right half of the screen.
- **Plain, calm tone of voice.** "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" says who does what and when. Keep that voice when you add the times.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Reflow at narrow widths and zoom. The line-spacing bug may also get worse or better at other sizes, so check in a live browser.
