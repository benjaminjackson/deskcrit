**First read.** The eye lands on "Project notes" and then drops into a stack of five heavy black-outlined boxes. What stays with me is the frames, not the words: five dark rectangles marching down the left two-thirds of the screen. It reads as a plain internal doc or a wireframe. It feels tidy, stiff and a little boxed-in.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's an internal page that shows a team the stages of a design project, from kickoff to handover, so they know what happens and in what order.

**Verdict.** It's readable and orderly, but it doesn't say what it is. The content is a five-step process, but the title calls it "notes" and the layout shows five interchangeable boxes. The near-black borders are also the loudest thing on the page and make it look like a wireframe.

**What would move it most**

1. **It's a sequence, but nothing shows the sequence (defect).** Kickoff → Research → Sketches → Review → Handover is clearly ordered: each step needs the one before it. But the five cards are identical, with no numbers, no arrows or connecting line, and no "Step 2 of 5". A reader skimming the titles gets a list of topics, not a path. They can't tell at a glance that Review comes after Sketches or how far along the project is. The title "Project notes" makes this worse: it promises loose notes, so the reader doesn't look for an order. To fix it, make the order visible and say what the page is. Numbering the stages (1–5) and retitling the page as the process ("How a project runs" or "Project stages") would do most of the work. If the page is also meant to track progress, it needs a current or done state on the stages, which it doesn't have.

2. **The borders outweigh the content (convention).** Each card is boxed in a thick, near-black rule, about 3 px. That's about as dark as the text and much heavier than any stroke in the letters. Squint and you see five dark frames, not five stages. The boxes add nothing that the spacing doesn't already give you: the 18–20 px gaps between cards already separate them. They also make the cards look like input fields or big buttons, which the reader may then try to click. Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 157, says rules and boxes "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell." Remove the borders, or replace them with space or a pale tone so they stop competing with the content. If the cards need an edge, a light grey hairline or a faint tint is enough. Better still, put that visual weight into the step numbers from finding 1.

**Smaller things.**
- The descriptions are good, but the card titles are bare nouns ("Review", "Handover"). A verb-led title ("Pick a direction", "Hand over the brief") would let someone skim only the titles and still get the process.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is clean:** three sizes (about 34 / 20 / 16 px) with clear jumps, plus weight on the titles. Each level has an obvious job and nothing collides. Keep it.
- **The descriptions are concrete and complete.** Each one says who does what: "Visit three sites and write up what the teams there need", "Write the brief and pass it to the build team". A stranger could act on them. Don't trim them.
- **Spacing and alignment are consistent:** one left edge, even gaps between cards, the same inner padding in every card, and comfortable line lengths.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are clickable, and if so what hover and focus look like. The heavy borders suggest they might be.
