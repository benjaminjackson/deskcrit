**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then drops into a body block that isn't where the heading said it would be. The heading starts at the left edge, but every paragraph is centred, so each line starts at a different place. It reads as a plain, calm information page for people taking a small river ferry. It feels tidy, but slightly off, as if two pages were laid on top of each other.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or printed notice telling passengers when the ferry runs, how long a crossing takes, how bicycles work, and where to find the timetable. Readers check it quickly before they travel.

**Verdict.** It mostly works: it's readable, quiet and well sized. Two things hold it back. The centred paragraphs under a left-aligned heading make it slower to read and break the page's one alignment, and the copy never gives the actual times a passenger needs to catch a boat.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are centred under a flush-left heading (convention, with a real cost).** All three paragraphs (about 4 lines each, ~65–70 characters) are centred on an axis around x≈440. The heading sits flush left at x≈120. With a ragged edge on both sides, the eye has to hunt for the start of every line, which slows reading in exactly the text people came for. The heading and body also share no edge, so the page has no single alignment and the block looks placed by eye. Tschichold rejects centring by default, and Bringhurst (2.1.3) treats ragged setting as a choice that has to suit the text. Centring is for a few short lines, not running paragraphs. Fix: set the paragraphs flush left on the heading's left edge (x≈120). That one change fixes the ragged left edge, the two competing axes and the missing grid line together.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on (defect).** "Between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "in winter" are the details people plan around, and none of them can be pinned down. Does "early morning" mean 6:00 or 7:30? Does "before dusk" mean 15:45 or 16:30 in December? Which months count as winter? A passenger who guesses late is stranded on the wrong bank. If the times change, say so ("first boat 6:40, last boat 21:20; winter, November to February: hourly, last boat 15:50"). Or point to where the exact times are in the sentence that gives the rule, not two sentences later. Fix direction: put real times, dates and months next to each rule. Consider pulling the timetable facts out as a short list above the prose, since that's what people check.

**Smaller things.**
- "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes in calm weather, and a little longer when the tide turns against the boat": "a little longer" has no figure. Give a range ("up to 20 minutes") so people can plan a connection.
- "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded": the subject switches from bicycles to riders without saying so. "Wheel your bicycle on after foot passengers have boarded" speaks to the reader and is clearer.
- The prose is all third person ("Riders…", "The crew asks…"). Addressing the reader as "you" would make the rules feel like instructions rather than descriptions. This is taste, but it fits a notice.

**What's working.**
- The type is restrained and the scale is clear: one bold heading and one body size, a big gap between them, and nothing in between to muddle it. Keep it.
- The body measure (~65–70 characters) and the generous line spacing are right for comfortable reading. Keep both when you move the text to flush left.
- The content is in a sensible order (normal service, then bicycles, then winter changes and where to check), and each paragraph covers one topic. That order already does the job of headings, so you don't need to add any.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this is live text or a picture of text, and how the column reflows on a phone. Check both in a browser, because passengers will often read this on a phone at the landing.
