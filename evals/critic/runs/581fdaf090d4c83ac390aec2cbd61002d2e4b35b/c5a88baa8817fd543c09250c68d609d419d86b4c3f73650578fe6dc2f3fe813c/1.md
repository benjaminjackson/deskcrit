**First read.** The eye lands on "Landing stage rebuild", then goes straight to the solid blue bar in the picture. It is the brightest thing in the article column and has the same blue and rounded shape as the Sign up button, so for a moment it looks like a control. Then the eye moves right to the boxed "Get timetable changes" form. It reads as a calm, tidy news page from a local ferry service, with a sign-up box on the side.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a ferry operator's news page about a rebuilt landing, with a sidebar that signs riders up for timetable-change alerts.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read, but the sign-up box, which is its one action, can't work as shown: it asks for a name and a landing but never asks where to send the note. The picture also fails to show the rebuild the article is about, and its blue bar copies the button.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sign-up has no way to reach anyone.** "Get timetable changes" asks for *Your name* and *Home landing*, then says "We send one short note…", but there is no email or phone field. A rider who signs up has no idea where the note will arrive, and the operator has nowhere to send it. This is a defect: the form can't do its job and the page never explains how it works (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, conceptual model; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: a page should explain itself). Fix: add the channel field and label it plainly, e.g. "Email" or "Mobile number for texts". Make the button and the note below it name that channel too: "Email me when times change", "We email you once when the timetable changes." If name isn't needed to send an alert, drop it so the form stays at two fields.
2. **The blue bar in the picture looks like a second button.** The rounded bar at the right of Figure 1 uses the same saturated blue and corner radius as Sign up. That pulls the eye away from the real action, makes a picture look clickable, and gives one colour two meanings on the same screen. This is a defect against Nielsen's consistency heuristic and Norman on affordances. Keep the button blue for things you can press: redraw the bar in the figure's grey-blue range and drop its rounded corners, so the only saturated blue on the page is the action.
3. **The picture doesn't show what the words claim.** The caption says "the new north landing, seen from the water at low tide". The text says the ramps "meet the deck at every tide" and that benches and a kiosk were added. The figure is four flat rectangles with no ramp, no tide line, and nothing you could recognise as a landing. A rider learns nothing from it, and it looks like a placeholder. This is a defect if this is the shipped image; it's fine if a real photo was meant to load here (check the live page). Fix: use a photo or drawing that shows the one thing the article sells, the ramp meeting the deck. Point the caption at that: "The new ramp meets the deck even at low tide."

**Smaller things.**
- *Home landing* is a free-text field for what is surely a fixed list of stops. Typos and different spellings will break the matching. Make it a select list of landings, so riders pick a name instead of recalling and typing it (Nielsen 5 and 6; Norman, constraints).
- The figure stops at about 656 px while the text runs to about 685 px, so the right edges of picture and text almost match but don't. Size the image to the full text column (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- The article's most useful fact for riders, that prams and wheelchairs now roll straight on, sits alone in one sentence below the picture. Taste: move it into the opening paragraph, where scanners will see it.

**What's working.**
- The type is controlled: about five sizes (32, 22, 18, 16, 14), each with a clear job, one family, and a body measure of about 65 characters with comfortable leading. Keep this scale.
- The form layout is sound: labels sit above each field and close to it, the fields are in one column, input borders are clearly visible, and the small print below the button promises little and plainly ("one short note… stop at any time"). That builds trust; keep it.
- The opening paragraph is concrete and written for a rider: wider ramps, a covered waiting area, benches facing the water, tea and rolls while the boat loads.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover, focus, and error states on the form, and what happens after Sign up (whether there is a confirmation, and whether it says where the note will go). Also how the sidebar reflows on a phone.
