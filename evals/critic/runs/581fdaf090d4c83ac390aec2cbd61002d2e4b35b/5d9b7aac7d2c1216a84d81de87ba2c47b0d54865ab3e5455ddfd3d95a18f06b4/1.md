**First read.** The eye goes to the purple "Sign in" button first, then up to the red-bordered email field and the red message under it. It reads straight away as a sign-in page for a product called Quillpost, shown in the state where the email address was rejected. It feels calm, tidy and familiar. A competent standard pattern with nothing out of place.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the sign-in screen of a web app, shown after a failed email check, for returning users who need to fix their entry and get in quickly.

**Verdict.** The page does its job. The layout, type and hierarchy are sound. The two things that cost users time are the error message, which describes the right format but doesn't say what's wrong with this entry, and a "Create an account" link that doesn't look clickable.

**What would move it most**
1. **The error message makes users find the mistake themselves** (defect). Under the email field, "Enter an email address in the format name@example.com" gives a general rule. The user then has to compare "dana.ortiz@exmaple" with "name@example.com" letter by letter to see that the ending (".com" or similar) is missing. Because the example contains "example", it also invites them to notice the "exmaple" typo while missing the actual problem. Nielsen's 9th heuristic asks an error message to say what went wrong as well as how to fix it, next to the field. That second part is met here, so this passes WCAG 3.3.3 but falls short of Nielsen 9. Direction: name the problem with this entry in plain words, said to the user. For example: "This address is missing its ending, like .com. Check what comes after the @." If the system can tell a domain is a likely typo, suggest the fix ("Did you mean example.com?").
2. **"Create an account" doesn't look like a link** (defect). In "New here? Create an account" at the foot of the card, the link is the same grey, weight and size as "New here?". There's no underline and no colour change, so new users, the only people this line is for, see a sentence with nothing to click. It also breaks the page's own rule: "Forgot your password?" just above uses the brand purple for a link. Direction: style it like the other link, in the same purple. Add an underline if it ever sits inside running text (WCAG 1.4.1, technique F73).

**Smaller things.**
- The password field's border looks pale grey on white, probably under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Check the ratio, and darken the default border until the field's edge is clear without relying on its label. The red error border shows the shape the border should have.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear: one filled purple button, one text link below it, labels above the fields, and a heading that says "Sign in". Nobody has to wonder what to do. Keep the single strong action.
- The error is handled correctly on the basics. It sits right under the field, it's in words and not only red, the text the user typed is kept, and the red border marks which field failed. Rewrite the message, but don't change how it's set up.
- The type scale is tight: about three sizes (heading, wordmark/button, body/labels), with labels set apart by weight rather than another size, and everything aligns to one left edge inside the card.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the fields, links and button, and whether they are clear enough (WCAG 2.4.7 and 2.4.13). This matters for a form that is often filled in by keyboard.
- When validation runs (when the user leaves the field, or on submit) and whether the error is announced to screen readers.
