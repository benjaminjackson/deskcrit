**First read.** The eye lands on "Crossing the river", a heavy brush script, then jumps right to "Good to know", which uses the same script, then to the blue button. Only after that does it come back to the two plain sections on the left. It reads as a ferry passenger guide, but the script makes it feel like a café menu or a wedding site. It's calm and uncluttered, and the type is louder than the content needs.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public information page that tells passengers on one ferry route when boats run and how bicycles and luggage work, and sends them to the full timetable.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and quick to scan. What lets it down is the type: three unrelated typefaces, and paragraph text set two ways on the same page. The page looks put together one piece at a time rather than designed as a system. The copy also avoids the one thing a passenger came for, which is actual times.

**What would move it most**
1. **Three typefaces in conflict, and the script carries the wrong tone.** The headline and the card heading are in a brush script. The subtitle, the section headings and the card text are in a serif. The main paragraphs and the nav are in a sans. Each kind of text uses a different kind of letter, so the faces have no relationship to each other (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 7–8). The script also does two jobs it shouldn't. It makes the side card shout as loudly as the page headline, so two elements compete for first look. And it gives a transit notice a party-invitation voice, which undercuts trust in information people plan journeys around. Direction: drop the script. Pick a serif-and-sans pairing and give each face one role, such as serif for all headings and sans for all reading text. Then make the H1 dominant through size and weight in that face. *Convention (Dair). The tone point is taste: a ferry guide should read as dependable, not festive.*
2. **The paragraph text is set two ways.** The main body is sans at about 18 px. The card text is serif at about 20 px with looser line spacing. Side by side, these read as two different products. That breaks the scale: I count about nine sizes, roughly 60, 44, 28, 24, 22, 20, 18 and 16 px. The 18/20 and 24/28 pairs are too close to read as separate levels (Bringhurst, *Elements*, §3.1.1). Direction: set all reading text in one face at one size. Cut to about four levels: H1, section heading, body, and small UI text. Make the card heading the size of a section heading, not close to the H1. *Convention.*
3. **The copy leaves out the facts passengers came for.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves before dusk" give no first boat, no last boat, and no clock time at all. "Before dusk" changes every day, so a passenger can't plan around it and could miss the last boat. "In winter" doesn't say which months. The card copy describes where printed timetables are kept but doesn't tell this reader anything they can act on now. Direction: put real times in. For example, "First boat 6:20, last boat 21:40; every 40 minutes. November to February: hourly, last boat 16:00." Change the card to something that helps now, such as "Next boats from North Landing" or a link to this week's changes. *Defect: times are what passengers act on, and a vague time can strand someone.*

**Smaller things.**
- The card's right edge stops about 34 px short of the nav's right edge ("Landings"), and its top doesn't line up with the H1. It looks accidental. Snap it to the same right edge and the same top line as the headline (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- The card's text lines don't sit on the same rows as the main column's lines. That comes from the different size and line spacing, so it is fixed by item 2.
- The card paragraph ends with "week." alone on the last line. Re-break it or adjust the copy so the last line isn't one short word (Bringhurst §2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The main column scans well. Short sections each sit under a plain heading, with more space above each heading than below, so each one clearly belongs to its text. The line length (about 68 characters) is comfortable.
- There is one clear filled button, "See the timetable", with nothing else competing as an action. Keep it the page's only strong action.
- The quiet nav and plenty of open space keep the page calm. That restraint is right for a utility page, and the type should match it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the nav and the button.
- How the side card reflows on a phone: whether it lands above or below the main sections matters for passengers checking at the landing.
