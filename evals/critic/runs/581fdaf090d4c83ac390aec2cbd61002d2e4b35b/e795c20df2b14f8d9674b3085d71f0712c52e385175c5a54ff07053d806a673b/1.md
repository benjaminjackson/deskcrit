**First read.** My eye goes straight to the dark band of cells from mid-morning to mid-afternoon on weekdays. Next it goes to the heading at top left, then to the three big numbers on the right. Within two seconds it's clear this is a support team's report of when tickets come in. It feels calm, sober and trustworthy, like a well-made internal tool.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a support lead or ops manager uses this report page to plan staffing by hour and day. It works by seeing the weekly pattern of incoming tickets at a glance.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, disciplined piece and it does its job. The pattern reads instantly. The one thing that really hurts it is a number that disagrees with its own chart. "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" sits beside a heatmap whose darkest column is 11:00, and that's the figure a manager would staff around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The busiest-hour KPI contradicts the heatmap. Defect.** Each tick label (00:00, 03:00, 09:00…) starts at the left edge of its cell. That puts the darkest cells, 30+ on Monday and Friday, in the 11:00 column. The 10:00 column is one step lighter (25–29) on every weekday. A reader who compares the two either stops trusting the panel or staffs up an hour early. Find out which is wrong before shipping:
   - It may be a data or labelling bug.
   - Both may be right. 10:00 could win on raw averages while the bins hide the difference, or the hour could mean "the hour ending at 10:00". In that case the page has to say so, for example "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00–11:00, avg 27 tickets", and the bins have to show that 10:00 is the peak.
   
   A figure people will act on has to read only one way.

2. **The title names the topic, not the finding. Taste.** "When new tickets arrive" plus a long subtitle tells the reader what the chart is about, then leaves them to work out the answer. The answer is plain and worth saying: tickets pile up from about 09:00 to 17:00 on weekdays, peak late morning, and weekends are light. A title that states it, for example "Most tickets arrive 09:00–17:00 on weekdays, peaking late morning", turns the page from a chart you read into a decision you can make. Keep the current subtitle as the factual line underneath. I'm arguing this from common sense; no approved source here backs it.

**Smaller things.**
- The "New tickets, September" KPI says September, but the chart covers 1 to 28 September. Write "1–28 Sep" on the KPI too, or explain the difference. Otherwise a reader may divide 3,412 by four weeks and get a figure that doesn't match the heatmap.

**What's working.**
- **The colour ramp.** It's one blue going from light to dark in seven labelled bins. More means darker, it survives colour-blindness and greyscale, and the binned legend gives exact ranges, not a vague gradient. Keep it.
- **The context lines.** Every KPI has a period and a comparison ("Up 4% on August", "Down 6 min on August"), so the reader can tell good from bad. The source line says what the hour means: when a ticket was opened, in team-local time. That removes the obvious misreading.
- **Restraint.** There are no gridlines, frames or decoration. The only colour is the data. One left edge runs from the heading through the legend to the source line. Nothing competes with the pattern.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cells show exact values on hover or focus, and whether the chart has a text or table alternative for screen readers. For a staffing tool, exact counts per cell matter.
- How the 24-column grid reflows on narrow screens.
