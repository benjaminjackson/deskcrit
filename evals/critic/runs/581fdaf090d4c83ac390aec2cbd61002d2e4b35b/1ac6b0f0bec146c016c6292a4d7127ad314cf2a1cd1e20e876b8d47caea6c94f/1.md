**First read.** The headline hits first and hard: "Plan the week your team actually works," in a very large, tightly set heavy sans. The eye drops to the grey subhead, then to the blue button, and then has nowhere to go: the right half and the bottom third of the screen are blank white. It reads as the top of a landing page for a team-planning SaaS. It feels confident and clean, but bare, as if the hero image never arrived.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the first screen of Weekplan's marketing site, meant to get team leads and engineering managers to start a trial.

**Verdict.** The type and hierarchy are solid, but the page doesn't do its job yet. It promises "one honest picture of the next five days" and then shows no picture. The viewer is asked to start a trial on trust, without ever seeing what they'd get.

**What would move it most**
1. **The product is missing from the screen. The main empty area should show it.** The bottom ~35% of the frame and everything right of the subhead are empty. That's leftover space, not space that frames anything. The copy makes a visual claim ("one honest picture") that the page never backs up, so the eye path ends at the button and falls into white. A first-time viewer can't tell what Weekplan *is*: a calendar, a board, a report? *Defect* (the viewer can't picture the product). Breaking the visual-and-headline pairing is *taste*, argued here because the headline is literally about a picture. Fix direction: put the actual week view in that space, at a size where its structure reads, either below the button or to the right. It should show tickets, meetings and someone's leave sitting together in one five-day view, so the subhead's claim can be checked at a glance. If an image is supposed to be there and didn't load in this capture, check it in a live browser. The finding still applies if it loads below the fold.

2. **The ask is generic and asks for trust instead of showing value.** "Start a free trial" could sit on any SaaS page. It doesn't say what the viewer will see or do first. It also gives no trial length and doesn't say whether a card is needed, and people decide on exactly those details. The strongest ask would let the viewer compare Weekplan with what they already have: *their* tickets and calendar, shown as *their* next week. *Convention / copy defect.* Fix direction: name the first concrete step and the result in the button or a line under it, e.g. connecting the tools they already use and seeing their own next week. Add the trial terms (length, card or no card) in small text beside the button.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "your team / actually works" splits the phrase that carries the meaning. Breaking after "week" ("Plan the week / your team actually works") keeps that phrase whole on one line.

**What's working.**
- The headline. It's specific and has a point of view: "actually works" names the gap between the planned week and the real one. It's also set with real scale contrast and tight leading, so it reads as one unit. Keep both the line and the treatment.
- The subhead says who does what, to what: three concrete inputs (tickets, meetings, leave) go in, and one result over a stated time span comes out. Its measure is comfortable (~50 characters per line) and the grey still looks readable.
- There's one clear primary action and quiet text-only nav, so nothing competes with the button.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the blank lower area is the design or an image or animation that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check in a live browser.
- The button's hover and focus states, and how the oversized headline reflows on narrow screens.
