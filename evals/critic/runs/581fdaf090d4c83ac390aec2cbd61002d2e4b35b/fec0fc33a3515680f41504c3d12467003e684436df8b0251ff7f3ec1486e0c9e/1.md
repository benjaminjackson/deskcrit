**First read.** In both versions the eye goes to the bold "$1,385.00" total and the dark green "Continue to payment" button on the right, then back to "Review your order" and the three line items. It reads as the last review step of a furniture checkout, and it feels calm and orderly. In version 2 the delivery note jumps out on the second look as a dense grey block of capitals, so it reads like legal small print rather than useful advice.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a checkout review screen where a buyer checks a large furniture order (table, benches, delivery) before paying. It must also make sure they take in the delivery terms, because getting those wrong means a failed delivery.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The two screens are the same except for one change: version 2 sets the four-line delivery note in all capitals, which makes the most important caution on the page the hardest thing on it to read. Version 1 is a sound, clear review screen. Its main gap is that the buyer has no visible way back to change the order.

**What would move it most**
1. **The delivery note: the only difference between the versions, and v2 makes it worse (defect).** The amber box in the left card holds the one piece of text that can go wrong for the buyer: weekday-only delivery, a two-hour window by text, and "measure doorways and stairs first, because our drivers cannot take doors off their hinges." In v2 that whole paragraph is in capitals. Every word becomes the same rectangle, so readers lose word shapes and slow down. The block also grows from three lines to four and starts to look like boilerplate terms, which people skip. The cost is real: a buyer who skips it orders a table that won't fit through the door. Capitals suit headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions, not paragraphs (Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps"; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*). The capitals also look set at default letter spacing, which crowds them further. The amber bar and tint already give the note its emphasis, so the capitals add nothing. Fix: keep v1's upper and lower case. If the note needs more pull, bold only the instruction: "Measure doorways and stairs first."
2. **No way back to edit the order (convention, Nielsen 3: user control and freedom).** Both versions offer only one action, "Continue to payment." There is no "Edit order" or "Back to basket" link next to the line items. A buyer who sees the wrong bench count or wants a different table length has to rely on the browser's back button. Fix: put a quiet text link such as "Edit order" in the left card's header, kept well below the green button in emphasis.

**Smaller things.**
- No step indicator (for example Basket → Review → Payment), so the buyer can't see how far along they are or that payment comes next. Add a light progress line under the header.
- The right card stretches to match the left card's height, leaving an empty band under the button, larger in v2. Let the summary card size to its content, or let it stick as the page scrolls.

**What's working.**
- The total is clearly the dominant element: bold, larger, and set under a heavier divider than the Items and Delivery rows. It sits directly above the only filled button, so the eye goes total, then action without detours. Keep that pairing.
- Prices are right-aligned in tabular figures in both cards, so the $940 + $380 = $1,320 check is easy to make by eye.
- Version 1's delivery note is specific and written in the buyer's terms ("We will text you a two-hour window the evening before"). It gives the reason for the measuring instruction, so the warning is persuasive rather than bossy.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus and hover states on "Continue to payment," and how the two cards stack on a narrow screen. Check that the summary and button don't fall below the line items on mobile, out of sight.
