**First read.** The eye lands on the big serif headline, then drops to the subhead, then picks up the small brown "HARTLEY'S BAKERY · SINCE 1962" line above. Then it stops, because the bottom 40% of the screen is empty. It reads as the top of a small-town bakery's homepage. It feels warm, quiet and well made, but it has nothing to show you and nothing for you to do.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the opening screen of a local bakery's website, for people nearby, and that its job is to make them want the bread and then come in or order.

**Verdict.** It's handsome type with no destination. The visitor learns what's sold and where, then gets no next step, no bread to look at, and a headline any bakery could use. As a first screen it makes a good impression and then sends no one through the door.

**What would move it most**

1. **The page is a dead end. There's no ask and nothing to do next.** Below the subhead there's no button, no opening hours, no map link and no order option. There's no navigation either, so a visitor can't even find those things themselves. The eye ends at "on Mill Street." and lands in a large empty band (roughly y 520 to 800) that looks left over rather than planned. Someone who already wants bread now has to scroll or hunt for the two things they came for: when you're open and how to get some. This is a defect, because the visitor can't tell what to do next (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; Nielsen heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Fix it with one clear primary action under the subhead, and make it a specific one, like "Open today 7am to 2pm · Find us on Mill Street" or "Order tomorrow's loaves". Avoid a vague "Visit us". Give the empty lower band to that action and to the hours. If a nav bar exists and was cropped out of this capture, the missing action still stands.

2. **The headline makes a generic claim you have to take on trust, while the real case is in the small type.** "The finest bread in the valley" could sit on any bakery's site. It asks you to believe it, and nothing on the screen backs it up: no loaf, no oven, no photo at all. The convincing details (wood-fired oven, baked every morning, named loaves, Mill Street, since 1962) are all in the 20 px subhead and the 13 px eyebrow, where few people will read them. So the visitor's strongest takeaway is the weakest claim. This is a defect in the copy, and a matter of taste on the image. Fix it by putting the specific claim in the headline, something like "Baked every morning in a wood-fired oven since 1962". Or keep a short headline and put a real photograph of the bread or the oven beside it, so the visitor can judge "finest" for themselves rather than being told.

3. **The headline breaks badly and leaves "valley" alone on a line.** "The finest bread in the / valley" splits the phrase "in the valley" and leaves one word sitting alone at 120 px. That's the most visible type on the page, and the eye reads the break as a mistake. This is a convention: Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.4.2) rules out a lone short word on a paragraph's last line. For headings, breaking at the natural phrase is common sense. Fix it by breaking after "bread" ("The finest bread / in the valley"), with balanced wrapping or a slightly narrower box, so each line holds a complete phrase. If you rewrite the headline per point 2, check the new break the same way.

**Smaller things.**
- The empty lower ~40% of the screen: once the action and hours from point 1 are in, size the remaining space on purpose rather than leaving whatever is left over.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is clean.** There are three sizes with obvious jobs: the ~120 px serif headline, the ~20 px sans subhead and the ~13 px eyebrow. The eyebrow is set apart by colour, capitals and letterspacing, not just size. Keep that discipline when you add the action and the hours.
- **The serif and sans pairing contrasts clearly and suits the subject.** The old-style serif reads as craft and tradition, and Inter keeps the small text easy to read. The cream background with the warm brown accent fits a bakery without being cute about it.
- **The subhead copy is specific and concrete,** and its measure (about 60 characters) is comfortable. It's the best writing on the page, which is why point 2 says to promote it.
