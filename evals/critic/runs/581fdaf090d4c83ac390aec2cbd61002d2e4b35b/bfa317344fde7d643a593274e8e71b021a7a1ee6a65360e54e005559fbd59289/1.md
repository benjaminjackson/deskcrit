**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline, drops to the big blue 38% wedge, then has to go across to the legend on the right to find out what 38% means. It reads as a calm, well-built annual-report page about operating costs. It looks finished, but it reports numbers without saying what they mean.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one page of an annual report. It should let a shareholder or board member see in a few seconds where the company's operating money went in fiscal 2026, and whether anything there is a concern.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid but the page doesn't do its job. It shows five shares of a total it never states, with nothing to compare them to and no point made. The reader leaves knowing the split but not whether it is good, bad or changing. The second-biggest wedge is a quarter of all costs, labelled "Other", and the page says nothing about it.

**What would move it most**
1. **There is no total and no comparison, so the shares can't be judged.** The subtitle says "Share of total operating costs" but the total never appears, and there is no prior year. "Salaries 38%" could be good or bad, rising or falling. In an annual report the reader needs both to make any judgement. Add the total in currency to the subtitle or the legend, and put a prior-year column next to the percentages in the legend (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": put the unit, the period and a comparison next to each number). *Defect.*
2. **The headline names a topic, not a finding.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" repeats what the chart already shows, and nothing on the chart points to the one thing worth seeing. The four named slices are equally saturated, so no wedge stands out. Put the finding in the headline, such as what salaries amount to, or what grew or shrank. Then give that slice the strong colour and drop the rest towards grey (Few: keep a second colour for the one point the reader must notice). The takeaway-title point is common sense, not a cited rule. *Taste, argued: a report page that makes no point leaves each reader to find their own.*
3. **"Other" at 25% is the second-largest category and isn't explained.** An "Other" bucket is meant to hold slivers (Few's approved fix groups small slices into Other). Here it is bigger than rent, hosting and marketing combined, minus a point. A careful reader will ask what is in it, and the page looks like it is avoiding the question. Break out its biggest parts, or at least add a note in the source line that says what it contains. *Defect, for an annual report.*
4. **Names and numbers are split between the chart and the legend.** The wedges carry only percentages and the names are in a separate legend, so to name any slice you match colours across the page. Each percentage also appears twice. Label each wedge directly with its name and share. Then the legend is free to become the small table that finding 1 needs (total, amount, prior year). *Convention (Tufte, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information: label on the graphic itself).*
5. **The white "12%" on orange and "16%" on teal look too faint.** By eye they come to about 3:1. At roughly 18 px bold they are just under WCAG's large-text size, so they need 4.5:1. "9%" on purple looks borderline. Measure them, and either darken those two fills or set those labels in dark ink (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3). *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count about seven sizes. Header, eyebrow, legend and source line cluster at roughly 13, 14 and 15 px, and the slice labels (~18) sit close to the subtitle (~19). Merge the 13–15 group into one small size plus one body size, and use weight to separate them.
- The space under the legend is a large dead area (about x 620–1216, y 380–660), so the page feels left-heavy. Direct labels plus a legend-table sized to the pie's height would fill it with purpose. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- Five slices sorted from largest to smallest, starting at 12 o'clock, with Other last. That is the right way to draw a pie, and it is flat and has no chartjunk. Keep it.
- The headline, subtitle and source line answer what, when, and from where. The source line, including "rounded to whole percent", is exactly the right level of honesty.
- The legend's percentages are right-aligned, in bold, with tabular figures and hairline rules. It is already most of the way to being the table finding 1 asks for.
