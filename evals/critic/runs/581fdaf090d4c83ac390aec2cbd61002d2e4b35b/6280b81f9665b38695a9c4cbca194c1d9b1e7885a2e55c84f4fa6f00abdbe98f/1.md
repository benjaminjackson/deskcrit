**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline, "Notes for the summer fete", then drops into two short paragraphs. In the first pass through the text it catches only the three bold phrases: "labelled with their ingredients", "collected by four" and "on the field only". The two italic times, "arrive before ten" and "three o'clock sharp", barely show. It reads as a calm, well-set note to volunteers from a village committee. It is polite and orderly, but the details people will act on are hard to find.

**What it's for.** No brief was given, so I'm assuming this is a one-screen briefing for stallholders and helpers (and maybe visitors) at a village fete. They need to come away knowing when to arrive, what to do with cakes, when the raffle is and where to park.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is competent, but the page doesn't do its job well. It hides five instructions inside prose, and it marks the times in a weaker style than the lesser rules. The two things people most need to remember, when to arrive and when the draw is, are the hardest to find again.

**What would move it most**
1. **The emphasis makes the wrong things stand out.** There are five marked phrases in six lines of text. Three are bold and two are italic, with no rule for which gets which. Both are times, yet "arrive before ten" and "three o'clock sharp" are in italic, which barely shows against the regular text at this size. Meanwhile "on the field only" is in bold. Someone glancing back at the screen for "what time again?" finds the parking rule first and the start time last. When a page marks this many phrases, nothing stands out, and here what does stand out is the least important (*defect*; using one style for one kind of thing is the convention, Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*). Fix: give every time and deadline the same single style. Bold works because it's the one that shows. Unmark the lesser rules, or set them all in one consistent second style.
2. **The instructions are written as paragraphs, so they're hard to scan.** There are four or five separate instructions: arrive before ten, label the cakes, leave them on the trestle, the draw at three, claim by four, and park on the field. They're spread over two paragraphs, and the second one mixes the raffle with parking. People read a page like this twice: once now, and again on the morning to check a time. Prose makes them reread everything to find one fact (*defect*; Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Fix: make it a short list, one instruction per line, with the time or place at the start of each line. Group the lines by who they're for or by time of day.
3. **The deadline line can be read two ways.** "All tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box" could mean that organisers collect the ticket stubs by four. It could also mean, and probably does, that winners must claim their prizes by four. A winner who takes the first reading leaves early and loses their prize. Deadlines like this are what people hold the organisers to (*defect*). Fix: say who does what: "Winners must collect their prizes by 4 pm, or they go back in the box."
4. **The note doesn't say who it's for or when the fete is.** "Please arrive before ten" doesn't say who should arrive. Stallholders probably should, but a visitor reading this would think they need to be there before ten too. There's no date anywhere, and "the trestle by the door" and "the field" assume the reader already knows the site. Someone new to the fete has to guess which day, which door and which field (*defect*). Fix: put the date, and the venue if it isn't obvious, under the headline. Address the setup instructions to stallholders by name ("Stallholders: please arrive before 10 am").

**Smaller things.**
- In "o'clock" the apostrophe is straight, not curly. Use a curly apostrophe (’). This is common sense; the curly-quote rule has no checked source.

**What's working.**
- The type is set well for reading. The serif text face has generous line spacing, the lines run about 75 characters (at the upper limit of Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range), and the right edge is ragged. Keep this when you turn it into a list.
- There are only two clear type levels: a big, heavy headline and one text size. That gives the page one obvious place to start. A list doesn't need a third size; use weight to tell items apart.
- The colours are restrained: near-black on white, with strong contrast and nothing extra.
