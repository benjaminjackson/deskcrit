**First read.** The eye lands on "Landing stage rebuild", then drops to the grey-and-blue block figure. Next it goes to the boxed sign-up card on the right, pulled there by the blue "Sign up" button. It reads as a short news item on a ferry operator's site with a sign-up form beside it. It feels calm, plain and orderly, close to a government service page. The figure is the one part the eye can't make sense of: three flat blocks, one of them a blue rounded bar that looks like a second button.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a ferry operator's page telling passengers the landing stages have been rebuilt, with a sign-up so they hear about timetable changes for their own landing.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are competent. The page fails at its one action: the form promises to "send one short note" but never asks where to send it, so nobody who signs up can ever get the note. Fix that first. Then make the figure show the news, and make the headline say it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form has no way to reach the person who signs up.** It asks for "Your name" and "Home landing", then says "We send one short note when the timetable changes." There is no email or phone field. A reader who stops to think wonders how the note will arrive. A reader who doesn't signs up and never hears anything, which is worse, because they now think they're covered. The form gives a wrong picture of how the service works (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, the conceptual model), and the reader has to work out what's missing (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). The name field is the only thing asked for, and on its own it does nothing for the task. **Defect.** Fix: ask for the contact the note goes to, an email or a mobile number, and say which in the label ("Email for timetable notes"). Keep the name only if you use it, and mark it optional.
2. **"Home landing" is a blank text box.** The reader has to remember the exact name of their landing and type it, and a typo or a nickname means the note never fires for them. The operator knows the full list of landings, so the form should show it (Nielsen, heuristics 5 and 6: prevent errors, let people recognise rather than recall; Norman, constraints). **Defect.** Fix: use a select or a short list of radio buttons with the landings in it. Consider letting people pick more than one, since many people use two landings.
3. **The figure doesn't show the news, and part of it looks like a button.** The caption promises "the new north landing, seen from the water at low tide". What's there are three flat rectangles with no ramp, no deck, no tide and no wheelchair. The real story, ramps that meet the deck at every tide, is the one thing a picture could prove, and this one doesn't. Worse, the blue rounded bar uses the same blue and the same corner radius as "Sign up", so the eye pairs them and the bar reads as something to press (Norman: non-interactive things must not look interactive; Nielsen heuristic 4: one colour means one thing; Apple HIG: keep the accent colour for actions). **Defect** for the false button, **taste** for the weak picture. If this is a placeholder for a real photo, it's fine once the photo shows a ramp meeting the boat at low water. If it's the final art, redraw it so the ramp and the deck are recognisable and they meet. Either way, take the action blue out of the illustration.
4. **The headline names a project. It doesn't say what changed for the reader.** "Landing stage rebuild" has no verb and no benefit, so it could head a council works notice. The line that matters to passengers, "prams and wheelchairs roll straight on" at every tide, sits last, under the figure, where many readers won't get to. **Taste, argued:** for a passenger page, the news is the step-free boarding, not the building work. Fix: lead with it, for example "Step-free boarding at every tide". Move the ramp sentence up into the first paragraph and let the bench and kiosk detail follow. In the same way, the button "Sign up" names a category. A label like "Send me timetable changes" says exactly what the reader gets.

**Smaller things.**
- The form labels, the figure caption, the form's helper note and the footer are set in a very light weight at about 13 px. They're readable, but the strokes look thin. Bringhurst's support for this is partial, so this is my judgment: use the regular weight at small sizes.
- The form doesn't mark which fields are required. After you add the contact field, mark the required ones with an asterisk and a one-line legend, or mark the optional ones "(optional)" (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").

**What's working.**
- The type is disciplined: about four sizes with clear steps (headline, card heading, body, small text). Body text is a comfortable size, and lines run about 65 characters. Keep this scale when you revise.
- The helper note "You can stop the notes at any time" is exactly the reassurance the form needs, placed right under the button where the doubt comes up. Keep it.
- The form itself is well built: labels sit above the fields, it's one column, there's one filled primary button, and the input borders are dark enough to see. The card is the right container for it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- What happens after "Sign up" is pressed: whether there's a confirmation, and what it says.
- Error messages for the fields.
- Focus states on the inputs and the button.
- Whether the two columns stack properly on a phone, with the form after the article rather than lost below it.
