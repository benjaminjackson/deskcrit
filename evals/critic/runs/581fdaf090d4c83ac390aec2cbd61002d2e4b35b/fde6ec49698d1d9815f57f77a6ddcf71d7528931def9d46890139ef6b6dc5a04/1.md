**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big white headline on the saturated blue, drops to the subhead, and lands on the white "Try it free" button. That part works. Then it drifts right into an empty blue field half the width of the screen and finds nothing there. The three feature columns below feel like an afterthought. The page reads as a clean, confident, fairly generic consumer budgeting app.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the homepage hero for a consumer budgeting app, and its job is to get a first-time visitor to start the free trial.

**Verdict.** The words and the type do their job, but the composition is half finished. The headline promises "See where every dollar goes", and there is nothing on the page to see. The empty right half of the hero is the biggest thing in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The empty right half of the hero, roughly x 820–1216, y 62–462.** This is a defect. The headline is a visual promise ("See…"), but the visitor gets only words, so they have to imagine the product instead of judging it. Every bit of weight sits on the left, and the eye path dead-ends in a blue field that looks like a slot whose image never loaded (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I, on balance: a heavy side needs a counterweight). The empty half also gives a newcomer no picture of what the app is: a dashboard, alerts, or a spreadsheet (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). The fix is to put the product's answer there: one real-looking month view with categories, limits, and one "you're about to go over" alert. Then headline and picture make the case together. If an image was meant to be there and failed to load, check that in a live browser. As shown, it reads as leftover.
2. **The ask and the trust signal, in the buttons and the third feature column.** This is a copy defect. "Try it free" doesn't say what the visitor will get. A better ask names it and lets them judge the result against what they already have: for example, "Connect a card and see last month's spending", which they can compare with their own sense of where the money went. Meanwhile the strongest reason to connect accounts, "can never move money", is the third of three equal grey paragraphs below the fold line. For a finance app, fear about giving access is the main thing stopping someone from clicking. Move that reassurance next to the button, for example as one short line under it, and keep the column if you like.

**What's working.**
- The headline and subhead are specific and well written. The subhead gives three concrete steps ("connect, set a limit, get a heads-up") and speaks to the reader as "you". Keep that voice.
- The button hierarchy is right. The filled white primary button is clearly stronger than the outlined secondary one, and both read well against the blue.
- The headline is set tight and heavy, with a clear jump in size down to the subhead, so there's no doubt what to read first.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** About 220 px of blank white sits below the feature row (y ≈ 580–800). It could be the end of the page or content that hadn't loaded yet, so check it in a live browser. If the page really ends there, the feature row needs a closing section or a repeat of the call to action. I also couldn't see the hover and focus states on the two buttons, which matter on a saturated blue where the default focus ring may disappear.
