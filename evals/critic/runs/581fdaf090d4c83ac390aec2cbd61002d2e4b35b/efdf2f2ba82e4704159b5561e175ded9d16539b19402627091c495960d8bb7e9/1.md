**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold title, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then hits a solid grey block of sixteen lines with nothing to stop on. It reads as a plain, tidy information page for people using a small river ferry. It feels calm and competent, but you have to read all of it to get anything out of it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that a passenger looks at quickly, often at the landing or just before setting off, to answer one question: when is the next boat, where do I board with my bike, or how do I buy a ticket.

**Verdict.** The type is clean and comfortable to read, but as a passenger notice it fails. Everything is in one unbroken paragraph, so nobody can scan it for their own question, and the facts people actually plan around, the departure times, aren't given anywhere.

**What would move it most**
1. **One block holds five separate topics.** The paragraph runs through the timetable, boarding order, bicycle rules, tickets and passes, winter service and where to find notices, without a break. A passenger with one question, such as "can I buy a ticket on board?", has to read from the top until they find the answer, which is in the middle of line 10. Nothing in the layout shows where one topic ends and the next starts. Nielsen's research on how people read on the web shows they scan for the part they need, and a long unbroken block defeats that. **Defect.** Fix: split it into short sections under small headings in the bold already used for the title (for example: when it runs, boarding, bicycles, tickets, winter). Turn the bicycle rules, which are already a series of commands, into a short list. The type stays the same; the change is to add space between groups.
2. **The schedule has no times.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines a passenger acts on, and each can be read more than one way. Is early morning 6:00 or 7:30? Is "before dusk" 15:45 or 16:30 in December? Someone who guesses late misses the last boat home. The page then sends readers to a printed timetable in the shelter, which only helps once they're already standing at the landing. **Defect.** Fix: give first and last departures for summer and winter, plus the minutes past the hour (or a short table). Put this first, because it's the most common reason anyone opens the page.
3. **The title names a category, not what the page answers.** "Notes for ferry passengers" could be the heading of almost any page, and the opening sentence gives the route but not the times. **Taste.** Fix: lead with the facts. Name the route in the title (north landing to market quay) and put the departure pattern straight underneath.

**Smaller things.**
- The rules are written as bare facts, such as "Foot passengers board first". Saying what they mean for the reader helps: "If you have a bike, wait at the gate until the crew waves you on."
- "A season pass covers any number of crossings for a month" doesn't say whether that's a calendar month or 30 days from purchase. Prices and where to buy a pass are also missing.

**What's working.**
- The line length (about 65–72 characters) and the generous line spacing make the paragraph easy to read once you start. Keep both when you break it up.
- The two-level type scale suits a notice like this: a bold title and regular body text in one sans-serif family. Use that same bold, smaller, for the section headings rather than adding another typeface or a mid-size step.
- The copy is plain and concrete ("the ramp on the upstream side", "lock bicycles to the rail"). The words are good; only the structure is in the way.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely the main device at a ferry landing. Check it at 320 px wide.
