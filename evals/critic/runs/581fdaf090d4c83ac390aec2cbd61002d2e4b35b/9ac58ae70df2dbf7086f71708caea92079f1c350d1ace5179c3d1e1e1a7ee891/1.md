**First read.** The eye goes to "Pricing" first, then across the three prices, $9, $29 and $79. They all have the same size, weight and colour, so it reads them left to right like a table and stops at the bottom of the cards. Nothing tells it where to go next. The only coloured thing on the page is "Start free" in the top-right corner, and nothing ties it to the plans. It reads as a neat, standard SaaS pricing page for some kind of scheduling tool, though I had to work out "scheduling" from "calendars" and "booking page". It feels calm, clean and anonymous.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the pricing page of a booking and scheduling product. Its job is to help a visitor who is comparing tools pick a plan and start on it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. It shows prices but gives no way to buy any of them, no plan to steer towards, and not enough information to tell the plans apart. It is tidy, but a visitor who wants to pay has nowhere to go.

**What would move it most**
1. **None of the plans has an action.** None of the three cards has a button. After reading a price, the eye runs off the bottom of the card and finds empty white space. The only action on the page is "Start free", top right in the nav, far from the cards and unclear about which plan it starts. A visitor who has chosen Team has to guess whether "Start free" gets them Team, a trial, or a free tier that isn't shown. Every pricing page they've used puts a button in each card (Jakob's law, NN/g). That shared habit is the reason the pattern works, and leaving it out costs a lot here. Norman's natural mapping applies too: a control should sit next to the thing it acts on. **Defect.** Fix: put one action at the foot of each card that names what happens, e.g. "Start 14-day Team trial", if that's what it is. Make one of the three buttons the strong one (see 2) and the others outlined. Then rename the nav button so it clearly says the same thing as those buttons.

2. **Nothing tells the visitor which plan to pick.** The three cards are identical in border, weight and the size of the price. That gives three equal elements fighting to be seen first, with nothing to break the pattern, so the visitor has to rank the plans themselves. Tschichold says the order of importance should be shown through contrast. The page title is a single word and the cards are a flat row, so the page has no focal point beyond the heading. **Convention** (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Hofmann on contrast of much against little). Fix: make one plan clearly come first, most likely Team if it's the plan you want most people on. Use a filled primary button, a stronger border or tint, and a short label that gives a reason ("For teams sharing one schedule"), not just "Popular". The other two cards stay as they are.

3. **The copy leaves out what the price buys.** Three things a stranger can't work out:
   - **"/ month" for what?** It could be per person or per account. "Up to 10 calendars" adds to the doubt: is a calendar a person, a room, or a booking link? Someone comparing $29 with a competitor's per-seat price could read it either way, and if they read it wrong they'll feel cheated at checkout. For a price, that ambiguity is a **defect**. Fix: name the unit and say what a calendar is ("$29 / month for your whole team, up to 10 people").
   - **What each plan includes.** The Team card lists "Shared availability, Reports" but not whether it also has the booking page and email reminders from Solo. Business lists "Single sign-on" but doesn't mention booking pages. As written, a visitor can fairly conclude that Team has no booking page. Fix: open each higher plan with "Everything in Solo, plus:".
   - **The subhead could be anyone's.** "Simple plans for teams of every size" says nothing about Tidewell, and the page never says what the product does. Fix: say what the visitor gets and set it against what they use now, e.g. "Let clients book a time without the email back-and-forth." "Change or cancel whenever you like" is the good part: it lands on the viewer. Keep it, and move it next to the buttons where people hesitate.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav: "Pricing" looks the same as the other links, so nothing shows that you're on this page. Mark it with a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Subhead: it breaks so that "you like." sits alone on the second line. Rewrite it, or adjust the line length, so it ends on one full line or on two balanced lines (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- Bottom third (roughly y 570 to 800): an empty band with nothing in it. If the page ends there it looks unfinished. If an FAQ or a plan comparison follows below, it's fine, but show a hint of that above the fold. An FAQ about billing and seats would also answer point 3.

**What's working.**
- The type system is restrained and consistent. There is one sans family, and the prices are made heavy and large while "/ month" stays light and small, so the eye scans across the cards easily. Keep that contrast between price and unit.
- The three cards follow one rule set: the same padding, the same gaps between cards, and the same order inside (name, price, features). When one card is promoted, the other two will hold steady because of that.
- There is a single accent colour, and it is used only on the action. Keep doing that when you add the per-card buttons, and the recommended plan's button will stand out on its own.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav and on any card buttons you add. How the three cards stack on a phone: check that the recommended plan still comes first or stays clearly marked once they are in one column.
