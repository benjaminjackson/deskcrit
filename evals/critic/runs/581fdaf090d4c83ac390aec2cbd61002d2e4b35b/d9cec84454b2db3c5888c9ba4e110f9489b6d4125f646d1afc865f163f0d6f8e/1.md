**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline at top left, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three even paragraphs of grey-black prose and reads them in order. It reads as a plain, calm information page from a small local ferry service: tidy, quiet, a little bookish. Nothing on it stands out as the thing to remember. The right half and the bottom third of the frame are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling ferry passengers when the boat runs, what to do with bicycles, and where to find the timetable. Most will glance at it on a phone or laptop just before a trip.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is careful and easy to read, but the page doesn't do its job well. Every fact a passenger comes for is buried in running prose, and the facts that matter most, the times, are never given. A rider who needs to know when the last boat leaves still doesn't know after reading the whole page.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times are missing, so the viewer can't act on the page (defect).** All three paragraphs give vague times: "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". These are exactly the details a passenger will plan around and hold the operator to, and each can be read more than one way. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00. "Before dusk" moves by an hour or more across the winter, so someone who arrives at 16:30 in December may have missed the last boat without knowing it. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say which minute past the hour, so you can't arrive on time. The page then sends you to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which is the one place you aren't while you're reading this. Fix: give the first and last departure in each season as clock times. Say what "winter" means in dates. Either put the timetable on this page or link to it.

2. **The facts are written as prose, so you have to read every line to find one fact (defect; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** Each paragraph mixes facts a passenger would look up separately. The first gives route, frequency, hours and crossing time in two sentences. The second mixes a rule (bikes free, lower deck) with a boarding procedure. The third combines the winter service with where to find the notices. Skimming the left edge of the paragraphs, you only see "The river ferry…", "Bicycles…" and "In winter…", so nothing points you to times or the last boat. The page is a reference that's been written as an essay. Fix: split it into short labelled blocks such as "Times", "Summer", "Winter", "Bicycles" and "Changes", and put the times in a small table or list where the numbers can be found without reading.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "Notes for ferry passengers" describes a type of page, not what's on it. Something like "River ferry: times, bicycles, winter service" would tell a skimmer they're in the right place.
- "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle during the crossing": it's not clear whether this is a request or a rule, or why it matters to the rider. Say it directly to them ("Keep your bags on your bike…") and give the reason if there is one.

**What's working.**
- The measure and leading suit the text. The lines look like about 65–72 characters with roughly 1.5 line spacing, so the paragraphs read without effort (Bringhurst). Keep both when you add structure.
- The type scale is limited and clear: one bold headline size against one body size, with an obvious step between them. Any new labels should be the body size in bold, not a third size (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).
- The headline sits close to the text it introduces, and the paragraph spacing alone separates the paragraphs, with no extra rules or boxes. That restraint fits a modest public notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page behaves on a narrow phone screen (reflow at 320 px). That matters here because most passengers will check it on a phone at the landing.
