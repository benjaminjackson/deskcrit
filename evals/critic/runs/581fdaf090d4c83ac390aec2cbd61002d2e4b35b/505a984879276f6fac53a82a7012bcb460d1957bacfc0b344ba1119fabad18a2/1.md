**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline. Then it drops into two wide grey-black paragraphs and stops at the tinted box. It reads as a calm, plain help or notice page for a local ferry. It looks tidy and trustworthy, but it's a page you have to read through, not scan. Below the box, the bottom half of the screen is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public information page that tells a rider when the winter ferry runs, what can delay it, and what it costs for bikes, cars and vans. People will come to it to plan a crossing, probably on a phone and in a hurry.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean, but the page doesn't do its job yet. The facts a rider acts on are buried in prose, and the timetable contradicts itself: a boat every forty minutes from seven doesn't land on a six o'clock last crossing. Fix the facts and how they're presented before you touch anything visual.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable is wrong, or at best ambiguous (defect).** First paragraph: departures "every forty minutes" from seven in the morning go 7:00, 7:40 … 17:20, 18:00? No. Eleven hours is 16½ forty-minute steps, so the sequence runs 17:40, 18:20. "The last at six" can't be true under that rule. Either there's an irregular last sailing or one of the numbers is wrong. A rider who arrives for a 6:00 boat that doesn't exist, or misses a 6:20 that does, holds the operator to this line. Make the rule and the last departure agree. If the last sailing breaks the pattern, say so in words ("last boat 18:00, not on the 40-minute cycle").
2. **The schedule is written as prose, so nobody can scan it (defect; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; Nielsen heuristic 2).** Times are spelled out ("seven in the morning", "six in the evening") and mixed with the season and frequency in one long sentence. Riders look for times as clock figures. Written as words, they have to read every word and convert them. Pull the operating facts out into a short block a rider can take in at a glance: season (1 Nov–31 Mar), first boat 07:00, then every 40 min, last boat 18:00, departs North landing. Keep the prose for the ice warning, which really is a sentence.
3. **The page leaves out what a rider needs next (defect; common sense, since the anticipation rule has no approved source).** Three gaps:
   - "Leaves the north landing" says nothing about the return. What does someone on the south bank do?
   - "Cars pay the standard fare" names a price without giving it. The rider has to find it somewhere else (Nielsen heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Prices are what people hold you to, so put the figure here.
   - "Bicycles ride free on weekdays" reads two ways: bikes pay at weekends, or bikes aren't allowed at weekends. Say which, and what it costs.
4. **The lines are too long for comfortable reading (convention; Bringhurst, 45–75 characters, about 66 ideal).** The text column is about 880 px wide, so a line like "From November to March … The first crossing is at" runs to about 100 characters. Narrow the column to roughly 600–680 px at this size. On a page this short, that costs nothing.

**Smaller things.**
- The tinted box has no label, so the reader doesn't know why fares and vehicles are set apart from the schedule. Give it a short heading ("Fares and vehicles"), or drop the tint and use a subhead like the rest of the page.
- Inside the box there's clearly more padding below the text (about 45 px) than above it (about 30 px). The box looks like it lost a line. Make the padding even.
- "Check the board at the ticket hut" sends people to a physical board. If the same hold notices go online, link them here so people can check before they leave home.

**What's working.**
- One sans family, two sizes, and a bold headline make a plain hierarchy with no noise. Keep it to these two levels when you add subheads.
- The headline says exactly what the page is ("How the river ferry runs in winter"). Keep it that literal.
- The ice warning is well written. It gives the condition, what the crew does, and what you should do ("Check the board … before you queue"). Hold the rest of the copy to that standard.
