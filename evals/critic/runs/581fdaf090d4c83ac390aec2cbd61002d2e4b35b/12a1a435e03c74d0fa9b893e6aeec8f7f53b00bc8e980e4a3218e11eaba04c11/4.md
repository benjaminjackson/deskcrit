**First read.** The three big black numbers in the top row get seen first: 3.8, 64.20, 12.4. Then the eye drops to the orange bars, where Saturday is the tallest and Sunday falls away. Within a second it reads as an owner's overview for a small bakery that ships orders. It looks calm and tidy, but the numbers say nothing yet: there's no unit, no period and nothing to compare them to.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home screen a bakery owner opens to see how the shop is doing and what needs attention.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the chart is honest, but the screen doesn't do its job. Its three headline numbers can't be read without guessing, and none of them says whether things are good or bad. The KPI row is the most prominent thing on the page, and right now it's three unlabelled figures.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline numbers have no units, so the viewer has to guess what they measure (defect).** In the three cards along the top:
   - "3.8" conversion is probably a percent, but it could be read as a ratio.
   - "64.20" average order value has no currency.
   - "12.4" active subscribers can't literally be twelve and four-tenths of a person. It's either 12.4 thousand or a percentage, and the owner may read it as either.

   These are the numbers the owner will repeat to a partner or a bank, so a number that can be read two ways is a real risk. The fix is to put the unit on the number itself: 3.8%, £64.20, 12,400 (or 12.4k). The reader should never have to guess what a number measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2).
2. **The numbers are shown alone, with no period and no comparison, so the owner can't tell good from bad (convention).** Each card shows one bare figure. Is 3.8% this month or all time? Is it up or down? Without that, the owner can't act on it. Few flags exactly this in "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (pitfall 2). Next to each number, put the time period and one comparison, such as the change from last month or the target. The chart's source line says September 2026, so say whether the cards cover the same month. If they cover a different period, the page contradicts itself without telling you.
3. **The chart shows a pattern but doesn't say what it means (taste, my judgment).** "Orders by delivery day" only names the topic. The useful facts are only in the bars: Friday and Saturday together are roughly 2,150 orders, and Sunday is about a third of Saturday. The owner plans baking and staffing around exactly this, so say it in the title, for example "Saturday is your busiest delivery day; Sunday is about a third of it." Then highlight that bar in orange and draw the rest in a muted tone, so the colour points at something. A text summary also helps anyone who can't see the chart.

**Smaller things.**
- Chart y axis: there's no unit label. The title implies "orders", but a short "Orders" at the top of the axis removes the doubt once the title is rewritten.

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honest and plain.** The bars start at zero, the gridlines are pale, the days run in calendar order, it uses one colour, and there's a real source line with a date. Keep all of that when you add the emphasis.
- **The structure is solid.** One grid (three equal cards over one full-width panel), even 24 px gaps, and a shared left edge at 48 px make the page easy to scan. The KPI numbers are much larger and bolder than their labels, so the order of importance is unmistakable. That's exactly why the missing units hurt so much.
- **The type scale is tight.** About five sizes, each with a clear job, and the steps between levels come from weight as well as size.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the nav links and on any interactive bars.
- How the three cards reflow on a narrow screen.
