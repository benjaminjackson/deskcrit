**First read.** The eye lands on the three big black numbers (3.8, 64.20, 12.4) and then drops to the orange bars, where the Saturday peak and the Sunday cliff stand out. It reads at once as the admin home screen of a small bakery's online shop. It feels calm, tidy and competent, until you try to read the numbers. Then each one raises a question: 3.8 what, 64.20 in what currency, and 12.4 subscribers?

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the shop owner's overview screen: it should tell them in a few seconds how the shop did this period and whether anything needs their attention.

**Verdict.** The layout, chart and type are clean and well built, but the three headline numbers, the biggest things on the page, can't be read as they stand. They have no units, no period and no comparison. The owner gets three figures they can neither interpret nor judge, so the top of the dashboard doesn't do its job.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units, and one of them can be read two ways.** This is in the three cards in the top row. "3.8" is probably a percentage, but nothing says so. "64.20" has no currency. "Active subscribers: 12.4" is the worst: you can't have a fraction of a subscriber, so the viewer has to guess between 12.4 thousand, 12.4 percent, or some kind of average. Read as 12 people, the shop looks like it's failing. Read as 12,400, it's healthy. A business owner will act on and repeat these exact figures, so a number that reads two ways is a defect, not a style point. Fix: put the unit right next to every figure (3.8%, £64.20, 12,400 or 12.4k), in a lighter or smaller style so the number still leads. *Defect.*
2. **The numbers have no period and nothing to compare them against, so the owner can't tell good from bad.** This is the same three cards. There's no date anywhere above the chart, and the only period on the page is the source line under the chart ("September 2026"). The owner can't tell whether the KPIs cover the same month, and can't tell whether 3.8% is up or down. Few's "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (pitfall 2) names exactly this: a bare big number. Fix: state the period once and plainly at the top, ideally as a date-range control beside "Shop overview", and add a comparison under each number, such as the change from last month or progress toward a target. That small line is what makes the screen answer "is anything wrong?". *Convention, and here a defect, because the comparison is what the page is for.*
3. **The chart and the page headings name topics, not findings.** "Shop overview" and "Orders by delivery day" could sit on any shop's dashboard. The chart makes one clear point that the owner should act on: Friday and Saturday carry the week (roughly 1,050 and 1,130 orders, against about 380 on Sunday). Nothing on the page says so, and nothing links it to the KPIs above. I'm arguing this from common sense, not citing a rule: a title that states the takeaway, such as "Fridays and Saturdays bring in almost half your orders", tells the owner what to plan staffing and baking around. Without it, every visit means reading the chart afresh. *Taste, argued.*

**Smaller things.**
- The chart's main point only exists as bar heights. A one-line text summary under the title (see finding 3) would also serve screen-reader users.
- There's no visible way to change the date range, so the owner can't check last month or last week from this screen. Add the period control suggested in finding 2.
- Each KPI label should carry its unit or period ("Checkout conversion rate, Sept") if the number itself doesn't.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are hairline-pale, there's one flat colour for one series, the days are in natural weekday order, and the source and date are stated. Keep all of that when the takeaway title goes in.
- The brand orange is used only for the wordmark and the data. Because nothing else on the page is coloured, the bars own the lower half. Don't spend that colour on buttons or decoration.
- The type scale is short and every size has a job: big numbers, page title, card titles, labels, axis text. The KPI cards and the chart card share one grid and one gap. That's why the page feels calm.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the nav and any bar tooltips.
- How the three KPI cards reflow on a narrow screen.
