**First read.** The eye goes to the three big black numbers across the top: 3.8, 64.20, 12.4. Then it drops to the orange bar chart, where Saturday's peak and Sunday's drop stand out. It reads as the owner's back-office dashboard for a small bakery shop. It's calm, tidy and well spaced. Then you look back at the numbers and can't tell what any of them is.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: the bakery's owner or operator uses this home screen to see at a glance how the shop is doing and what needs attention.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the chart is honest, but the three headline numbers don't work, and they're the first thing anyone reads. They have no units, no period and no comparison, so the top of the page tells the owner nothing they can act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units, so each one can be read more than one way.** This is in the three cards in the top row. "3.8" next to "Checkout conversion rate" is probably a percentage, but nothing says so. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" next to "Active subscribers" reads as twelve and a bit people. It's almost certainly 12.4k, or something else entirely. The viewer has to rebuild each meaning, and on the subscriber count they may well get it wrong. These are the numbers an owner quotes to a partner or a bank. **Defect.** A chart or figure should never make the reader guess what a number measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2). It also breaks Krug's "don't make me think." Fix: put the unit on the number itself, as in "3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400" or "12.4k". If a figure is abbreviated, say so in the label.
2. **The headline numbers have no period and no comparison, so the owner can't tell good from bad.** In the same three cards, even with units, a bare "3.8%" doesn't say whether conversion is up, down, on target, or for which month. Only the chart's source line mentions September 2026, and it sits at the bottom of a different card. That leaves the owner to remember last month's figures, so the dashboard can't point to what needs attention, which is the point of a home screen. **Defect.** Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (pitfall 2), flags exactly this bare-number pattern. Fix: next to each headline number, add the period ("September") and one comparison, such as the change from last month or progress against a target. A small up or down marker with a signed delta is the usual approach. Don't let colour be the only cue.
3. **The chart title names a topic, not what the chart shows.** "Orders by delivery day" tells you what's plotted, not what it means: orders rise through the week to a Saturday peak around 1,130, and Sunday falls to about a third of that. For a bakery that's the actionable fact, because it affects staffing, baking volume and possibly a Sunday promotion. The owner is left to draw that conclusion, and a screen-reader user gets no summary at all. This is **taste** and common sense, not a cited rule: a dashboard chart earns its space by saying what it shows. Fix: make the title or a one-line caption state the finding in the owner's terms, for example "Saturday is your busiest delivery day; Sunday orders are a third of that."

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- The bar chart is honest and quiet. The axis starts at zero, there's one flat brand colour for one series, the gridlines are pale and thin, the days run in natural weekday order, and there's a dated source line. Keep all of this when you add annotation.
- The grid and spacing are disciplined. The 48 px left margin lines up the logo, the page title and the cards. The gaps between cards are equal. The type has a clear scale, with big bold figures, a medium page title, regular labels and small grey ticks. Don't add sizes when you add units and deltas. Set the deltas in the existing label size.
- Brand orange is used sparingly, only for the wordmark and the data. That's why the chart reads instantly.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the navigation links, and how the three KPI cards reflow at narrow widths and 400% zoom.
