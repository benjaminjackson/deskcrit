**First read.** The eye goes to the three big black numbers across the top, then down to the orange bars, where the tall Friday and Saturday bars and the short Sunday bar stand out. It reads at once as the back office of a small bakery that sells online. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy. Then the first question comes: 3.8 what? 64.20 in what currency? 12.4 subscribers?

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner or operations lead of a small bakery checks this to see how the shop is doing and to plan baking and delivery around the busy days.

**Verdict.** The chart is sound and the page is well built, but the three numbers at the top, which are the biggest thing on the page and the first thing anyone reads, can't be understood on their own. Each one has no unit, no time period and nothing to compare it with, so the viewer can't tell what it means or whether it's good.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline numbers have no unit, no period and no comparison** (top row of three cards). *Defect.* "3.8" could be a percentage or something else. "64.20" has no currency. "Active subscribers 12.4" can only make sense as 12.4 thousand, or possibly as a percentage, and a viewer who reads it as a count of 12 people reaches the opposite conclusion about the business. These are the figures an owner repeats to a partner or an investor, so one that can be read two ways is a defect, not a style note. None of them says what period it covers: the chart's source line says September 2026, but nothing links the cards to it. None shows a change or a target either, so even read correctly, the owner can't tell whether 3.8% is good news. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") flags exactly this bare big number. Fix: put the unit in the number ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400"). State the period once above the row, e.g. "September 2026". Add a small comparison line under each number, such as "▲ 0.4 pts vs August". That line also gives the cards a second level, so they stop being a label and one huge number.

2. **The chart shows a finding but doesn't state it** ("Orders by delivery day"). *Taste / common sense.* The data has a clear story: Saturday is about three times Sunday, and orders climb all week from Monday. The owner would act on that when planning staff, ovens and delivery slots. The title only names the topic, and every bar is the same orange, so the viewer has to find the pattern on their own and nothing on the page says it in words. Fix: make the title the takeaway, written to the reader, e.g. "Saturday is your busiest delivery day: about 3× Sunday". Then use the accent for that one bar (or Friday and Saturday) and a muted tone for the rest. Few's approved direction is one colour for the series and a second colour kept for the point the reader must notice.

**Smaller things.**
- The KPI cards show no trend. Once a comparison is added, a small sparkline under each number would show direction at a glance.
- The page title "Shop overview" is fine for navigation, but it's the only place a date range could go and it carries none. Putting the period next to it fixes the KPI period question for the whole page.

**What's working.**
- **The chart itself is honest and clean.** The bars start at zero, the weekdays run in their natural order, the gridlines are faint, there's one flat colour and a source line with the month. Keep all of that when you add the highlight.
- **The type scale is tight and each size has one job.** There are about five sizes (KPI figure, page title, card and chart titles, nav, axis and source), with big steps between them. Weight separates the levels, not near-duplicate sizes.
- **The layout is calm and sits on one grid.** There are three equal cards on one gutter, the chart panel spans the same width below, and the brand orange appears only in the logo and the data, so it reads as a crafted product and not a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Keyboard focus on the nav links, and whether the cards and chart reflow cleanly on a phone at 320 px. Both matter if the owner checks this on the move.
