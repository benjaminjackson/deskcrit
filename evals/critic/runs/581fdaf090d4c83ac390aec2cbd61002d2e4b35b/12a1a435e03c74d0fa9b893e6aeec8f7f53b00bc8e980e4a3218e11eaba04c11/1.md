**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers (3.8, 64.20, 12.4), then down to the tall Friday and Saturday bars. It reads as the admin dashboard of a small bakery that ships orders, and it looks calm, tidy and well built. The trouble starts on the second read: "3.8 what? 12.4 subscribers?"

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: the shop owner or operations lead opens this to see quickly how the shop is doing and which delivery days are busiest, so they can plan baking and staffing.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean, but the numbers don't do their job. None of the three headline figures says what unit it is in, what period it covers, or whether it is good. The chart's totals also hide an unequal month, so its main pattern is partly wrong. The page looks trustworthy but can't yet be trusted.

**What would move it most**

1. **The three headline numbers have no unit, period or comparison** (KPI row, top). This is a defect.
   - "3.8" is probably a percentage, but nothing says so.
   - "64.20" has no currency.
   - "12.4" next to "Active subscribers" can be read two ways: 12,400 people, or 12.4% of something. You can't have 12.4 subscribers, so the reader has to guess, and the owner could plan around the wrong guess.
   - None of the three says what period it covers. The chart below is September 2026, but nothing ties the cards to that.
   - Nothing says whether 3.8 is good or bad. A bare big number makes the reader remember last month's figure and compare it in their head. That is the classic dashboard failure (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*).
   - Fix: put the unit in the number ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400"). Add the period to the card label or one shared line under "Shop overview". Add one comparison under each number, such as "+0.4 pts vs August" or a small trend line. There is room: each card has about 150 px of empty space to the right of its number.

2. **The chart compares days that occur a different number of times** ("Orders by delivery day"). This is a defect.
   - September 2026 starts on a Tuesday and has 30 days, so it has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of every other weekday.
   - The chart shows monthly totals, so Tuesday and Wednesday each get an extra day's worth of orders.
   - Divided by the number of days, the bars read roughly: Mon 160, Tue 142, Wed 152, Thu 205, Fri 260, Sat 283, Sun 95.
   - So the gentle Monday-to-Thursday climb the chart shows is false. Tuesday and Wednesday are actually the quietest weekdays. The owner would staff midweek wrongly.
   - Fix: plot the average orders per delivery day. Or keep the totals and say how many of each day the month had. Better still, average over several months so one month's calendar doesn't shape the pattern.

3. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding** (chart header). This is my own judgment as a designer, not a published rule.
   - "Orders by delivery day" tells the reader what the chart is, then leaves them to work out what it means.
   - The finding they need is something like "Saturday is your busiest delivery day; Sunday carries about a third as much". Once the numbers are corrected, it should say what the reader can act on.
   - Fix: put the finding in the title, or in one line under it. Move "September 2026" up from the footnote into that line, so the period sits where people read first.

**Smaller things.** None beyond what is folded in above.

**What's working.**
- The grid is strict. The three cards and the chart panel share the same 48 px left edge, the same 24 px gaps and the same outer width, so the page reads as one deliberate system. Keep that discipline when you add units and comparisons to the cards.
- The chart is honest in form. The axis starts at zero, the gridlines are faint, there is one flat colour for one series, the days are in natural order, and there is a source line. Nothing needs stripping out.
- The single burnt orange used for both the brand name and the bars gives the page a warm, bakery feel without decoration, and it doesn't compete with the black numbers.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Keyboard focus styles on the navigation links, and how the three-card row reflows on a narrow screen (cards of this width will need to stack).
