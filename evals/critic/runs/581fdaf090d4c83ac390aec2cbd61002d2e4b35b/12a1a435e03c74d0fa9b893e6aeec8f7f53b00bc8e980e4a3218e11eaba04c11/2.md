**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers along the top: 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4. Then it drops to the orange bars, where the Saturday peak and the Sunday drop stand out. It reads as the owner's overview screen for a small bakery's online shop. It is calm, tidy and well spaced. Then you try to read the numbers and they stop making sense.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: the shop owner or staff check this screen daily or weekly to see how the shop is doing and plan their delivery days.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the chart is well made, but the screen doesn't do its main job. The three biggest things on it are numbers with no unit, no time period and no comparison, so the viewer can't tell what they measure or whether they're good. The fix is in the words, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three KPI cards don't say what their numbers are.** Top row. "3.8" is probably a percentage but doesn't say so. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" under "Active subscribers" is the worst: you can't have 0.4 of a subscriber, so the viewer has to guess it means 12,400, or 12.4 per something, or suspect a bug. Each card makes the reader rebuild a meaning they may get wrong, and these are the numbers people quote in meetings and act on. *Defect.* Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") flags exactly this: a bare headline number. Krug's *Don't Make Me Think* says a page should explain itself, and this one doesn't. Fix: put the unit in the number itself ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400"), or say the scale in the label ("Active subscribers, thousands").
2. **None of the numbers has a time period or a comparison, so nobody can tell good from bad.** Is 3.8% conversion for today, this month, or all time? Is it up or down? The only period on the page is in the chart's source line at the bottom, "September 2026". It is easy to miss, and it may not even apply to the cards. *Defect*, by Few's same rule: give each number a period and a comparison. Fix: one date range for the page, shown next to "Shop overview" (for example "September 2026"). Then a small line under each figure with the change from the last period ("▲ 0.4 pts vs August"), or a target. That small line turns three facts into an answer to "how are we doing?"
3. **The chart title names the topic and leaves the point unsaid.** "Orders by delivery day" describes the axes. The point the chart makes is that orders build through the week to a Saturday peak of about 1,130, then fall to about 380 on Sunday, roughly a third of Saturday. That pattern is what someone planning baking and delivery staff would act on. *Taste* (no approved source states it, but a title that gives the finding saves every reader from working it out). Fix: write the finding as the title, for example "Saturday is the busiest delivery day; Sunday has a third of its orders". Keep the current wording as a small subtitle if you want the topic too.

**What's working.**
- **One accent colour, used with restraint.** The bars are a single orange that matches the logo, and there's no rainbow of colours that mean nothing. The data marks are the strongest thing in the chart, against pale gridlines and a quiet axis. Keep this.
- **The bar chart is honest.** It starts at zero, keeps the days in their natural order and cites its source. Bar heights match the values.
- **Clear structure.** The cards and the chart panel share one grid, one gap size and one corner radius. The big KPI figures against the small grey labels give a clear two-level order. Once the copy problems are fixed, this layout will carry them without other changes.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the top navigation, and how the three-card row reflows on a narrow screen.
