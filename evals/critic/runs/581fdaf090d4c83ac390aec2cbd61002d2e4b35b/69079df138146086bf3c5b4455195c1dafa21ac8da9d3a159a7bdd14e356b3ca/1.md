**First read.** The eye lands on the bold black headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into two paragraphs that look washed out: mid-grey on white, as if they were disabled or still loading. It reads as a plain information page for people about to catch a small river ferry. It feels calm and tidy, but quiet to the point of fading. The line the traveller most needs to act on, "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard…", is the smallest and lightest thing on the page, and the eye reaches it last.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells first-time or occasional passengers when the ferry runs, how bikes work, and what to check before travelling.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but it doesn't yet do its job. Everything below the headline is too pale to read comfortably. And the page hides or leaves out the facts a passenger acts on: the actual sailing times and the winter warning.

**What would move it most**

1. **All the body text reads as faint.** This covers the two paragraphs and the note in the left column. They look like a light grey on white, roughly 2.5:1 by eye, which is at the bottom of the range where this defect turns up. Normal-size text needs 4.5:1 (WCAG 2.2, SC 1.4.3). People will read this on a phone, on a landing, maybe in sunshine or in the rain, and those are the conditions where pale grey disappears. Only the headline has real weight, so the body sits behind the white instead of on it. *Defect.* Fix: darken the text until it clears 4.5:1. A near-black, a step lighter than the headline, would keep the softer tone without the cost. Measure the hex values to confirm.

2. **The warning is the weakest line on the page.** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel" is the one line that can leave someone stranded, and it's set a few pixels smaller than the paragraphs in the same grey. That's not big enough to read as a deliberate level. It just looks like a slightly different paragraph. Dair's point is that a difference meant to be seen must be obvious at a glance in size, weight, colour or some other contrast; a one-step nudge looks like an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). On top of that, the size step demotes the most important practical fact into small print. *Defect* (the hierarchy is backwards for the reader's task) and *convention* (weak contrast). Fix: either set it at body size, or set it apart clearly, for example bold, a strong colour, or moved up under the headline. Don't shrink it.

3. **The copy leaves out what a passenger needs.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first sailing, no last sailing and no departure times, so the reader still can't plan a trip. "Timetables change in winter" doesn't say when winter starts or what changes. And it sends people to a noticeboard at the landing, which they only see once they've already travelled there. These lines carry times, and travellers will hold the operator to them. Vague wording here means a missed last boat. *Defect* (common sense). Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing, or a short timetable. Give the winter dates. If the winter times live elsewhere, link to them instead of only the physical noticeboard.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count three sizes, roughly 40, 20 and 16 px. The two smaller ones sit too close together to work as separate levels. Keep two text sizes clearly apart, or one text size and handle the note with weight or colour.
- The headline "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not the content. Something like "Times, bikes and winter changes" would tell a skimming reader what's here.

**What's working.**
- The single left column is about 75 characters wide, with comfortable line spacing and clear space between paragraphs, so it reads easily once the contrast is fixed. Keep the measure.
- The headline gets its emphasis from weight and colour, not only size, so it dominates without being oversized.
- The copy is short and each paragraph has one topic (schedule, then bikes), which suits people skimming on the move.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a narrow phone screen. That matters here because most readers will probably be on mobile.
