**First read.** The eye lands straight on the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then drops through the paragraph to the blue button. Within two seconds it reads as a ferry service's landing page. It feels clean and calm in a neutral way, like a software product's homepage that happens to be about a boat. On the way down the eye stumbles once, at the three plain centred lines under the paragraph. They don't read as a list or as part of the paragraph.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a ferry operator's homepage hero for locals and visitors deciding whether to take the boat, with the next step being to check departure times.

**Verdict.** The structure works: one clear headline, one clear action, and nothing in the way. What lets it down is what it leaves out. It doesn't say where the boat goes, what it costs, or when the next one leaves. Its look gives no feel of the bay or the open deck it describes, so it could be any company's hero.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page hides the facts a rider decides on (defect, from the copy).** "Cross the bay" never says where to. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no real times, and there's no fare anywhere. A visitor can't tell whether this boat gets them where they're going, today, at a price they'll pay, without clicking "See the timetable". The page already knows boats run every forty minutes, so it could just show the next departure. Point the action at what the viewer actually needs:
   - name both landings ("North Landing → [south side]");
   - give the first and last boat ("06:20 to 22:40");
   - show the fare, or at least "from £X";
   - consider putting the next departure right on the page, e.g. "Next boat 10:40". "See the timetable" can stay as the secondary path.
2. **The look tells you nothing of the bay or the deck (taste).** The words promise calm water, a harbour sliding past, and a picnic on the open deck. The design is white space, Inter-style type, and a stock corporate blue. Everything the piece is selling exists only in the text, so it has no pull on someone choosing a leisure trip and could be any company's landing page. Let one thing carry the place: a photo from the deck, the colour of the water, or a crossing line on a map. Pick one and let it set the mood. The type and layout don't need to change.
3. **The three feature lines don't read as a list (defect).** "Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board" are centred with no markers. They look almost the same size and weight as the paragraph above (roughly 18 vs 20 px by eye), and the gap between them is barely smaller than the gap to the paragraph. So they read as stray extra lines rather than three selling points, and the eye slows there before reaching the button. Either make them clearly a set, such as three short items side by side with small icons, or a different weight or colour, or fold them into the copy. Two of them already repeat the paragraph ("Bring a bicycle", "the open deck"), so cutting the list and adding the fare in its place would do more.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** It is one clear dominant element, and "twelve calm minutes" is a concrete, ownable promise: a number plus a feeling. Keep the line and its scale.
- **One action.** A single filled button with a specific label ("See the timetable") is stronger than a vague "Learn more" or "Get started". Keep it the only high-emphasis element.
- **Restraint.** One neutral range plus one blue, with blue used only for the brand and the action, keeps the path from headline to button clean.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The focus state of the button, which matters because it's the only action on the page.
