**First read.** The eye lands on the tall Organic search bar (1,840), goes up to the bold title "New sign-ups by channel", then runs down the bars to Events. The right-hand notes come last. It reads as an internal Growth dashboard for a marketing team: calm, sober and well made.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you didn't say: an internal report screen that tells a growth or marketing lead where Q3 sign-ups came from and what changes in Q4.

**Verdict.** It works. The chart is honest, clearly labelled and easy to read, and the copy is specific. The one real gap is that the chart and the notes don't talk to each other. The two things the notes say matter (organic's rise since Q2, and podcast and events being cut) can't be seen in the chart, so the reader has to take them on trust.

**What would move it most**
1. **The main claim has nothing to compare against.** The notes on the right say organic search went "up from a quarter in Q2", but the chart only shows Q3. Nothing on the page shows Q2, so the main finding exists only as a sentence. This is a defect: the reader can't check the claim and can't see whether the other channels went up or down. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2) asks for the earlier period to sit next to each key number. Direction of the fix: put the Q2 value next to each bar, for example a thin gray tick or a pale ghost bar, or show the change as "+X vs Q2" under each value label.
2. **Every bar is the same blue, so the chart doesn't point at what the notes are about.** The notes single out Organic search and the Podcast ads and Events pair, but all six bars look the same. The reader has to match the prose to the bars on their own. This is taste, and it follows Few's approved direction: keep one series in one color, and save a second color for the point the reader must notice. Direction of the fix: draw the bars the notes discuss in a stronger colour and the rest in gray, or label them right on the chart ("a third of all sign-ups", "budget moves to referrals"). Then the notes and the chart make one argument, not two.

**Smaller things**
- **The y-axis is redundant.** The y-axis labels (0 to 2,000) and their gridlines repeat values already printed above every bar. This is taste: remove the axis and gridlines, or fade them further, so the bars and labels carry the chart alone.
- **"Up from a quarter in Q2" reads awkwardly.** "Quarter" means both 25% and a three-month period, so the sentence makes the reader stop. Write "up from 25% in Q2".
- **The Q4 decision is the last sentence on the page.** "The events budget moves to referrals in Q4" is what the reader will act on, but it sits at the end. It could be the heading of its own note, or open the paragraph.

**What's working**
- **The chart is honest and labelled directly.** The bars start at zero and are sorted by value, every bar has its value printed above it, and there is no legend to decode. The gridlines are pale enough that the data is the darkest ink.
- **The copy holds together.** It is specific and checks out. The source line gives the date range and the total (5,400). The bars add up to that total, 1,840 is in fact about a third of it, and 410 + 260 is in fact under 700. The subtitle says exactly what a sign-up means ("by the channel of the first visit").
- **The type is quiet and readable.** Body text is 15 px (`font_size`, `aside > p`) at contrast 13.13:1, with lines of about 41–43 characters and line height 1.55. The muted gray for secondary text still measures 6.11:1. There are two clean left edges (x = 48 and x = 876), and the top of the notes lines up with the 2,000 gridline.
