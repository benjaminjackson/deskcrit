**First read.** The eye lands on "Booking links that fill your calendar", drops to the blue "Create your link" button, then jumps to the blue "New" pill on the first card. That pill is the only other saturated blue on the page, so it pulls like a second button. Next, card 1 stands out as broken: its title wraps and everything under it sits lower than in the other three. The page reads as a scheduling tool for small businesses. It feels clean and competent, and it could belong to any scheduling product.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a first-screen landing page that gets small service businesses (the clinics, tutors and coaches in the bottom row) to create a booking link.

**Verdict.** It works: the offer is clear and the main action is obvious. What holds it back is that it never shows the product or says who it is for, so it reads as one more generic booking tool. The broken first card is the most visible craft flaw.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page tells but never shows (taste, plus a copy defect).** The product is a booking page a client sees, yet nowhere on the screen can the viewer see one. The right half of the hero, from about x 700 to 1216, is empty white. "See a live example" is the only proof, and it is a text link. The headline adds to this: "fill your calendar" promises more bookings, which a link cannot create. A stranger can read it either as "more clients" or as "your calendar fills itself". Both readings are generic, and the first makes a claim the product can't keep. Fix: put the client's view of a booking page in the empty right half, so viewers judge it against the booking process they use now (phone calls, back-and-forth emails). Change the headline so it names what the viewer stops doing, for example the back-and-forth over email.
2. **Card 1 breaks the row (defect).** The "New" pill sits at the card's right edge instead of straight after the title, so "Shared calendars" wraps to two lines. Card 1's text and "Learn more" sit 30 px lower than cards 2–4 (body at y 580 vs 550 and link at 662 vs 632, from the `box` fields of `div.card > p` and `span.link`). That leaves a dead band of about 55 px at the bottom of the other three cards. The row reads as broken, and card 1 looks like a different component. Fix: give the title room to stay on one line (inline pill as in the other cards, or a pill above the title), so the four cards line up across the row.
3. **The customers have no label (defect).** "Harbor Dental … Orchard Vets" is 14 px grey text (`div.logos > span`, `#59636e`) with no heading and no logo marks, sitting under a thin rule. It reads as footer links. This row is the strongest clue about who the product is for, and right now viewers miss it. Fix: give it a label that says these are customers ("Used by…"), and use their real logos or a short quote if you have them.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count 8 sizes (`type_sizes`: 46, 20, 19, 17, 16, 15, 14, 12). The card titles at 20 and the hero subhead at 19 are effectively the same size, and 17/16/15/14 run together. Four levels would carry this page, for example 46 / 20 / 15 / 12, with weight and color doing the finer work (Refactoring UI; Bringhurst).
- Badges: "Auto" and "Email" describe features, while "New" and "Beta" are statuses, yet all four share one pill style. Feature facts belong in the text; keep pills for status only. "New" is in the button blue (`#1f5fe0`), so it competes with the CTA; give it a quieter treatment.
- Two primary buttons: "Start free" in the header and "Create your link" in the hero are both filled blue with different labels. If they do the same thing, use one label. "Start free" can also mean a free plan or a free trial; say which, because people hold you to anything you say about price.

**What's working.**
- The subhead (19 px, 65 characters per line, contrast 10.22) is concrete and spoken: who does what, in order. Keep that voice.
- The card copy ends on what each feature does for the viewer ("so nobody has to do the math", "cut down on no-shows"). That is the right way to write these.
- "See a live example" is the right second ask: it lets viewers judge for themselves instead of taking the claim on trust. It deserves more presence than an underlined link (see finding 1).
