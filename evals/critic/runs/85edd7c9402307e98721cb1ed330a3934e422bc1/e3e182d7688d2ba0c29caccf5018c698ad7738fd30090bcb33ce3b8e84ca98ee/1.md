**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Booking links that fill your calendar", drops to the blue "Create your link" button, then moves across four feature cards. The next thing you notice is the big empty block at the top right, which is where the product should be. It reads as a scheduling SaaS for small service businesses. It looks clean and calm, and could belong to any startup in this space.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a marketing homepage hero that gets small practices (dentists, tutors, physios) to create a booking link.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and readable, and it doesn't sell. The page never shows what a guest or owner actually sees, and the headline promises something every competitor promises, so a visitor has no reason to choose this over what they already use.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero has no product, and its right half is empty.** About 600×330 px at the top right is blank, so all the weight sits on the left and the page looks unfinished, or like an image failed to load. More importantly, the viewer has to take "pick a time that works" on trust. They never see the booking page their patients or clients would get. *Defect.* If an image is meant to be there and didn't load, check it live. Either way the fix is the same: put the guest's booking view there (a week of open slots, in a real-looking practice's name), so the picture shows the subhead's promise and fills the space. The "See a live example" link is the right instinct, but it hides behind a click the evidence that should be on the page.
2. **The headline could sit on any booking tool's page, and it over-claims.** A link doesn't fill a calendar. Demand does. So "fill your calendar" is either a vague outcome or a promise the product can't keep, and nothing in it names who this is for or what it replaces. The subhead is better because it says what happens. *Defect (copy).* Direction: set the headline against what the viewer does today, for example ending the back-and-forth of phone calls and emails over appointment times, or the no-shows the Reminders card already mentions. Then the viewer judges the value on their own terms instead of grading a claim.
3. **The customer strip doesn't work as proof.** Five pale grey names with no label sit under a rule, so they read as footer links or a second nav, not as "practices that use this". Even read correctly, plain-text names of small businesses no one has heard of carry little weight. *Defect.* Label it, and add the one thing that makes it credible to a peer, such as a short quote or a number from one of them ("Kestrel Physio cut no-shows by…"). Only use a number they actually have.

**Smaller things.**
- Card row: the "New" badge squeezes "Shared calendars" onto two lines, so that card's body text and "Learn more" sit about 30 px below the other three and the row's baselines break. Put the badge after the title or below it, as the other cards do, so all four titles stay on one line.
- Badges: "New" is solid blue and right-aligned, while the others are grey and inline. "Auto" and "Email" don't mark a status at all. Keep badges for status (New, Beta), give them one style, and drop the other two. Also, the solid blue badge competes with the primary button.
- Two solid blue buttons, "Start free" and "Create your link", probably do the same thing under different names. Use one label for both, or make the nav button secondary.
- Four "Learn more" links can't be told apart out of context (WCAG 2.4.4) and repeat the same words four times. Name each one after its feature, or make the whole card the link.
- Icons mix an outline calendar with solid clock, envelope and layout glyphs, and the intake-form icon reads as a page layout. Use one style and an icon that actually shows a form.
- The headline breaks between "your" and "calendar". It's tolerable, but a break at a phrase boundary would read more cleanly.

**What's working.**
- The type hierarchy is clear: a heavy, tightly set headline, a calm subhead, and card titles bold enough to scan. Keep this scale.
- The card body copy says what each feature does and why it matters to the viewer ("so nobody has to do the math", "so the first minutes are useful"). That's the voice the headline should have.
- The palette is restrained, one blue on greys, so the primary action is never in doubt.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty hero area should hold an image that didn't load. Check in a live browser.
- Hover and focus states of the buttons and links.
