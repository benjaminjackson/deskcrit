**First read.** My eye goes straight to the big white headline, "See where every dollar goes this month". From there it drops to the subhead, then to the two buttons, and stops. After that it drifts into the empty right half of the blue band, finds nothing, and falls to the three feature columns. This reads as the landing page for a personal budgeting app. It feels clean, confident and very familiar: a saturated-blue fintech hero that could belong to a dozen products.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the marketing home page for a budgeting app. Its job is to get people who want to control their monthly spending to start a free trial, and to trust it with their bank accounts.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The words are clear, the order of importance is clean and the type is well set. What holds it back is that the page promises "see where every dollar goes" and then shows nothing: half the hero is blank blue where the product should be. A visitor has to take the core claim on trust, and money apps can least afford that.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero tells but never shows, and its right half is dead space.** Everything in the blue band sits in the left 60%. The right ~500 px is an unbroken field of blue. *Defect:* the eye finishes reading the buttons and runs into empty colour with nowhere to go. The headline's promise ("see where every dollar goes") has no evidence beside it, so the visitor can't judge the product. The band also tips to the left: all the weight is on one side and nothing balances it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). This isn't open space gathered on purpose; it's left over, and an empty area with no reason is a fault (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*, "Contrasts"). **Fix:** put the product on the right, at a size that holds its own against the headline. That means one real screen: a month's spending by category, with one category close to its limit and the "heads-up" alert showing. Then the picture proves the headline instead of repeating it. If an image was meant to be there and failed to load, check it in a live browser; a correctly loaded hero image would settle this.
2. **"Try it free" is a generic ask.** *Defect (copy):* it could sit on any SaaS page. It doesn't say what the visitor gets in the first minute, and it asks them to trust the claim rather than check it. The subhead already names something concrete: connecting accounts and seeing your categories. **Fix:** make the button name that first result, along the lines of "See last month's spending", so the visitor holds the app against something they already know (their own bank statement) and judges it on their own terms. Say "free" next to the button, not as the button.
3. **Nothing near the ask earns the trust that connecting a bank account needs.** *Taste, argued:* the strongest reassurance on the page is "can never move money". Right now it's the third of three equal columns below the fold line of the hero, set in grey 15 px body text. This is the visitor's biggest worry at the moment they decide to click. **Fix:** put a one-line version of the read-only promise right under the buttons, inside the hero, and keep the column as the longer explanation.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** It's specific, in the reader's own terms ("every dollar", "this month"), and big enough at ~56 px bold to clearly lead. Keep the wording and the scale.
- **The subhead.** It does exactly the job a subhead should: three verbs (connect, set, get a heads-up) that say who does what and when, in about 70 characters per line.
- **The buttons.** A filled white primary and an outlined secondary make the ranking unmistakable, and everything sits on one 64 px left edge from the logo down to the feature columns.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** About 220 px of blank white sits below the feature columns. It may be where the page ends, part of the capture, or a section that hadn't loaded yet. Check in a live browser whether that space is intentional.
