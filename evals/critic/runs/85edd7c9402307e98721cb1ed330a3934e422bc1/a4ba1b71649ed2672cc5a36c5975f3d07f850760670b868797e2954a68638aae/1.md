**First read.** The serif headline lands first, then the eye jumps to the tallest bar (Q3 2026, 705) and drops along the row of numbers above the bars. It reads as a slide from a board deck, and it looks calm and competent. What pulls at the eye is the chunky bevelled bars: they look like an office chart preset, and they make the page look cheaper than its type.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is one page of a board sales review. It shows the board the seasonal shape and recent course of shipments, with one quarter forecast, so they can discuss the plan.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound. There's a headline that states a finding, a zero baseline, labelled values, units and a source. But the chart shows the forecast as if it were an actual result, and the headline leaves out the most important thing the data shows. Fix those two things and remove the 3D, and this is a good slide.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast looks like a result** *(defect)*. In the right-hand bar, Q4 2026 (560) is drawn in the same solid blue as the seven actual quarters. The only sign that it's a guess is a parenthesis in the subtitle and a clause in the source line. A board member scanning the chart will take 560 as booked. If it's missed, they'll hold the team to a number that was never achieved, and that number shapes what they remember about the year. Projections should look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Mark it on the bar itself: use a pale or outlined fill, or hatching, and put "Forecast" on the bar or its axis label. If the forecast has a range, show the range.
2. **The headline states the less important finding** *(taste, copy)*. "Shipments peak each third quarter" is true, but the board probably knows the business is seasonal. What it may not have seen is that every 2026 quarter beats the same quarter of 2025: 455 vs 410, 590 vs 520, 705 vs 610, and a forecast 560 vs 480. That's growth of roughly 11 to 17% year on year. The headline stops at how the data behaves and doesn't say what it means for the viewer. I'd lead with the growth and let the seasonality explain the shape, for example "Every quarter of 2026 is ahead of 2025; Q3 peaked at 705k." The layout supports this too: put the two years next to each other quarter by quarter (paired bars, or the same quarters lined up), so the comparison can be seen and not just worked out from the numbers.
3. **3D bars** *(convention, and it costs something here)*. Each bar has a side face and a top face, which adds ink but no data. It also leaves the value edge unclear: is the value at the front edge or the back edge of the top face? Here it sits at the front, but the viewer has to work that out. On a board slide it also makes the page look like a default template. Draw the bars flat, so each value is read from one length (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, 26.1; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 7). With values printed on every bar, you could also drop the gridlines and the y-axis, or keep them very faint.

**Smaller things.**
- **Too many type sizes, too close together.** I count about six small sizes: subtitle (~16 px), chart title (~15 px bold), the units line and value labels (~14 px), axis labels (~13 px), footer (~12 px). Sizes this close read as accidents, not as levels. Settle on two small sizes and separate the rest by weight and grey (Bringhurst; Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*).
- **Empty band at the bottom.** About 120 px is left empty under the footer, so the page looks unfinished. Either pin the footer to the bottom edge or give the chart that height.
- **Subtitle says "Q4 2026" twice.** The parenthesis "(Q4 2026 is the forecast)" repeats the end of the sentence. Once the forecast is marked on the bar, cut the parenthesis.

**What's working.**
- **The page structure.** Headline, subtitle, chart title with "Thousands of units", the chart, then the source line with forecast provenance. A director can audit every number, so keep this order.
- **Restrained palette.** One blue for the data and navy for the brand mark, with everything else grey. That leaves room to give the forecast a different treatment with no added noise.
- **Honest scale and direct labels.** The bars start at zero, the quarters run in time order, and values sit on the bars, so nobody has to read values off the grid.
