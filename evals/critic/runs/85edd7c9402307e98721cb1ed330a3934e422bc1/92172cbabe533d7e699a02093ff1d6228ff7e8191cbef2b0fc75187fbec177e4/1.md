**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is an internal growth dashboard page. Its job is to show a team how Q3 sign-ups split by channel and why that justifies moving the events budget to referrals in Q4.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The chart is honest and easy to read, and the sums check out (they add to 5,400; organic is 34%; podcast plus events is 670). But the chart and the argument are disconnected. The colour points at nothing, and the two claims the page makes (organic is up from Q2, and events is moving to referrals) don't appear on the chart at all.

**What would move it most**
1. **Six colours that mean nothing, while the bars that matter get no emphasis** (defect). Every bar is already labelled on the x-axis, so the blue, orange, red, teal, green and purple carry no information. They make the viewer look for a legend, and they make the bars compete with each other. The red on Paid social reads as a warning and the green on Podcast ads reads as good news, which is the opposite of what the note says about podcasts. Meanwhile the three bars the argument is about (Organic search, Events, and Referral, where the budget is going) look no different from the rest. The fix: draw all six bars in one neutral colour and give one accent colour to the bars the note talks about (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 19.1).
2. **The title names the topic, and the chart can't show the main claim** (taste/common sense; the source for this rule isn't on the approved list). "New sign-ups by channel" only says what the chart is. The finding sits in a side column the eye reaches last. The page's main claim is "up from a quarter in Q2", but no Q2 figure appears anywhere on the chart, so the viewer has to take it on trust. The fix: make the title the finding ("Organic search brought a third of Q3 sign-ups"), and show the Q2 comparison on the chart, for example as a pale Q2 marker or bar next to each Q3 bar, or at least a "Q2: 25%" note on the Organic bar. Then the evidence for moving the budget is on the chart itself, not only in prose beside it (Few: put a comparison next to the figure).

**Smaller things.**
- The y-axis and gridlines repeat the value labels on top of each bar. Drop the axis and keep the labels, or the other way round (taste).
- The heading "What changed this quarter" also covers a Q4 budget decision, which isn't a change this quarter. Something like "What changed, and what we're doing" names what the column actually holds.
- The note heading (y ≈ 174 px) lines up with neither the subtitle nor the top of the chart (≈ 195 px), so the column looks dropped in rather than placed. Align it to the top gridline or to the subtitle.
- I count about four small type sizes that are close together (note about 15, x-labels about 14, source about 13, y-labels about 12 px). Merge them into two, and use grey for the secondary ones instead of shrinking them further. These are guesses by eye; measure them to confirm.

**What's working.**
- The chart itself is honest and readable: the bars start at zero, they're sorted by value, every bar has its exact figure on top, and the gridlines are faint. Keep all of this when you change the colours.
- The subtitle and source line define the data precisely: "by the channel of the first visit", 1 July to 30 September, 5,400 in total. A viewer can trust what's being counted.
- The note's copy is specific and ends in a decision ("The events budget moves to referrals in Q4") rather than vague commentary.
