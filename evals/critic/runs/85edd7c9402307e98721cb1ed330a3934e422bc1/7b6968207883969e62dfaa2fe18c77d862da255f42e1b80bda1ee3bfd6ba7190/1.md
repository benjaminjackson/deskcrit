**First read.** My eye lands on the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month". It then drops straight to the tall June bar, which is the loudest shape on the page. On the way it gets snagged by the three big bold numbers on the right, which are almost as large as the headline. It reads as a calm, trustworthy member report from a credit union. The striped bars make it busier than it needs to be.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in the credit union's member report that shows members how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026.

**Verdict.** Most of the craft is sound. The axis starts at zero, labels sit on the bars, every big number has a comparison, and the source is given. The biggest problem is the story the chart tells. It shows deposits nearly doubling, January to June, and the headline backs that up. But the true year-on-year growth is 6.1%, and the June spike is mostly a one-off bonus payroll that only the footnote mentions. A member will leave with the wrong conclusion.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart and headline suggest growth that the page's own numbers don't support (defect).**
   - The bars climb from $612k to $1150k. The headline says "rose every month". So the reader concludes deposits are booming.
   - The first card says the half-year total is up only 6.1% on 2025. The 13 px footnote says June "includes the mid-year bonus payroll".
   - The chart shows only these six months and no 2025 bars. So nobody can tell whether a January-to-June climb is growth or just what happens every year. The biggest jump on the chart, May to June, is the one that is mostly a one-off.
   - A chart that stops exactly where the claim looks strongest gives too little context to judge the trend. Cairo makes this point in *How Charts Lie*. He isn't on my approved list, so take it as common sense: the members reading this are being asked to trust a financial institution's numbers.
   - Fix:
     - Put January–June 2025 beside each month as grey bars, so the real gap shows on the chart.
     - Move the bonus-payroll note onto the June bar as a label.
     - Rewrite the headline around what actually changed for members. For example, the 6.1% rise on last year, said as something they did ("You saved 6.1% more than in the first half of 2025").

2. **The striped fill on the bars is decoration and should go (convention).**
   - Every bar is filled with diagonal blue-and-white stripes. Across six wide bars this shimmers, and it's the busiest texture on the page. It shows no data, because there is only one series.
   - Patterned fills in charts are chartjunk (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 11).
   - Fix: draw the bars in plain flat blue. If you add the 2025 bars from point 1, a flat grey for 2025 next to a flat blue for 2026 separates the years without any pattern.

3. **The headline and the three big numbers are nearly the same size, so nothing clearly leads (convention).**
   - The h1 is 32 px (`font_size`, `h1`). The three card figures are 30 px bold (`div.num`). That's four items 2 px apart, and the bold sans figures actually look heavier than the serif headline.
   - The page uses seven type sizes in all (`type_sizes`: 32, 30, 22, 16, 15, 14, 13), and 16, 15, 14 and 13 sit only a pixel apart.
   - A difference that small reads as an accident, not a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8; Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1).
   - Fix:
     - Decide whether the headline or the totals lead, and open a real gap between them. For example, drop the card figures to about 24 px, or enlarge the headline.
     - Fold the small sizes into two: one for text, one for labels and notes.

**Smaller things.**
- The value formats clash: the bars say "$1150k" and the axis "$1200k", while the card says "$4.82M". Use $1.15M / $1.2M, or put a thousands comma in the k values.
- "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025": a US reader may take "on" as a date. "vs June 2025" or "from June 2025" reads as one meaning only. This is common sense rather than a rule.

**What's working.**
- Every card has the same three lines: a plain label with its period, the figure, and a comparison worded as "Up…". Keep that pattern. It's why every big number can be trusted at a glance.
- The chart is honest in its mechanics: the axis starts at zero, values are labelled on the bars so no legend is needed, the gridlines are pale, and the unit is in the chart title.
- The single green accent is kept to the comparison lines, so colour means one thing on the page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the nav items are at least 24 px tall as click targets. The text boxes measure 23 px high, and the padding wasn't measured.

**Checklist.**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 8 yes
- What matters first: 3 lines, 1 yes, 2 no (one element dominates; distinct type levels)
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 13 yes
- Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
- Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
- Pictures: skipped, no pictures apart from the chart
- Charts and data: 16 lines, 13 yes, 3 no (data-ink / no chartjunk; enough time range; title states the real takeaway)
- Interface: navigation only; current page shown, yes; target size unsure, not measured; all other lines don't apply
