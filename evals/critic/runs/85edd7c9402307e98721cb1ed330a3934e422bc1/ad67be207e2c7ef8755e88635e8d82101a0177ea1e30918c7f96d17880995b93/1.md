**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board" right away, takes in the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it, then drops into three short paragraphs. It reads as a help page from a small river ferry service. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, close to a printed notice. Nothing pulls against the heading, but once you're in the text nothing stands out either.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an info page for ferry passengers, meant to tell them when boats run, how long a crossing takes and how bikes work, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The typography and layout are sound and need little work. The page falls short on its job because the words never give the facts a passenger plans around. No departure times, no first or last boat, no dates for winter. And the one pointer to the real timetable sends people to a shelter they haven't reached yet.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule is too vague to plan by (defect: copy).** The first paragraph says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Someone deciding whether to leave now can't tell if "early morning" means 6:00 or 8:00, or if "late evening" means 20:00 or 23:00. The time that matters most is the last boat, because missing it strands you. The third paragraph says "in winter" without giving dates, so a reader in late October or March can't tell which timetable applies. This is the kind of line people act on and later hold the operator to, so a line that can be read two ways is a real failure. To fix it, give the first and last departure from each landing as clock times. Give the winter period as dates, plus its own first and last boats.
2. **The real timetable is placed where the reader can't use it (defect: copy).** "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" tells someone reading this before the trip to go to the landing to find out when to go to the landing. That last line should give them the timetable here, either as a short table of departures under the prose or a link to one. If the shelter copy matters, keep it as a backup.
3. **The facts are buried in prose (taste).** Every fact a passenger needs (how often boats run, crossing time, bikes free, boarding order, the winter service) sits mid-sentence at the same weight. A passenger checking this on the way to the boat has to read all three paragraphs to pull out four facts. I'd give the frequency and the last boat their own line, or lead each paragraph with its fact ("Bikes travel free."), so a quick scan answers the question. Leave the calm prose voice as it is; just bring the facts to the front.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run about 80 to 85 characters (for example "The river ferry runs … every forty minutes"), a little past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. Narrow the column by roughly 10% to bring them back inside the range.

**What's working.**
- The type plan is clean: one sans-serif family in three steps (a small tracked-caps grey label, a large bold heading, regular body text). Each step is clearly different from the next, so the order of reading is never in doubt. Keep that restraint when you add the times.
- "Before you board" is a good heading. It speaks to the reader's own moment and says what the page holds.
- The spacing groups correctly: the label sits tight to the heading, the heading sits clear of the body, and the paragraphs have even, generous gaps. It looks considered, not templated.
