**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board", catches the grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it, then reads down three short paragraphs. It's clearly a ferry service's passenger info page. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, a little like a well-set notice board. Nothing on it fights for attention.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and how bicycles work, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and doesn't get in the way. What lets the page down is the words. Someone planning a trip can't get the one thing they came for, which is when the boat actually leaves, and the facts they do get are buried in running prose.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times can't be acted on (defect, copy).** First paragraph: "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Third paragraph: "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" and "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing." A passenger can't tell whether "early morning" means 6:00 or 8:00, or whether the last boat is 21:00 or 23:00. The second reading is the one that leaves them stranded on the wrong bank. "In winter" doesn't say which dates. The only place to get the full timetable is a paper copy at the landing, so someone reading this at home has to go there to find out when to go there. Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing, the exact dates of the winter timetable, and the timetable itself on this page or a link to it. Times, dates and frequency are exactly what passengers will hold the operator to, so they have to be stated exactly.
2. **The key facts are hidden in prose (taste, with Krug behind it).** A passenger scans this page for four facts: how often, how long, bikes free, winter hourly. All four sit in mid-sentence in grey running text with nothing to make them stand out, so the reader has to read every line to find them. Krug's *Don't Make Me Think* argues for pages you can scan. Fix: put these facts into a short list of label-and-value pairs, or start each paragraph with the fact in bold ("Every 40 minutes, 7:00–22:00."). That turns a page you have to read into one you can glance at.
3. **The lines are too long (convention).** The body paragraphs run 82.5, 83 and 87 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `main.article > p.body` 2–4) in a 760 px column. Bringhurst's comfortable range is 45 to 75. With paragraphs only two or three lines long, the cost is mild, but at 18 px in a column with nothing beside it the eye does have to travel to find the next line. Fix: narrow the text column to about 620–660 px, which gives roughly 65–70 characters at 18 px. The empty right side shows there's room to spare.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clear: 36 / 18 / 14 px (`type_sizes`), and each size has an obvious job. The eyebrow gets its difference from capitals, letterspacing and grey (#59636e, 6.11:1), not from yet another size. Keep it that tight.
- Every block hangs from one left edge (`left_edges`: all 5 at x=120), and the spacing groups things correctly. The eyebrow sits 12 px above the headline and the body starts 24 px below it, so label, title and text read as one unit.
- Body text is easy to read: 18 px, 1.5 line height, 15.8:1 contrast.
