**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers first, with 3.8% and €64.20 pulling hardest. Then it drops to the orange bar block, sees Friday and Saturday peak, sees the Sunday drop, and stops. It reads at once as the overview screen of a small bakery's online shop. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, and also a bit stock: apart from the orange wordmark and bars, it could be any shop's admin page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's daily glance screen. It should show whether the shop is doing better or worse, and which delivery days carry the load, so they can plan baking and staff.

**Verdict.** The layout and the KPI tiles are solid. The chart, the biggest thing on the page, gives the wrong answer to its own question. September 2026 has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but four of every other weekday, so plotting raw monthly totals inflates those two bars. The real day-to-day pattern is hidden behind what looks like a gentle rise through the week. Fix that before anything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bars compare different numbers of days. (Defect.)** September 2026 starts on a Tuesday, so the Tuesday and Wednesday bars each add up five delivery days and the other five bars add up four. That's a 25% head start drawn into bar length, against Tufte's lie factor (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2). The owner sees a steady climb from Monday (~640) to Thursday (~820). Divide by the number of days and the order changes: Monday ~160 orders a day, Tuesday ~142, Wednesday ~152, Thursday ~205. Tuesday and Wednesday are actually the quietest weekdays, and the owner would plan around a pattern that isn't real. Plot average orders per delivery day, or orders per day over a whole number of weeks, and say which one in the caption.
2. **The chart names its topic, not its finding, and has nothing to compare against. (Taste and common sense: the sources for these two rules are off the approved list.)** Each tile above gives a change against the last period. The chart is the largest and most colourful element on the page, yet it gives one month with no reference. The owner can't tell whether the Saturday peak or the Sunday dip is new, normal or getting worse. "Orders by delivery day" makes them work the point out alone. Put the takeaway in the title once the data is per-day, such as "Saturday is your busiest day; Sunday runs at a third of it". Show the previous period as a pale grey bar or tick beside each orange bar, so the chart works like the tiles.
3. **The tiles and the chart don't say they cover the same period. (Defect in the copy.)** Two tiles say "Last 30 days", Active subscribers says "As of 30 September 2026", and the chart caption says "orders delivered in September 2026". A stranger can't tell whether "last 30 days" means September, whether the conversion rate and the chart come from the same orders, or why one tile compares to "previous 30 days" and another to "31 August". Pick one window for the whole page, name it once under "Shop overview" (for example "September 2026, compared with August"), and make every delivery line use the same comparison.

**Smaller things.**
- **Type scale:** I count eight sizes: 48, 30, 26, 19, 17, 15, 14 and 12 px (`type_sizes`). Several pairs are too close to read as separate levels: 15 px tile names vs 14 px tile lines, 19 px brand vs 17 px chart heading, and 26 px H1 vs the 30 px unit glyphs. Cut to four or five sizes and let weight and grey do the finer distinctions (Bringhurst; Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*).
- **Source line contrast:** the source line under the chart is 12 px grey #737b84 at 4.29:1 (`contrast`, `p.src`), below the 4.5:1 minimum in WCAG 1.4.3. Darken it to the #5d6670 used for the tile sub-lines, which measures 5.83:1.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI tiles.** Each one has a name, a big number, a period and a coloured delta with ▲ and a sign, so it reads correctly without colour (Few's point about giving metrics context). Setting €, % and k smaller and grey keeps the figure itself dominant. Keep all of this.
- **The chart's restraint.** Bars start at zero, gridlines are faint, there's one colour for one series, days are in calendar order, and there's no legend. Almost all the ink is data, which is why fixing the numbers alone will make this chart good.
- **Alignment.** The tile contents and the chart heading share one left edge (x = 74), and the tiles and chart card share outer margins. The page reads as one ordered sheet.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Nav items.** The facts file shows the nav items as `span`s with no measured targets. Check in a browser that Orders, Subscribers and Products are real links, at least 24 px tall.
- **Tiles.** Check whether the tiles click through to the Orders and Subscribers pages. Nothing on screen suggests they do.
