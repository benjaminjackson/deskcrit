**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, then to the big blue 38% slice. Then it stalls on the fan of thin slivers at the top left, with their tiny labels. To find out what any slice is, it has to cross the page to the legend and match colours. It reads as a tidy, calm annual-report page that shows the costs but makes no point about them.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a page in an annual report that shows shareholders and staff where the operating money went and what that means for the business.

**Verdict.** It's competent and clean, but it's a lookup table dressed as a chart. Fourteen slices in a pie with a separate legend make the reader decode colours to learn one thing (salaries are the biggest cost), and the headline never says what that means.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pie has 14 slices, and 9 of them are 4% or less.** The reader can see the big blue slice and nothing else. From Travel at 4% to Postage at 1%, the slivers can't be told apart or ranked by eye, so the page hands every comparison to the numbers. Each of the 14 numbers appears twice: once on the pie and once in the legend's % column. And because every colour is decoded through the legend on the right, the eye shuttles back and forth on every slice. With 14 hues some will collide. Red Travel sits right next to green Insurance, and several slivers at the top are close in lightness, so a colour-blind reader can't match them at all. **Defect.** Pies are for about five slices; past that, the approved fix is to group the slivers into one "Other" slice or switch to a sorted bar chart (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*). Here, use a horizontal bar chart sorted by value, with the category name at each bar and the share at its end. That removes the legend, the duplicate numbers and the 14-colour palette in one move. Draw the bars in one colour, and keep a second colour only for the bar you want noticed (Few). If the pie must stay, keep the four or five largest categories and fold the rest into "Other, 18%".
2. **The headline names the topic, not the finding.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" tells the reader what the chart is, which the subtitle already says. It doesn't tell them what to take away. This is an annual report, so the reader wants to know whether costs are sensible, rising, or under control. There is also no comparison with fiscal 2025, so even a careful reader can't tell whether 38% on salaries or 12% on cloud hosting is good, bad or new. **Taste**, argued on common sense: a report page that only describes leaves every reader to draw their own conclusion, and some will draw the wrong one. Make the headline say the finding, for example: "People, premises and cloud took two-thirds of operating costs". The exact sum is 66%; check it against the unrounded figures. Then add last year's share next to each category (a second marker or a change column) so the reader can see the movement for themselves (Few, on giving metrics a comparison).

**Smaller things.**
- The outside pie labels at the top left (2%, 2%, 2%, 1%) look about 11 px and are crammed onto very short leader lines. They are hard to read and hard to tie to their slices. This goes away with the bar-chart fix; if the pie stays, group those slices into "Other".
- Nothing tells the reader how much money is involved. Shares alone hide whether 1% is a rounding error or a large sum. Put the total operating cost in the subtitle.

**What's working.**
- **The type system.** A bold serif headline over a serif subtitle, sans-serif data text, and a small letterspaced brown kicker give three clear levels with no near-duplicate sizes. Keep this when you rebuild the chart.
- **The legend is already a good table.** It's sorted by value, the numbers are right-aligned in bold, and the rules are hairline. It's most of the way to a bar chart already: add bars to it.
- **The page frame is honest and well kept.** The running header shows where you are in the report, and the source line states the rounding. The shares add up to exactly 100%, so the rounding note holds.
