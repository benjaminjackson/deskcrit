**First read.** The eye lands on "Changing your pickup time" at top left, then steps down four bold headings of the same size, like a list. It reads as the help or FAQ page for a bakery's order-and-collect service. It feels calm and plain, maybe too plain: nothing above the headings tells you whose page this is or that you're on the help page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help page for customers who have ordered bread online and need quick answers about collecting, changing or cancelling the order.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and easy to read. What lets it down is the words: the two answers people will act on, cancelling and collecting for someone else, can each be read two ways. The allergy answer only helps someone already standing in the shop. On a page this simple, the copy is the design, and that's where the work is.

**What would move it most**

1. **The answers about deadlines and handover can be read two ways (defect).** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead": a day ahead of what? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup time or any time the day before. Someone who reads it the generous way and cancels the evening before an 8 am pickup will feel cheated when the bakery says no. In "Collecting for someone else", "along with a name" doesn't say whose name, theirs or the one on the order. The friend gets to the counter not knowing what to say. "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact and never says what happens if you're later (does the order go back on the shelf, and do you lose your money?). That's the line a late customer will hold the bakery to. Fix: give each line an exact deadline, a named person and a consequence, said to the reader. For example, "Cancel by [time] the day before your pickup", "They'll need your order number and the name on the order", "If you can't come within two hours, [what happens]". "Open your order" has the same gap: it doesn't say where. Name the place (the confirmation email, or the account page) or link to it.

2. **The allergy answer only works in the shop (defect).** "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps someone who's already at the counter. The person reading this page is usually choosing online, and their real question is whether they can safely order. As written, they can't find out before they pay. Fix: say where to see ingredients before ordering (on each product page, or a list linked here), or how to ask the baker before ordering. Keep the in-shop card as the second sentence.

3. **There's no page title, so the first question acts as the headline (defect, mild).** All four headings are 30 px bold (`font_size`, `font_weight`, `main.help > h2`), and nothing sits above them. Someone arriving from a search or a link sees "Changing your pickup time" first and has to work out from the body text that this is a bakery's help page covering four topics. Having the four questions at one level is right, because they're equals. What's missing is the level above them. Fix: add a title that's clearly bigger, such as "Help with your bread order", plus the bakery's name. Or keep the questions as they are and drop them to about 22–24 px so a title can lead (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance through size).

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run 90–95 characters (`characters_per_line`, `main.help > p`). That's past Bringhurst's 45–75 range. It costs little here because no paragraph runs past two lines, but narrowing the 820 px column to about 600–640 px would bring it into range.
- The allergy paragraph ends with "anything." alone on its second line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). A narrower column or a small rewrite fixes it.

**What's working.**
- The spacing does the grouping: 8 px from each heading to its answer and 24 px between topics, so every question clearly owns its answer. Keep that ratio if you resize things.
- The reading basics are right: 18 px Inter, 1.5 line spacing, 15.8:1 contrast (`contrast`), one left edge at 160 px for every block.
- The voice is plain and starts with the action ("Open your order…", "Give the person…"). The cancellation answer gives a human reason ("the bread has already been baked for you"), which makes the rule easy to accept. Keep that tone when you tighten the wording.
