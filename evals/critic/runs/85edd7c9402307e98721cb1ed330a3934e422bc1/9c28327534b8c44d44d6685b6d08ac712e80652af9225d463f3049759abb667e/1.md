I've checked the page against the checklist and the reference entries. Here is the critique.

**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "River ferry timetable", then drops into a heavy black grid. It reads as a plain, generic timetable for one ferry crossing. The 2 px black rules around every cell are as dark as the times, so the cage gets noticed before the times inside it. "Runs", the column that decides whether a sailing happens at all today, sits at the far right and looks exactly like the rest. It feels clean but stiff, like a default HTML table.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a public timetable on a web page for riders going from North landing to market quay. They come to answer one question: "when is the next boat I can catch today?"

**Verdict.** It's readable and correct, and it gets to the times quickly, but it's an unstyled data dump rather than a timetable built around that question. The biggest problem is that the condition that can cancel a sailing ("Weekdays" or "Weekends") is the last thing read and the least visible. The heavy grid makes the scan slower than it needs to be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The days a sailing runs are hidden in the last column (defect).** Three of the seven sailings don't run every day: 12:40 and 17:10 are weekdays only, and 19:30 is weekends only. That fact sits in the rightmost column, set at the same 20 px regular weight as "Daily" (`font_size` 20, `font_weight` 400 on every `tbody td`). A Saturday rider reads down "Leaves north", spots 17:10, and has to cross about 800 px of the row to learn it doesn't run. The row that gets missed is the one that leaves them on the landing. Make the exceptions stand out, not the rule: drop "Daily" as the default and mark only the exceptions next to the time (for example "17:10 · weekdays only"). Or split the table into Weekday and Weekend columns so the rider reads only their own list.
2. **The grid is louder than the data (convention).** Every cell is boxed in near-black 2 px rules, as dark as the text. The table also stretches the full 1080 px for entries of 4 to 9 characters (`characters_per_line`), so each row is held together only by its box. This is the case Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, 23.2) and Tufte (*Visual Display*, ch. 5) describe: lines that only hold the data together compete with it. The direction is to make the table only as wide as its content needs, so the cells in a row sit close enough to group by distance. Then drop the vertical rules and keep only thin, pale horizontal ones, or a light tint on every other row. The times should be the darkest thing in the table.
3. **The Period column repeats information the times already give (taste).** "Morning, Morning, Midday, Midday…" uses a whole column, the first one the eye reads, to say what "06:40" already says. If grouping by time of day helps, use it as a small label above each group of rows instead of repeating it on every row. Otherwise remove it, so the departure time is the first thing in each row.

**Smaller things**
- In the departure and arrival columns, the times use proportional figures: the colon in 11:00 and 17:10 sits left of the colon in 06:40. Turn on tabular figures (`font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` in Inter) so the times line up (Bringhurst 4.4.3).
- Every crossing takes 12 minutes, so the "Arrives quay" column repeats the same offset seven times. A single line, "Crossing takes 12 minutes", next to the departures would do the same job with less to read (taste). Keep the column only if riders really plan around arrival times.

**What's working**
- The title and subtitle name the route, the direction, and the season in two lines. The subtitle's grey (`contrast` 6.11, `p.sub`) sets it apart without hurting readability. Keep that header.
- 20 px text at 15.8:1 contrast in a single plain face makes every time easy to read, including on a phone at the landing. Keep the sizes.
- Everything sits on one left edge at x = 100, and the cell text starts at consistent column edges (`left_edges`). The page reads as calm and orderly, which suits a timetable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the table reflows on a narrow phone screen. A 1080 px, four-column table is the part most likely to scroll sideways, and riders will mostly check it on a phone.
