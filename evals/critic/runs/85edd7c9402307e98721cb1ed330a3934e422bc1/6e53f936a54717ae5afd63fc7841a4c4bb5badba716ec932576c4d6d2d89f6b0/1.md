**First read.** The serif headline lands first. The eye drops to the row of four cards and snags on the first one: a dim photo of a wood-panelled, leaded-window lounge, next to three bright flat illustrations. From there it scans across the card titles and the green prices, and then it stops, because nothing tells it what to do next. It reads as a local home-services company's landing page. It feels friendly, calm and tidy, until the photo, which looks like a stock image dropped in by mistake.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the home page for a home-services company. It should let a homeowner see what's offered and what it costs, then book a visit.

**Verdict.** This is a competent, clean page that nearly does its job, but it has three problems. The window-cleaning photo breaks the set and shows the opposite of what the card sells. The prices have no units, so the viewer can't tell what they'd pay. And the card row ends without an action, leaving the only "Book a visit" button small and up in the corner.

**What would move it most**
1. **The window-cleaning photo (card 1, top left) undercuts the whole row and its own message.** It is defect and convention together. The other three cards use flat teal-and-coral illustrations on a pale mint tile. This one is a dark photograph of an old, wood-panelled hall with night behind the leaded panes. The glass reads as black, not "streak-free", and the scene looks like a stately home, not a house this crew would visit. Because it sits first, the viewer meets it first, and it makes the set look assembled from parts rather than designed together. Fix it with one rendering style for the set: commission a fourth illustration in the same style (a window with a squeegee or sparkle, on the mint tile). If you'd rather use photos, switch all four to bright photos of ordinary homes.

2. **The prices don't say what they're a price of (bottom of each card).** This is a defect, because prices are what the viewer acts on and later holds you to. "From $89" for windows could mean per visit, per window or per hour. "From $24" for laundry could mean per bag, per pound or per week. "From $65" for garden care could mean per visit or per month. A viewer who reads $24 as per week and gets billed per bag will feel misled, and one who can't tell will hesitate to book. Add the unit to each price ("From $24 per bag", "From $89 per visit, up to 10 windows") so it can only be read one way.

3. **The page ends at the prices with no next step.** This is a defect. The eye moves headline, subhead, cards, price, and then hits a dead end. The only action is the small dark "Book a visit" button in the top-right corner, far from the moment the viewer has picked a service. Nothing on the cards shows whether they can be clicked. The subhead even promises a flow ("Pick a service, choose a day"), but the page doesn't let you start it. Give each card an obvious action, such as "Book window cleaning", or make the whole card a visibly clickable target. Put the main booking action where the eye arrives after the cards.

**Smaller things.**
- **Copy contradicts itself.** The subhead says "choose a day", but the laundry card fixes pickup to Monday and return to Wednesday. "A vetted crew arrives with their own supplies" also doesn't fit laundry pickup. Either qualify the subhead or say on the laundry card that its days are fixed.
- **Headline breaks mid-phrase.** "on your / schedule" splits the key phrase and leaves "schedule" alone on line 2. Widen the measure so it sits on one line, or break after "house," so the line becomes "Help around the house, / on your schedule".
- **Generic headline.** "Help around the house, on your schedule" could sit on any competitor's page. The subhead's specifics ("vetted crew", "their own supplies") are the real difference, and one of them could carry the headline.

**What's working.**
- The type plan is clear. The serif display headline and wordmark contrast cleanly with the sans text, and there are about four sizes (headline, subhead, card title, body/price), each with an obvious job. Keep it this tight.
- The illustrated cards for deep cleaning, laundry and garden share a two-colour palette, a tile shape and a level of detail. That consistency is the standard the first card has to meet.
- The restrained warm-neutral ground with teal and coral accents feels calm and trustworthy, which suits a service that strangers do inside your home.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are clickable and how they respond to hover or focus. The affordance problem above stands either way.
- The empty band below the cards looks like the screen cut off before the next section. Check in a browser that the page actually continues.
