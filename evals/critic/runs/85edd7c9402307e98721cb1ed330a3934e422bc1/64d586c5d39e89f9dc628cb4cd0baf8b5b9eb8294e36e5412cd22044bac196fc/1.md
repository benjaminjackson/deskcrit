**First read.** The eye goes first to the red and orange block in the middle of the grid, late morning on weekdays. Then it jumps to the three large numbers on the right. Within a couple of seconds it reads as a support-desk report on when tickets arrive. The page is calm and well ordered, but the chart is loud, and the large field of strong blue makes the quiet night hours look as heavy as the busy ones.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a support lead uses this to see when ticket volume peaks, so they can plan staffing for those hours.

**Verdict.** The page is well built, and the layout, captions and context are mostly right. It is let down by two things: the "Busiest hour" figure contradicts the chart, and the rainbow colours make the reader decode the legend instead of seeing the shape of the week.

**What would move it most**
1. **The "Busiest hour" figure and the chart give different answers (defect).** The right column says "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00". In the grid, the only red (30+) cells are the 11:00 column on Monday and Friday. On Tuesday to Thursday, 10:00 and 11:00 are both orange (25–29). I checked the column positions against the 09:00 and 12:00 labels: yellow is 09:00, orange is 10:00 and red is 11:00. A lead who reads the chart staffs up for 11:00. One who reads the summary number staffs up for 10:00. One of them is wrong by an hour, and this is the number someone will act on. There are three possible causes: the figure is wrong, the hour labels are shifted by one column, or "busiest hour" is calculated differently, for example over all weekdays combined. Find which one it is. Then make the figure and the grid agree, and say how the figure is worked out if it differs from what the eye sees.
2. **Rainbow colours on a low-to-high count (convention, and a defect in its effect).** The seven steps run blue, cyan, green, lime, yellow, orange, red. These are unrelated hues, and their lightness does not follow the count: the 0–4 blue is darker than the 20–24 yellow. So the emptiest hours, which fill about two thirds of the grid, are the heaviest-looking area. The reader cannot rank a cell by eye and has to look down at the legend to tell 15–19 from 10–14. In grayscale, the night and the 10:00–11:00 peak come out at roughly the same darkness. The scale also leans on telling green, lime, yellow, orange and red apart. With red-green colour blindness those steps run together, and nothing else on the cells shows the value (WCAG 1.4.1). Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization* (ch. 4.2, 19.2), and the approved fix both point the same way: show values from low to high with one hue going from light to dark, so the colour order follows the value order. Make 0–4 nearly white. The peak then becomes the only dark area, and it reads at a glance without the legend.

**Smaller things.**
- **Type scale:** there are six sizes (28, 26, 18, 16, 15, 13 px, `type_sizes`). The 28 px h1 and the 26 px KPI values (`aside … div.value`) are almost the same size, and so are the 18 px brand, the 16 px `p.sub` and the 15 px nav. Keep about four sizes, for example 28 / 26→22 / 15 / 13, and use weight rather than a one- or two-pixel size change to separate levels (Bringhurst; Refactoring UI).
- **The title names a topic, not the finding:** "When new tickets arrive" leaves the reader to find the peak themselves. A title that states it ("New tickets peak late morning on weekdays") does that work for them. This is my judgement as a designer; no approved source covers it.
- **Copy:** the subtitle covers "1 to 28 September", but the first KPI says "New tickets, September". It is unclear whether 3,412 counts 28 days or 30. Give both the same period.

**What's working.**
- **The captions explain the data.** The subtitle (all queues, average week, the date range) and the source line ("when a ticket was opened, not when it was answered", local time) answer the questions a careful reader would ask. Keep both.
- **Every number has a comparison.** Each KPI has a label, a period and a change from August, so the reader can tell good from bad without looking elsewhere.
- **The layout is quiet.** It has two left edges (40 and 1034 px, `left_edges`), uses only greys besides the chart, and leaves plenty of white space. With a calmer colour scale, the chart would be the obvious and only focal point.
