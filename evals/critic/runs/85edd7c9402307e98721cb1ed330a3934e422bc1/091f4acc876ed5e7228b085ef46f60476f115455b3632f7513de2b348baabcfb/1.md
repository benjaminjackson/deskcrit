**First read.** My eye goes straight to the big black headline, drops to the italic standfirst, and then into the body. The order is clear and nothing competes. It reads as a long-form feature on a small independent magazine site. It feels calm and literate, but also anonymous. **What it's for.** My assumption: the opening screen of a feature article on a web magazine, meant to pull a casual visitor into reading a long piece about a community garden.

**Verdict.** It does the basic job. The page is easy to read, the hierarchy is clear, and the words are strong. But it is visually generic, and the opening screen offers no picture even though the byline promises photographs by the residents, so the warmth of the story rests on the copy alone. The body text is also set a little too wide for comfortable long reading.

**What would move it most**
1. **The opening screen holds back the story's best asset.** The byline says "Photographs by the residents", yet there is nothing between the headline block and the text, and the whole first screen is grey Inter on white. A visitor deciding whether to commit to a long read gets no sense of the place, the people, or the bathtubs-as-beds that make this story different. With the masthead swapped, this could be any blog post. Fix: put one resident photograph on the first screen, as a lead image above the headline or straight after the standfirst, so the picture and the headline make one idea together. *Taste, argued: on a feature that credits its photographers, showing nothing of the place up top costs the click into the story.* (If a lead image was meant to sit in the blank band below the text and simply didn't load, see "Couldn't judge".)
2. **The body text runs too long per line.** The paragraph lines run about 80–85 characters, for example "the transport authority to ask whether the empty car park could be lent to residents". Over a long feature, finding the start of the next line gets slower and the reader tires sooner. *Convention*: Bringhurst gives 45–75 characters, about 66 ideal. Fix: narrow the text column to about 620–640 px, or raise the body size a step, so lines land near 65 characters. The headline can stay at the current width.
3. **The headline breaks badly.** "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" leaves one short word alone on its second line and splits "bus line" across the break. The best-written element on the page looks like an accident. *Convention* (clean breaks at phrase boundaries in headings). Fix: balance the break, e.g. "The allotment at the end / of the bus line", using `text-wrap: balance` or a hand-set break.

**Smaller things.**
- **"Subscribe" in the nav:** it is set exactly like "Features · Food · Places". The one action the publication most wants is styled as a section link. Make it a quiet button or set it apart by weight or colour.
- **Nav position:** you are reading a Feature, but "Features" isn't marked as the current section. Show the current section with weight or an underline.

**What's working.**
- **The copy.** The headline names a specific, odd place. The standfirst turns on concrete details: forty households, beans, squash and herbs, the queue at the shared water tap. The first paragraph opens on a person and a letter. This is what pulls a reader in, so keep it.
- **The hierarchy is clean.** There is one dominant headline. The italic standfirst is set apart from the body by its italic form rather than an extra size, and the small green "Feature" label sits tight above the title. Three or four clear levels, no size collisions.
- **Comfortable leading and generous gathered white space** around the text column, with clear margins and a quiet header rule.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Blank lower third:** everything below the third paragraph is empty. It could be the end of a short page, a lead image that didn't load, or a lazy-load gap. Check in a live browser whether something should appear there.
- **Nav links:** they look about 14 px tall with only a middot between them. Check that their real hit areas meet the 24 px minimum (WCAG 2.5.8) and that they have a visible hover and focus state.
