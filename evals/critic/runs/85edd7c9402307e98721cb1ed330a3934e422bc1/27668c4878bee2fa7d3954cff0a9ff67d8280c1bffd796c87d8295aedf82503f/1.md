**First read.** My eye goes to the heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then gets pulled straight into three heavy blocks of brush script. It reads as a practical notice for people using a river ferry, but it feels like a café chalkboard or a party invitation. Reading is slow: I'm working out letter shapes when I should be taking in times and rules.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling ferry passengers the route, timings, bike rules and winter changes, which they read once to plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. All the body text is set in a connected script, which slows down the information passengers came for. The copy also never gives an actual time, so even a careful reader leaves without knowing when to turn up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is in a script face.** All three paragraphs (about 10 lines, from "The river ferry runs…" to "…each landing.") use a heavy connected brush script. Joined, slanted letters are slower to recognise than a plain text face. That cost falls hardest on readers with low vision or dyslexia, on non-native readers, and on someone checking the page on a phone at the landing. Phrases like "every forty minutes" and "twelve minutes" are exactly what the eye skims for, and here they blur into the rest of the line. **Convention** (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1: choose faces that suit the task. A text face is "unlikely to be carrying excess ornament".) **Fix:** set the paragraphs in a face made for reading. The plain sans already used for the heading would work. Keep script, if you want it at all, for a few words of display.
2. **The two faces split the piece, and the script sets the wrong mood.** The heading is a sober, modern sans; the body is a casual brush script. They don't read as one design. The script also signals leisure and celebration on a page about timetables and rules, where passengers want to feel the service is dependable. **Taste** (common sense, no approved source): a transit notice earns trust by looking plain and consistent. **Fix:** one type family across the page. If the operator wants warmth, get it from the wording or a single accent, not from the body text.
3. **The copy leaves out the facts people will act on.** "Between early morning and late evening", "every forty minutes" with no first or last time, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" (which quay, and how long before dusk?). Readers are left to fill in the times passengers plan around. Someone who reads "before dusk" as "at dusk" can miss the last boat and be stranded on the wrong bank. Sending people to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing" means they only get the answer once they're already standing there. **Defect.** **Fix:** give the first and last departure from each landing, for summer and winter, in the passenger's terms ("Last boat from the market quay: 15:40 in winter"). Put the winter cut-off where it can't be missed. This is also where a passenger's eye should land after the heading.

**What's working.**
- One left-aligned column with a generous left margin and a clear gap between paragraphs. The structure is calm and easy to follow, so keep it when the face changes.
- Near-black on white gives strong contrast throughout.
- The three paragraphs each take one topic (route and timing, bicycles, winter). That's the right way to split it, and short subheads would make it easy to scan.
