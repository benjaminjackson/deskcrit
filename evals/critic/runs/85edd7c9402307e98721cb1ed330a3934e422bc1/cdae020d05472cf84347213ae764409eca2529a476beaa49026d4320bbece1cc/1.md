**First read.** The eye lands on the big dark-blue wedge and its "38%". It moves to the bold serif headline, then right to the legend list. It reads as a page from an annual report showing what a company spends its money on, and it feels calm and credible. Then the eye snags on the rainbow fan of thin slices at the top left. It has to bounce between tiny labels like "2%" and the legend to work out which slice is which, and it gives up.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in an annual report that tells shareholders and staff where the operating money went in fiscal 2026, and which costs matter.

**Verdict.** The page is well made and easy to trust, but the chart is the weakest part of it. A 14-slice pie with 14 colours turns half the data into slivers that can't be told apart, while the table beside it already does the whole job. The page shows the breakdown without saying what it means.

**What would move it most**

1. **The pie has too many slices for the form to work.** This is a convention break that costs something here. Nine of the 14 slices are 4% or less. At the top left they make a fan of slivers whose labels crowd together ("2%", "2%", "2%", "1%" at about x 200–300, y 220–245), and nobody can match Utilities to Equipment repair by colour. To identify any small slice, the reader has to compare its colour with a legend 300 px away. With 14 hues, several of them close (two teals, two blues, olive next to sage), that matching is unreliable. For anyone with colour-blindness it's hopeless: red Travel sits beside green Insurance. A pie only works for about five slices at most (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1). The fix: keep the four big slices (Salaries, Rent, Cloud, Marketing, which add up to 75%) and group the other ten into one grey "Other, 25%" slice. Or replace the pie with a sorted horizontal bar chart labelled directly at each bar, which can also absorb the table. Either way, use one colour plus an accent rather than a rainbow (Few).

2. **The chart and the table say the same thing twice, so the page has no point of view.** This is a defect. The table on the right already gives every category with its exact share, sorted. The pie adds only the impression that salaries are big, at the cost of half the page and all the colour noise. Choose which one carries the page. For exact lookup across 14 lines, the table is the right tool (Few, *Show Me the Numbers*, ch. 3). For the overall shape, a simplified chart is. If you keep both, the chart should show the overall shape and the table the detail, not the same 14 colours repeated.

3. **The headline names the topic, not the finding, and nothing gives a comparison.** This is taste, which I'll argue for. "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" leaves readers to find the story themselves. In an annual report the question is "what does this tell me?", and the honest answer is right there: people cost 38%, and three lines (staff, premises, cloud) take two-thirds of the spend. Say that in the headline or the subhead. Shares with no prior year also give readers no way to tell whether 12% on cloud hosting is up, down, or planned. Adding last year's share as a second column in the table, or a change arrow, turns a static list into something a shareholder can judge (Few, "Common Pitfalls", pitfall 2, on numbers without comparison).

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: the table is 15 px (`font_size`, `td.name`), the header is 14 px (`span.brand`, `nav`), and the kicker and source line are 13 px (`p.kicker`, `p.source`). Three sizes 1 px apart read as accidents. Drop to two small sizes, for example 15 for content and 13 for the header and notes, and separate them by colour and weight.
- Grid: the table's right edge is at 1060 px, while the header and its rule run to 1216 px. The right column ends short of the page margin for no visible reason. Either align the table to the 1216 edge or deliberately narrow the whole content area.
- Labels inside the slices: white "12%" on orange and "16%" on teal look close to 3:1. That's acceptable for bold type at this size, but check the contrast if the labels get smaller.

**What's working.**
- The table: sorted by value, right-aligned bold percentages, hairline row rules, generous rows of about 32 px. It's the clearest thing on the page, so keep it exactly like this.
- The header stack: the small brown "4.2 COSTS" kicker, the 32 px bold Source Serif headline (`h1`), and the 18 px serif subhead with the exact period ("twelve months to 30 June 2026"). It has clear levels and an editorial tone that suits an annual report.
- Honest footnotes: the source line names the accounts and says the shares are rounded, which pre-empts the "these don't add to 100" question (they do add to 100).
