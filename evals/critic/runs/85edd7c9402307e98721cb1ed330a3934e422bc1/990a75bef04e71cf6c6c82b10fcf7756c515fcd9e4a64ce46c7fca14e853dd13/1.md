**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline and drops to the tallest bar, 705 in Q3 2026. Then it runs along a row of eight identical chunky blue 3D blocks. It reads at once as a board-deck slide about seasonal shipments. It feels tidy and corporate, but the extruded bars look like an old office-chart default next to the modern type. Nothing on the bars tells me Q4 2026 is a forecast, so I read it as a real result.

**What it's for.** Assumed: one slide in a 12-page board sales review. It shows directors the seasonal pattern and how this year compares with last, and it must keep actuals and forecast apart.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job: the headline states a finding the data supports, and the axis, units, labels and source are all present. But it shows a forecast as if it were an actual, and the 3D extrusion adds noise and false height. To a board that holds management to these numbers, the first is a real problem.

**What would move it most**

1. **The Q4 2026 forecast looks exactly like the seven actuals.** Defect. The far-right bar (560) has the same blue, the same solid fill and the same label weight as the other bars. "Forecast" appears only in a parenthesis in the subtitle and in the 13 px source line, and directors scanning the bars will not read either. Someone will quote "560 in Q4" as a result, or read the drop from 705 as a real fall in demand. Projections must look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Fix: draw the forecast bar as an outline or a light tint, label it on the chart ("Q4 2026 forecast"), and add a range if the September forecast has one.

2. **The 3D extrusion on every bar.** Convention, and it costs something here. Each bar has a lighter top cap and a dark side face about 10–14 px deep, which adds a third dimension the data doesn't have. The values sit on the front faces, but the eye reads the top of the cap. That makes every bar look a few units taller than its label and its gridline. It also makes the slide look like a 2005 spreadsheet default, which undercuts the careful type around it. Wilke (ch. 26.1, "Avoid gratuitous 3D") and Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 7) both reject this. Fix: draw the chart flat, with no depth, sides or tilt, so each value is read from one length.

3. **The chart doesn't show what the headline claims.** Taste, argued. The headline says "peak each third quarter", but the two Q3 bars look like all the others. The viewer has to read the x-axis labels to find which bars prove the point. Meanwhile the stronger story for a board, that every quarter of 2026 beat the same quarter of 2025 (455 vs 410, 590 vs 520, 705 vs 610), is left for them to work out. Fix: highlight the Q3 bars in the accent colour and grey the rest (Few's "one colour, plus one for the point to notice"). Or, if year-over-year growth is the real message, pair the quarters by year and put that in the headline. Either way, end the headline on what it means for the board, such as "Q3 is our peak, and it grew 16% this year". As it stands, it only describes a pattern.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count six sizes (34, 16, 15, 14, 13 px per `type_sizes`). Four of them sit within 3 px of each other. The running header (`div.doc`, 14 px) and the footer (`div.foot`, 13 px) do the same job at sizes 1 px apart. Set both meta lines at one size, and fold 15 into 14 or 16.
- The subtitle says Q4 2026 is the forecast, and the source line says it again. Once the bar itself is marked, cut the parenthesis from the subtitle.

**What's working.**
- The headline states a finding, not a topic, and the data backs it: both Q3s are their year's peak. Keep that habit on every slide.
- The chart's labelling is complete and quiet: a zero baseline, pale gridlines, a value on every bar, a unit line under the chart title ("Thousands of units"), and a source. Nothing needs a legend.
- All text holds one 80 px left edge (`left_edges`), and every grey still meets contrast (lowest 4.83:1 on `div.doc` and the footer). The page reads calm and ordered.
