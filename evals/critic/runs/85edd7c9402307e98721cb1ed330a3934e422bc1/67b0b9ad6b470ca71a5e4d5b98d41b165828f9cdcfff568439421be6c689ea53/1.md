**First read.** I look first at the bold title, then the four purple bars, and the tallest one (Growth, 4.0%) holds me. Then I go across to the small table on the right and down to the Definition. It reads as one tab of an internal SaaS analytics dashboard, a conversion report for a growth or finance team. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a recurring internal report that lets product, growth and finance people compare trial-to-paid conversion across plans for Q3 2025 and decide whether any plan needs attention.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid and the chart is honest, but the page doesn't pin down what its one number measures. The subtitle and the Definition give conflicting conversion rules, and nothing tells the reader whether 3–4% is good, bad or changing. As it stands, it's a tidy display of a figure people can't confidently act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The subtitle and the Definition describe two different metrics.** This is a defect. Under the title, the subtitle says the bars show trials "that became paid accounts **within 30 days**." The Definition at right says a trial converts "when the first invoice is paid," and trials "still open **on 30 October**" count as not converted. That's a fixed cutoff date, not a 30-day window per trial. A stranger can read it two ways:
   - **(a)** Each trial gets 30 days.
   - **(b)** Any Q3 trial paid by 30 October counts, which gives a July trial up to about 120 days and a late-September trial only 30.

   Under (b), the rates can't be compared with any other quarter, and plans whose trials are skewed toward early or late in the quarter are distorted. The source line adds to the doubt: it cites "trial events, 1 July to 30 September," but conversions are counted until 30 October. This is the number people will quote and hold the team to, so it has to be unambiguous (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2: never make the reader guess what a number measures). Fix: pick one rule and use the same wording in the subtitle, the Definition and the source line. For example: "Paid within 30 days of trial start; trials started 1 Jul–30 Sep, payments observed to 30 Oct."

2. **No comparison, so the reader can't judge the numbers.** This is a defect. The four rates stand alone, with no previous quarter, no target and no company-wide rate. A reader can see that Growth beats Starter by 0.9 points but can't tell whether 3.1% is a problem or business as usual, which is the question this page exists to answer. Fix: put the comparison next to each value, for example last quarter as a pale bar or tick beside each purple bar, or a target line (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Also add an "All plans" figure (about 3.4%, from 627 of 18,420).

3. **The title names the topic but doesn't say what it found, and the ranking it implies may be noise.** This is taste. "Trial to paid conversion by plan" is a label. The chart's only visible story is "Growth is highest," but that's weaker than it looks. Growth (118 of 2,950) and Team (185 of 5,140) differ by 0.4 points, which is about one standard error. Enterprise rests on just 38 conversions. A reader skimming the bars will rank the plans anyway. Fix: write the actual finding as the title, such as "All plans convert at 3–4%; no plan stands out," or name what really changed against last quarter once finding 2 is in. If you keep the ranking, mark how uncertain the small-sample bars are, for example with a note or light range marks on Enterprise.

**Smaller things.**
- Right column: the table gives Trials and Paid but not the rate. A reader checking a bar has to divide in their head. Add a "Rate" column (3.1%, 3.6%, 4.0%, 3.4%) so the table and chart agree at a glance.
- Lower right: the right column ends about two-thirds of the way down while the chart runs to the bottom, which leaves a large dead area. It looks unfinished rather than deliberate. This is taste. Use the space for the comparison from finding 2, or end both columns at the same depth.
- "Paid" in the table header is ambiguous: does it mean paid accounts, invoices, or conversions under rule (a) or (b)? Name it to match the definition once that's settled, for example "Converted."

**What's working.**
- The bar chart is honest and quiet. It starts at zero, the gridlines are pale, every bar is labelled with its value, and one colour is used for one series. A 0.9-point spread looks like a 0.9-point spread. Keep this restraint when you add comparison marks.
- The raw counts and a written definition sit on the same screen as the chart. Few dashboards show their working like this, and it's why the definition conflict is even catchable. Keep it; just make it agree with itself.
- The type does a lot with little: one family, roughly four sizes, weight for the subheads and a gray for the secondary text. The hierarchy is clear without decoration.
