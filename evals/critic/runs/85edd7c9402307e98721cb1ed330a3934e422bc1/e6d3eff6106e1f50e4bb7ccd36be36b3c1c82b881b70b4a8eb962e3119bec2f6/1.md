**First read.** My eye lands on the bold black headline, then drops straight into the body text. There's nothing else on the page to look at. It reads as a long-form feature on a small magazine or newsletter site, and it feels clean, calm and anonymous. It could be any Substack or Ghost blog. Then the copy ends and nearly a third of the screen is white.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the opening screen of an online magazine feature that should pull a casual reader into a long piece about a community allotment.

**Verdict.** The text is legible and well ordered, but the page doesn't do enough to pull a reader in. The byline promises "Photographs by the residents" and none appear. A story about a place has no picture of the place, and with Inter everywhere and grey on white, nothing on the page belongs to this story or to this magazine.

**What would move it most**
1. **The photographs are missing.** The byline credits "Photographs by the residents", but there's no photo between the standfirst and the copy, and the bottom ~220 px of the screen is blank. The reader is told there are pictures, sees none, and is asked to picture beans and bath tubs from words alone. Headline and image should work as one idea here: "the end of the bus line" is a picture waiting to happen. If a lead image is meant to sit under the byline or after the first paragraph and didn't load in this capture, check it in a live browser. As shown, though, it's a defect. Fix: put a residents' photograph of the plot above the fold so it carries the place while the headline carries the story. *Defect.*
2. **The page looks templated, and that drains the story.** One sans (Inter) at every level, near-black on white, a thin rule under a text wordmark, and a single green label are the defaults of a thousand blogs. The copy is specific and warm (Halima Osei, the four-month reply, pallets and bath tubs). The setting is generic and cool, so the reader gets no signal that this is a feature worth slowing down for, or that "Groundwork" is a magazine with a voice. Fix: give the feature one element of its own. That could be a display or serif face for the headline, a warmer paper tone, or the photograph doing that work. *Taste, argued: a feature competes on atmosphere, and this page has none to offer.*
3. **The lines are too long for the reading the page asks for.** The body column runs about 720 px, and the first line holds about 83 characters ("Ten years ago … Today forty"). That's past the 45–75 range and well past the ~66 ideal (Bringhurst). Over a long feature, the eye has to work to find the start of each next line. Fix: narrow the column to about 600–640 px at this size, or raise the body size a step. *Convention, and here it costs something because this is long-form text.*

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "line" sits alone on the second line, splitting "bus line" and leaving a one-word runt under a full line. Break it as "The allotment at the end / of the bus line", or balance the lines (`text-wrap: balance`).
- Headline tracking looks like the face's default at ~44 px. Pulling it in slightly (−1 to −2%) would make it hold together better as a display line. This is marginal, so I'm treating it as taste.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean and has exactly the right number of steps: label, headline, byline, body. There are four clearly different levels, and the headline dominates by both size and weight, so nobody wonders where to start.
- The standfirst-style opening paragraph is good editorial copy. In four sentences it gives place, change, a human detail (the queue at the water tap as the news exchange) and what the piece is ("We spent a season…"). Keep the design out of its way.
- The leading (~1.6) and the space between paragraphs are comfortable, with no indents doubling up the paragraph marks.

- Whether the nav links (14 px text separated by " · ") have hit areas of at least 24 px. Measure them in the browser.
