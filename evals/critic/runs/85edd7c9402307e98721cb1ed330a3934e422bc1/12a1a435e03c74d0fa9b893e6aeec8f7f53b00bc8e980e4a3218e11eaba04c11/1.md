Then it stops, because none of them says what it counts: 3.8 what? 64.20 in which currency? 12.4 subscribers, or 12.4 thousand? Next it drops to the orange bars, picks out Saturday as the peak and Sunday as the dip, and reads them unaided. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, a well-built starter dashboard for a small bakery shop.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner or operator of a small online bakery opens this overview to see how the shop is doing and to plan delivery days.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, well-made layout, but the three numbers at the top don't do their job yet. They have no units, no time period and no comparison, so the owner sees the figures and still can't tell whether things are good or bad. Fix the cards before you touch anything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI cards have no units, no period and no comparison** (all three cards in the top row). *Defect.* "3.8" probably means a percentage, but the viewer has to guess. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" is the worst: as a subscriber count it reads either as twelve-point-four people, which is impossible, or as 12.4k, which the viewer has to supply. That's the kind of figure someone repeats wrongly in a meeting. None of the cards says what period it covers. The chart footnote says September 2026, but nothing ties that to the cards. None of them shows a change, a target or a previous value either, so the biggest type on the page can't answer "is this good?" Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (pitfall 2): a bare headline number is the classic dashboard failure. Fix direction, from the approved fix: put the unit ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400"), the period ("September") and a comparison ("+0.4 pts vs August", or the target) next to each number. The type sizes already leave room for a small second line under each figure.

2. **The chart title names the topic, not what it found** ("Orders by delivery day"). *Taste, common sense.* The data has a clear story: Friday and Saturday carry the week, with Saturday at about 1,130 orders, and Sunday falls to about 380, a third of Saturday. That's the reason an owner would look at this chart (staffing, baking volume, which day to promote). The title makes them work it out themselves. A title such as "Friday and Saturday bring the most orders; Sunday brings the fewest", or one annotation on the Sunday bar, turns the chart into an answer. If Sunday is low because delivery is limited that day, say so on the chart, or the dip reads as a problem.

**What's working.**
- **The grid is strict.** The cards and the chart panel share the same left and right edges (about 48 and 1232 px) and the same gutter, so the page reads as one calm system. Keep that when you add the comparison lines to the cards.
- **The chart is honest and quiet.** The bars start at zero, the days run in their natural order, there's one series in one colour, the gridlines are pale, and the source line is there. Nothing competes with the data.
- **The colour is restrained and on brand.** The burnt orange appears only in the wordmark and the bars, against neutral greys. It feels warm and bakery-like without shouting. Keep the orange for data and brand, and don't spread it into chrome.
