**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers", top left, then drops into three paragraphs that are centered under it. Their ragged left edge wanders away from the heading's straight one, so the column looks slightly out of true. It reads as a plain, calm notice from a small ferry service. The answers a passenger came for (how often, how long, the last boat) are hidden inside running sentences.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, the rules for bicycles and the winter changes, so they can plan a trip at a glance.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The type is clean and readable, but the centered paragraphs break the page's only alignment. Worse, the timetable facts a passenger needs are buried in prose and given as vague words instead of times.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts a passenger acts on are buried and vague (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour" and "before dusk" sit mid-sentence in three look-alike paragraphs. Someone checking whether they can catch a boat has to read every line, and even then gets no times. "Early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk" can't be planned around: one person reads "before dusk" as 4 pm in December, another as 5:30, and one of them misses the last boat. People skim screens rather than read them (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Fix: put the timetable facts at the top as a short scannable block with real first and last departure times, summer and winter. Keep the bicycle rules as their own short section under a heading that names them. Name the dates winter runs between.
2. **Centered paragraphs under a flush-left heading (convention, and it costs something here).** All three paragraphs are 4-line blocks set centered, so both edges are ragged. The heading starts at x≈120, and the paragraph lines start anywhere from about 124 to 160 px. The result is a near-miss: the column looks misaligned rather than deliberately centered. Each new line also starts somewhere new, which slows reading. Nothing on the page explains the central axis. Fix: set paragraphs of several lines flush left, and keep centering for a few short lines (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.3). This also gives the heading and text one shared left edge.

**Smaller things.**
- "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what's inside. A heading like "Ferry times and bicycle rules" would tell a skimmer they're on the right page. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- The type is plain and doesn't get in the way. There's one sans family, the body looks about 18 px with comfortable leading, and lines run roughly 65 to 72 characters, which sits in Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Keep this setting when you change the alignment.
- The hierarchy is simple: two sizes, with the heading set apart by both size and weight. Nothing competes with it.
- The copy is calm, specific about procedure ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded") and free of filler.
