**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold sans heading and then drops into a calm, well-set serif column. It reads as a plain information page from a small local ferry service, and it feels quiet and trustworthy, like a notice from a parish office. After reading all three paragraphs, I still couldn't tell you when I can catch a boat.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling passengers how to use a local river ferry, mainly when it runs, how long it takes and the rules for bicycles. These are people planning a trip.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and does its job. The page doesn't. Every fact a passenger would act on is left vague ("early morning", "late evening", "before dusk", "a little longer"), and the actual timetable is in a shelter at the landing. That means you have to be at the ferry already to plan a trip on it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times can't be acted on (defect).** Paragraphs 1 and 3 hold the facts people will plan around and later hold the operator to:
   - "between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing.
   - "every forty minutes" gives no starting minute, so you can't work out a departure.
   - "before dusk" changes by more than an hour across the winter, and the line doesn't say which quay. "The quay" could be the market quay or either end.
   
   One passenger reads "before dusk" as about 4:30 and another as 5:15. The second one misses the last boat and is stranded on the wrong bank. The page also says printed timetables are "kept in the shelter at each landing", which admits the real information is somewhere the reader isn't. Fix: state the first and last departure from each landing in clock times, for summer and for winter, and replace "before dusk" with a time or a rule ("last boat from the market quay at 16:20, November to February"). If the timetable exists in print, put it on this page.
2. **The schedule is buried in prose when it should be scannable (taste, argued).** The three paragraphs cover three separate topics: the normal service, bicycles and winter changes. All three sit in identical running text with nothing to tell them apart. A passenger checking times on the way out the door has to read every sentence to find one number. Keep the prose for the bicycle rules. Pull the service facts (frequency, first and last boat, crossing time) into a short table or a few bolded lead-ins above it, so the numbers are what the eye finds after the heading.

**Smaller things.**
- "A little longer when the tide turns against the boat" gives no figure. Say "up to 20 minutes" or whatever is true.
- "Bicycles … at no extra charge" assumes the reader knows the fare, which the page never gives. Add the foot-passenger fare or a link to it.
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not the content. Something like "Ferry times and bicycles" would tell people what's here before they read.

**What's working.**
- **Type pairing:** a bold sans heading over a regular serif body gives two clearly separate levels with only two sizes. Keep this lean scale; don't add sizes when you add structure.
- **Text setting:** the body is comfortable to read. Lines are about 65–75 characters, leading is generous without drifting, the rag is soft, the paragraphs are spaced and not indented, and the contrast is strong.
- **Restraint:** no ornament, boxes or colour that does nothing. That suits a public-service notice.
