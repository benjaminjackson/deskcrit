**First read.** My eye lands on the big white headline, "See where every dollar goes this month", drops to the subline, then to the white "Try it free" button. Then it drifts right into a large empty blue field, waiting for something that never arrives. It reads as a budgeting app's landing page. It feels clean, confident, and unfinished.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the landing page for a consumer budgeting app. Its job is to get a stranger to start the free option, and that means trusting it with their bank logins.

**Verdict.** The words and the type do their job. The headline is specific and the hierarchy is clear. But the page asks for trust in your bank accounts and gives you nothing to look at. Half the hero is empty blue, and the one fact that earns trust is the smallest text on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half of the hero is empty (defect).** From x≈800 to the right edge, the blue band (y 62–462) holds nothing. That's about 480×400 px of solid colour beside the only content. The viewer reads this as an image that didn't load, or as a template with its picture missing, and the page tips hard left. The headline promises that you'll *see* where every dollar goes, then shows nothing to see. If an image failed in this capture, check it in a live browser. If it's meant to be empty, put the product there: one real-looking month view with categories, a limit, and the "heads-up" alert. Then the picture proves what the headline promises instead of repeating it.
2. **"Try it free" can be read two ways, and the ask asks for trust (defect, copy).** "Free" on a finance product can mean a free plan or a trial that later charges a card. The viewer will hold you to whichever they assumed. "Try it" also doesn't say what happens next, which here means linking bank accounts. Say exactly what free means ("Free for 30 days, no card", or "Free forever for one account"). Better still, make the ask let them judge the value against something they already know, such as "See last month's spending in 2 minutes". Their own past month is a result they can check.
3. **The trust message is buried (defect).** "Read-only access … can never move money" answers the first fear anyone has before connecting a bank. It sits third in a row of equal cards in 15 px grey (`font_size`, `section.facts > div.fact:nth-of-type(3) > p`). Move it next to the primary button as a short line under it, in the viewer's terms: "We can see your balances. We can never move your money."

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count five sizes, 52, 20, 19, 17 and 15 (`type_sizes`). The 20 px subhead and the 19 px card headings are one step apart and look like an accident (Bringhurst). Keep 52 / 20 / 15 and set the card headings at 15–16 px bold, so they differ by weight rather than size (Refactoring UI).
- Spacing: the feature row starts 34 px below the blue band (band ends at y 462, the h3s start at 496), while the band has about 64 px of top padding. Below the row there are about 224 px of dead white. Give the row the same breathing room as the hero and end the page on purpose. If the page really ends there, add a closing ask or a footer.

**What's working.**
- The headline is specific and speaks to the viewer: a person, a unit (every dollar), and a time frame (this month). The subline says who does what, in order: connect, set a limit, get warned. Keep both.
- The primary and secondary buttons are clearly ranked: a filled white button and an outlined one, both 54 px tall (`box`, `span.b1`/`span.b2`). Contrast is strong throughout, from 6.77:1 white on blue up to 15.8:1.
- The three-column feature row sits on a clean grid (left edges at 64, 461 and 859) with blurb lines of 42–47 characters, so it scans at a glance.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the right half of the hero holds an image or animation that didn't render in this capture. Check it in a live browser before acting on finding 1 as a design choice.
