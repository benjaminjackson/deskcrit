**First read.** The eye lands on "Pricing", drops to the three prices, scans the short feature lists, and then has nowhere to go. It reads as a pricing page for a SaaS scheduling or booking tool. It looks tidy, calm and templated, and below the cards it stops dead.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the pricing page of a booking or calendar product, meant to get a visitor to pick a plan and start on it.

**Verdict.** It's clean, but it fails as a pricing page. It shows three prices and then leaves the visitor with no way to choose one and no way to act on the choice. The missing per-plan action and the unclear tier contents decide whether this page converts, and both are currently broken.

**What would move it most**

1. **No plan has a button, so the page ends at a dead end** (defect). The visitor reads a card, decides "Team looks right", and has nothing to click. The only action is the header's "Start free" (`a.cta`, at top right), which is 600 px away from the cards and doesn't say which plan it starts. The 230 px of empty white below the cards is where that decision should land.
   - Fix: give each card its own action at the bottom, and name the plan and terms in it. "Start Team free for 14 days" beats "Get started".
   - Keep the header CTA as the general entry point, but make the in-card buttons the obvious next step.
   - The standard is Nielsen's "recognition rather than recall" and Krug's "don't make me think": the action belongs next to the decision.

2. **The cards don't say what you actually get, so you can't compare them** (defect).
   - **The tiers look unrelated.** Each tier lists three different features. Does Team include the Booking page and Email reminders? Does Business include Shared availability and Reports? As written, a buyer could read Business as *losing* shared availability. On a price comparison, that ambiguity is what makes people leave to "check later".
     - Fix: make inheritance explicit. Start Team's list with "Everything in Solo, plus:" and Business's with "Everything in Team, plus:". Or add a short comparison table below.
   - **"/ month" can be read two ways.** It could mean per account or per user. "Teams of every size" makes per-user a natural reading, and then $29 for a 10-person team means $290. A buyer remembers the price and holds you to it, so this is the costliest line on the page to leave ambiguous.
     - Fix: say "per month, whole team" or "per user / month". Also state whether there's an annual rate.
   - **"Reports" and "Priority support" name categories, not outcomes.** One concrete clause each would help, such as "See which booking slots fill first".

3. **Nothing tells the visitor which plan is for them** (convention plus taste).
   - **The scale points the wrong way.** The largest type on the page is the word "Pricing" (`h1`, 44 px). It repeats the nav and tells the visitor nothing. The prices are 40 px, almost the same size, so the heading and the prices compete instead of the prices leading.
   - **The three cards are identical in weight**, with the same border, the same type and no highlight. The viewer has to do all the deciding.
   - Fix: let the prices or the plan choice lead. Shrink or drop the h1 (the nav already says where you are, or should; see below). Then mark the plan most teams should pick, with a tinted border or a "Most teams pick this" label, so the eye has a default.
   - Tie each plan name to who it's for. "Solo: just you", "Team: up to 10 people" lets a visitor choose by recognising themselves, not by decoding feature lists.

4. **The headline copy could be anyone's** (taste). "Simple plans for teams of every size. Change or cancel whenever you like." could sit on any SaaS pricing page, so it tells the visitor nothing about what Tidewell does or why to pick it.
   - "Change or cancel whenever you like" is the one useful promise, and it's buried in the second sentence.
   - Fix: use the lead to say what they're buying and remove the risk, in the viewer's terms. For example: "Every plan starts with a free 14-day trial. Switch plans or cancel any time, with no call needed." (Only if that's true.)

**Smaller things.**
- **Type scale:** there are seven sizes (44, 40, 22, 20, 18, 16, 15 in `type_sizes`). 44/40, 22/20 and 16/15 are too close to read as different levels. Cut to about four, and use weight or colour for the finer distinctions (Bringhurst; Refactoring UI).
- **Feature text size:** the plan features are 15 px (`font_size`, `ul > li`), smaller than the 16 px nav links. Set them at 16 px or more, since they are the page's body text.
- **Nav state:** the "Pricing" link isn't marked as the current page. `a.current` has the same #4a5368 colour and 400 weight as the other links. Give it the ink colour, a heavier weight or an underline.
- **Widow in the lead:** the lead (`p.lead`) breaks with "you like." alone on its second line. Rebalance the break or narrow the box.

**What's working.**
- **Grid:** the three cards share one grid exactly, with left edges at 191/519/847 px and matching baselines. The comparison reads as a set, and adding buttons or a highlight later won't fight the layout.
- **Colour:** the colour is disciplined. One blue appears only on the wordmark and the CTA, so whatever you make blue next (the in-card buttons) will stand out without extra effort.
- **Contrast:** all text is 6.8:1 or better (`contrast`), so the restrained grey styling costs nothing in legibility.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus styling on the nav links and the CTA, which matters on a page whose whole job is one click.
