Deep reads are done; here's the critique.

**First read.** The huge black headline grabs the eye first, and the eye stays stuck there because the headline's four lines overlap. The descenders of "Quayside ferry" cut into "crossings by daylight," and "spring through early" runs into "summer." It looks like a broken page, not a deliberate style. The eye then drops to a short, calm paragraph, and after that there's nothing. The right side and the bottom 40% of the screen are empty, so this reads as a timetable page with no timetable on it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the top of a web page for a local river ferry's spring timetable, meant to tell riders when the boats run.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The headline is so tightly set that its lines collide, and there's no timetable on the page, so a rider can't find a single departure time.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline's lines overlap.** This is a defect. The h1 is 76 px Inter Bold with a line height of 50.2 px (`line_height_ratio` 0.66, `h1`). That's two-thirds of the type size, so every line's descenders hit the capitals and ascenders of the line below ("ferry" into "crossings," "through" and "early" into "summer"). Readers have to pull the words apart before they can read them, and the page looks broken on its first and largest element. Bringhurst allows negative leading for display type "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide." This collides on every line. Fix: open the line spacing until no letter touches the next line. Around 1.0–1.1 of the type size will still look tight and hold together as one unit.
2. **A timetable page with no times on it.** This is a defect. The eyebrow says "Spring timetable" (`p.eyebrow`) and the measured page ends at 800 px, so there's nothing below the fold. The only timing is "every forty minutes," with no first or last sailing, no days, and no dates for "spring" or "early summer." A rider still can't answer "when can I catch it?", which is the whole reason they came. "By daylight" is the vaguest part, because it's the operating window and it shifts across the season. Fix: put the first and last crossing times and the season's start and end dates where they can be seen at a glance. Turn "by daylight" into actual times, or a rule the reader can act on, such as "first boat 7:00, last boat at sunset (8:40 pm by June)."
3. **The headline is too long to be a headline.** This one is taste. Thirteen words over four lines at 76 px is a sentence set as a poster, and it carries information (season, hours) that would be clearer in smaller type. A short headline ("Quayside ferry: spring timetable"), with the season and hours set below as facts, would stop the display type from fighting itself and leave room for the schedule.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline ends with "summer" alone on its last line, and the paragraph ends with "weather." alone. Rebreak both so the last lines carry two or more words (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (76 / 20 / 15 px), each with an obvious job, and the eyebrow sets itself apart by weight and grey (`#59636e`, 6.11:1) rather than by another near-miss size.
- The lead paragraph reads well: 20 px, 65.5 characters per line, line height 1.5, contrast 15.8:1. Keep this setting for the timetable text.
- Everything hangs from one left edge at x=120, which gives a calm, firm structure that a schedule table can drop into.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the 76 px headline wraps on a phone, where tight leading and long words will make the collisions worse.
