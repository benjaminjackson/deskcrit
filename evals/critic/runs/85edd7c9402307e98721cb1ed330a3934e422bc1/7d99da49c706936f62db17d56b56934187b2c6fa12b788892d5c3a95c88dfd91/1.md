**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It starts at the top left and reads "Changing your pickup time" as the first line of a paragraph, then slowly works out that there are four question-and-answer pairs. It feels like a plain text file: calm and readable, but flat, and it doesn't say whose page this is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the help or FAQ section of a small bakery's pre-order site, for customers who have ordered or are about to order bread for pickup.

**Verdict.** The answers are short and mostly clear, but it doesn't work as a help page yet. The headings look like body text, so you can't scan for your question. Two of the answers that involve money or safety can be read in more than one way.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headings don't read as headings.** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two are set in the same face, weight and colour as the answers, and they look a pixel or two *smaller*. Someone who came with one question has to read every line to find it, and each pair looks like one paragraph. There is also no page title, so nothing says "Help with your order" or which bakery this is. *Defect.* Dair: a contrast must be obvious at a glance, or it looks like an accident. Fix: make each question clearly different from its answer in weight or colour, for example semibold in the same family, and leave the size alone if you like. Add a page title above it all as the one dominant element.

2. **The policy lines can be read two ways.** These are the lines people hold the bakery to.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead": ahead of what? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup time or any time the day before. A customer who cancels at 9 pm the night before a 10 am pickup will think they're in time, and the bakery may not agree. State the cutoff as a time ("until 24 hours before your pickup time").
   - "Show it at the counter along with a name": it could be the collector's name or the name on the order. If a friend gives their own name and staff expect yours, they get turned away. Say "your name, as it appears on the order".
   - "Open your order": it doesn't say where. Link it or name the place ("in your confirmation email").

   *Defect.*
3. **The allergy answer sends people to the shop.** "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps someone already standing at the counter. It doesn't help someone ordering online, who needs to know before they pay. For a customer with a nut or sesame allergy, that means ordering blind or not ordering. *Defect, common sense.* Fix: point to the ingredients and allergens on each product online, and keep "ask the baker" as the backup.

**Smaller things.**
- The answers in groups 1, 2 and 4 run about 90–100 characters per line, past Bringhurst's 45–75. They're only one or two lines, so the cost is small. Narrowing the column to about 600 px would also make the page read as a column rather than a stretch of text.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergy answer (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewording it or narrowing the column will fix it.

**What's working.**
- The spacing between groups is about three times the gap between each question and its answer, so the pairs hold together even with no type contrast. Keep those intervals when you restyle the headings.
- The answers are short and plain, and "After that the bread has already been baked for you" gives the reason behind a rule kindly. That's the right voice for a small bakery.
- The text is near-black on white in one plain sans, so it reads comfortably at this size.
