**First read.** The eye goes straight to the red and orange cells from late morning to midday, then to the three big numbers on the right. It reads clearly as a support team's "when do tickets arrive" report. But the eye doesn't rest on the peak. The night hours are a solid block of saturated blue-violet that takes up most of the chart and is as loud as the peak. The whole grid feels like a weather radar.

**What it's for.** My assumption: support leads use this to plan shifts, to see which hours and days need more people and which can run thin.

**Verdict.** The structure is right: a sound chart form, honest framing, and good supporting numbers. The colour works against the job, though. A rainbow scale hides the order of the values, and the busiest-hour card seems to disagree with the chart under it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The rainbow scale makes the quiet hours loud and hides which cells are busier.** *Defect.* The seven bins go violet, blue, teal, green, yellow, orange, red. They don't get steadily lighter or darker: yellow (20–24) is the lightest step and sits in the middle, and the lowest bin (0–4) is a strong blue-violet. So:
   - The emptiest hours, about two-thirds of the grid, carry as much colour as the busiest ones.
   - "Is green more than teal?" needs a trip to the legend every time.
   - Viewed in greyscale, the 20–24 cells stand out more than the 30+ cells.
   - For red-green colour-blind viewers, green (15–19), orange (25–29) and red (30+) get close, and those are the exact bins that decide staffing.

   Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*) is direct about this: show values that run from low to high with one hue that goes from light to dark, so the order of the colours follows the order of the values. Make 0–4 nearly white or a pale tint, so the night falls back and the peak shapes itself. If 30+ is the alarm level, it can be the darkest step.
2. **The busiest-hour card doesn't match what the chart shows.** *Defect, unless I'm misreading the columns.* The card says "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00". But the only red (30+) cells sit in the 11:00 column, on Monday and Friday. 10:00 is orange on every weekday, and on Tuesday to Thursday 10:00 and 11:00 are the same bin. Someone planning shifts sees two answers and has to decide which one to trust. Confirm which hour is really busiest. Then either fix the card or the column labels, or make the chart show the same thing the card claims, for example by outlining the peak cell.
3. **The title names the topic and leaves the reader to find the point.** *Taste.* "When new tickets arrive" is a label. The page already knows the answer: weekdays peak from about 09:00 to 17:00, highest around late morning, and weekends stay low. Saying that in the title, or in the subtitle, turns the chart into evidence for a staffing decision instead of a puzzle.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline total and the chart cover different periods: "New tickets, September" versus "1 to 28 September" in the subtitle. Say which one 3,412 covers. Also check it against the chart: adding up the lower ends of the bins already comes to more than 3,412 for four weeks.
- The palette is seven saturated hues on an otherwise grey UI, so the chart looks louder than the rest of the product. This is fixed by the single-hue ramp in finding 1.
- Red, amber and green carry "bad / warning / fine" meaning everywhere else in a help-desk tool. Here they only mean counts, which suggests the midday rush is an error. This is also fixed by finding 1.

**What's working.**
- An hour-by-day heatmap is the right form for this question. Weekday versus weekend and the working-hours band show up at a glance. Keep the form and only change the colour.
- The source line ("when a ticket was opened, not when it was answered", in local time) heads off the obvious misreading. Keep it.
- Each stat on the right comes with a comparison to August, and the type scale is tight: title, subtitle, big figure, small label. Nothing competes with anything else by accident.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether hovering a cell shows its exact count. With binned colours, that's the only way to get a real number.
