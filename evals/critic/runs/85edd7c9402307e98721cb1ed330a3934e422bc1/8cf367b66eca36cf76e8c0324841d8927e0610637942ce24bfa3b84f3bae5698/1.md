**First read.** The bold heading at top left catches the eye first: "Notes for ferry passengers". Then it drops into three centred grey-black paragraphs. These form a soft, lozenge-shaped block that drifts away from the heading's left edge. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry. It feels calm and legible, but slightly unfinished, as if the paragraphs were centred by default rather than on purpose.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page that passengers scan to plan a crossing. They want to know when the boat runs, what to do with a bike, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** The type itself is sound: one clear heading, readable body size, good line length and strong contrast. Two things let it down. The paragraphs are centred, which breaks the left edge and makes the text harder to read. And the copy gives no actual times, which is the one thing a passenger came for.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are centred, so the left edge is broken.** *Convention.*
   - **Where:** all three paragraphs. Each runs to four lines of about 66 to 68 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `main.article > p`).
   - **What the viewer gets:** every line starts at a different point, from about x=125 to x=158. The eye has to hunt for the start of each new line, and with four lines per paragraph you feel it.
   - **The mismatch with the heading:** the heading is flush left at x=120 (`box`, `h1`), while the text below hangs on a central axis at about x=440 that nothing else on the page uses. The text sits 5 to 40 px off the heading's edge, close enough to look like a missed alignment. The facts file reports all four blocks on no shared edge (`blocks_on_no_shared_edge: 4`).
   - **Sources:** Tschichold, *The New Typography*, rejects central-axis setting by habit. Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.3, makes ragged setting depend on the text and the page.
   - **Fix:** set the paragraphs flush left on the heading's x=120 edge, and keep centring for a few short lines. That one change fixes the alignment, the rag and the grid together.
2. **The copy describes the service but doesn't give passengers what they need to act.** *Defect.*
   - **Times:** "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last departure. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can be read two ways. It could mean a fixed time a bit before sunset, or a time that changes every day. A passenger who guesses wrong misses the last boat home.
   - **Which quay:** "leaves the quay" doesn't say which side. The page names both "the north landing" and "the market quay".
   - **The page sends readers elsewhere:** for the real numbers, it points to printed timetables in the shelter. So the page sends people to the landing to find out when to go to the landing.
   - **Fix:** put actual first and last departure times from each side, for summer and winter, where the eye lands. A small table or two lines under the heading would do.

**Smaller things.**
- **Heading** (top): "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what the page says. A heading like "Timetable, bikes and winter service" would tell a scanning reader what's here. This is common sense, not a published rule.

**What's working.**
- **Type scale:** there are just two sizes, 36 px bold and 19 px regular (`type_sizes`). The heading leads clearly, by both size and weight, with no near-miss sizes. Keep this.
- **Body setting:** 19 px Inter, a line height of 1.5, about 66 to 68 characters per line, and 15.8:1 contrast (`contrast`). That is comfortable reading. Once the paragraphs are set flush left, it will read very well.
- **Spacing:** one topic per paragraph, with a 20 px gap between paragraphs and 24 px under the heading. The three subjects are easy to tell apart without extra decoration.
