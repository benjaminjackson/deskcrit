**First read.** The eye lands on the title, then gets caught by the table's heavy black grid. It is 28 identical boxes with thick dark edges, and they are the strongest thing on the page, stronger than the times. It reads as a simple timetable for one ferry route. It feels like a spreadsheet printout: orderly and correct, but heavy, with the times sitting inside the boxes instead of being what you see first.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a passenger, on a phone or at a printed notice at the landing, checks when the next ferry leaves north landing today and whether it runs on this particular day.

**Verdict.** The content is there and it's legible, but the design doesn't do the one thing a timetable has to do quickly: show a rider which boats run on *their* day. The day restriction sits in the last column at the same weight as everything else, the grid shouts over the data, and the table is stretched so wide that each row has to be read like a sentence.

**What would move it most**

1. **Which days a sailing runs is hidden in the far-right column.** Three of the seven sailings don't run every day (12:40 and 17:10 are weekdays only, 19:30 is weekends only). The only place that shows is the "Runs" column, set in the same regular weight as "Daily". A weekday rider reading down the departure column sees 19:30, plans around it, and stands on the quay with no boat coming. Their real last ferry is 17:10. A rider has to read every row out to the right edge to filter the list, and that's exactly the step people skip. This is a **defect**: it misleads on the one thing the viewer acts on. Fix direction: arrange the table by day type. Either use two columns of departures (Weekdays | Weekends), or two short lists, so each rider reads only their own column. If you keep one list, mark the exceptions so they stand out from "Daily" (bold, or a note on the time itself) and say the last sailing in words: "Last boat weekdays 17:10, weekends 19:42 arrival."

2. **The heavy grid outweighs the data.** Every cell sits inside a near-black rule about 2 px thick, so the lines are as dark as the numbers and darker than everything except the title. Each row, which is the real unit (one sailing), gets cut into four separate boxes. Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 157: table rules "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell." Tufte and Wilke make the same point about non-data ink. This is a **convention** broken at a cost: the eye reads the boxes before the times. Fix (approved direction): remove the borders, or replace them with space or a pale tone, so the lines don't compete with the content. Keep thin pale horizontal rules or alternate row tints at most, and no vertical rules.

3. **The table is stretched to the full page width.** Columns about 240 to 300 px wide hold five-character values, so departure and arrival sit about 300 px apart while rows are only about 57 px apart. Space should hold the row together, but it pulls the row apart, and the thick rules are left to do that job. This is a **convention** (Arnheim, on proximity). Fix direction: size the columns to their content so the whole table is a compact block about half the current width. Leave the spare space to the right as one deliberate open area. That also makes fix 2 possible, because tight rows no longer need lines to hold them together.

4. **The copy leaves gaps a stranger has to fill.** "Spring season" gives no dates, so a rider can't tell whether this table applies today. "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" have been clipped to the point of sounding like code. The page also gives only one direction, with no sign of whether there's a return table. Fix direction: give the date range ("1 March to 31 May"), write the stops in full ("Leaves North Landing", "Arrives Market Quay"), and link to or name the return timetable. **Defect** for the missing dates, since a wrong season means a wrong boat. The rest is **taste**.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Period" column (Morning, Morning, Midday…) only repeats what the times already say and takes a quarter of the width. Drop it, or turn it into light group labels between rows.
- The times are set in proportional figures: in "11:00" and "11:12" the colon sits left of the colons above and below, so the column edge wobbles. Switch to tabular figures (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 4.4.3).

**What's working.**
- The order is right: rows sorted by departure time, with departure before arrival. A rider scans down one column and finds their time, so keep that order whatever layout you choose.
- The type is calm and plain, with a clear three-level scale: a bold title, a grey subtitle, and a bold header row over regular body text. Nothing is decorative and nothing is too small.
- Both times use the 24-hour format with leading zeros (06:40), which leaves no doubt about am and pm on a timetable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the table behaves on a narrow phone screen. At four columns this wide it will probably scroll sideways or squeeze, so check it at 375 px.
