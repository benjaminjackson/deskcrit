**First read.** The eye lands on "Pricing", drops to three dollar figures of equal weight, and reads across them as peers. Then it goes back up to the blue "Start free" in the top-right corner, because that is the only thing on the page you can act on. It reads as a pricing page for a scheduling or calendar SaaS. It is clean, calm and could belong to anyone. When you reach the bottom of the cards there is nowhere to go.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the pricing page for a calendar and booking product. A small-team buyer should be able to work out which plan fits and start on it.

**Verdict.** It is tidy but it does not do its job. It shows prices and gives the buyer no way to choose one. The cards have no action and no plan is suggested. The feature lists leave open what each plan actually includes, and that is the question a buyer came here to settle.

**What would move it most**

1. **The cards end without an action, and the only button doesn't say what it starts.** This is a defect. Each card stops after "Email reminders" / "Reports" / "Priority support". There is no button and no "Choose Team", so a buyer who has decided has to travel to the nav's "Start free" (`a.cta`, 1107,14). That button doesn't say which plan it starts, or whether "free" means a trial, a free tier or a free Solo plan. Nothing on the page mentions anything free, so a careful buyer will hesitate at exactly the point where they should be committing. Pricing pages conventionally put an action at the foot of every plan (Jakob's law, Nielsen/NN/g). Putting the next action where the decision happens is common sense; the principle behind it is not on the approved reading list.
   - Give every card its own button, named for the plan and what happens next ("Start 14-day Team trial").
   - Make one of them the filled primary, on the plan you want most people on, and badge that card "Most popular". At the moment the three cards are drawn identically, so the page offers no default and the buyer does all the comparing on their own.
   - Then change the nav button's label to match the offer.

2. **You can't tell how the plans relate, so you can't tell what you're paying for.** This is a defect, and it sits on prices, which are the lines buyers hold you to. Each list names three different features. Read literally, the $29 Team plan has no booking page and no email reminders, and Business has no shared availability or reports. Most buyers will assume each tier includes the one below it, but the page never says so, and some will read it the other way and stop. The "/ month" (on `div.price` for all three plans) can also be read two ways:
   - per account, so $29 covers the whole team;
   - per user, so $29 × the team.

   "Teams of every size" in the lead makes the per-user reading likely. The wrong reading means either a surprise invoice or a buyer who walks away from a plan they could afford. This fails Norman's conceptual model (*The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2): the screen doesn't show how the parts relate. The fix:
   - Start the Team and Business lists with "Everything in Solo, plus:" / "Everything in Team, plus:".
   - Say "per month, billed monthly", and say "for your whole team" or "per user", whichever is true.
   - Say what "1 calendar" and "Up to 10 calendars" mean in people, since buyers count people, not calendars.

3. **The page title and the prices are almost the same size.** This is a convention. "Pricing" is 44 px bold (`h1`) and the prices are 40 px bold (`type_sizes`: 44 and 40). They read as one level, so the page's largest type is spent on a word that the underlined nav item already tells you. The same near-collisions run down the page:
   - 22 vs 20: brand vs plan names
   - 16 vs 15: nav and "/ month" vs the feature lists

   That makes seven sizes where four would carry the page. Bringhurst (3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale") asks for a few distinct sizes, and Dair (ch. 8) says a contrast must be obvious or it looks like an accident. The fix: drop the h1 to a modest size, or replace it with a line that does work (see Smaller things). Let the prices be the largest thing on the page, and merge 22/20 and 16/15.

**Smaller things.**
- The lead paragraph (`p.lead`) breaks to a two-word runt, "you like." Rebalance the break or shorten the line (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The lead copy ("Simple plans for teams of every size") could sit on any SaaS page. Spend it on what a buyer actually worries about, such as which plan fits a team of their size, or the free trial if there is one. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- **The three cards.** They sit on a clean grid: equal 300 px cards, equal gutters, matching left edges at 191/519/847. Plan name, price and features line up on the same baselines across all three, which makes comparing across a row easy. Keep that alignment when you add the buttons.
- **Restrained colour.** There is one blue accent on cool neutrals, and the lowest text contrast is 6.8:1 (`a.cta`, `span.brand`). Once the per-card buttons use that blue, it will mark the action unmistakably.
- **Current-page marker.** The nav marks the current page with an underline, not colour alone.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Lower page.** The page ends 230 px below the cards with no FAQ, billing terms or footer (`page_height` 800). In a live browser, check whether that is intended. Billing questions usually get answered there.
