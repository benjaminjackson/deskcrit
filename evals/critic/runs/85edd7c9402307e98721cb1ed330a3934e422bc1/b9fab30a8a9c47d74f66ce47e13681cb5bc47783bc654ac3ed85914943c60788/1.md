**First read.** The bold serif headline gets the eye first, then the large navy slice with "38%". The second thing it goes to is the grey "25%" at top left, because "Other" is the second-largest slice on the page. Only after that does it cross to the legend to learn what the colours mean. It reads as a page from a company annual report, written for shareholders or the board. It feels calm and competent, and it gives you nothing to react to.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in an annual report that tells shareholders and board members where the company's operating money went in fiscal 2026, so they can judge how it is being spent.

**Verdict.** The craft is clean, but the page doesn't do its job. It shows five shares of an unknown total, with nothing to compare them to, so a shareholder learns the mix but can't tell whether anything is big, small, up, down, good or bad. The quarter of costs filed under "Other" makes that worse.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no total and no comparison, so the numbers can't be judged (defect).** The title "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" (`h1`) and the dek give a topic and a period. They never say how much money the 100% stands for, and they never compare with fiscal 2025. A reader can't tell whether 12% on cloud hosting is a worry or an achievement. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2) says a figure needs its unit, its period, and a comparison such as the change from last period. Fix: state the total operating cost in currency, give each category its amount and its change from last year, and rewrite the headline so it states what that comparison shows rather than the topic. That headline point is my own judgment, but the data to support it has to be on the page first.
2. **"Other" is 25%, the second-largest category, and nothing explains it (defect, in the content).** The grey slice at top left and the last legend row hide a quarter of spending under a label that tells the reader nothing. On an annual report page a reader will take that as either sloppy or evasive. Fix: break "Other" down until no single unlabelled share is bigger than the smallest named category (9%). Or add a footnote saying what it contains.
3. **The labels are split from the data, and every number appears twice (convention).** The slices show percentages but no names. The legend at right shows names, swatches and the same percentages again. So the eye has to match colours across about 300 px to learn that "16%" is rent, and the second set of numbers adds nothing. Tufte and Wilke both call for labelling at the data rather than through a legend. Fix: put the category name with its percentage on or beside each slice and drop the legend. Better still, use a sorted bar chart, which leaves room for the amount and the prior-year figure from finding 1.

**Smaller things.**
- The white percentage labels on the coloured slices look below 4.5:1. "12%" on orange looks about 3:1 or lower, "16%" on teal about 3.2:1, and "9%" on purple just under 4.5:1. At about 18 px bold they don't count as large text. The facts file didn't measure these SVG labels, so measure them. Fix: use dark text on the lighter slices, or deepen the fills (WCAG 1.4.3).
- The page is heavier on the left. The pie fills the left half, the legend stops at y≈375 and at x≈1060 while the header runs to 1216, and the lower right (about 600×300 px) is left empty. If you move to bars with direct labels, the chart can span the full content width and the gap goes away.

**What's working.**
- The slices run clockwise from 12 o'clock in descending order, with "Other" last in neutral grey. That is the right order, and the grey correctly keeps the catch-all out of the foreground. Keep both if you keep a pie.
- The type is well layered. A 32 px bold serif headline (`h1`, 15.1:1 contrast) and an 18 px serif dek (`p.dek`) sit over 15 px Inter in the table. A letterspaced, coloured kicker marks the section. Each level has one job and all the text clears contrast. Only the source line is close to the limit, at 4.52:1 (`p.source`).
- The dek and source line do their jobs. "Twelve months to 30 June 2026" removes any doubt about what fiscal 2026 means, and "rounded to whole percent" gets ahead of totals that don't add to 100.
