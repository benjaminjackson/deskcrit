**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Plan the week with your whole team", jumps right to the blue "Create account" button, then reads up the form. It's plainly a sign-up page for a team planning tool. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, but nothing on it is specific to this product.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the sign-up page for a team planning SaaS, meant for someone deciding whether to try it, and its job is to get them through the form.

**Verdict.** It's well built and easy to complete, but it gives a stranger almost no reason to sign up. Half the screen is a headline any planning tool could use, with nothing that shows the product. The one concrete promise, "free … for up to five people", can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The left half doesn't sell anything (defect).** The headline, "Plan the week with your whole team", and the subhead could sit on any of a dozen competitors' pages. Below the subhead, about 60% of the left column is empty background. Someone who arrives undecided learns what category this is but not why this product, so the form on the right asks for five fields of commitment and has earned none of it. The empty lower left also makes the page lopsided: a short text block floats high on the left against a tall card centred on the right, so the right side carries most of the weight (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Direction: use the left column to show the product, such as a real screenshot of a planned week. Or write a line that sets the product against what the viewer uses now, e.g. "Your team's week on one board, not in a spreadsheet and three Slack threads", so they can judge the value on terms they already trust. That same content fixes the balance.
2. **The free offer can be read two ways (defect).** "Start a free workspace for up to five people" is the only commitment on the page, and the viewer will remember it. Reading A: it's free for good while you stay at five people or fewer. Reading B: it's a free trial, and the page doesn't say how long. "You can invite the rest of the team later" adds a third question: does inviting a sixth person start a bill? If the viewer assumes A and the truth is B, you lose their trust the first time you send an invoice. Direction: say it outright, e.g. "Free forever for up to 5 people. Paid plans start when you add a sixth." If there's no card required, say that too, since it's the doubt that keeps people from filling in forms.
3. **The form hides two things the user needs (defect).** The password rule, "At least 10 characters", is only placeholder text. It disappears on the first keystroke, so people have to remember it or find out when they get an error (Nielsen, heuristic 6: recognition rather than recall). Also, nothing marks which fields are required. "Company" in particular could be optional for a freelancer or a small team, and with no mark people will either stall or make something up. Direction: put the password rule under the field as helper text that stays visible. Mark optional fields with "(optional)" after the label, or put asterisks on required fields and explain them once (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").

**Smaller things.**
- "Sign In" at the foot of the card is the same grey, size and weight as "Already have an account?", with no underline, so it doesn't look clickable. Make it the brand blue with an underline (WCAG 1.4.1). Also change it to sentence case, "Sign in", to match "Create account".
- The input borders are a pale blue-grey on white and look well under 3:1, so the field edges are faint for low-vision users. Check the border colour against white, and darken it until it reaches 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11).
- The "Tallyboard" wordmark (around y 150) and the card title "Create your account" (around y 148) almost share a line but miss by a couple of pixels. Either align them exactly or move one clearly off the line.

**What's working.**
- The form itself is well built: labels sit above the fields and stay visible, first and last name are the only fields side by side, the spacing groups each label with its field, and there's one unmistakable primary button. Keep all of that.
- The type is disciplined. There are about four sizes with clear steps (headline, card title, body, labels), it's all one family, and each level uses weight and colour as well as size. The palette is one neutral range plus one blue, so the button is the only strong colour and the eye goes to it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus rings on the fields and button (whether they're visible, and at 3:1); whether the password rule is checked as the user types; how the two-column layout stacks on a phone.
