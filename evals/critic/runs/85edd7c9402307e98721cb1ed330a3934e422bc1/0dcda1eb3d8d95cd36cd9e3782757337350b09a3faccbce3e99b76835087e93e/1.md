**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "River ferry timetable", drops to the header row, then runs down the first column: "Morning, Morning, Midday, Midday, Afternoon…". It reads as a clean, quiet table from a generic web template. It's easy on the eye, but the first thing I scan tells me nothing the times don't already say. The day each boat runs, which is what decides whether I can catch it, sits in the far right column.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a public timetable page where a rider checks when the next ferry leaves north landing on a given day.

**Verdict.** It's legible and tidy, but it's laid out for the person who wrote the schedule, not the person catching the boat. Which boats run on my day is the main thing, and it's hidden in the last column, on the far side of three columns of lesser or repeated information.

**What would move it most**
1. **Which day a boat runs is the deciding fact, and it comes last.** On a Saturday the 12:40 and the 17:10 don't run. On a weekday the last boat is 17:10, because the 19:30 runs only at weekends. To find either fact you have to read every row across about 850 px to the "Runs" column and filter in your head. The person most likely to get this wrong is a weekday evening rider, who sees 19:30 at the bottom and turns up for a boat that doesn't come. That is a **defect**: it misleads on the one thing the viewer acts on. The fix is to organise the table by day, not by time slot. Use two lists, Weekdays and Weekends, each with only the times that run. Or keep one list but leave "Daily" unmarked and flag the exceptions right next to the time. Either way, the last boat on each kind of day should be visible at a glance.
2. **The "Period" column holds the lead position and adds nothing.** "Morning" for 06:40 and "Afternoon" for 15:20 just repeat the time in words, and four of the seven labels are repeats. The column that gets read first should hold the departure time, the number the rider is looking for. This is a **defect** in that it slows the scan (Nielsen's heuristic 8, aesthetic and minimalist design: every extra piece of information competes with the ones that matter). Drop the column. If the time-of-day grouping matters, show it with a little extra space between groups of rows, not a repeated label.
3. **The table is stretched across the full content width, so each row falls apart.** There's about 300 px of empty space between "06:40" and its arrival "06:52", and again before "Daily". The column starts look like about 120, 360, 660 and 950 px, so the gaps are uneven too (Müller-Brockmann: one fixed interval between columns). The eye has to follow the faint rule to match a departure with its arrival and its days. That's a **convention** break, and here it costs reading accuracy. Size the columns to their content and pack them left, so a row reads as one line: "06:40 → 06:52 · Daily". Every arrival is exactly 12 minutes after departure, so you could also say "Crossing takes 12 minutes" once and drop the arrivals column entirely. That's my **taste**, but it halves what the eye has to read.
4. **The copy leaves out things a rider will act on.**
   - "Spring season" has no dates, so a rider in late May can't tell whether this timetable still applies. Give the start and end dates.
   - The page covers only one direction. A rider going north to the market will want to know the times back from market quay. If there's no return service, say so; if there is, show it.
   - "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are clipped versions of the place names in the subtitle ("North landing", "market quay"). Use the full names so the headers need no decoding.
   
   This is a **defect** because dates and times are exactly what riders hold the operator to.

**Smaller things.**
- The times use proportional figures. "11:00" and "17:10" are narrower than "06:40", so the colons wobble down both time columns. Switch on tabular figures so the colons line up (Bringhurst).

**What's working.**
- The type is controlled: one sans-serif family and three clear levels (bold title, grey subtitle, bold column headers over regular cells). Nothing is fighting, so keep that restraint when you restructure.
- The row rules are pale and thin, so they guide the eye along a row without boxing anything in. That's the right weight for a table (Lupton). Keep them light if the table gets narrower.
- The subtitle names the route ("North landing to market quay") straight under the title, so the direction is clear before the table starts.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the table reflows on a phone, where most riders will check it. Four widely spaced columns probably won't fit at 375 px. Check it in a narrow browser.
