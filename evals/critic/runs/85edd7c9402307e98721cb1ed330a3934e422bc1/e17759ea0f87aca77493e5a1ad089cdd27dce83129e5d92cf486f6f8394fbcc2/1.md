**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, "Shipments peak each third quarter," then to the tallest bar (Q3 2026, 705), then across a row of eight identical blue bars. It reads as a calm, competent slide from a board deck. It feels trustworthy, maybe a bit too even: every bar, including the forecast, carries the same weight.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is page 3 of a 12-page sales review for a company board. It should tell directors in a few seconds how shipments are going and what to expect for the quarter that hasn't happened yet.

**Verdict.** It's clean and easy to read, but it makes two mistakes that matter in a boardroom. The Q4 2026 forecast is drawn exactly like the seven real quarters. And the headline reports the seasonal pattern the board already knows, while the more important news (every quarter is up 11–17% on the year before) is left for directors to work out across a four-bar gap.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast bar looks like a fact.** The Q4 2026 bar (560, far right) has the same solid blue, the same bold label and the same x-axis label as the seven real quarters. The only sign that it's an estimate is a bracket at the end of the subtitle and the source line in small grey type at the bottom. A director glancing at the chart will read 560 as something that already happened, and numbers like that get quoted back later. *Defect* (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16: projections should look different from observed data). Fix: make the bar look different, for example with a lighter tint, an outline only, or hatching. Label it "Q4 2026 (forecast)" on the axis. If the September forecast has a range, show it as an error bar.

2. **The headline states the obvious and leaves out the news.** "Shipments peak each third quarter" describes the pattern but doesn't tell the board anything new. Outdoor gear peaking in summer won't surprise anyone in the room, and two years is a thin basis for "each." Look at the same quarters a year apart, though: Q1 410→455, Q2 520→590, Q3 610→705 and Q4 480→560 (forecast). Every quarter grew, and growth is speeding up. To see that now, a reader has to compare bars four positions apart and do the maths themselves. *Taste, argued on the job:* a board slide should end on what the numbers mean for the board. Fix: give the headline the growth, in the viewer's terms. For example: "Every quarter shipped more than a year earlier; Q3 was up 16%." Then make that comparison easy to see. The usual way is to group the bars by quarter, with 2025 in grey and 2026 in blue, and put the change above each pair. The Q3 peak still shows in that layout.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: the subtitle, the "Units shipped by quarter" chart title, the "Thousands of units" line, the header, and the axis labels look like about 16, 15, 14, 14 and 13 px. Sizes that close don't read as separate levels. Set them to two sizes and let weight and grey do the rest.
- Three lines of text stack up before the data starts: the subtitle, the chart title and the unit line. The chart title repeats what the subtitle says. Combine them into one line, such as "Units shipped to retailers, thousands," and fold the forecast note into the axis label.

**What's working.**
- Every bar has its value printed on it, the gridlines are pale, and the y-axis starts at zero. The data is clearly the darkest thing on the chart and the scale is honest. Keep this when you restyle the forecast bar.
- The page frame is disciplined. The brand mark and meeting line at the top, the source and page number at the bottom, and the headline all share one left edge, which makes it feel like part of a consistent deck.
- The serif headline against sans-serif body text gives one clear entry point without needing colour.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- There's an empty band about 120 px tall below the footer. If the real slide looks like that, the content sits too high. Check whether that's the slide's actual proportions or just the capture size.
