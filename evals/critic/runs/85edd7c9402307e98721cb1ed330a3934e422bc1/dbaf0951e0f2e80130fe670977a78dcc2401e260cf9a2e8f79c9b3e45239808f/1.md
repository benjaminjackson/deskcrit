**First read.** The eye goes first to the tall blue bar at the left, then down the row of six differently colored bars, then to the bold black data labels. The title registers after that. The text on the right is read last, if at all. It reads as a clean, calm internal growth report for a marketing or leadership team. It looks tidy, and a bit like a chart-library default.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a growth page where a marketing lead or executive sees where Q3 sign-ups came from and what is changing because of it.

**Verdict.** The chart is honest and easy to read, but it doesn't make the page's point. The conclusion, that organic search grew and podcast ads and events are weak, sits only in the sidebar. The chart's six random colors give competing signals, and the chart can't back up the "up from Q2" claim.

**What would move it most**

1. **Six colors for one series, and the wrong ones carry meaning.** This is a defect and a convention.
   - **Where:** every bar has its own hue: blue, orange, red, teal, green, purple. The x-axis labels already name each channel, so the colors encode nothing.
   - **What the viewer gets:** the colors suggest meaning anyway. Red on Paid social reads as "problem" and green on Podcast ads reads as "healthy". The sidebar says roughly the opposite: podcast ads are one of the two channels that brought "fewer than 700 accounts". And no bar stands out for the two things the page wants you to notice.
   - **Source:** Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (pitfall 12), and Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization* (ch. 19.1). One series gets one color, plus a second color for the one point the reader must notice.
   - **Fix:** draw the bars in one muted neutral. Accent Organic search, the gain. If the budget move matters as much, give Podcast ads and Events a second, quieter mark. The chart will then say what the sidebar says.

2. **The story's comparison isn't in the chart.** This is a convention.
   - **What the viewer gets:** the sidebar's main claim is "up from a quarter in Q2", but the chart shows Q3 alone. The viewer has to take the growth on trust and can't tell whether Referral or Email also moved.
   - **Source:** Few, pitfall 2. A figure needs a comparison, such as the previous period, to tell good from bad.
   - **Fix:** put Q2 next to each bar, either as a pale paired bar or as a tick or "▲ from 1,210" note beside the label. The claim is then visible, and so is any change in the other channels.

3. **The title names the topic, and the conclusion is in a column nobody reads first.** This is taste.
   - **What the viewer gets:** "New sign-ups by channel" tells them what the chart is, not what happened. The finding sits 68 px to the right in body-size text, under a 15 px heading. The eye reaches it last, and nothing links it to the bars it discusses.
   - **Why it's taste:** the approved sources don't state this rule. My case is that on a page whose job is a decision (moving the events budget), the decision should be the first words the viewer reads.
   - **Fix:** make the title the finding ("Organic search now brings a third of sign-ups") and keep the current title as the subtitle. You could also tie each sidebar note to its bar with a short label on the chart.

4. **The copy: "a quarter" can be read two ways.** This is a defect.
   - **Where:** "up from a quarter in Q2" sits under the heading "What changed this quarter". "A quarter" can read as one fourth or as a three-month period, and the second reading makes no sense, so the viewer has to read the line twice.
   - **Same problem:** "a third" and "fewer than 700" are vague where you have exact numbers (1,840 of 5,400 is 34%; podcast ads and events together are 670).
   - **Fix:** "up from 25% in Q2", "34% of new accounts", "together brought 670 accounts (12%)". Leaders will quote these numbers back and hold you to them.

**Smaller things.**
- **Type scale:** five sizes, 26, 18, 15, 14 and 13 px (`type_sizes`). The 15 px body (`aside > p`), 14 px nav and 13 px source line read as near-misses rather than levels. Use one size for the nav and the source line, and use color for the rest (Bringhurst; Refactoring UI).
- **Y-axis:** every bar already has its exact value, so the axis numbers and gridlines repeat the data labels. Drop the axis numbers or the data labels. This is taste; the gridlines are already pale, so it costs little.

**What's working.**
- **Bars:** sorted by value from zero, labeled directly at each bar, so you get both the ranking and the exact figure without a legend.
- **Source line:** gives the date range and the total (5,400), so the reader can check the claims in the sidebar.
- **Text:** every block passes contrast comfortably (lowest 6.11:1, `p.sub`, `p.source`, nav). Inter at 15 px with 1.55 line height in the sidebar reads easily.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Nav targets:** the text boxes are 21 px tall (`span.on`, `nav > span`), under the 24 px minimum. Check the real click area and the focus styling in a live browser.
