**First read.** The eye goes to the bold headline, then down into the three paragraphs, and nothing pulls it anywhere else. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry, and it feels calm and tidy but also anonymous, like default page styling. The first thing I wanted was "when is the next boat?", and I had to read every paragraph to find that the page never says.

**What it's for.** You didn't tell me, so I'm assuming it's a web page that tells passengers when the ferry runs and what the rules are, often read on the way to the landing.

**Verdict.** The type is clean and easy to read, but the page fails at its main job. The facts a passenger plans around are vague and spread through running prose, so nobody can use this page to catch a boat.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times can't be acted on (defect).** "Between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last boat. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can be read two ways: the market quay only, or both sides. It also gives no clock time, and dusk moves by more than an hour across the winter. "In winter" gives no dates for when the hourly timetable starts. If a passenger at the north landing reads "the quay" as their own side, they miss the last boat home. These are the lines people remember and hold the operator to, so they have to be exact. The page also sends readers to the printed timetable and the noticeboard "at each landing", which a reader on the web can't see. **Fix:** give the first and last departure from each landing, in numbers. Give the winter start and end dates and the winter last sailing. Then either show this week's changes on the page or say where online to find them.
2. **The facts are buried in sentences (defect).** The numbers a passenger scans for ("forty minutes", "twelve minutes", "one boat each hour") are written as words in the middle of paragraphs. Someone checking on a phone at the landing has to read all three paragraphs to find them. The paragraphs already split by topic (crossings, bicycles, winter), but nothing labels those topics. **Fix:** put the timetable facts in a short block near the top: route, how often, crossing time, first and last boats, winter hours. Use numbers, not words. Below it, give the bicycle and winter notes a short heading each so they can be found at a glance.
3. **The headline doesn't say which ferry (copy).** "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any ferry's page, and "Notes" names a category, not the content. **Fix:** name the route, for example "North landing to Market quay ferry: times and rules". Then the reader knows at once that they're on the right page and what it holds.

**What's working.**
- The text is set well:
  - The lines run about 65 to 70 characters (in the 45 to 75 range Bringhurst gives).
  - The line spacing is comfortable.
  - The right edge is evenly ragged.
  - There are two clear levels: a bold headline and plain body text in one family.
  Keep all of this when you add structure.
- Each paragraph covers one topic, and the bicycle paragraph says who does what and in what order. That plain, specific voice is right for this page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page reflows on a phone, where most passengers will read it. Check that the body text stays large and the column fills the narrow screen.
