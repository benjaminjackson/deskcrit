**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline first, then drops to the big blue slice and its "38%". From there it has to jump about 300 px right to the legend to find out what 38% means, then jump back to match the other colours. It reads as a calm, well-made annual-report page about costs, and it is easy to trust. Then two questions come up and the page answers neither: what is the 25% grey "Other", and 25% of how much money?

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's one page of a company's annual report, read on screen by shareholders, staff or lenders, and its job is to show where the operating money went and what that means.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and correct, but it doesn't make its case yet. The chart sits apart from its own labels, the title gives a topic instead of a finding, and a quarter of all costs is in a slice called "Other" that nothing on the page explains. The reader leaves knowing the proportions but not the story or the amounts.

**What would move it most**
1. **The second-biggest category is "Other", at 25%, with no explanation.** *(defect)* The grey slice at top left is bigger than rent, cloud hosting or marketing. To an annual-report reader, an unnamed quarter of operating costs looks like something being smoothed over, even if it isn't. The footnote talks about rounding, not about what "Other" contains. Fix: break "Other" into its next two or three named lines so the leftover is clearly the smallest slice. If that isn't possible, add one line under the legend that says what it holds ("travel, insurance, professional fees…").
2. **The labels are in a separate legend, so the reader has to look back and forth.** *(convention: Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*)* Each slice shows a percentage but no name. The names are in a legend across a wide gap and can only be matched to slices by colour swatch, which is the hardest case for anyone with weak colour vision. The legend also repeats every percentage already printed on the slices, so the page states each number twice and still makes you decode. Fix: put the category name on or next to each slice, with leader lines for the small ones, and drop the legend. That also lets the pie move right or grow, which fixes the next point.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** *(taste, common sense)* "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" only promises a chart. The reader has to work out the point themselves, which is probably "people are our biggest cost". On a report page most readers skim, the headline is the only line many of them will take away. Fix: keep the topic as the kicker ("4.2 Costs" already does that job) and make the title the finding, for example "Salaries and benefits took 38% of operating costs".
4. **There are shares but no amount and no comparison.** *(convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design")* Every number is a percentage of a total the page never states, and nothing compares it with fiscal 2025. The reader can't tell whether marketing at 9% went up or down, or whether 12% on cloud hosting is a lot of money. In an annual report, the change is usually what a reader came for. Fix: give the total operating cost in the subtitle, and add last year's share, or the change in points, as a second column in the label list.

**Smaller things.**
- The white "12%" on orange looks well under 3:1. The white "16%" on teal looks borderline. Measure both, and use dark text on those two slices, as the grey slice already does.
- The lower right quadrant is empty: the pie fills the left side, while the legend is a small block at the top right. The page looks heavy on the left with leftover space at the bottom right. Direct labels (point 2) would solve this. Otherwise, centre the legend vertically on the pie and pull it closer.

**What's working.**
- The order of the slices is right. They run clockwise from 12 o'clock from largest to smallest, with "Other" last, and the legend uses the same order. Keep that order when you relabel.
- The type system is calm and clear. There's a serif headline, a serif subtitle at a clearly smaller size, a letterspaced small-caps kicker in a warm brown, and a sans for data and footer. Each level is distinct, and all of them share the 64 px left edge.
- The subtitle states the measure and the exact period ("twelve months to 30 June 2026"), and the footer gives the source and the rounding. That's the kind of care that makes a report page believable.
