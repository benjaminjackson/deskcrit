**First read.** The eye lands on "See where every dollar goes this month", drops to the subhead, then to the white "Try it free" button. Then it drifts right into a big empty navy field and finds nothing there. It reads as the landing page for a personal budgeting app, aimed at individuals and couples. It feels calm, trustworthy and competent, but it is a template: navy hero, a sans-serif face, two buttons, three feature columns.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a consumer budgeting app's landing page, meant to get a visitor to start a free trial.

**Verdict.** The words and the order of importance do their job. The page is let down by what it doesn't show: the headline says "See", and there is nothing to see. The empty right half of the hero leaves the page leaning left and leaves the product as a claim the visitor has to take on trust.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero promises a view and shows none.** The headline is about seeing your money, but the right ~40% of the navy band (roughly x 800–1216) is empty. All the weight sits in the left column: headline, subhead and both buttons. The right side has nothing to balance it, so the band tips left and looks like it is waiting for an image that never loaded (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). The bigger cost is to the pitch. A visitor can't tell this tracker from the one they already use, because nothing on screen shows what "where every dollar goes" looks like. Put the product on that side: one real-looking month of category bars with one over-limit alert. That one picture sells the headline, the subhead's "heads-up before you go over", and the alerts feature all at once. If the space is meant to be empty, the headline column needs to widen or be centred so the space looks chosen, not left over. *Defect* (balance, and the core claim isn't shown). If an image is meant to be there and failed to load, check that first.
2. **The strongest reason to trust this app is in the smallest type on the page.** "Read-only access… can never move money" is the line that answers a newcomer's biggest worry about connecting their bank. Right now it sits third in a row of 15 px feature text, below the fold for many laptops. Move it up next to the buttons, for example as a short line under "Try it free" ("Read-only: Penny Path can see your balances but can never move your money"). That way it lands at the moment of commitment. *Taste*, and I'd argue it hard for a finance product.
3. **"Try it free" is a generic ask.** It doesn't say what you get or how long the trial lasts, and it gives the visitor nothing to compare against what they already use. Name what the first step gets them, and the terms if there are any: "Connect an account and see this month free", or "Free for 30 days, no card". The tour button already offers a lower-commitment option, so the primary button can afford to be specific. *Taste / copy*. If there are trial terms, leaving them out of a commitment button is a defect.

**Smaller things.** None from the checklist.

**What's working.**
- **The headline and subhead are specific and spoken to the visitor.** They name the outcome ("every dollar… this month") and the mechanism (connect, set limits, get warned). Keep this voice.
- **The button order is right.** A solid white primary sits next to an outlined secondary of the same size, so there's no doubt which one to press.
- **The grid is disciplined.** A 64 px left edge runs from the logo through the headline to the first feature. The three feature columns are evenly spaced, and each short bold heading sits tight to its text. The page reads cleanly because of it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**

- **The empty right half of the hero.** Confirm whether an image is meant to be there.
- **Hover and focus states on the two buttons and the nav links.** A still can't show them.
