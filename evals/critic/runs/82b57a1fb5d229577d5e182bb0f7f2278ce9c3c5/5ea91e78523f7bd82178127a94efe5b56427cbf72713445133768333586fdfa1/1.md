**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Before you board" and then hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, which read as a grey block. It looks like a notice board for a river ferry, aimed at passengers. The heading is calm and modern, but the body text feels like it's shouting, so the page reads like a warning even though it's just routine information.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web info panel that tells ferry passengers how the service runs (frequency, crossing time, bikes, winter hours) so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the order of importance is clear, but the body text in capitals makes the useful part the slowest part to read. Behind that, the copy leaves out the one fact a passenger plans around: when the first and last boats actually run.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are set in all capitals.** All three paragraphs below the heading, about 70 characters a line, are in caps. Capitals take away the shapes of the words. The reader has to work through each line letter by letter, and on a page that people scan for one fact ("can I bring my bike?") that is exactly the wrong cost. It also splits the piece into two styles: the heading and the small "PASSENGER NOTES" label are restrained, and the body reads as alarmed. That changes the tone from "here's how it works" to "WARNING". This is a convention break and a defect: Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps": "Don't capitalize whole paragraphs"; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read." Fix: set sentences and paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions. The "PASSENGER NOTES" label can stay in caps as it is.
2. **The timetable copy is vague exactly where the passenger has to act on it.** "Between early morning and late evening" gives no times, so a reader can't tell whether a 6:30 boat or a 22:00 return exists. "In winter" has no dates, so someone travelling in late October can't tell which timetable applies. "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends them to the landing to find out what they needed to know before leaving home. Times and dates are what a reader holds the operator to, so leaving them open to several readings is a defect, not a style note. Fix: give the first and last sailings, the dates of the winter timetable, and a link to the timetable itself, not just a note that one exists. (This is common sense; no catalog entry covers it.)

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean: a small grey tracked label, a bold sentence-case heading, then the body. That's three clearly different levels with no sizes that nearly match. Keep it when you change the body case.
- One left edge carries the label, heading and paragraphs. Paragraph lines run about 70 characters with comfortable leading, and the white space is gathered on the right and at the bottom rather than scattered. Once the text is in mixed case, the setting is already right.
- The bike paragraph is good copy. It says who does what, in what order, and where: "riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail along the side of the deck."
