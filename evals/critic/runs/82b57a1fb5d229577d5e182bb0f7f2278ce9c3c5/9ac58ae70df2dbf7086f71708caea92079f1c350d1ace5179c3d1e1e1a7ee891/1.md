**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: this is a public pricing page for a calendar/booking product. Its job is to get a visitor to pick a plan and start on it.

**Verdict.** It fails its one job. Nothing on the page lets you choose a plan, and nothing helps you choose. The type and spacing are tidy, but the cards are three equal, dead-end boxes. The prices and feature lists leave open exactly what you'd be paying for.

**What would move it most**
1. **No plan has an action, so the page ends where the decision should happen.** Below each card's feature list there is only white space. The only button is the nav's "Start free", which names no plan, and "free" contradicts three paid prices. Does it mean a trial? Which tier does it start? A visitor who has just picked "Team" has to guess that this button is the way in, and still doesn't know what it commits them to. Pricing cards that each end in their own button are the pattern people expect here (Nielsen, Jakob's law). **Defect.** Fix: put one button at the foot of every card that names the plan and the terms, like "Start 14-day Team trial". Make the recommended plan's button the only solid one. The nav button then becomes secondary or repeats the trial wording.
2. **No plan stands out, so the visitor has to do all the comparing.** The three cards match in size, border and weight, and the prices are equally loud. When three or more elements compete like this, nothing reads as the place to start (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: make one element clearly the first thing seen). For a pricing page that means no plan says "most teams pick this". **Defect.** Fix: mark one plan, probably Team, with a label, a coloured border or a tinted background, and keep the other two quiet.
3. **The copy leaves open what you're paying for.** "$29 / month" doesn't say whether that's per team or per user. On a plan called "Team" both readings are likely, and the wrong one means a bill several times bigger than expected. That makes it a defect, not a style note. The feature lists also look like they lose features as you move up: Team doesn't list a booking page or email reminders, and Business doesn't list reports. Nothing says "Everything in Solo, plus…", so a careful reader may think the higher tiers drop features (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*: the screen should show how the parts relate). "Reports" and "Shared availability" name categories, not what you get. The subtitle "Simple plans for teams of every size" could sit on any SaaS pricing page and doesn't tell you why this one. **Defect** (the price unit and inheritance), **taste** (the subtitle). Fix: write "per user" or "per team" next to every price, add "Everything in Team, plus:" above each list, and say what a report shows.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav: "Pricing" looks exactly like the other links, even though you're on that page. Mark it with one cue, such as bold, an underline or the brand blue (Laubheimer, NN/g menu checklist).
- Subtitle: "you like." is a runt alone on the second line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Widen the measure slightly or rebreak it so both lines are roughly equal.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale.** I count about four sizes (title, price, plan name, body), with big steps between them. The prices are bold and large next to a light, small "/ month", which is a good way to make the number read first. Keep that.
- **Restraint.** Blue is used only on the wordmark and the one button, so whichever plan button you make solid will instantly be the strongest thing on the page.
- **Alignment.** The three cards share a grid and line up baseline to baseline across columns, so comparing them row by row is easy.

- Hover and focus states on the nav and the button.
