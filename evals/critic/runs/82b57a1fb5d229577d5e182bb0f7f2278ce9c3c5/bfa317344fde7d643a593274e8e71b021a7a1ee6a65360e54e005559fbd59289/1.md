**First read.** My eye lands on the bold serif headline, then drops to the big dark-blue slice and its "38%". After that it goes back and forth between the pie and the legend at top right to find out what each colour means. The next thing it notices is the grey "Other" slice, which is the second-biggest piece of the pie. It reads as a calm, well-made page from an annual report, and it feels tidy but tells me very little.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: one page of a company's annual report that shows shareholders and staff where the year's operating money went.

**Verdict.** The page is well crafted, but it is a neutral pie that answers no question a report reader brings to it. The biggest problem is that the page shows shares and nothing else: no finding in the title, no total, no comparison with last year, and a quarter of costs filed under "Other". A careful reader leaves knowing proportions but not whether anything is good, bad or changing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart gives no context and no conclusion** (defect). There are four gaps:
   - **No total.** The subtitle says "Share of total operating costs" but never gives the total, so "38%" has nothing to stand on.
   - **No comparison.** Without last year's figures the reader can't tell whether salaries growing to 38% is news or business as usual (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a headline number needs a period and a comparison).
   - **A large unexplained "Other".** At 25% it is bigger than rent, hosting or marketing. In a report this looks evasive even when it isn't.
   - **A topic for a title.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" names the subject but doesn't say what happened. That last point is my judgment, not a cited rule.

   Fixes:
   - Put the total in currency in the subtitle or next to the chart.
   - Add last year's share beside each legend value, such as "38% (34%)", or a small change column.
   - Split "Other" into its two or three largest parts, or footnote what it contains.
   - Rewrite the title so it states the finding, for example "Salaries took more than a third of operating costs".
2. **The legend sits apart from the pie, and the layout leaves the right half empty** (convention, partly taste).
   - **Labels away from the data.** The slices show only percentages, and their names are 300 px away in a legend that repeats every number. The reader has to match colours to decode five slices (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, and Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*: label at the data). The legend does at least follow the clockwise order of the slices.
   - **Leftover space and weak alignment.** The legend is pinned to the top of the right column, so a block of about 600 × 300 px below it is empty. The page tips toward the heavy pie on the left (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Nothing in the chart lines up with the rest of the page: the pie starts about 50 px inside the 64 px text margin, and the legend's right edge (~1060 px) lines up with nothing (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).

   Fix: either put the category names on or beside the slices and drop the legend, or treat the right column as a real table with the year-on-year figures from finding 1. A table like that would fill the column and centre it vertically against the pie. Then fix the chart's edges to the page margins.

**Smaller things.**
- The white "12%" on the orange slice looks like about 2.9:1, under the 3:1 needed even for large bold text. The white "16%" on teal looks borderline at about 3.2:1. Measure both. Darken the orange or use dark text on it (WCAG 2.2, SC 1.4.3).

**What's working.**
- The type plan is clear. A serif carries the headline and subtitle, a sans carries everything else, and the sizes step cleanly from about 32 to 18, 15 and 12 px. Keep it.
- The categories are sorted by size with "Other" last and in grey, and the slices start at 12 o'clock and run clockwise in the same order. The ordering is right; only the labelling needs work.
- The chart is restrained: thin white slice borders, no 3D or shadows, and a source line that says the figures are rounded. All of that earns trust.
