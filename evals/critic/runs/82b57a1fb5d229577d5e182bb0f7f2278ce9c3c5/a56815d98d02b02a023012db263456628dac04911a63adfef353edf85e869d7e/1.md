**First read.** The eye goes straight to the red and orange blob in the middle of the weekday rows, then out to the big numbers on the right. Within a second it reads as "support tickets peak late morning on weekdays". The page around the chart is calm and well organised. The chart itself is loud: it looks like a weather map, with red as the alarm colour.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a support lead or team manager uses this reporting screen to see when tickets arrive, so they can staff the desk to match.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the main pattern comes through, but two things get in the way. The rainbow colour scale makes the counts hard to read in order. Worse, the headline stat "Busiest hour, weekdays 10:00" contradicts the chart beside it. A manager could put extra staff on the wrong hour.

**What would move it most**
1. **The busiest-hour stat disagrees with the chart (defect).** The hour labels sit centred over their cells. "09:00" is over the yellow column, 10:00 is the orange column, and the only red (30+) cells are in the **11:00** column on Mon and Fri. On weekdays 11:00 is red, orange, orange, orange, red. 10:00 is orange all five days. So the chart says 11:00 and the stat says 10:00. A viewer who sees both stops trusting both, and one who reads only the stat staffs an hour early. Either the stat or the column-to-label mapping is wrong. Check the data, and if the hour cells are meant to cover something like "10:00–10:59", label them so. Then make the two agree.
2. **A rainbow scale for counts that run from low to high (convention, and Jev found it at 0.88).** The legend runs blue-violet → cyan → teal → green → yellow → orange → red for 0–4 up to 30+. The hues have no built-in order, so the viewer has to check the legend to tell whether green (15–19) is more or less than cyan. Lightness doesn't follow the counts either. Yellow (20–24) is the lightest cell on the chart, so the middle values jump out and the 25–29 orange looks darker and quieter than the yellow. The quiet hours are a fairly dark saturated violet, so two-thirds of the grid is heavy colour that carries no news. Green also reads as "good", in a chart where green just means "medium busy". Use one hue going from light to dark, very pale for 0–4 and darkest for 30+, so that the order of the colours follows the order of the values (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*). The peak then comes forward on its own and the night hours fall back.
3. **It can't be read without full colour vision (defect).** The busy end of the scale is green / yellow / orange / red side by side, around 12:00–16:00 Tue–Thu, and these are the pairs that merge for red-green colour-blind viewers. Nothing but colour carries the count (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1). The single-hue lightness ramp from finding 2 fixes most of this, because it then reads in greyscale. Printing the count in the peak cells, or on hover, would make it exact.

**Smaller things.**
- Title and subtitle name the topic ("When new tickets arrive") but not what it shows. Saying the finding ("Tickets peak late morning on weekdays; weekends stay light") would save the viewer from working it out. This is common sense rather than a published rule.
- "New tickets, September 3,412" sits next to a chart covering "1 to 28 September". The viewer can't tell whether 3,412 covers the whole month or only those 28 days. Name the range on the stat.
- Jev's rainbow-colour finding is covered in finding 2.

**What's working.**
- Every headline number has a comparison under it ("Up 4% on August", "Down 6 min on August"). Keep that.
- The source line says exactly what an hour means: when the ticket was opened, in the team's local time. That heads off the most likely misreading.
- The layout is clean: the chart, legend and source share one left edge, the stat column lines up with the top of the grid, and the hour labels every three hours keep the axis quiet without losing your place.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether cells show exact counts on hover or focus. That matters here, because the bins (for example 25–29) hide the difference between the top cells.
