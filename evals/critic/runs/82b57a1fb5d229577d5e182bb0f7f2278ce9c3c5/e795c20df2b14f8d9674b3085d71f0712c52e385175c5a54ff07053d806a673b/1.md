**First read.** The eye goes to the title, drops to the dark blue block in the late morning of the heatmap, then moves right to the three big numbers. It reads as a monthly arrival-pattern report in a support-desk tool, for whoever plans shifts. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, and that trust is exactly what the numbers then put at risk.

**What it's for.** My assumption: it shows a support lead when tickets arrive across the week, so they can staff to match.

**Verdict.** The layout, palette and chart choice are sound, and the pattern is visible in a second. But the page contradicts itself. The headline stat names one busiest hour and the heatmap shows another, and the monthly total looks too small for the cells. A staffing report that disagrees with itself fails at its one job.

**What would move it most**
1. **The busiest-hour stat disagrees with the chart.** The 09:00 label sits over the left edge of the column that starts at 09:00. Counting from there, the 10:00 column is 25–29 every weekday. The 11:00 column is as dark or darker, and it holds the only two 30+ cells (Mon and Fri). So the chart says weekdays peak at 11:00, while the right rail says "Busiest hour, weekdays 10:00". A lead who staffs from the number will be one hour early for the real peak.

   Part of the cause is that the hour labels are left-aligned to cell edges. A viewer can't tell whether "09:00" names a cell or a boundary, so the same chart can be read with every hour shifted by one.

   This is a defect: the display misstates the data (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2, graphical integrity and clear labelling). Fix it in two steps:
   - Check which value is right and make the stat and the cells agree.
   - Centre each hour label over its column, or label it as a range ("10–11"), so the axis can only be read one way.
2. **The monthly total doesn't square with the heatmap.** By eye, a typical weekday row adds up to roughly 200–250 tickets using bucket midpoints (seven cells at 20+, a dozen night cells at 0–4). Weekends add about 60–80 each. That makes an "average week" of roughly 1,200–1,400 tickets, or about 5,000 over 1–28 September. The rail says 3,412.

   Maybe the buckets are per-queue, or the total counts something else. Either way, a careful reader who does this sum stops trusting both figures. This is a defect. Sum the underlying cells and reconcile the two. If they really measure different things, say what each one counts in its label.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** "When new tickets arrive" makes the reader work out the answer, which is "weekdays 9:00–17:00, peaking late morning; weekends barely register." A title that states that answer would do the work for them, and stating it in words would also have caught the 10:00/11:00 clash. This is taste: the source for this entry is off the approved list, but for a report page read once a month I'd argue it strongly.

**Smaller things.**
- The 0–4 and 5–9 shades are very close, both in the legend and in the cells around 07:00 and 17:00–18:00. Open up the step between the lightest two colours so the edges of the shift can be read.
- The rail's sub-labels ("Up 4% on August") and the source line are mid-grey. They look like they pass contrast, but measure them, since they carry the comparisons.

**What's working.**
- **The chart form is right for the question.** A day × hour heatmap on a single blue lightness ramp, with a labelled, binned legend, shows the weekday block and empty weekends instantly. Darker means more, with no rainbow scale. Keep it.
- **Every headline number has a comparison.** Each figure in the rail comes with a previous-period comparison ("Up 4% on August", "Down 6 min"), so the figures mean something on their own.
- **The source line is useful.** It says tickets are counted when opened, not when answered, in the team's local time, which heads off the most likely misreading.
