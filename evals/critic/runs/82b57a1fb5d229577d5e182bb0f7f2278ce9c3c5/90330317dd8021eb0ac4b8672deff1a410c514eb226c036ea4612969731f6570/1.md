**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Savings deposits rose every month" and then drops to the tallest bar, June at $1150k. From there it moves right to $4.82M. It reads at once as a credit union's half-year report to its members, and it feels calm, careful and trustworthy. The one thing that disturbs the eye is the blue-and-white diagonal striping on the bars, which shimmers slightly next to an otherwise quiet page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a page in a public member report that tells ordinary members how much they saved in the first half of 2026 and whether that's good news.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The claim, the evidence and the totals are all clear on first read, and the structure is sound. Two things hold it back. The striped bar fill is the loudest thing in the chart and shows no data. The June bar's jump is mostly a one-off bonus payroll, but the chart shows it as momentum and only a grey footnote says otherwise.

**What would move it most**
1. **Drop the hatching on the bars.** This is a convention (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5). All six bars use high-contrast diagonal stripes, which are the strongest texture on the page. Up close they shimmer, and from a distance they make the bars look lighter and fuzzier than the solid figures beside them. The pattern encodes nothing: there's one series and no estimates or forecasts to tell apart, so it's pure ornament. Draw the bars in plain flat blue. If you want the stripes to mean something, save them for one marked case, such as June (see 2), and leave the others solid.
2. **Mark the June bar's one-off payroll on the bar, not in a footnote.** This is a defect (my judgment, not a published rule). June jumps about 32% on May ($868k to $1150k), while the other months rise 6–10% each. A member reading the headline and the bar together will take that as savings speeding up. The only explanation is the last clause of the grey source line, "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll", which most people won't read. The same payroll also inflates the two June cards, $214 and 5,374, though they are compared with June 2025, which may have had a bonus payroll too. Put a short note right at the June bar, such as "includes mid-year bonus payroll". If the 2025 comparison also had one, say so on the cards. Without that, readers will hold the credit union to a growth rate it didn't earn.

**Smaller things.**
- The June bar label "$1150k" sits next to "$4.82M" in the first card. That's two styles of unit for the same kind of number, with no thousands separator. Use one style, such as $1.15M, or $1,150k throughout.
- The "Average deposit, Jun 2026: $214" card reads as the average per deposit, but $214 × 5,374 ≈ $1.15M, so it's the average per account that made a deposit. A member comparing it with their own deposit will draw the wrong conclusion. Say "Average deposited per account, Jun 2026".
- "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025" is a stiff phrase. "Up 6.1% from the same months in 2025" says who is compared with what without making the reader parse it.
- Jev flagged "A chart carries decoration that shows no data" (0.94). It's the bar hatching, covered in finding 1.

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding instead of the topic, and the subtitle and the chart title pin down exactly what is counted, with units. Keep this.
- The chart scaffolding is honest and quiet: a zero baseline, even ticks, pale gridlines, and direct value labels so nobody needs to read the axis.
- Each stat card has a clear hierarchy (label, big figure, green "Up" line with a comparison), and the "Up" text carries the meaning on its own, so the green isn't doing the work alone.
