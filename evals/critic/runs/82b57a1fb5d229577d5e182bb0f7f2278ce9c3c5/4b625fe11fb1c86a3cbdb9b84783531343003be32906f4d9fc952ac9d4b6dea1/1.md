**First read.** The eye lands on "Low-light houseplants" and then moves along four identical cards, left to right. It reads at once as a category page for a small online plant shop. It feels calm, tidy and competent, but also anonymous: the four cards are so alike that nothing pulls you toward any one of them, and the pictures don't help you tell the plants apart.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is a shop page that helps someone with a dim room choose one of four plants and add it to their cart.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and works, but the page doesn't help anyone choose. The pictures are generic stand-ins instead of the actual plants, and the only facts on each card are pot size and height. Someone shopping for a dark corner learns nothing about what makes one plant better than another for them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures don't show the plants (defect).** All four cards use the same pot with a few leaf shapes on top. The pothos is drawn as two upright leaves, though the card itself says it trails to 6 ft. The snake plant has a rounded leaf, when its leaves are tall, stiff blades. For houseplants, how the plant looks is most of the buying decision, so a shopper either can't judge or has to leave the page to search for photos. If these are placeholders waiting for real images, the page isn't finished. If they are the intended art, they need to look like the species. Either way, use real photos or species-accurate drawings that show each plant's actual shape: upright blades, glossy stems, broad leaves, trailing vines.
2. **The cards don't answer the question the page asks (defect; common sense).** The subtitle promises plants "that do well more than six feet from a window." But each card only says "6 in pot, grows to 2 ft", which reads like a stock-list entry. Someone choosing for a dim room wants to know how little light each plant can take, how often to water it, and whether it's safe for pets. Those are the differences that would make them pick one plant over another. Right now the only differences shown are height and price, so price ends up making the decision. Add one short line per card in the shopper's own terms, such as "Handles a windowless hallway" or "Water every 3 weeks." That line is where each plant gets its reason to be chosen.

**Smaller things.**
- **Product name and price compete:** the names ("Snake plant") look about 18 px bold and the prices look about 20 px bold. They are two levels a couple of pixels apart in the same weight and colour, so neither clearly leads. Make the step plain: keep the name as the card heading and set the price smaller or lighter, or the other way round (Bringhurst, 3.1.1).
- **Nav doesn't show where you are:** "Houseplants" in the top nav looks the same as "Pots" and "Care guides." Mark the current section with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- **Copy could belong to any shop:** "Low-light houseplants" is accurate, but it says nothing the shopper doesn't already know. The subtitle carries the real promise ("more than six feet from a window") and is set as small grey text. Consider giving that promise more prominence.

**What's working.**
- **Clear structure:** a strong H1, a one-line subtitle that gives a concrete, measurable promise ("six feet from a window"), and a strict four-column grid where image, name, details, price and button share the same left edge in every card. Keep that structure when you add real images and the extra line.
- **Calm, consistent palette:** sage panels, terracotta pots and teal buttons give a calm, plant-appropriate mood, and one consistent button per card is the right convention for a product grid.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the product names and images link to a detail page. Nothing marks them as links, and if they do link, shoppers need a way to find out more before buying.
- What "Add to cart" does when clicked, for example whether "Cart (0)" updates and whether the button confirms the add.
