**First read.** Your eye goes straight to the four "Add to cart" buttons and bounces along them: teal, purple, orange, blue. Only after that does it reach the headline and the plants. It reads as a small online shop's category page for houseplants that cope with low light. It's calm and neat everywhere except that bottom row of buttons, which looks like a box of crayons.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a category page where a plant buyer compares four low-light plants and adds one to the cart, so it needs to show the plants first and the buy action clearly.

**Verdict.** This is a clean, competent page. One decision hurts it: each card's button is a different saturated colour. That makes the buttons the loudest thing on the page, hints at differences between the plants that don't exist, and clashes with the soft plant palette. Make the four buttons one colour and the page mostly works.

**What would move it most**
1. **Four colours for one action** (defect; convention). The four "Add to cart" buttons are teal, purple, orange and blue. They all do exactly the same thing, but a shopper reads a colour change as a change in meaning. They'll wonder whether the orange Cast iron plant is a sale or the blue Pothos is a different kind of item, and nothing answers that. The orange one also jumps forward, so one product looks favoured for no reason. Same job, same colour (Material Design 3, colour roles; Apple HIG, "Color"; Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind follow one program). The fix: give every Add to cart button one shared colour, and keep any other colour for a real difference, such as a sale or out-of-stock state.
2. **The buttons outweigh everything, including the plants** (defect). Those four solid blocks are the biggest areas of strong colour on the page, so they beat the headline, the pictures and the prices for first attention. The product never gets to lead. Four equal-weight, equally loud elements leave the eye without a starting point (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Apple HIG on keeping the accent for the main action). With one shared colour, ideally a deeper green or the terracotta already in the pots, the buttons step back into a regular rhythm. Then the plant pictures and the "Low-light houseplants" headline lead.
3. **The palette fights the subject** (taste). Everything else is sage, leaf green, terracotta and soft grey, which suits a plant shop. Purple, bright blue and orange belong to an app dashboard, not a greenhouse. I'd build the action colour from the plant greens or the pot terracotta so the page feels like one shop, not a theme with four leftover swatches.
4. **The pictures don't help anyone choose** (taste, common sense; no checklist line covers it). All four illustrations are the same terracotta pot with two or three generic leaves. The Snake plant isn't drawn upright and sword-leaved, and the Pothos, which the copy says "trails to 6 ft", is drawn as two upright leaves. A shopper deciding between four plants gets nothing from the pictures and has to read the spec lines to tell them apart. Use photos, or drawings that show each plant's real shape: snake plant upright, ZZ glossy, cast iron broad-leaved, pothos trailing.

**Smaller things.**
- Top nav: "Houseplants" looks the same as Pots and Care guides, so nothing shows you're on that page. Mark the current page with one cue, such as bolder weight or an underline (Nielsen Norman Group, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Jev also found "Several vivid colors compete at equal weight" and "Elements that do the same job have different colors". Both are the button row and are covered in findings 1 and 2.

**What's working.**
- The type is set well. The headline, the bold product name, the grey spec line and the bold price each have a clear job, and the name and price are told apart by weight, not by a size that's nearly the same.
- The copy is specific and useful. "Four plants that do well more than six feet from a window" gives a real distance, not "low light" as a vague label. Lines like "6 in pot, grows to 3 ft" give the two numbers a buyer actually needs.
- The four-card grid is aligned and evenly spaced, with generous margins, so the cards are easy to compare across the row.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the buttons, and whether the four-column row reflows cleanly on a phone.
