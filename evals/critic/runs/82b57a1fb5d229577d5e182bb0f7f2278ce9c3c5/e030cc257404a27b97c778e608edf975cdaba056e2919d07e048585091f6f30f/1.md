**First read.** My eye goes straight to the big white headline on the navy band, "See where every dollar goes this month", then down to the solid white "Try it free" button. Next I notice that the right half of the navy hero is completely empty, and so is the bottom quarter of the screen. It reads as a personal budgeting app for individuals and households. It feels calm and trustworthy but bare, like a wireframe with the picture left out.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about it, so I'm assuming this is the first screen of a marketing home page for a consumer budgeting app, meant to get first-time visitors to start a free trial.

**Verdict.** The words and the order of importance are good, but the page never shows the product. A headline that promises "see where every dollar goes" sits beside a big blank space, so the visitor has to take the promise on trust. The space is also badly spread: the hero leans hard to the left and the feature row floats against a large empty bottom.

**What would move it most**
1. **The empty right half of the hero (about x 800–1280, y 62–462).** The headline is entirely about seeing something, but nothing is there to see. A visitor deciding whether to link their bank accounts wants to know what they'd look at every month, and the page leaves them to imagine it. That makes "Try it free" a leap of faith rather than a choice they can judge. The fix: put the promise in that space, as a real screen showing this month's categories with a limit and one "heads-up" alert. Then the picture and the headline make one case together: the headline says it, the screen proves it. If an image was meant to be there and failed to load, then this is a capture problem rather than a design one. Check it in a live browser. *Defect.* Visitors can't judge what they're signing up for. The headline-and-picture pairing itself is taste, argued on its own terms.
2. **The weight is lopsided and the space is left over rather than placed.** All the text and both buttons sit in the left 60% of the hero, while the right side is flat navy. Below the hero, the three-column feature row sits only about 46 px under the band, and roughly 220 px of white is left empty beneath it. So the empty space hasn't been gathered into areas that do a job. It is what's left after the content ran out. The page tips to the left, and the feature row looks stuck to the hero instead of being a section of its own (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"; Ruder, *Typography*, "Contrasts"). The fix: give the hero a counterweight on the right (the screen from finding 1 does this). Then give the feature row more space above it than inside it, so it reads as its own band, or bring the next section up so the fold doesn't end in blank white. *Convention.*
3. **The hero doesn't answer the visitor's next two questions.** For an app that connects to bank accounts, those questions are "is it safe?" and "what does it cost after the free part?" The strongest trust line on the page, "can never move money", is small gray body text in the third column. "Try it free" also doesn't say how long the trial lasts or whether a card is needed. The fix: move the read-only promise up next to the buttons as a one-line reassurance, and give the button its terms (for example, the trial length and "no card needed", if true). That is what visitors will later hold you to, so it should be exact. *Taste / common sense*, about the copy and what to show first.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- **The headline copy.** It is specific and ties to a time frame ("this month"), so it doesn't read like it could be on anyone's page. The subhead says exactly what the product does, in three verbs: connect, set a limit, get a heads-up. Keep both.
- **The buttons and type sizes.** There is one clear primary button (solid white) and one plainly secondary one (outline). The type steps are clean: headline, subhead, feature heading, body, with no sizes that nearly collide.
- **The feature headings name what they offer.** Each one comes with a one-sentence explanation that speaks to the visitor ("you can fix any of them in one tap").

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the hero's right side was meant to hold an image or animation that hadn't loaded when this was captured (check live). Hover and focus states on the two buttons.
