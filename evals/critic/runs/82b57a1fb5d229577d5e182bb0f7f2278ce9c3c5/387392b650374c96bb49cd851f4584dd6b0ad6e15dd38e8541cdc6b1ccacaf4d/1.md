**First read.** The eye lands on "New sign-ups by channel", drops to the tall Organic search bar, and runs down the staircase of bars to Events. It reads as an internal growth-report panel: clean, calm and competent, but also generic. You reach the explanation in the right-hand column last, and only if you go looking for it. The panel's real point, the budget decision, is the least visible thing on the page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal growth page that tells the team and leadership where Q3 sign-ups came from and what changes next quarter because of it.

**Verdict.** The chart is well made, but it lays out the data without arguing anything. The title names a topic, all six bars are the same blue, and the two findings that matter (organic search grew; podcast ads and events get cut) sit in a side note that nothing in the chart points to. Close that gap between chart and story and this page does its job.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart doesn't show the story the note tells.** The title "New sign-ups by channel" only repeats what the axis already says. Every bar is the same mid-blue, so Organic search stands out only because it's tallest, and Podcast ads and Events (the bars the decision is about) look like every other bar. Someone skimming reads "a ranked bar chart", not "organic is working, events are being cut". The note at the right, which starts at about y=174, is reached last and has no visual link to the bars it describes.
   - **Fix:** make the title state the finding, e.g. "Organic search brought a third of Q3 sign-ups; events budget moves to referrals". Draw the bars in a muted grey and give the two or three bars the note discusses an accent colour, so the chart and the note point at the same thing (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, 19.1). Then the note can sit directly beside or under those bars instead of in a separate column.
   - **Type:** defect. A reader who stops at the chart misses the decision.

2. **The key claim has no comparison on the page.** The note says organic search went "up from a quarter in Q2", but the chart only shows Q3, so the reader has to take the trend on trust. The same goes for the cut: nothing shows whether 410 and 260 are below target or below last quarter.
   - **Fix:** put Q2 next to Q3 for each channel, as paired bars or a faint Q2 tick on each bar. Or at minimum, add each channel's share and its change from Q2 to the labels (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2).
   - **Type:** defect. The one trend the page argues from is asserted, not shown.

3. **"Up from a quarter in Q2" can be read two ways.** In a sentence about quarters, "a quarter" reads as a time period before it reads as 25%. The line also carries the page's main causal claim ("after the guide pages were rewritten"), which the team will repeat and be held to.
   - **Fix:** use numbers: "34% of new accounts (1,840), up from 25% in Q2". Likewise, "fewer than 700" should say what that means for the reader, e.g. "670 accounts, 12% of the total, from the two costliest channels", if cost is the reason for the cut.
   - **Type:** defect (copy).

**Smaller things.**
- The right-hand note's top edge (about y=174) lines up with neither the subtitle, the top of the chart (2,000 label at about y=195), nor the title. Align it with one of them on purpose.

**What's working.**
- **Sorting:** the bars are sorted by value, so you read the ranking without effort. Keep that sort.
- **Labels:** the value labels sit directly on each bar, the gridlines are pale and thin, and the axis starts at zero. The chart is honest and light on ink.
- **Source line:** it gives the date range and the total (5,400), which checks out against the bars (they sum to 5,400). That's the kind of footing a report page needs.
