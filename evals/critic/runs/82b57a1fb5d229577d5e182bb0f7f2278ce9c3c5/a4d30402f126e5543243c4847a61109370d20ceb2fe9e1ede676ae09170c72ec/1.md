**First read.** Nothing catches the eye first. It starts at the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time", and then reads down a single even block of dark grey text, because the four questions look almost exactly like their answers. It reads as the help section of a bakery's order-ahead service: calm and plain, but flat. You have to read it to find your question; you can't scan for it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help page for customers who have ordered bread online and want to change, hand off, or cancel the order, or check allergens. They arrive with one question and want to find its answer fast.

**Verdict.** The words are mostly clear and the setting is tidy, but the page fails at the one thing a help page has to do: let you jump to your question. The headings are set like body text, so four topics become one block of prose. Two answers that people will act on (the cancellation deadline and whose name to give) can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The questions look the same as the answers** (defect). In all four entries, "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" look the same weight and colour as the answers below them, and even a touch smaller (roughly 18 px against 19 px by eye; measure to confirm). Someone who came to cancel has to read every line from the top to find "Cancelling", which sits last. Dair's point is that a difference meant to show must be obvious at a glance; a one-pixel size gap reads as an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). It also fails in grayscale: everything is the same near-black, so the structure lives only in the spacing. Jev flagged the same thing at 0.98. Fix: make each question plainly different from its answer in one clear way. The usual move is a heavier weight (semibold) at the same or a slightly larger size, or keep the weight and set the answers in a lighter grey. Either way the four questions should form a column you can scan in two seconds.
2. **The page says nothing about what it is, and has no starting point** (defect, unless a title sits just above this capture). There's no title, bakery name, or "Pickup and orders" heading above the first question, so nothing stands above the rest (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order by differences of size). A visitor coming from a link or a search has to read the first answer to work out where they are (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Fix: add one clearly larger title naming the page, such as "Collecting your order". With the stronger questions from finding 1, that gives the page three plain levels: title, question, answer.
3. **The deadline and the hand-off rule can each be read two ways** (defect, copy). These are the lines people hold you to.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." Ahead of what: the pickup slot, or the bake? And does "a day" mean 24 hours, or any time on the day before? Someone picking up at 8 am who cancels at 7 pm the night before will think they're in time, may not be, and will feel cheated. State the cutoff as a time: "Cancel by 6 pm the day before your pickup." Then end on what it means for them: "After that we've already baked your bread, so we can't refund it" (if that's the policy).
   - "Show it at the counter along with a name." Whose name: the person collecting, or the person who ordered? If the counter checks the orderer's name and the friend gives their own, the friend leaves without the bread. Say "along with your name" or "along with their own name", whichever is true.
4. **The allergy answer leaves out the person choosing online** (defect, copy). "Every loaf has a card by the till" only helps someone already standing in the shop. A customer with an allergy is deciding before they order, on this site, and the answer tells them they can't check here. If ingredient lists exist online, link to them. If not, tell them how to ask before ordering, for example a phone number. "Ask the baker" assumes they're already at the counter.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: answers that fit on one line, such as "Give the person your order number…" and "Orders can be cancelled…", run about 95 to 100 characters, well past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. That's minor with one-line answers, but narrowing the column to about 640 px would also fix the next item.
- "Anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergy answer (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrowing the column or rewording the answer removes it.

**What's working.**
- The spacing already groups each question with its answer: the gap inside a pair (~10 px) is clearly smaller than the gap between pairs (~36 px). Keep that rhythm when you strengthen the headings.
- The questions are phrased as tasks the customer is doing ("Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else") rather than as system terms. Keep that voice.
- One typeface, one text colour, one left edge, generous margins. It's calm and appropriate for a small bakery, and doesn't need decoration added.
