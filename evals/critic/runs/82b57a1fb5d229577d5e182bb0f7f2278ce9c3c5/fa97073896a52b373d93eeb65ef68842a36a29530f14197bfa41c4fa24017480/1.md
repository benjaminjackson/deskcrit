**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page or notice that tells river ferry passengers when the boat runs, how bicycles work, and what changes in winter, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The type itself is sound and the page is readable, but it doesn't do its job well. The body is centred under a flush-left headline, which breaks the page's one alignment axis and slows reading. The copy also leaves out the clock times a passenger actually needs. Fix those two and this becomes a good notice.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body paragraphs are centred under a left-aligned headline** (all three paragraphs, x≈125–755). Each line starts at a different point, so the eye has to hunt for the start of every line. Over three paragraphs of 4 lines each, that adds up to slow, choppy reading. Because the headline sits flush left at x≈120 and the centred lines' widest starts land at about x≈125, the two edges almost line up but don't, so it looks like a mistake rather than a choice. This is a **convention** and a **defect**. Tschichold (*The New Typography*) and Bringhurst (*Elements*, 2.1.3) keep centring for a few short lines and set paragraphs of several lines flush left. Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance") explains why a near-miss placement makes the eye waver. **Fix:** set the paragraphs flush left on the same left edge as the headline, so the page has one strong edge from top to bottom.
2. **The copy describes the timetable but never gives a time.** Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says "one boat each hour" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger can't plan a trip from "early morning" or "before dusk", and the last boat is exactly the line people will hold you to. This is a **defect**. "The quay" can also be read two ways: the market quay only, or the last boat from either landing. If a rider at the north landing reads it the first way, they could miss the last crossing back. **Fix:** give the first and last departure from each landing as clock times, with the summer and winter times side by side. Keep the sentence that says where printed timetables are posted as backup, not as the answer.

**Smaller things.**
- **Placement and balance:** the 640 px column is pinned to the left of a 1280 px frame, and nothing sits to the right. The page tips left and reads as if a sidebar failed to load (convention, Arnheim, "Balance"). Either centre the column as a whole block, keeping the text inside it flush left, or give the right side a job, such as the timetable itself.
- **Grid:** once the body is flush left, the headline and paragraphs share one column edge, and the near-miss at x≈120/125 goes away (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).

**What's working.**
- **Line length and spacing.** The measure is about 65–70 characters, and the generous leading suits it (Bringhurst). Keep both when you change the alignment.
- **Type scale.** There are just two sizes, a large step apart, and the headline is also bold. The hierarchy is unambiguous with nothing extra.
- **Plain, specific prose.** "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" says who does what and when. Write the times in that same voice.
