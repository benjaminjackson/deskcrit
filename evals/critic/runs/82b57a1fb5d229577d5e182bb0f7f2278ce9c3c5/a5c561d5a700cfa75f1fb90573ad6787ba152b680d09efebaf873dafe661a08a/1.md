**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline "Savings deposits rose every month", then right away to the tall June bar, then across to "$4.82M". It reads as a credit union's half-year report to members: calm and trustworthy, a statement you can take in within five seconds. The striped bars are the one thing that looks busy.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page on a credit union's member site that reports how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026, for ordinary members rather than analysts.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, clear page and it mostly works. The biggest problem is that its main visual, the June spike, is a one-off bonus payroll, and that fact appears only in a small grey line under the chart. The page therefore shows a surge that didn't really happen.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June bar tells a story the footnote takes back.** June jumps from $868k to $1150k, a step about four times bigger than any earlier month. It is the tallest, darkest thing on the page, and the eye goes straight to it. The reason ("June includes the mid-year bonus payroll") sits in 12 px grey text below the chart card, where most members won't read it. A member leaves thinking deposits took off in June, and the credit union can be held to that. **Defect** (it misleads). Fix: put the explanation on the bar itself, with a short note next to the $1150k label such as "includes mid-year bonus payroll". If you can, show June without the bonus as well, so the real month-on-month trend can be seen.
2. **"Rose every month" can't be judged without last year.** Six months that each rise could be real growth or just the normal pattern every January to June, especially if a June bonus payroll happens every year. The cards already compare with 2025 ("Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025"), but the chart doesn't. That leaves the headline's claim resting on a range the viewer can't put in context. **Common sense** (the reference source for this rule isn't on the approved list). Fix: add 2025's months as pale grey bars or ticks behind each 2026 bar. Then the headline can say what the comparison shows, such as "higher than last year every month".
3. **The diagonal hatching on the bars.** The thin blue-and-white stripes shimmer at this size, and they make the bars look textured and less solid. Many readers also see hatching as "estimate" or "projected", which is the wrong signal for posted figures. The stripes show no data. **Convention** (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 5, chartjunk). Fix: use plain flat blue. If you take finding 1, flat colour also frees the pattern to mark the bonus part of June.

**Smaller things.**
- "Average deposit, Jun 2026: $214" can be read two ways. The number works out as $1150k ÷ 5,374, so it is per depositing account, but most members will read it as the size of a typical single deposit. Say "Average deposited per account". This is common sense, and it matters because this is a figure people remember.
- The chart title "Deposits into savings accounts, US dollars per month" repeats the subtitle just above it. Cut the title down to the unit, or drop it.
- Every bar already has its value printed on top, so the y-axis labels and gridlines repeat that information. Remove them or make them fainter.
- Number formats change between the chart and the cards: "$1150k" and "$1200k" have no thousands separator, while the cards use "$4.82M" and "5,374". Use "$1.15M" or "$1,150k" consistently.
- "Up $11 on Jun 2025" is clipped. "Up $11 from June 2025" reads the way people talk.

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding as a sentence rather than naming a topic, and the subtitle gives the exact measure and period. Keep that pattern, but fix the claim as described in finding 2.
- Each card has a clear three-step structure: a plain label with its period, a big number, and a green "Up" line that says the change in words, so the meaning doesn't rely on colour. Every headline figure comes with a comparison.
- The layout is tidy: a two-thirds/one-third split, the card tops lining up with the chart top, one left edge running from the eyebrow to the source line, and calm neutrals with a single blue.
