**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers" and then drops into three paragraphs that all look the same. None of them stands out, so the eye just starts reading at the top. It reads as a plain, calm notice, like one from a council or a small operator. You only get the answer to "when does the boat go?" by reading prose. The right half of the frame and the bottom third are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells passengers when the ferry runs, how long a crossing takes, the bicycle rules and the winter changes. The reader's real question is "when is the next boat, and when is the last one?"

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and comfortable to read, but the page doesn't do its job. The facts a passenger acts on are buried in sentences and are too vague to plan by. The page has no actual times, and "before dusk" is the only stated deadline for the last boat.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule is prose, with no times.** This is a defect in the copy. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines people act on, and you can't plan a trip from either. Someone trying to catch the last boat can't tell if that means 4 pm or 7 pm, and a guess that's too late leaves them stranded on the wrong bank. "In winter" also has no dates, so a reader in early November can't tell which timetable applies. And the page sends them to "printed timetables kept in the shelter at each landing", which they'll only see once they're already at the river. Give the first and last departure from each landing as clock times, give the winter dates, and give the actual last-boat time, or say exactly how it's worked out, such as "30 minutes before sunset" with today's time next to it.
2. **Facts are buried in three undivided paragraphs.** This is a convention: Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web". Frequency, crossing time, bicycle rules, winter changes and where to find updates all sit mid-sentence in blocks of the same weight, so a skimming reader has to read all ~110 words to find the one fact they came for. Pull out the times as a short timetable or a few labelled lines (Summer, Winter, Last boat, Crossing time). Then put the bicycle rules under their own short heading. The friendly prose can stay as a lead-in.
3. **The headline names a format, not what the page offers.** This is taste. "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any ferry's page, and it tells the reader nothing they'd come for. A headline that names the route and the answer, such as "North landing ⇄ Market quay: times and bicycle rules", lets someone landing from a search know straight away that they're in the right place.

**Smaller things.** None. Apart from the chunking, every checklist line passed.

**What's working.**
- **The reading setting is right.** Body text looks about 19 px, with lines of roughly 68 to 72 characters and comfortable line spacing. The text is near-black on white, flush left with an even rag, and paragraphs are separated by space only. Keep all of this when you add structure.
- **The hierarchy is clear but restrained.** It's one family, with a bold headline over regular body text, so you know where to start. When you add subheads, put them between those two levels, using weight more than size, so you don't add a muddle of near-identical sizes.
- **The tone is plain and human.** Lines like "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" sound like someone who works the crossing. Keep that voice in the lead-in once the hard facts are moved out.
