I ran the crop script; at 1280×800 the screenshot fits on one screen, so I worked from the whole image and one close crop of the bottom of the form. The Jev file flagged one defect: the form does not mark its required fields. It is the top finding below.

**First read.** The eye lands on "Create your account", runs down six empty fields, and stops on the blue "Create account" button. It reads as the signup page for a notes app. It feels calm, tidy, and familiar, even templated. Nothing on the page pulls attention away from the task.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a first-time visitor to "Quillnest" should be able to sign up for a free notes workspace with as little friction as possible.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The layout is clean and the one action is clear. What gets in the way is the form itself: six fields and no sign of which ones are required. So the user has to fill in everything, including questions that only help the company, before the "free" promise pays off.

**What would move it most**
1. **The user can't tell which fields are required, and the optional ones are mixed in with the essentials.** Not one label (First name, Last name, Email address, Password, Workspace name, How did you hear about us?) carries an asterisk or "(optional)". "How did you hear about us?" is almost certainly optional, and "Workspace name" could be filled in for the user, but both look exactly like Email and Password. So the user treats all six as required, stops at the dropdown to pick an answer that does nothing for them, and is slower to reach the button. *Defect.* Nielsen Norman Group (Budiu, "Marking Required Fields in Forms") says to mark required fields. Nielsen's progressive-disclosure and minimalist-design guidance says secondary questions shouldn't sit beside the main task. Fix: if any field stays optional, put "(optional)" after its label, or mark required fields with an asterisk and explain the asterisk once. Better still, take the referral question out of the signup step: ask it after signup, or not at all. You could also fill in the workspace name from the user's name, so the form comes down to name, email, and password.
2. **Password gives no rules until the user gets it wrong.** The Password field has no hint text about length or the characters it needs. If there are rules, the user learns them from an error after pressing "Create account", which is the worst moment to learn them. *Defect* (Nielsen 10, help near the step it concerns). Fix: put the rules in one short line under the field, before the user types.

**Smaller things.**
- **"How did you hear about us?" dropdown:** it shows a blank box with no prompt such as "Select one", so at first glance it looks broken or already answered. Add the prompt text (Nielsen 5, error prevention).
- **"Sign in" link:** it is the same grey as "Already have an account?", with no underline, so it doesn't look clickable. Underline it, or make it the brand blue plus an underline (WCAG technique G183).
- **Input borders:** the pale grey-blue field outlines look close to or under the 3:1 needed against white. Measure them, and darken them if they fall short (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.11).
- **Subhead "Start a free workspace for your notes.":** this could sit on any notes app's signup page, so it gives a hesitant visitor no reason to pick this one. *Taste:* use the line for the one thing Quillnest does that others don't, or what "free" includes.

**What's working.**
- **One clear action:** the full-width filled button is the only strong colour in the card, so there's no doubt how to finish. Keep it the single filled element.
- **Labels and spacing:** each field has a bold label above it, sitting closer to its own field than to the one above. The groups read at a glance, with nothing hidden in placeholder text.
- **Type scale:** there are three clear levels (heading, labels and button, body and helper text). The heading is set apart by both size and weight, and nothing fights it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the fields show a visible focus ring, and how validation errors look and when they appear.
- How the two-column name row behaves on narrow screens.
