**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline, drops to the tallest bar (705), then moves across eight blue bars that all look the same. It reads as a tidy board-deck chart slide. It feels calm and competent, but it could come from any company: nothing on the chart itself points at anything.

**What it's for.** Assumption: slide 3 of a board sales review. Directors should come away knowing the seasonal pattern, how the business is trending, and what Q4 2026 is expected to bring.

**Verdict.** The slide is clean and honest, but it doesn't make its case. The chart doesn't show the pattern the headline claims. The forecast bar looks exactly like the actual results. The slide also leaves out its strongest news: every quarter of 2026 is up on the same quarter of 2025.

**What would move it most**
1. **The Q4 2026 forecast looks like an actual result.** The rightmost bar (560) has the same solid blue as the seven real quarters, and only the subtitle and the source line say it's a forecast. A director scanning the chart reads 560 as a number that has already happened. In a board meeting that's the number people repeat and later hold management to. *Defect* (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16: projections must look different from observed data). Fix: give that bar a lighter tint, a hatch, or an outline-only style, and label it "Q4 2026 (forecast)" at the bar itself. If the September forecast has a range, show it as a range.
2. **The chart doesn't show what the headline says.** The headline is "Shipments peak each third quarter", but Q3 2025 and Q3 2026 are the same blue as every other bar. The viewer has to compare eight bar heights to confirm the claim. *Defect, against an approved rule.* Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") says to use one colour for the series and keep a second colour for the one point the reader must notice. Fix: show the Q3 bars in the accent colour and the other quarters in a muted blue or grey. Then the chart proves the headline at a glance.
3. **The more important story is missing.** Each 2026 quarter beats the same quarter of 2025: 455 vs 410, 590 vs 520, 705 vs 610, and 560 vs 480 forecast. That's roughly +11% to +17%, and the Q3 peak is up about 16%. Neither the headline nor the chart says so, so the board gets a pattern with no sense of whether things are going well. A seasonal peak is also something they likely knew already. *Defect* (Few, pitfall 2: give metrics a comparison such as the previous period). Fix: rewrite the headline to make the claim and land it on the board. For example: "Every quarter is up on last year, and the Q3 peak grew 16%." Better still: "Q4 is on track to beat last year by 17%", if that's the decision in front of them. You could also add a small year-over-year change label above each 2026 bar. *Taste on the wording*: "each third quarter" claims a recurring pattern from only two cycles. The growth claim is the safer one and the stronger one.

**Smaller things.**
- Above the plot there are three headings in a row: the subtitle, "Units shipped by quarter", and "Thousands of units". The chart title repeats the subtitle. Fold the unit into the subtitle ("…shipped to retailers, in thousands of units") and cut the chart title, so the chart sits closer to the headline (Nielsen, aesthetic and minimalist design).
- The subtitle reads "to Q4 2026 (Q4 2026 is the forecast)", which says Q4 2026 twice. Write "…eight quarters through Q4 2026, the last a forecast", or drop the note once the bar itself is marked as a forecast.
- With a value label on every bar, the y-axis and gridlines add little. Taste: remove them or lighten them further.

**What's working.**
- Both the bars and the y-axis start at zero, and the bars sit in plain time order. The scale is honest and easy to compare, so keep it.
- The headline is a sentence that states a finding, not a topic label like "Q3 Shipments". Keep that approach and point it at the strongest finding.
- The single sans family, the one restrained serif for the headline, the shared left edge, and the quiet header and footer make it read as a serious board document. Nothing on the slide competes with the data.
