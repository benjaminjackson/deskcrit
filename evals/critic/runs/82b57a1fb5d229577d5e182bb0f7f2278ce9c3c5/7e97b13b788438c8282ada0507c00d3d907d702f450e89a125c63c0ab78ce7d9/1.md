**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline, drops to the big navy 38% wedge, and then has to go across to the legend to learn what that wedge is. From there it goes down a fourteen-row list. It reads at once as a sober, well-made annual-report page about where the money went. It feels calm and competent, but it tells you nothing you would remember.

**What it's for.** Assumption: one page of an annual report that shows board members, shareholders, and staff how operating costs split up in fiscal 2026, and what stands out.

**Verdict.** It's clean and carefully set, but as a chart it doesn't do its job. A 14-slice pie with a separate colour legend makes the reader match colours to find out what any wedge is, and the page never says what the split means. The biggest problem is the chart form. The headline and the missing context come right after it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pie has too many slices for anyone to read** (convention). Ten of the fourteen wedges are 1–4%: the thin fan from Travel round to Postage in the upper left. By angle they look the same, so the reader can't see that Travel (4%) is bigger than Insurance (3%), or that Training is bigger than Bank fees. The only big point the pie makes, that salaries are over a third, is lost among the slivers. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1) limit pies to about five slices. Fix: keep the four big categories and group the ten small ones into a single "Other, 21%" wedge. Better still, use a sorted horizontal bar chart with each name and value at its bar. The list is already sorted, so a bar chart would turn what is now the legend into the chart itself.
2. **To know what a wedge is, you have to match its colour across the page** (defect). Each wedge carries only a percentage, and its name is 300 px away in the legend. With fourteen hues, several look nearly the same. Rent's teal and Equipment repair's pale teal could be confused. So could Salaries navy, Software licences blue, and Utilities blue-violet, or Cloud hosting orange and Training brown. Travel red sits right next to Insurance green. Anyone with a colour-vision deficiency can't reliably link the small wedges to their names (WCAG 1.4.1). Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*) and Wilke both call for labels placed on the data. Fourteen categorical hues is also well past what an eye can keep apart. That point is common sense, not a cited rule. Every percentage is also printed twice, once on the wedge and once in the legend. Fix: put the name and value at the mark itself, and cut the palette to a few colours, or to one accent plus grey. Fixing item 1 mostly fixes this too.
3. **The headline names a topic, not a finding** (taste; I'd argue it hard). "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" is a heading any company could use for this page. The reader has to work out the point for themselves. In an annual report, the line people quote is the one that says what happened, for example "People and premises took over half of operating costs." Use the headline or the subtitle for the finding, and let the chart back it up.
4. **The shares have no scale and no year to compare against** (convention, Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). There's no total figure, so "38%" is a share of an unknown amount. There's also no fiscal 2025 figure, so the reader can't tell whether Cloud hosting at 12% is up, down, or flat. For an annual report, the change is usually the story. Fix: put the total operating cost, with currency, in the subtitle, and add last year's share or the change next to each of the main categories.

**Smaller things.**
- The legend's right edge stops at about x=1060, while the header and its rule run to the 1216 margin. The chart block isn't tied to the grid, and it leaves a dead strip on the right. Run the legend, or the bar chart that replaces it, out to the right margin (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).

**What's working.**
- The type: a bold serif headline, a quieter serif subtitle, and a small letterspaced "4.2 COSTS" kicker. The levels are clearly separate, and the serif/sans split between page text and data text looks deliberate. Keep it.
- The order: slices run clockwise from largest to smallest and the legend matches. The percentages are right-aligned in bold, so the column of figures scans cleanly. Carry this over to a bar chart.
- The source line, including "Shares are rounded to whole percent". That's honest, and it tells the reader in advance why the shares might not quite add up.
