**First read.** The eye lands on the leaf pattern, a full-width band of saturated greens, yellows and red dots, before it finds any words. Then it gropes for the white headline. You can make out "Plants for shade and small spaces", but the letters dissolve into the pale yellow and lime leaves, especially along "small" and "spaces". After that the eye drops to the grey intro line and the three plain cards, and then stops, because there's nothing to do. It reads as a tidy, friendly, rather templated local garden centre homepage.

**What it's for.** You didn't tell me anything about the piece, so I'm assuming this is the homepage of a local garden centre. Its job is to get nearby customers to come in or browse its shade-tolerant, small-space plants this weekend.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The one line that says what's on offer is hard to read over a busy illustration, and once the visitor has read it, the page gives them nothing to do.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline is set straight on a busy pattern.** This is a defect. Pure white type sits on scattered pale yellow and light green leaves with no scrim or panel. At the worst points, the right half of "small" and the "es" of "spaces", the leaves behind are nearly as light as the letters, and the white dots and leaf tips read as part of the type. The visitor has to work to read the only sentence that says what this shop is offering. The contrast has to hold at the worst point behind the text, not on average (W3C, Techniques for WCAG 2.2, F83). Put a dark scrim, a see-through overlay or a solid panel behind the headline, or move it to a calm area of the image, so every letter stands clear of what's directly behind it.
2. **The pattern is louder than the message.** This is taste, and I'll argue it. The hero is about 40% of the screen and full of high-contrast, saturated marks, so it wins the first glance over the headline it's meant to support. It also says nothing specific. It's generic sunny foliage, not ferns or hostas, and not shade or small spaces, so image and headline don't make one idea. Quieten it (lower contrast, fewer marks, or a darker field on the headline side), or swap in a picture of what's actually being sold, such as a fern and hosta in a pot on a narrow balcony. Then the picture and the words make the offer together.
3. **There's no next step.** This is a defect. The headline promises "plants for shade and small spaces" and the intro says they're "ready to pot this weekend", but there's no button, link or route to those plants. The nav's "Plants" is generic, and the three cards are plain information with no action. A visitor who's interested has to guess where to go. Add one primary action that names the exact thing, such as "See this week's shade plants", or "Plan your visit" if footfall is the goal. Make it the only filled, dominant control on the screen (Material Design 3, "Buttons": one primary action per view).

**Smaller things.**
- The headline's left edge sits at about x=72, while the logo, intro and cards all start at about x=56. That 16 px near-miss reads as an error, not a choice (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Align it to 56, or offset it by a clearly deliberate amount.
- The headline breaks as "small / spaces", which splits the phrase. Break it as "Plants for shade / and small spaces", or widen the measure (Bringhurst, 2.4).
- In the opening-hours card, "until 8 / pm" splits a number from its unit. That card holds the one fact a visitor acts on, so use a non-breaking space or "8pm".

**What's working.**
- The type pairing works: a serif wordmark in deep green, with a plain sans for everything else. It feels local and trustworthy without fuss, so keep it.
- The three cards answer the practical questions a walk-in customer actually has (hours, workshops, delivery) in concrete words: "ten places in each class", "free within ten miles on orders over $60". That copy is specific and believable, which is rare.
- The type scale is clean, with four clear levels (headline, intro, card heading, card body) and nothing nearly the same size.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the nav links.
- How the headline lands on the pattern at other widths. It may fall on even busier areas.
