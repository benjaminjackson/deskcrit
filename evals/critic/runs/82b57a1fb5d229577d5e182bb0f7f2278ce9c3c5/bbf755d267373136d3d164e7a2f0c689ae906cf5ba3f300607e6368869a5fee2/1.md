**First read.** The eye lands first on the bright orange Referral bar and the tall blue Organic search bar, before the bold title. The colours pull harder than the heights. Then it reads the bars left to right and reaches the note on the right last. That note is where the actual news is. It reads as a calm, tidy internal growth report, but the rainbow suggests six different kinds of thing when there is only one.

**What it's for.** Assumed: an internal Q3 report telling a growth or marketing team where new accounts came from, and why the events budget moves to referrals in Q4.

**Verdict.** A competent, honest chart with clear labels, correct totals and good copy. But it shows a flat ranking while the story is about change and a decision. The colours spend the viewer's attention on nothing, and the two bars the note is about are neither marked nor compared with anything.

**What would move it most**

1. **Six colours for one series, with red and green that read as judgements.** Defect. Every bar is one measure (Q3 sign-ups), yet each has its own saturated hue. The viewer first looks for what blue, orange, red and green stand for, and they stand for nothing. Worse, red on Paid social reads as "problem" and green on Podcast ads reads as "doing well". The note says the opposite: podcasts and events underperformed. Few names this pitfall: one series should be drawn in one colour, with a second colour kept for the one point the reader must notice (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 19). Red and green are also status colours (Apple HIG and Material). Fix: make all bars one muted colour, and give an accent only to the bars the note is about. That means Organic search, or Podcast ads plus Events, or Events plus Referral to show the budget shift. Then the chart says what the note says. Jev flagged this too.

2. **The chart can't show the claim the note makes.** Defect. The headline finding is "a third of all new accounts, up from a quarter in Q2". But the chart shows only Q3, so the viewer has to take the change on trust and can't see whether other channels moved too. Few's rule is that a figure needs a comparison, such as the earlier period, to tell good from bad (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Fix: show Q2 next to Q3. Paired bars or a pale Q2 marker on each bar both work. Or add the change to each value label ("1,840, +x% on Q2").

3. **The point is in the sidebar, and the title only describes.** Taste, argued: the decision (events money moves to referrals) is what this team will act on. Yet "New sign-ups by channel" names a category, and the note sits off to the side where the eye reaches it last. Make the title the finding, e.g. "Organic search now brings a third of sign-ups; events budget moves to referrals". Keep the note for the reasons behind it.

**Smaller things.**
- The note's heading "What changed this quarter" sits at about y174. That lines up with neither the subtitle (about y145) nor the chart's top gridline (about y195). Hang it from the chart top so the two columns share a line (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- Every bar has a value label and there is also a y-axis with gridlines. That is light redundancy, harmless here since the gridlines are pale. You could drop the axis to quiet the chart further.

**What's working.**
- Bars are sorted by value, start at zero, and carry direct labels with no legend, so the ranking reads in one pass.
- The copy is specific and checks out: 1,840 of 5,400 is 34%, and 410 + 260 = 670, under 700. The source line gives the period and the total, so the numbers can be trusted.
- The type scale is tight: title, subtitle and body, then a small source line. Each size has a clear job.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Nothing that matters for this piece.
