**First read.** The eye goes first to the three big figures across the top: 3.8%, €64.20, 12.4k. Then it drops to the orange bars and gets pulled to the tall Saturday bar and the short Sunday one. It reads as a small online bakery's admin home page, and it feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. It looks a bit stock, but in a way that suits the job.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing screen of a shop owner's admin tool, meant to answer "how is the business doing, and where is the load?" in a glance.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The structure is clean, the hierarchy is right and the figures are honest. What holds it back is the chart. It is the largest thing on the screen, but it only shows a pattern. It never says what the pattern means or what the owner should do about it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart names its topic, not its finding, and leads nowhere** (lower two-thirds). "Orders by delivery day" is a category label. Most of the screen goes to a chart whose point (Friday and Saturday carry most of the week, and Sunday is about a third of Saturday) the owner has to work out alone, along with why it matters: baking capacity, delivery slots, staffing. Rewrite the title to state the finding, for example "Saturday is your busiest delivery day, at 1,130 orders". If Sunday is low because of limited delivery rather than low demand, a short note on that bar would stop it being read as a problem. Also consider one step the owner can take, such as a link to Saturday's orders or to delivery-slot settings. This is taste: no approved source covers chart titles. The argument is that a dashboard is for deciding, and this one stops at describing. The KPI cards above already give their numbers context (▲ +0.4 pts vs previous 30 days), but the chart has none. A faint bar for August behind each day would show whether the Saturday peak is growing.
2. **The time periods are worded four different ways** (KPI card subtitles and the chart source line). The cards say "Last 30 days", "As of 30 September 2026" and "vs previous 30 days" / "vs 31 August", and the chart says "orders delivered in September 2026". A stranger can't tell whether "last 30 days" and "September" are the same window. If the screen is viewed on 12 October, they aren't, and the chart and the conversion figure then describe different months without saying so. Use one stated period for the whole screen, such as a single "1–30 September 2026" line under "Shop overview", and one comparison phrase ("vs August"). Taking these off every card also removes a line of grey text from each one. This is a defect: the owner may compare figures that don't cover the same days.

**What's working**
- **The KPI cards.** Each pairs a large figure, a de-emphasised unit (the grey %, € and k) and a comparison that carries meaning through the ▲ and + sign as well as the green. That is exactly what a headline number needs, so keep this pattern as the template for any new metric.
- **The chart's restraint.** It uses one hue, faint gridlines, a zero baseline and weekdays in their natural order. It has no legend or other clutter, so all the ink is data.
- **One grid.** The three cards line up exactly with the chart card's edges, and the gutters match. That is why the page reads as one system and not as a set of widgets.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the KPI cards and the bars are clickable or have hover detail (exact counts per day), and whether the period can be changed anywhere. If it can't, the wording issue in finding 2 matters more.
