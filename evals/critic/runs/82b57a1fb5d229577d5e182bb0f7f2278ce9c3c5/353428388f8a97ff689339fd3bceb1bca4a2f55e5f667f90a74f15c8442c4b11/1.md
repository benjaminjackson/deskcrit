**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "River ferry timetable", then gets pulled straight into the heavy black grid, which takes up most of the frame. It's clearly a timetable for one crossing in one direction. It feels plain and trustworthy but caged, more like a printed spreadsheet than something you check while walking to the landing.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a rider checks it on a screen or a posted sheet to find the next sailing from North landing and whether it runs today.

**Verdict.** It works. The content is correct, ordered and easy to look up, and nothing stops a rider from finding a sailing. The main thing holding it back is the full black border on every cell. It makes the grid the loudest thing on the page, so the times you came for sit inside a cage of lines that are as dark as the numbers.

**What would move it most**
1. **The grid is louder than the times** (convention, and also flagged by Jev). Every cell in the table has a near-black border about 2 px thick, the same darkness as the times. With 8 rows by 4 columns, the frame has more ink than the data. A rider scanning down "Leaves north" for the next sailing has to read past a horizontal rule between every row and two vertical rules on each side. Tufte (*Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5) and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, 23.2) both say the lines that hold a display together should be lighter than the data. Fix: drop the vertical rules and the outer box, and make the row dividers thin and pale grey (or use a light tint on every other row). Keep one firmer rule under the header row. The times then become the darkest marks on the page.
2. **Each departure sits far from its arrival** (taste). The table is stretched to the full 1080 px width, so "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are each about 300 px wide. The time pair (06:40 → 06:52) is split across roughly 300 px of empty cell. Your eye has to jump that gap and can slip a row on the way, which is worse once the row rules are lighter. A timetable is read across rows, so related times should sit close together. Fix: size each column to its content and let the table end short of the page width, or keep the width and move the space to the right of the "Runs" column. Every crossing takes 12 minutes, so you could also state "12-minute crossing" in the subtitle and make the arrival column quieter.

**Smaller things.**
- **Times in the two time columns** look like they're set in proportional figures: "11:00" ends visibly short of "06:40" above it. Use tabular figures (Inter has `tnum`) so the digits line up down the column (Bringhurst 4.4.3). This needs checking in the source, because the difference is fine to judge from a screenshot.
- **Headers "Leaves north" / "Arrives quay"** are clipped labels: you have to look back at the subtitle to know what "north" and "quay" mean. "Leaves North landing" and "Arrives Market quay" fit easily in these widths.
- **The "Runs" column** is the only thing that tells you whether a sailing runs today, yet it carries the same weight as everything else. "Weekends" on the only evening sailing is easy to miss. Consider making the non-daily entries stand out with weight rather than colour alone.
- **Jev: heavy border on every cell.** This is finding 1: the full black grid in the table body.

**What's working.**
- **Clear type hierarchy:** a bold title, a grey subtitle, bold column headers and regular body text. That's four distinct levels with no near-duplicate sizes. Keep it.
- **The subtitle** answers "which direction and when is this valid" before you read a single time. That's exactly the context a rider needs.
- **Rows in time order with a period label.** "Morning / Midday / Afternoon / Evening" gives you a quick way into the list before you read exact times.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the table reflows on a phone: at 4 wide columns it will either scroll sideways or squeeze. Check it at 375 px wide.
