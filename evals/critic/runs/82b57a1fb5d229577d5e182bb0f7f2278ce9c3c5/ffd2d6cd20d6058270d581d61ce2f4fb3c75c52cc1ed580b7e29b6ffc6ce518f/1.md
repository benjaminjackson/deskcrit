**First read.** My eye goes to the bold sans headline "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into a wall of heavy brush script. That script is what you notice and remember. The headline says plain, practical notice. The body says party invitation or café chalkboard. Then I have to slow down and read the script word by word.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short information page for ferry passengers about the route, how long a crossing takes, bicycles, and winter hours. People skim it for facts such as when the boat runs and where the timetables are.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The layout is clean and the copy is clear, but every sentence is in a decorative script that's slow to read. Everything here is information people read to act on: how often the boat runs, when it stops, where to find a timetable.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are set in a brush script** (all three body paragraphs, x 120–800, y 140–520). This is a convention break and a defect. Linked, heavy brush letters at body size make readers decode each word instead of taking in a whole phrase. The facts people need are the slowest to read: "every forty minutes", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour", "before dusk". The script also hurts readers with low vision or dyslexia most. The rule is that paragraphs go in a face made for reading, and script is kept for a few words of display (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1). The fix is to set the paragraphs in a plain text face. The headline's sans would do and would give the page one family. If you want a hand-made feel, keep the script to a few words, at most.
2. **The two faces don't belong to one piece** (headline vs body). This is taste and common sense, since no approved source covers it. The geometric grotesque headline is plain and sounds like official information. The brush script sounds casual and festive. They share nothing except colour, so the page looks like two templates pasted together, and you can't tell whether this is official ferry information or someone's flyer. Fixing item 1 fixes this too: choose one voice for the page, and for a timetable notice that voice is plain.

**Smaller things.** Jev's only flag, "Paragraphs are set in a decorative face" (0.92), is the same as finding 1, so there's nothing more to add. One copy note: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" is a rule passengers have to follow, so it could speak to them directly ("Wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded").

**What's working.**
- The structure is sound. There's one clear headline and three short paragraphs, one topic each (route and timing, bicycles, winter), with even gaps and one crisp left edge at about x=120. Keep this structure.
- The copy is concrete and complete, with real numbers and places ("north landing", "market quay", "twelve minutes", "shelter at each landing"). Once it's in a readable face, it will do its job.
- The line length is about 75 characters at most, and the text is dark on white, which is easy to read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this page reflows to a phone width. Passengers are likely to read it on a phone at the landing.
