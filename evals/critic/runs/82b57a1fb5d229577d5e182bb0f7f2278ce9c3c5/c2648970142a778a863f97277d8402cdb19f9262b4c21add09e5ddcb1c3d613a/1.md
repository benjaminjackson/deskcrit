**First read.** The eye lands on the bold black headline, stalls on "line" sitting alone on the second line, then drops to the italic standfirst and into the body. It reads as the opening of a long-form feature in a small independent magazine about local life. It feels calm, quiet and well-behaved, and also a little anonymous: one grey sans-serif and no picture, so it could be any blog.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the opening screen of an online magazine feature, meant to pull a general reader into a long article.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. It's clean, readable and in a clear order, and the writing is strong. What holds it back is craft at the two places every reader meets first: the headline breaks badly, and the lines of text are too long for comfortable long-form reading.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline breaks on "bus / line"** (top of the column). "line" sits alone on the second line. The biggest type on the page ends on a runt, and the break splits the phrase "bus line", so the eye reads "the end of the bus…" and then has to pick up the noun on its own. It looks like nobody set the headline; it just wrapped. Bringhurst (2.4.2) covers the short last line. Splitting the phrase is a convention the catalog has no confirmed source for, so treat that part as common sense. **Defect** for a headline this size. Fix: break at a phrase boundary, for example "The allotment at the end / of the bus line". Get there by narrowing the headline's measure or balancing its lines, not by shrinking the type.
2. **The text lines are too long** (standfirst and body). Lines run about 83 to 87 characters ("It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to"). For a piece people are meant to read to the end, the eye has a long way back to the start of each line, and reading tires sooner. **Convention**: Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 characters, about 66 ideal (2.1.2). Fix: narrow the text column to roughly 65 characters at this type size. The headline can stay wider than the text if you want it to.
3. **The first screen has no picture, though the byline promises "Photographs by the residents"** (the whole screen, with a blank band across the lower third). This is a feature about a place and its people, and its selling point is pictures taken by the people in it. The opening shows none, so the page looks like a generic template: a sans-serif headline and grey text that could belong to any publication. **Taste**: I think a lead photo by a resident, between the byline and the standfirst or straight after it, would do more to pull readers in than anything else here. If an image is meant to sit in that blank band and simply hadn't loaded, see below.

**Smaller things.**
- Standfirst (lines 2 to 5 of the article): four full lines set in italic, which Jev flagged with high confidence. Long runs of italic are harder to read, particularly for dyslexic readers. The BDA's advice says so, but it is not an approved source here, so this is common sense rather than a rule. Keep the standfirst roman and set it apart by size or colour, or make it short enough that italic doesn't matter.
- Top-right navigation: nothing marks which section this article belongs to. Give "Features" a single cue such as weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- "Subscribe" (top right) is the site's one commercial action but is styled exactly like the section links. This is taste: give it a quiet button or link treatment so it reads as an action rather than a section.

**What's working.**
- The writing. The headline names a specific, odd place, and the standfirst lands on a concrete human detail: the queue for the tap as the best place to hear the news. The first body paragraph opens with a person, a date and a condition. Keep this voice. It is the strongest thing on the page.
- The hierarchy is clear: a small green "Feature" label, a heavy headline, a grey byline, then the text. Four levels, each obviously different, with green used only once.
- The spacing is generous and consistent. The single centred reading column and the clean header suit a long read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The empty band below "Most of them are still in use." could be the end of the capture or a lead image that hadn't loaded. Check it in a live browser. If a photo belongs there, finding 3 is about where it sits rather than whether it exists.
- Whether the navigation links' tap areas reach 24 px: they look small, so measure them.
