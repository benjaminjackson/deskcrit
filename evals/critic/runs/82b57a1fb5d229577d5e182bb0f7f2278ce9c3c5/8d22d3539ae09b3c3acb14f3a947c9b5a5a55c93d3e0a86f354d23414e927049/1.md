**First read.** The eye lands on the huge four-line headline, which fills most of the screen, and then drops to the small two-sentence paragraph. The page looks like the top of a ferry timetable: calm, neutral and competent. Then the reading stops, because no times, dates or departures appear anywhere. The feel is clean but generic; this could be any service's page.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the header of a web page for passengers who want to know when the spring ferry runs.

**Verdict.** As a timetable it fails. The kicker promises a timetable, the headline takes four lines to describe the season, and the viewer leaves without one departure time. The single biggest problem is that the copy and the space go to describing the service rather than giving people the information they came for.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable has no times** (defect). The page is labelled "Spring timetable", but the only schedule information is one line of body text: "every forty minutes" from the north landing. The viewer can't answer the two questions a timetable exists for: when the first and last boats leave, and which dates are covered. "By daylight" and "spring through early summer" sound like answers, but they make the reader guess. Daylight changes by more than two hours across those months, and "early summer" could end in June or in July. Someone planning a trip will make up their own times and dates, and some will get them wrong. Below this text there are about 200 px of blank screen. Either the times are missing, or they start off-screen. Either way, the first screen should carry the facts: the date range ("23 March – 30 June"), first and last sailings from each side, and the 40-minute interval. Make one of these the dominant element, for example the next departure or the first and last sailings.
2. **The headline takes the space and tells the viewer least** (defect / taste). At this size the headline is a 13-word sentence over four lines, roughly 330 px tall. That is about 40% of the screen, spent on a description whose words are all vague ("by daylight", "early summer"). The concrete facts (every forty minutes, about twelve minutes per crossing, north landing to market quay) sit in small body text the eye reaches last. The hierarchy itself works, with one clear dominant element and three distinct levels. The problem is what got put at the top. Shorten the headline to a name or a promise ("Quayside ferry: every 40 minutes, north landing to market quay") and use the space you gain for the times.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline, lines 3–4: "early / summer" breaks one phrase across two lines, and "summer" ends up alone on the last line. Rebreak it so "early summer" stays together, or let a shorter headline fix the break. This is common sense: the reference's heading-break source is unverified.
- Body paragraph: "weather." sits alone on the last line. Adjust the measure slightly or add a non-breaking space so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: one family in three clearly separate levels (a small bold grey kicker, a heavy display headline, a regular body). Each size has an obvious job, so keep that when you add the times.
- The body text is set well. It is comfortably large, about 68 characters per line, with generous leading and good contrast, so it's easy to read.
- Everything shares one firm left edge, and the space is gathered into a few large areas rather than scattered gaps. Keep this when the schedule goes in.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the timetable sits further down the page. If it does, it still needs to appear on this first screen, or at least be signposted here.
- How the four-line headline reflows on a phone. At this size it would probably fill the whole first screen there.
