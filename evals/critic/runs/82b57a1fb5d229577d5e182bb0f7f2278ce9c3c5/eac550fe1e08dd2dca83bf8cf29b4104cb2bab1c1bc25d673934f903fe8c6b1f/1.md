**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, then down two calm, easy paragraphs, then gets to a third line that is a little smaller, as if someone missed a style. It reads as a plain information page for people using a local river ferry. It feels quiet, clear and a bit bare.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers how the service works, so they can plan a crossing and know the rules for bicycles.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and does its job. What holds the page back is the content and one size choice: the page never gives the times a passenger would plan around, and the one warning they must act on is the smallest thing on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page has no times a passenger can plan with (defect, copy).** The first paragraph says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening." A reader can't tell when the first boat leaves, when the last one leaves, or which minutes past the hour it sails. It also says the ferry runs "from the north landing to the market quay" and never says whether it comes back, so someone at the quay can't tell if they can get home. The page states facts about the service and stops before what the reader needs to do with them. Fix: give the first and last sailings from each side (e.g. "First boat 6:40 from the north landing, last 21:20 from the market quay"), or a short timetable. Then "every forty minutes" becomes something people can use.
2. **The winter warning is set one small step smaller than the body text, so it reads as a mistake and as less important (convention + defect).** The body paragraphs look about 19 px. The last line looks about 16 px, in the same face, weight and colour. That's too close to read as a separate level, so it looks like a styling slip (Bringhurst, *Elements*, §3.1.1: build sizes from a few distinct intervals; Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: a weak contrast is worse than none). It also turns the hierarchy upside down. "Timetables change in winter" is the line most likely to leave someone waiting on an empty landing, and it carries the least weight on the page. Fix: set it at body size, or make it clearly different as a notice (bold lead-in, a tint, or placed near the times). Don't keep it a step smaller.
3. **The warning sends readers to a noticeboard at the landing, which they only see once they're already there (defect, copy).** "Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel" asks people to check before they travel, at a place they reach only by travelling. Fix: say when winter hours start and give the winter times here, or point to something they can check from home.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing separately.

**What's working.**
- One left edge, one column of readable width (about 70–75 characters a line in the body), and generous line spacing make the page effortless to read. Keep the measure if you add a timetable.
- The heading uses size and bold weight together, so the start is obvious and there's only one place for the eye to land.
- The bicycle paragraph is good copy. It says who does what, in what order and where ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"), and a rider could follow it with no questions.
