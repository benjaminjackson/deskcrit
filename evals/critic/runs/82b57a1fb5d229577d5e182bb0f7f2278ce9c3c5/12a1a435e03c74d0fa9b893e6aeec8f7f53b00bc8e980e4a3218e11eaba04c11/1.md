**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, then drops to the orange bars and picks out Saturday as the tallest and Sunday as the shortest. It reads as a small bakery shop's admin overview. It looks calm, clean and competent. Then the questions start: 3.8 what? 64.20 in which currency? And "12.4 active subscribers" can't be true as written.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the shop owner's home screen: a quick look at how the business is doing right now, and where to act.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are well made. But the three numbers at the top, which are the first thing anyone reads, tell the owner almost nothing. They have no units, no time period and no comparison. Until they say "3.8% this month, up from 3.1%", this is a well-made frame around numbers that don't tell you anything.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPIs have no units, and one can't be taken literally** (defect). This is the top row, all three cards:
   - "3.8" is probably a percent, but the viewer has to guess.
   - "64.20" has no currency sign.
   - "12.4" under "Active subscribers" must mean 12.4 thousand or something similar, because you can't have 0.4 of a subscriber. As written, anyone who doesn't already know the real figure could read it as 12, or as 12,400.

   These are the biggest type on the page, so a guessed meaning here is the one the owner carries away. Tufte and Few both hold that a reader should never have to guess what a number measures. Fix: put the unit on every figure: "3.8%", "£64.20" (or the right currency), "12,400" or "12.4k".
2. **No time period and no comparison, so the owner can't tell good from bad** (convention). None of the three cards says what period it covers, and none sets the figure against last period, a target or a trend. Is 3.8% better than last month? The screen doesn't say, so the owner has to remember it or go and look it up. That defeats the point of an overview. Few's "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" names this exact pitfall. Fix: next to each number, add the period and one comparison, such as "Nov so far · ▲0.4 pts vs Oct". Show the change in words or with a sign, not only in colour. A small sparkline would also work.
3. **The chart's period doesn't match the cards, and the chart doesn't say its point** (taste/common sense). The source line says September 2026, but the cards have no date at all. So the owner can't tell whether the cards and the chart describe the same time. If today really is late November, September data on an "overview" is also stale. Separately, the title "Orders by delivery day" names the topic and leaves the reader to find the finding: Saturday is about three times Sunday, and Friday and Saturday together carry the week. (The rule that a title should state the takeaway comes from sources outside the approved list, so I'm arguing it as common sense.) Fix: use one period across the page, shown once near "Shop overview". Turn the chart title into the finding, e.g. "Friday and Saturday bring in a third of the week's orders", or label the peak directly on the bars.

**What's working.**
- **The page is easy to scan.** It has one clear grid: three equal cards over one full-width panel, with matching gutters and left edges that line up from the wordmark down to the source line.
- **The type scale is tight.** I count about five sizes (page title, big figures, card labels, chart title, small axis and source text), each with a clear job and big steps between them. Hierarchy comes from weight as well as size.
- **The chart itself is honest and plain.** A single hue, bars starting from zero, light gridlines, days in their natural order, and a source line. Keep all of it. Only the title and the period need work.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether a focused nav link shows a visible focus ring. Whether hovering a bar shows exact values, which would matter because the axis only gives values in steps of 300.
