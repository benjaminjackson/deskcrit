**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline, "Shipments peak each third quarter", drops to the tallest bar (705), then moves across a row of chunky blue 3D columns. It reads as a corporate board slide. It feels tidy and competent, but a little dated because of the extruded bars.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: slide 3 of a board deck, showing directors eight quarters of retail shipments, the last of them forecast, so they can judge the trend and the outlook.

**Verdict.** The slide mostly works. The headline states a true finding, the bars are labelled, the axis starts at zero, and the layout is clean. The biggest thing in the way is that the forecast quarter is drawn exactly like the seven real ones, so a director scanning the bars will take 560 as shipped. After that, the 3D styling adds ink and no information.

**What would move it most**
1. **The Q4 2026 forecast bar looks like an actual** (defect). The last bar, at the far right, has the same solid blue, the same extrusion and the same bold label as the seven actual quarters. The only signs that it is an estimate are a parenthesis at the end of the subtitle and a clause in the source line at the foot of the slide. A board remembers numbers and holds management to them. Someone who glances at the bars sees 560 as booked and reads the Q4 rise over 480 as done, not expected. The rule is that projections must look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). The fix is to mark the bar itself: an outline or hatched fill, or a lighter tint, plus "Forecast" written under or on the bar. If the September forecast has a range, show it as an error bar.
2. **The bars are drawn in 3D** (convention, which Jev also flagged). All eight columns have a lighter top face and a darker right side, about 14 px deep. That depth shows no data. It doubles the ink on every bar, and the cap makes each bar look a little taller than its value, so the eye has to choose between the front edge and the top face. Here the cost is limited, because every bar has its value printed on it and the front faces do sit on the right gridlines. But on a slide for decision-makers it looks like a spreadsheet default, which undercuts the care shown everywhere else. The fix: "Draw the chart flat, with no depth, sides, or tilt, so that each value is read from one length" (Wilke, ch. 26.1; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 7). Use flat blue bars and keep the labels.
3. **The headline states the pattern but not what it means for the board** (taste). "Shipments peak each third quarter" is accurate, but seasonality is what this board most likely already knows. The news in the data is growth: Q3 went from 610 to 705, about +16% year on year, and the forecast Q4 is 560 against 480 a year earlier. As it stands, the viewer has to compare bars four positions apart and do the arithmetic. If growth is the point, say it in the headline, for example "Q3 shipments up 16% on last year; the seasonal peak holds." Then consider tinting or labelling the two Q3 bars so the comparison is visible.

**Smaller things.**
- The value labels already give every number, so the y-axis and gridlines repeat them. They are pale enough not to compete, but you could drop them for a quieter plot.
- Jev flagged a 3D chart, which is covered in finding 2. It found nothing else.

**What's working.**
- **The headline is a finding, not a topic.** It turns the chart into a claim the viewer can check at a glance. Keep that pattern even if the wording changes.
- **Honest, well-labelled chart.** The axis starts at zero, there is a units line ("Thousands of units"), every bar is labelled directly, there is no legend, and there is a source line. A director never has to guess what a number means.
- **A restrained page.** There is one blue, quiet grey for secondary text, a clear type hierarchy (serif headline, sans for everything else), and consistent margins between the header, the chart and the footer.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- There is an empty band of about 130 px under the footer. It is probably the capture viewport rather than the slide, so check it at the real slide size.
- With a board meeting in October, check whether Q3 2026 is fully closed or partly estimated as well. If it is partly estimated, it needs the same marking as Q4.
