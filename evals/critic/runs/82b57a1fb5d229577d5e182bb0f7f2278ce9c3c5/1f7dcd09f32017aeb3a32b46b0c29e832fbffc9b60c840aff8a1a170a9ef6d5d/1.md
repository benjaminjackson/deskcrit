**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month", then drops to the blue bars and climbs the staircase to the June bar, which jumps well above the others. Next it goes to "$4.82M" at the top of the right column. It reads as a calm, trustworthy credit union report for members. It's tidy and plain, and it looks like a lot of good news.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public page telling members how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026, so they come away with an accurate sense of whether the credit union is growing.

**Verdict.** It's well made and easy to read, but the main message is misleading. The headline and the chart tell members deposits are surging. The page's own small print and card say otherwise: June's jump is a one-off bonus payroll, and the real growth is 6.1% on last year. Until the headline and the chart tell the same story as the numbers, the craft is working for the wrong message.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline and chart sell seasonality as growth.** *Defect.* The bars go from $612k to $1,150k, nearly double, under a headline about rising every month. Anyone reading the chart sees runaway growth. But the "Total deposited" card says the half year is only up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025, so most of the January-to-June climb is what happens every year. Showing six months of one year leaves out the comparison that would reveal this. That's common sense rather than a cited rule, because the catalog's source for this point isn't on the approved list. The June bar, the biggest thing on the page, is explained only in a 12px grey footnote ("June includes the mid-year bonus payroll"). The headline and the bars agree, but the data underneath them doesn't. For a credit union, where members trust what they read, a reader who later spots the footnote will doubt the whole report. Fix direction:
   - Make the year-on-year comparison the story. Show 2025 next to 2026 for each month (a grey bar beside each blue one, or a grey line), or chart change against the same month last year.
   - Rewrite the headline around what's true for the member, for example deposits up 6.1% on last year.
   - Move the bonus-payroll note onto the June bar as a direct label, not the footnote.
2. **The subtitle and the chart title say the same thing.** *Convention* (Nielsen, heuristic 8: aesthetic and minimalist design). "New money paid into member savings accounts, by month, January to June 2026" and "Deposits into savings accounts, US dollars per month" are two lines in a row restating one fact. The reader reads both and learns nothing new from the second. Keep the units in the chart title. Use the subtitle for something only it can say, such as the year-on-year context in finding 1.

**Smaller things.**
- Number formats don't match: "$1150k" on the June bar and "$1200k" on the axis, next to "$4.82M" in the card. Use $1.15M and $1.2M so the eye compares like with like.
- The three cards cover different periods (Jan to Jun, then June only, then June only), but the cards look identical. A reader may take the average deposit and account count as half-year figures. Either cover the same period or make the difference more visible.
- "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025": "on" for "compared with" reads oddly to many US readers. "Up $11 from June 2025" can't be misread.

**What's working.**
- The bars are labelled directly with their values, start from zero, use one colour and faint gridlines. Nothing on the chart is decoration. Keep that discipline when you add a 2025 comparison series: make it grey and label it directly, with no legend.
- The type plan is clear. A serif for the brand and the headline against a plain sans for everything else gives one obvious voice. The letterspaced capital eyebrow ("MEMBER REPORT, FIRST HALF 2026") tells you where you are without competing.
- Each card puts its comparison right under the figure ("Up 6.1% on…"), so no number stands alone. That's exactly what makes finding 1 fixable: the honest figure is already on the page.
