**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline. Then it goes straight to the four blue-outlined cards, which pull about as hard as the "Create your link" button between them. Almost everything is the same blue: the nav, headline, buttons, card borders, icons, titles, badges, links and the divider. It reads as a scheduling-link SaaS homepage for small service businesses. It feels tidy but flat and generic, and the right half of the hero is empty. (Disclosure: I opened the Jev file together with the image, before writing this. It flagged the same blue problem I saw.)

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage hero of a booking-link product, meant to get small-business owners (clinics, tutors, coaches) to click "Create your link".

**Verdict.** It's clear and competent, but it won't sell. The blue accent is on almost every element, so the one button that matters doesn't stand out. And the empty right half of the hero leaves the product unseen in a pitch that has nothing else to show it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The blue is everywhere, so it marks nothing (defect).** The same saturated blue is on the headline, the four nav links, both buttons, the four 2 px card borders, four icons, four card titles, four badges, four "Learn more" links and the rule above the customer names. The viewer gets no signal of where to act: "Create your link" is one blue thing among about 25. Blue text also stops meaning "clickable", so the blue card titles look like links and the blue headline looks clickable. This is convention (Apple HIG, Colour; Material Design, colour roles): keep the accent for the main action and at most one key highlight. Fix direction: make the headline, card titles and icons near-black or a dark neutral, the card borders a pale grey or a light tint (or remove them), and the badges neutral. Keep blue for the two "Create/Start" buttons and the links. The button then becomes the obvious second stop after the headline.
2. **The right half of the hero is empty, and the product is never shown (defect, unless an image failed to load).** From x ≈ 660 to 1216, above the cards, there is nothing. The page looks left-heavy and unfinished (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). More importantly, a booking-link product is easy to show: a guest's view of the time-slot picker would prove "let people pick a time that works" at a glance. As it stands, the viewer has to imagine the product or click "See a live example". If an image is supposed to be there, check that it loads. If not, add one: the booking page as a guest sees it, in their own time zone.
3. **Four boxed cards of equal weight make it look templated (taste and convention).** Four heavy blue outlines turn the lower half into a row of identical compartments. They compete with the hero and look like every SaaS feature grid, so nothing says why this tool and not the one the viewer already uses. Tufte and Lupton both argue for space or a pale tone instead of rules and boxes (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*). Remove the borders or soften them to a hairline grey and let the spacing group each card. Also make the copy compare: the hero line "Booking links that fill your calendar" could be on any rival's page. Name what's different, such as no back-and-forth emails, or fewer no-shows than with phone booking. That lets the viewer weigh it against how they book today.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Shared calendars" title wraps to two lines, so that card's text and "Learn more" sit about 30 px lower than in the other three cards. Shorten it ("Team calendars"), or give the title row a fixed height, so the cards line up.
- The "Email" and "Auto" badges don't tell the viewer anything new. Only "New" and "Beta" carry real news. Drop the other two.
- The customer strip is plain grey names with no logos, numbers or quotes, so it reads as filler rather than proof. Use real logos, or one line with a number (for example, how many bookings or practices) and a short quote.
- Four identical "Learn more" links make vague link text (common sense). They pass WCAG 2.4.4 in context, but "See how reminders work" would make each one clearer.

**What's working.**
- The hero copy is plain and concrete. "Share one link, let people pick a time that works" says what the product does in one breath. Keep it.
- The card body copy lands each feature on the viewer: "so nobody has to do the math", "cut down on no-shows", "the first minutes are useful". That's the right voice; push the headline toward it.
- The type is clean: one family, a clear scale (headline, card title, body, caption), a tight two-line headline, and comfortable line lengths. And the call to action is a clear pair: a filled primary button and an underlined secondary link.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half of the hero is a choice or an image that didn't load. Check in a live browser.
- Hover and focus states on the cards and links.

**Checklist.**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 6 yes, 2 no (main image and headline make one idea; every element does a job)
- What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 9 yes, 4 no (one grid / near-miss alignment; baselines across columns; white space gathered; balance)
- Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
- Color: 9 lines, 8 yes, 1 no (strong colour kept to a small share)
- Pictures: 12 lines, 11 yes, 1 no (every image shows its content)
- Charts and data: skipped, no chart
- Interface: 34 lines, 33 yes, 1 no (controls look like what they do: blue headings read as links)
