**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", which is clean, dark and clearly in charge. Then it hits three blocks of capitals and slows to a crawl. Each line becomes a grey bar of same-height letters that you decode word by word instead of reading. It reads as a ferry service's passenger information page. The top feels calm and competent, and the body feels like it's shouting small print at you.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and how bikes work, so they can plan a trip and board without fuss.

**Verdict.** The layout is sound, but the body is set in all capitals, and that makes the one part people came for the hardest part to read. Fix that, then fix the vague times. As it stands a passenger can't actually plan a trip from this page.

**What would move it most**
1. **All three paragraphs are set in capitals (defect).** About eight lines of running text in caps lose the word shapes that ascenders and descenders give lower case. The reader has to spell their way through "FORTY MINUTES BETWEEN EARLY MORNING AND LATE EVENING" instead of taking it in at a glance. It also reads as shouting, which fights the calm heading. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says don't capitalize whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) notes that text in capitals is especially hard to read. Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for short labels like the "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow, which already does this well.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing, so nobody can tell whether they'll catch the last boat home. "In winter" has no dates. "One boat each hour" doesn't say at what minute past. The page then sends you to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", but by then you're already at the quay. These are the lines people plan around and later hold the operator to, and right now each can be read several ways. Fix: give the actual first and last departure times and the winter date range, and either show the timetable here or link to it.
3. **The bike rule is stated but never said to the rider (taste, common sense).** "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" describes a procedure without telling the cyclist what to do: wait at the back of the queue, and bring a lock, since bikes are locked "to the rail". Fix: address it to the rider with "you": "Wait until foot passengers are on board, then wheel your bike onto the lower deck and lock it to the rail."

**What's working.**
- **Clear hierarchy.** The type scale is tight: a small tracked grey label, a large bold heading, and one body size. Three levels with big steps between them, so you always know what's a label, what's the title and what's the text.
- **Comfortable spacing.** Lines run about 65–75 characters with generous leading. The paragraphs are short, separated by space alone, and flush left. Once it's in lower case, this column will be easy to read.
- **The eyebrow label.** "PASSENGER NOTES" is the right use of capitals: short, letterspaced, and lighter in colour so it sits back from the heading.
