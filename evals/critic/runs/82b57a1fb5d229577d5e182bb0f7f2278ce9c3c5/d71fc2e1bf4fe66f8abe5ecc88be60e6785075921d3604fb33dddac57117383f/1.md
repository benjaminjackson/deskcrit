**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Plan the week with your whole team", goes across to the blue "Create account" button, then up through the form. It reads at once as the sign-up page of a team-planning SaaS called Tallyboard. It feels calm, tidy and competent, and also like many other sign-up pages: nothing here could only belong to this product.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a desktop sign-up page that should turn a team lead who arrived from marketing into a new workspace, and let returning users get to sign-in.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the form is short and well labelled, and there is one obvious action. What holds it back is the form detail and the copy. The password rule disappears as soon as you start typing, the sign-in link doesn't look like a link, and the offer line can be read two ways on the one thing a buyer will hold you to: what is free.

**What would move it most**
1. **The password rule is only in the placeholder** (Password field, "At least 10 characters"). Once the user types the first character, the rule is gone. Then they find out they missed it from an error after submitting. **Defect**: users have to remember something the screen should keep showing them (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Fix: move the rule into helper text that stays under the field, and use the placeholder for nothing, or for an example.
2. **"Sign In" doesn't look like a link** (the line under the button). It's the same grey, size and weight as "Already have an account?", with no underline and no brand blue. Returning users often land on sign-up pages, and they will hunt for the way in or start making a duplicate account. **Defect**: a link must differ from the text around it by more than colour, and here it doesn't even differ by colour (WCAG Technique G183; NN/g, Moran, "Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention"). Fix: set it in the Tallyboard blue with an underline. Also make it "Sign in" in sentence case, to match "Create account".
3. **The offer line can be read two ways, and the ask doesn't repeat it** (left column body and button). "Start a free workspace for up to five people. You can invite the rest of the team later." One reading: free forever for five, and paying once you add a sixth. The other: free for now, and everyone else joins later at no cost. The viewer will hold you to whichever they choose, and the wrong one shows up as a surprise bill or a lost upgrade. **Defect** (copy, common sense). Fix: say what happens at person six ("Free for up to 5 people. Add more on a paid plan, from $X per person"). Separately, the headline and the button are generic. "Plan the week with your whole team" fits any planner, and "Create account" names a chore, not the thing they're getting. **Taste**: make the button say what they get ("Start your free workspace"). Give the headline one concrete detail that's only true of Tallyboard, ideally something the viewer can compare with what they use now ("Your team's week on one board, not in five Slack threads").

**Smaller things.**
- Required fields: nothing tells the user which of the five fields are required (Company in particular). Mark the optional ones with "(optional)", or drop Company from sign-up and ask for it later (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- Input borders: the pale grey-blue outlines look like they may fall below 3:1 against white. Measure them, and darken them if they fail (WCAG 1.4.11).
- Balance: the left column's text ends about halfway down (y≈360), while the card runs to y≈665. The lower-left quarter is empty and the page tips right. Move the copy block down to the card's optical centre, or let a product glimpse or one proof line fill that space.

**What's working.**
- One clear focal point and a clean three-level type scale: the headline in heavy weight, then the card title, then the labels and body. Every level is obvious at a glance.
- The form is done properly. Labels sit above the fields, only first and last name share a row, and the order is logical. There is exactly one filled primary button, in the page's only accent colour.
- Restraint in colour: grey neutrals with one blue, used only for the wordmark and the action, so the button can't be missed.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indicators on the fields and button; how errors and validation appear; how the two-column layout reflows on a phone.
