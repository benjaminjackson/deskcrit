**First read.** The eye lands at once on "Planning a vegetable bed" and then runs straight down the left edge through the three section headings. It reads as a short gardening how-to, a blog post or help page for a beginner. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, and a bit bare, like a well-set default stylesheet that nobody has tuned yet.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web article that teaches a first-time gardener to set up a vegetable bed, read top to bottom and scanned again later while they do the work.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The order is clear, the type is clean and the groups are spaced correctly. The main thing in the way is the reading measure: the paragraphs run too wide for comfortable reading. After that, the headings rely on size alone.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are too wide.** The text column runs from x≈160 to ≈950, and lines like "Dig to the depth of a spade and mix in compost from last year's heap. Rake the surface level" come to about 90 characters. Bringhurst gives 45–75 as comfortable and 66 as ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style* §2.1.2). At this width the eye has a long way back to the start of each line and can land on the wrong line. Most of the paragraphs here are two lines, so the cost is small now, but it grows as soon as any section gets longer. Narrow the text column to about 600–640 px at this body size, or raise the body size a step and keep the column. Either way, aim for about 65 characters per line. *Convention, with a real reading cost.*
2. **Headings differ from the text only in size.** "Preparing the soil", "Choosing what to plant" and "Watering" use the same regular weight and the same near-black as the body, and so does the title. They work because the size step is large. But someone going back to the page mid-job with dirty hands has only size to scan by, and the headings look like larger body text rather than markers. Dair's approach applies here: pair the size step with a second change. Make the headings semibold, which would also let them be a little smaller, or give them a colour. That makes the four steps stand out at a glance. *Convention (Dair, Design with Type, ch. 8–9).*

**Smaller things.**
- Title tracking: at about 56 px, "Planning a vegetable bed" looks set at body-text spacing, so it looks a little loose. Tighten the letter spacing slightly, about −1% to −2% (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 80; Bringhurst §2.1.1).
- Straight apostrophe in "last year's" (Soil section). Use a curly ’. This is common sense; the reference has no confirmed source for curly quotes, and at body size it is a detail.
- Copy: "gets six hours of sun" leaves out "a day". Most readers will fill it in, but this is a number the gardener will act on, so say "six hours of direct sun a day."
- Copy: the page is called *Planning* a bed but never says how big to make it, even though "Mark the corners" assumes a size. A beginner has to guess. Give a starting size, e.g. "about 1 m × 2 m, narrow enough to reach the middle."

**What's working.**
- **Spacing that groups correctly.** Each heading sits tight above its own paragraph, with a much bigger gap above it. The page reads as four clean chunks with no effort. Keep this ratio when you change the heading style.
- **A clear type scale.** There are three sizes (title, heading, body) with big steps between them, and each size has an obvious job. Don't add a fourth.
- **Plain, specific copy.** "Things that forgive mistakes: radishes, lettuce, and bush beans" and "twice a week, rather than a little every day" are concrete and spoken to the reader. This voice is the best thing on the page.
