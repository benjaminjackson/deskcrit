**First read.** The eye goes to "Low-light houseplants," then straight to the row of four buttons, which carry the only strong colour on the page: teal, purple, orange, blue. The orange one pulls hardest. The plants and prices come last. It reads as a tidy category page for a small plant shop, calm everywhere except for that row of mismatched buttons, which looks like a demo of colour options more than a store.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an e-commerce category page that helps someone with a dim room choose one of four plants and add it to their cart.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the page would mostly work. It is let down by one loud, unexplained decision: four identical actions in four colours. It also gives the shopper too little to choose with. The drawings don't show the real plants, and the copy gives size and price but nothing about how each plant copes with low light.

**What would move it most**
1. **Four colours for one action.** Each card has the same "Add to cart" button, but every button is a different saturated hue. Viewers assume a difference in colour means a difference in meaning, so they look for one: is purple a premium plant, is orange a warning or a sale? There is no answer to find. These buttons are also the most saturated things on the page, so they outrank the plants and prices. Orange is the worst case: it sits on the third card for no reason and comes forward the most. Defect (Material Design 3 colour roles; Apple HIG, "Color"; Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind share one style). Fix: give elements that do the same job one shared colour, and save a different colour for a different role or state. A single green taken from the leaves would tie the buttons to the illustrations and the brand, and would leave the plants as the most colourful part of each card.

2. **The pictures don't show what you're buying.** All four drawings are the same pot with two or three generic leaves. The snake plant has round paddle leaves instead of upright blades. The pothos, whose own caption says it "trails to 6 ft", is drawn as two upright leaves. On a shop page the picture is how most people tell products apart and decide which one fits their room. Here the shopper learns nothing from the images and has to choose from the names alone. Taste, on common-sense grounds. Fix: use photos, or illustrations that show each plant's real shape (tall and spiky, glossy and upright, broad, trailing). Then the four cards look different at a glance.

3. **The copy stops before the reason to choose.** The subtitle is good: "do well more than six feet from a window" is concrete and speaks to the buyer's own room. Each card then gives only pot size, height, and price. The question the page raises, which of these four suits me, gets no answer: nothing on how dim is too dim, how often to water, or whether it's safe for pets. Defect (missing information the shopper needs next). Fix: add one line per plant that says what it means for the buyer, such as "Survives a north-facing hallway; water monthly." A plain sentence like that does more than a spec.

**Smaller things.**
- The top navigation doesn't show where you are. "Houseplants" looks the same as "Pots" and "Care guides". Mark the current item with one cue, such as bolder weight or an underline (Nielsen Norman Group, "Menu-Design Checklist").

**What's working.**
- The page sits on a clean four-column grid. Cards, images, names, specs, prices, and buttons line up on shared baselines, so you can compare the four plants by reading straight across. Keep that.
- The type scale is clear: page title, product name, a small grey spec line, a bold price. Each size has one job, and the prices stand out without shouting.
- The headline and subtitle say exactly what the page is and who it's for, in the buyer's own terms ("six feet from a window").

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the buttons and nav links. The empty lower third is probably just the viewport height, not a design choice.
