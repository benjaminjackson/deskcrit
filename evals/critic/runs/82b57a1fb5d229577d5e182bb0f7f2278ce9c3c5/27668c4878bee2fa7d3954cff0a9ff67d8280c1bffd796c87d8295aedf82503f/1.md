I'm assuming this is a short web info page for ferry riders: they open it to find out how often the boat runs, how long the crossing takes, the bike rules and the winter hours.

**First read.** The eye goes to the heavy brush-script paragraphs, not to the headline. The script is darker, denser and busier than the clean sans heading above it. At a glance it looks like a café chalkboard or a party invitation. Then you start to read and it turns into work: each word has to be decoded from connected, swashy letters. The tone feels at odds with what this is, which is a practical timetable note.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a passenger looks up crossing frequency, duration, bike rules and winter hours, quickly, maybe on a phone at the landing.

**Verdict.** It fails its job. Three paragraphs of plain, practical information are set in a heavy display script, so the information is slow to read and easy to misread. That one choice is nearly the whole problem; the layout around it is sound.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in a brush script.** This covers all three paragraphs, from "The river ferry runs…" down to "…each landing." Connected, heavy script letters slow down every word. Letters that should be easy to tell apart look alike in this face. "forty" and "twelve" are the facts a passenger acts on, and in this face they are the words most likely to be skimmed wrong. Readers with low vision or dyslexia lose the most. Bringhurst's rule is that a text face is "unlikely to be carrying excess ornament", and script belongs to a few words of display, not to paragraphs (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1). **Convention, and here a defect.** Fix: set the paragraphs in a plain text face. The sans already used for the headline would do. If the page needs warmth, put the script in two or three words of display at most, or drop it.
2. **The script takes over the hierarchy.** The headline is the larger type, yet the body's heavy, dark texture pulls the eye first. The page's own label ("Notes for ferry passengers") reads second. Tschichold asks that the order of importance be shown plainly, and here the body is fighting the heading (*The New Typography*). **Defect.** Fix 1 cures it: once the body is a normal-weight text face, the bold headline takes the lead without any other change.
3. **Two moods in one page.** The headline is a neutral, modern grotesque. The body is a casual, celebratory script. They don't share a voice, so the page looks assembled rather than designed, and the festive tone undercuts information people rely on to catch a boat. **Taste** (no approved source). I'd argue it on trust: a timetable should look dependable. Fix 1 settles this too.

**Smaller things.**
- Line spacing in the script paragraphs looks tight: ascenders and swashes nearly touch the line above (for example between "every forty" and "minutes between"). With a text face at the same size, give it about 1.4–1.5× leading.
- Copy: "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "before dusk" give no times, and a passenger can't plan a trip from them. Give the first and last departures, the winter last sailing, and say which months count as winter. Then the reader doesn't have to go to the printed timetable in the shelter to find out. (This is copy, not layout, but it decides whether the page does its job.)

**What's working.**
- The structure is right for the content: one heading, three short paragraphs each on one topic (frequency and duration, bikes, winter), with clear space between them. Keep that chunking.
- The single left-aligned column with a generous left margin is calm and easy to follow. Lines run about 70–75 characters, near the top of the comfortable range. Keep the measure about where it is, or slightly shorter, when you change the face.
