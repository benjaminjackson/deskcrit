**First read.** The eye lands on the big dark-blue 38% wedge, then goes up to the serif headline. Next it gets pulled to the fan of thin slivers and tiny percentages at the upper left of the pie, then across to the legend to work out what those slivers are. It reads as an annual-report page about where the money went, and the type and margins feel calm and well made. The chart itself is busy and keeps sending you back and forth.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in a printed or PDF annual report that tells shareholders and board members what the company's operating costs consist of and what the main costs are.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The frame is sound but the chart is the wrong form for 14 categories. Ten of the slices are 1–4% slivers that nobody can tell apart or name without the legend. The page also never says what the reader should take away.

**What would move it most**
1. **The 14-slice pie is the wrong chart for this data.** (Convention, and a defect.) The pie shows one thing clearly: salaries are the biggest cost. Everything else is hard work. The ten slivers from Travel to Postage are crammed into the upper-left quarter, labelled only with "3%", "2%" or "1%". To find out which one is "Utilities", the reader has to match a small colour swatch across about 400 px of page. The sliver labels also hide something that matters: those ten "small" costs add up to 25%, which is more than Rent (16%). That makes them, taken together, the second-largest block of spending, and the pie makes them look negligible. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1) both limit pies to about five slices.
   - **Fix:** Keep the four largest slices and group the rest into one labelled Other (25%), with the breakdown in the table. Or drop the pie and turn the list you already have on the right into a sorted horizontal bar chart, with the names and values on the bars. The list is already sorted and aligned, so that is a short step.
2. **The headline names the topic but not the finding, and the numbers have nothing to compare against.** (Taste, argued, plus convention.)
   - **The headline:** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" is a label. The reader has to work out the point for themselves, for example "people are nearly two-fifths of what we spend" or "four categories make up three-quarters". In an annual report, the reader wants the point stated.
   - **No comparison:** 38% means little without last year. A reader can't tell whether salaries grew, whether cloud hosting is climbing, or whether any of this is a change at all. Few's guidance is to put the earlier period next to the headline numbers.
   - **Fix:** Write the headline as the finding. Add a column for fiscal 2025, or the change in points, to the table.

**Smaller things.**
- **Crowded labels:** The 2% / 2% / 2% / 2% / 1% labels and their tick marks at the top of the pie nearly touch each other. This goes away once the small categories are grouped.
- **Every value appears twice:** each percentage is on the pie (inside or outside the slice) and again in the legend. Once the chart is labelled directly, keep only one.
- **Colour-only matching:** slices are linked to names only by colour. Insurance green, Equipment repair teal-green and Bank fees olive are close, and so are Software licences blue and Utilities blue-violet. A colour-blind reader can't match them reliably (WCAG 1.4.1). Direct labels fix this.
- **Status colours on categories:** red on Travel and green on Insurance. In a finance document, red can read as a flag that Travel is overspent. Use neutral category colours, or keep red for a real warning.
- **Jev also flagged it:** "A pie or donut has too many slices" (0.92). This is the same problem as finding 1: 14 slices, 10 of them slivers in the upper left.

**What's working.**
- **Type and page frame:** The page frame and type are well judged. The small spaced capitals for "4.2 COSTS", the strong serif headline and the plain serif subtitle make three clear levels. The subtitle gives the exact period ("twelve months to 30 June 2026"), and the source line says the figures are rounded. Keep all of that.
- **The list on the right:** it is sorted by value, the percentages are right-aligned in bold, and the thin rules between rows are light. It is the most readable thing on the page and could easily become the chart.
