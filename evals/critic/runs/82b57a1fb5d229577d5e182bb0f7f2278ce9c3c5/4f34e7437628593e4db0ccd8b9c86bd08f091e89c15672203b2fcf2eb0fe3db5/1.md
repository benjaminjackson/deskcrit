**First read.** The eye goes to "Revenue overview" and then to $4.82M, moves along the three KPI cards, and drops to the bar chart. The chart's black frame and black gridlines make it the darkest, heaviest thing on the page. It reads as a calm, well-organised SaaS revenue dashboard that you can take in within a few seconds.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard where founders or a finance team check how revenue did in 2025: the headline numbers, the monthly trend, and the split by plan.

**Verdict.** This is a solid, disciplined dashboard. The layout, type and colour are mostly right. Two things hold it back: the numbers don't agree with each other, and the chart is boxed in harder than its data. The first is the serious one. On a finance screen, figures that disagree cost the viewer's trust in every other number.

**What would move it most**
1. **Two different "2025 revenue" totals on one screen. Defect.** The table on the right is titled "Revenue by plan, 2025" and totals **$4.82M**. Adding up the twelve monthly bars, titled "Monthly revenue, 2025", gives roughly **$4.49M** (about 312 + 328 + … + 447k). The $4.82M exactly matches the ARR card, so the table is very likely showing annual recurring revenue by plan, labelled as 2025 revenue. A finance reader who adds the bars or compares the two totals finds a $330k gap. They then have to work out which number is wrong, and they start doubting the others. The Dec bar (~$447k) also doesn't match $4.82M ARR (≈$402k a month), which adds to the confusion. The heading should name exactly what the figures are, for example "ARR by plan, Dec 2025" (WCAG 2.4.6, headings describe their content). If the table really is 2025 revenue, the chart and the table need to come from the same basis. Check the data before you touch the design.
2. **The chart's frame and gridlines outweigh the bars. Convention.** The plot has a full black box around it and solid dark gridlines at every 100k. They're the only pure-black lines on an otherwise soft grey page, so the chart's scaffolding is louder than the KPI cards and fights the blue bars for attention. Remove the frame and make the gridlines thin and pale grey (or keep only a few), so the bars are the darkest marks in the chart (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 23.2; Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 5).
3. **The chart title names the topic but not the finding. Taste.** "Monthly revenue, 2025" tells the viewer what they're looking at but not what happened. The story is steady growth from about $310k to $447k, with small dips in April and July. A title or subtitle that says that ("Monthly revenue grew 43% through 2025") gives the glancing viewer the point without making them read the axis. I'm arguing this as common sense for a dashboard, not citing a rule.

**Smaller things**
- "Jan to Dec 2025" (top right) is a grey pill with no chevron or other cue, so it's unclear whether it's a date-range filter or just a label. If it's a control, make it look like one, for example with a chevron or a border.
- The "Monthly revenue, 2025" and "Revenue by plan, 2025" headings sit a few pixels off each other vertically. Put them on the same baseline so the two columns start together.

**What's working**
- The KPI row: each card has a clear label with its period, one large figure, and a comparison with last year. The sign and the words carry the meaning, so the green isn't doing the work alone. Keep this pattern.
- The chart is honest. The bars start at zero, the subtitle gives the units ("Thousands of US dollars, recognised revenue"), and the source line says what's included ("closed invoices only"). That kind of caption is what lets a finance reader trust a chart.
- The type is restrained: about four sizes with clear jobs (title, KPI figures, section heads, body and labels), with weight rather than extra sizes separating the levels. The plan values are bold and right-aligned so they're easy to compare.
