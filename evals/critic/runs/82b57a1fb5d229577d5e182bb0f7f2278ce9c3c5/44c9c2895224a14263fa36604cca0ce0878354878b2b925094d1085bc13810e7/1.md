**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three calm serif paragraphs. It reads as the plain information page of a small local ferry service. It feels quiet, tidy and trustworthy, like a well-set notice.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells someone about to take the river ferry when the boats run, how long the trip takes, and what to do with a bike. They will act on it by turning up at a landing at a certain time.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound. The page still fails at its job because it never gives a time. Someone planning a trip learns that boats run "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and that the last winter boat leaves "before dusk". They can't plan from that, and the page sends them to a printed timetable in a shelter at the landing, which is where they are trying not to have to go.

**What would move it most**
1. **There are no actual times.** This is in paragraphs 1 and 3. "Early morning", "late evening", "every forty minutes" with no starting time, and "one boat each hour" with no minute past the hour all leave the passenger unable to work out when to arrive. If they guess wrong they could wait up to 40 minutes, or up to an hour in winter. Telling them the printed timetable is at each landing confirms the real information is somewhere else. *Defect.* Fix: put the first and last departures from each landing, and the minutes past the hour, on the page itself. A small table for the summer and winter timetables is the usual way to do it. Then make that table the first thing under the heading.
2. **The last-boat line can be read two ways, and it's the one line people will act on.** This is paragraph 3: "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". "The quay" could mean the market quay only, or "quay" could loosely mean any landing. Someone at the north landing can't tell whether their last boat goes at the same time, earlier, or later. "Before dusk" also moves by more than an hour across the winter. If they read it wrong, they're stranded on the wrong bank. *Defect.* Fix: give a clock time (or "30 minutes before sunset") for the last boat from each landing, and name both landings.
3. **The key facts are buried in running prose.** The page holds four things a passenger needs: how often boats run, how long the crossing takes, that bikes go free, and the winter change. They are spread across sentences and spelled out in words ("forty", "twelve"), so a glance doesn't find them. Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: people scan pages; they don't read them. *Taste*, for a page this short, but it becomes a real cost once the times from finding 1 are added. Fix: use numerals for times and durations, and give the timetable and the bicycle rule short labels or a list of their own so each fact can be found without reading the whole page.

**Smaller things.**
- Heading: "Notes for ferry passengers" names a kind of page, not what's on it. Something like "River ferry: times, crossing and bicycles" tells the scanner what they'll find (taste).
- Paragraph 1: "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" doesn't say how much longer. Give a figure ("up to 20 minutes") so people can plan connections.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing. The bold sans heading against the serif text gives a clear two-level hierarchy without an extra size. Keep it.
- The body setting. Lines run about 72 characters, the leading is generous, the right edge is gently ragged, and paragraphs are separated by space only. It's comfortable to read; keep it when you add the timetable.
- The restraint. Dark text on white with nothing decorative suits a public-service notice and will carry a plain table well.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where most passengers will read this standing at a landing.
