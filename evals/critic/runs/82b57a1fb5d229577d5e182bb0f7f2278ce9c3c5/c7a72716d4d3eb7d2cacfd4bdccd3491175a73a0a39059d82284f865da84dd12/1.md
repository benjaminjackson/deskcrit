**First read.** My eye goes to the headline first, "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks", then down to the orange button. Then it stalls on the grey "Keep your streak going" card under the button before it reaches the three steps. Within two seconds it reads clearly as a sign-up page for a language-learning app aimed at adults who want to speak. It feels calm, clean and competent, and also generic: the teal speech-bubble panel could be on any chat, support or language product.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of a consumer language app. Its job is to get a first-time visitor to start the free week.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. There is one clear message and one clear action, and the order is easy to follow. Two things hold it back. The streak card under the button talks to people who are already learning, and it pulls attention at the point where a newcomer should be deciding. And the right half (the speech-bubble panel) adds nothing the headline hasn't already said.

**What would move it most**
1. **The streak card is aimed at the wrong reader, in the most valuable spot** (grey card under the button, left column). "Keep your streak going" assumes the reader already has a streak, and this page is for people who don't. A visitor reads it as a second message right after the ask and has to work out who it's for. The stat in it, "finish the course twice as often", gives no comparison: twice as often as whom? The useful part is that short daily practice works, and that is an argument for signing up. **Defect** (wrong audience, and an element that slows the reader at the moment they decide; this is common sense, not a cited rule). Fix: move it out of the hero, or rewrite it as a reason to start, aimed at the newcomer, with a clear comparison ("Learners who practise five days a week finish twice as often as those who don't").
2. **The picture repeats the headline instead of proving it** (teal panel, top right). Two empty speech bubbles say "conversation", which the headline already says in larger type. The page's real difference is "a tutor that listens" and "feedback on each word", and nothing on the page shows it. So the visitor has to take the promise on trust, and the page looks like anyone's product. **Taste**, argued on its own terms: the image is half the hero and should carry half the argument. Fix: show the product doing its job, such as a spoken phrase with per-word feedback, or a short exchange with the tutor in the chosen language. That lets the visitor judge the value for themselves instead of taking the claim on trust.
3. **The hero ignores the page's own grid** (the panel stops at about x = 1076, while the nav and the step row run to the 1224 margin). That leaves a dead strip about 150 px wide to its right, and an empty hole under the panel next to the streak card. The hero split doesn't line up with the three columns below it either, so the top half looks placed by eye. **Convention** (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*; Ruder on gathered space). The cost here is that the page looks less finished, not that anyone misreads it. Fix: run the panel to the right margin, and take the hero split from the same columns as the step row. Then make the panel's bottom line up with the end of the left column.

**Smaller things.**
- **Type scale:** I count about five sizes below the headline, and they nearly collide. The subhead, the card heading and the button all look about 18 px, the step headings about 17 px bold, and the card and step body text about 15 px. Settle on headline, one subhead size, one small heading/body pair, and let weight do the rest (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1).
- **Headline break:** "conversation in / ten weeks" leaves "in" dangling at the end of a line. Break it as "Hold a real conversation / in ten weeks".
- **Copy:** "Short daily lessons" gives no length; say "10-minute lessons" if that's true, because the ten-week promise depends on it. "A tutor that listens" doesn't say whether the tutor is a person or software, and readers will guess both ways. "Start your free week" doesn't say what happens on day eight (card needed? auto-billing?). The reader will hold you to that, so add one line under the button.

**What's working.**
- **The headline is a concrete promise with a deadline.** "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks" names an outcome the reader wants and puts a time on it. That makes it far stronger than "learn a language". Keep it.
- **There is one primary action, clearly marked.** The saturated orange button is the only warm colour on a neutral and cool page, so it comes forward with no competition.
- **The three steps are honest and specific.** Listen, Speak, Use it, each with one plain sentence, sit on a clean three-column row with thin rules. They explain the method faster than the hero does.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Button contrast:** the white button text looks readable, but at about 18 px bold it sits just under WCAG's large-text size. Measure the white-on-orange ratio against 4.5:1.

What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (distinct text levels)
