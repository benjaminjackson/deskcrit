**First read.** The eye goes straight to the red and orange block in the middle of the grid, then up to the headline, then across to the three numbers on the right. I read it as an operations report that tells a support lead when to staff up. The data is good and the layout is calm and well organised, but the chart feels loud, almost like an alarm. About two thirds of it is a strong indigo that shouts just as much as the busy hours do.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a Reports screen in a help-desk product, where a support manager can see when tickets pile up and plan shifts around it.

**Verdict.** The layout and labelling are good. Two things let it down. The colour scale works against the data, and the summary numbers on the right don't agree with the chart beside them. The second is the more serious problem, because a manager will staff from these figures.

**What would move it most**

1. **The side numbers contradict the chart (defect).** "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" sits next to a grid where the 11:00 column is the hottest. Monday and Friday are red (30+) at 11:00 but only orange (25–29) at 10:00, and Tuesday to Thursday are orange in both columns. The total doesn't fit either. The chart's own bins give a lower bound of roughly 180–220 tickets on each weekday, which comes to well over 3,412 across the four weeks of 1–28 September, before you count any 0–4 hours. A manager who notices either mismatch stops trusting the whole report. One who doesn't notice may staff for the wrong hour. Possible innocent explanations: the hour axis is labelled by end of hour, the KPI covers different queues, or the heatmap shows something other than real hourly counts. If one of these is true, the page has to say it. Otherwise, fix the numbers or the binning so the stat and the grid agree.

2. **The rainbow scale hides the order and gives quiet hours the loudest colour (convention, plus a defect).** The seven bins run indigo, cyan, teal, lime, yellow, orange, red. These are seven unrelated hues, and the eye can't put them in order without checking the legend. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, says ordered values should use one hue running from light to dark. The specific costs here:
   - The 0–4 bin, which is most of the grid's area, is a fully saturated indigo. The empty night hours pull as hard as the peak (Itten, saturation contrast; Refactoring UI).
   - In grayscale, yellow (20–24) is the lightest cell and indigo (0–4) is about as dark as red (30+). So lightness, the cue people read first, gives the order wrong: quiet and peak look the same, and the middle looks like the top (Albers, *Interaction of Color*).
   - Neighbouring steps that differ mainly in hue are hard to tell apart for many colour-blind viewers: green vs lime, lime vs yellow, yellow vs orange (WCAG 1.4.1).
   - Red reads as "error" in a product UI (Apple HIG, Material), so a normal Monday morning looks like an incident.

   Direction: use one hue that ramps from near-white for 0–4 to dark for 30+. Then the quiet hours fall back, the peak stands out, and the legend becomes a check rather than a decoder.

3. **The title names the topic, not the finding (taste, argued).** "When new tickets arrive" makes the manager pull the answer out of the grid themselves. The page clearly knows the answer, so say it in the title or subtitle, in staffing terms. For example: "Weekday tickets peak 10:00–12:00, so you need the most people on from 09:00 to 13:00", with the weekend pattern as a second line. A conclusion they can act on is the reason this screen exists.

**Smaller things.**
- The hour axis labels only every third hour. If you keep it that way, align each label clearly to its cell so "09:00" can't be misread as the column next to it. That ambiguity may be part of why finding 1 happened.

**What's working.**
- The structure is right for the question: a day-by-hour heatmap answers "when?" in a single view.
- The subtitle and source line close the obvious gaps: the scope (all queues, average week, 1–28 September), the time zone, and "opened, not answered". Keep them.
- Every KPI on the right comes with a comparison to August ("Up 4%", "Same as August", "Down 6 min"), and the three stats share one clear pattern: label, big figure, change.
