**First read.** The eye goes to the yellow Glasshouse tile, then the red Rose arch tile, then wanders over six coloured blocks of equal weight without settling. The heading "Garden show gallery" is read last. It reads as a wireframe or a page whose pictures never loaded. Nothing on the screen looks like a garden.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web gallery where garden-show visitors browse six exhibits and pick one to see.

**Verdict.** As shown, it doesn't do its job: a gallery with no pictures gives the viewer nothing to choose between. The type and grid are clean, so once real photos are in this could work. The one thing in the way is the six flat placeholders standing where the garden should be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures are placeholders, and they set the whole page's colour and mood.** This is a defect. All six tiles are flat colour fields, each with a pale circle cut off flush against its bottom edge. The viewer can't see a rose arch, an herb bed or a pond, so the captions have nothing to back them up and there's no reason to click one exhibit over another. The six unrelated saturated hues (red, green, blue, ochre, yellow, purple) also fill most of the screen. That makes the page loud and random rather than gardeny, and the yellow wins attention by accident, not because it matters most. If this is a capture taken before the images loaded, re-check it live. Otherwise the fix is to put a real photo of each exhibit in every tile, all graded and cropped the same way. Then the colour comes from the gardens and the palette problem goes away. If placeholders have to ship, use one quiet neutral tint for all six (common sense; Refactoring UI argues for a small, defined palette).
2. **Nothing tells the eye where to start.** This is a convention (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance through differences of size). The page title "Garden show gallery" is set at roughly 20 px over a grid about 1160 px wide, so it's weaker than every tile below it, and the six tiles share the same size and weight. Equal tiles are the right choice for a gallery of peer exhibits, so don't make one tile bigger without a reason. Instead, give the page a clear start: make the title clearly larger, with more space below it than the current ~14 px. If one exhibit is the show's highlight, that's the place to break the equal grid on purpose.

**Smaller things.**
- The Rose arch caption (top left) sits about 2 px higher for the title and about 4 px higher for the description than the other five. So the first row's titles and descriptions don't line up across the columns, and the card reads as from a different build. Give every card the same spacing below the image (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*; Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- "Tomatoes in late July." can be read two ways: the exhibit shows tomatoes ripening in late July, or you can only see it in late July. If it's a date visitors will act on, spell it out.
- "Garden show gallery" could be any show. Naming the show and year would tell the viewer whose garden this is.
- Jev found "three or more elements compete to be seen first" and "all the main elements are about the same size". Both are the six equal tiles with a weak title, covered in finding 2.

**What's working.**
- The captions are concrete and short ("Thyme, sage and chives", "Reeds and water lilies"). Each one names what you'd actually see. Keep that voice.
- The three-level text scale works: bold card titles, smaller grey descriptions and a distinct page title. Each level is easy to tell apart, so keep it.
- The 3×2 grid is steady: equal tiles, even column gutters, and a row gap clearly larger than the gap between each picture and its caption, so each card reads as one unit.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the coloured tiles are unloaded images or deliberate placeholders. Check in a live browser.
- Whether the cards are links and how they show hover and focus. Nothing on the screen marks them as clickable.
