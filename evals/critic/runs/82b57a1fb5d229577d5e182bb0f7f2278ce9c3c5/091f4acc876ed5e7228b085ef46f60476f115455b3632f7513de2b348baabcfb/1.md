**Verdict.** The reading basics are mostly sound and the writing is strong. But the opening screen gives nobody a reason to stay except the words. The biggest problem is that a story about a place and its people shows neither, so the first screen looks like any text page on any site.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no lead picture, though the page credits photographs.** The byline under the headline reads "Photographs by the residents", yet the whole first screen is type, with an empty band below the second paragraph. Someone landing here sees nothing of the allotment: no pallets and bath tubs, no queue at the tap, no Halima Osei. That is exactly the material that would make a stranger care. The photos also come from the residents, which is the angle that sets this story apart, and the page hides it. A picture and the headline should make one idea together (the headline gives the place, the photo gives the people), and right now one half is missing. If a hero image is meant to sit here and just didn't load in this capture, then the layout is fine; check it in a live browser. If the photos really only start further down, move one up to sit with the headline or straight after the standfirst. **Defect** for this kind of piece: it costs readers on the first screen. This is my judgment as a designer; the catalog entry for this rule has no approved source.
2. **The body text runs too wide.** The body paragraphs ("It started with a letter…") look like about 85–90 characters a line, above the 45–75 range Bringhurst gives for a single column (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). The text is large and well spaced, so it isn't a disaster. But this is a long read, and over many screens the eye will sometimes lose its place going back to the next line. Narrow the column, or slightly enlarge the type in the same column, to land near 65–70 characters. Measure the rendered `characters_per_line` to confirm. **Convention**, and here breaking it costs something.
3. **The headline breaks badly.** It wraps as "…the end of the bus / line", leaving "line" alone on the second line. It splits the phrase "bus line", and a one-word runt in the largest type on the page looks like an accident. It also weakens the headline's rhythm, which should end on the bus line as one image. Rebreak it so "bus line" stays together ("The allotment at the end / of the bus line" reads well), or adjust the measure or size so it doesn't split. **Convention** (phrase-boundary breaks; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*). The catalog marks part of this rule's sourcing as unverified, so treat it partly as common sense. Either way, it's the thing on the page a reader looks at most.

**Smaller things.**
- Top-right navigation (Features · Food · Places · Subscribe): nothing marks the section you're in. This is a Feature, so give "Features" one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Taste: the whole page is one default-looking sans in grey and black, with a single small green kicker. It's clean but could be any publication's template. A reader gets no feel for Groundwork, or for soil and allotments, from the type and colour alone. A text face with some character for the headline or body would do more than any ornament.

**What's working.**
- The copy. The standfirst sets a scene (the car park nobody used), gives a concrete number (forty households) and a human detail (the queue for the water tap), and ends with a promise ("We spent a season with the people who built it"). The opening line, "It started with a letter.", pulls you in. Keep the writing exactly this concrete.
- The text levels are clearly told apart without extra sizes: green bold kicker, heavy headline, grey byline, italic standfirst, upright body. The standfirst and body are close in size, but italic and colour split them cleanly, which is the right tool to use.
- Everything in the article hangs off one left edge at x≈280, with even, sensible gaps between the parts. Keep that discipline when the pictures go in.
