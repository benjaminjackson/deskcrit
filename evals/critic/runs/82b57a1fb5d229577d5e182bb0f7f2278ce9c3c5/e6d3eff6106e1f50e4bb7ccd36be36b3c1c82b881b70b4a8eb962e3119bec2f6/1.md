**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, then into the first paragraph. It reads as a feature on a small, independent community magazine: calm, tidy, all grey type on white. The byline says "Photographs by the residents", so I expect to see a photograph, and there isn't one on screen. The page feels competent but anonymous.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the top of a long-form feature on a magazine site, and it has to pull a casual visitor into a local human-interest story and keep them reading.

**Verdict.** It's readable and well ordered, but it doesn't make anyone want to read on. A story about forty households growing food on an old car park opens with no picture, so the screen could belong to any publication's article template. The text column is also a bit wider than comfortable.

**What would move it most**
1. **The opening has no image, though the byline credits photographs.** The byline promises "Photographs by the residents", but nothing above the fold shows the allotment, the bath tubs or the queue at the tap. The headline does the work alone, and all the reader sees is grey text. For a story this visual, the picture is what earns the scroll. Without it the page looks like everyone else's template, and the photos the residents took, which are the point of the piece, are hidden. If the lead photo is further down the page, or failed to load in this capture, the fix is still the same: put one strong resident photo between the byline and the first paragraph, or above the headline, so the headline and the picture make one idea, such as the bus stop beside the beds. *Defect* (it fails the piece's job). The idea that headline and image should work together is my own view as a designer: none of the approved sources covers it.
2. **The text column is a bit wide for reading.** The lines in the three paragraphs run about 80 to 85 characters (for example, "Ten years ago … nobody used. Today forty" is one line). Bringhurst's comfortable range is 45 to 75, with about 66 ideal (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, §2.1.2). The generous line spacing helps, but over a long feature the eye has to work harder to find the start of each next line. Narrow the column to about 600 to 640 px at this type size, or raise the body size a step. Measure the real character count in the browser. *Convention*, and here breaking it costs the reader.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "…the bus / line", leaving "line" alone on the second line and splitting the noun "bus line". Rebalance it: a slightly narrower or wider headline box, or a non-breaking space between "bus" and "line", so the break falls at a phrase boundary such as "The allotment / at the end of the bus line". *Common sense*: the reference entry says its source for this rule is unconfirmed.

**What's working.**
- **The hierarchy is clean.** The headline clearly dominates. The green "Feature" label, the grey byline and the body are each a separate level, told apart by weight and colour rather than by sizes that nearly match. Keep this type scale.
- **The opening copy is strong.** It has concrete details (forty households, beans, squash, herbs, the Saturday queue for the water tap), a clear "we spent a season" promise, and the line "It started with a letter" as a hook. It sounds like a person telling the story. Don't cut it to make room for the image.
- **Space and restraint.** The body line spacing is generous, the paragraphs are short, there's one accent colour, and the space is grouped clearly: the label sits tight to the headline, and there's a clear gap before the body.
