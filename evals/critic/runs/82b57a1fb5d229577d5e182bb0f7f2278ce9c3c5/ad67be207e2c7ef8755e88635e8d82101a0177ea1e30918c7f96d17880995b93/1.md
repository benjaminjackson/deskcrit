**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", picks up the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it, then drops into three short paragraphs. It reads as a help page for a small river ferry service: calm, plain and easy to trust. Nothing on it pulls against the reading. It also says very little you could act on.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: a web page that tells riders how the ferry runs so they can plan a crossing and know the rules for bikes.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job. It is clean, the order of importance is clear, and the type is well set. The copy doesn't. A rider who reads every word still doesn't know when the first or last boat leaves, when "winter" starts, or what it costs. The page sends them to a printed timetable at the landing, which is exactly where they don't need it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable copy leaves out the facts people will act on.** This is a defect. In paragraph 1, "between early morning and late evening" and "every forty minutes" don't say when the first and last boats leave or what minute past the hour they sail. A rider will plan their day around this line and miss the last boat. Paragraph 3's "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" can be read as running from a fixed date or from whenever the weather turns, and someone who picks the wrong reading turns up to an hourly gap. "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" puts the one useful document at the end of the journey, not at the planning stage. There's also no fare: "at no extra charge" for bikes suggests a fare exists, but it's never stated. To fix it, give the real times: first and last departure from each landing, the departure minutes, the winter dates, the fare, and a link to the timetable on this page. Once the figures are in, set them as a short list or table so they can be scanned. The bike rules can stay in prose.

**Smaller things.**
- Body measure: the first line of paragraph 1 runs about 84 characters, which is past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. The generous leading makes up for most of it. Narrow the column by roughly 10% so lines land around 66 characters.

**What's working.**
- Three type levels with clear jobs: a small tracked capital label, a large bold heading and regular body text. The steps between them are big enough that nothing competes. Keep this scale when the timetable is added rather than bringing in a fourth size.
- The spacing groups things correctly. The label sits tight to the heading, the heading has more space below it, and the paragraphs are evenly separated. Every block hangs from one left edge, so the page reads as one calm column.
- The tone fits the service. The neutral greys and a single sans-serif feel like a public notice, not a sales page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone, where most riders will read this standing at the landing.
