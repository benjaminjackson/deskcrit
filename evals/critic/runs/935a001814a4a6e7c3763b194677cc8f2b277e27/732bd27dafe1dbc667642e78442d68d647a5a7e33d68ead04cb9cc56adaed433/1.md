**First read.** My eye lands on the big cream serif headline, "Know when the water is slack before you leave the car". It moves to the card on the right, then down to the amber button. Within a few seconds it reads as a tide and current app for cold-water swimmers on the Cornish coast. It feels calm, local and grown-up: deep sea green, a bookish serif, no gimmicks. In the card, the only color lands on "Next". It doesn't land on "Avoid", the one word there about danger.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a landing page that gets open-water swimmers to add their beach and then pay for the $4 plan.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, focused page with a headline that earns its size. Its weak spot is the sample card, which is meant to prove the product. It doesn't show the "safe window" the copy promises, and it gives the warning less weight than a time marker.

**What would move it most**
1. **The card gives "Avoid" no weight and "Next" all of it.** In the card's right column, "Swim" and "Avoid" are the same muted gray. "Next" at 12:25 is the only amber word, so a viewer's eye goes to the time marker and slides past the warning. "Next" also takes the place of that row's verdict, so the viewer can't tell whether low water at 12:25 is a swim or not. On a safety product, that's the exact question they came to answer. Amber also usually means caution (Apple HIG, Material Design: color roles), so "Next" in amber can read as "careful" rather than "coming up". This is a **defect**. Fix direction: keep the verdict column for verdicts and give "Avoid" a clear difference in weight or value from "Swim". Show "next" another way, such as highlighting the whole row, so 12:25 keeps its Swim or Avoid.

2. **The chart doesn't show what the product promises.** The body copy says Slackwater "marks the safe window for each swim". The bar chart has no hours, no unit for what the bars measure, and no mark showing where the swimmable stretches are. The viewer has to guess that the bars are tide height and that they run from about 6am to 7pm. They also have to match them to the four rows below by eye. This is a **convention** (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, on clear labeling; Few). It costs a lot here because the card is the page's only proof. Fix direction: put a few hour ticks under the bars and mark the safe windows on the chart itself, for example with a band or a change of bar value. Then the picture and the "Swim" rows say the same thing at a glance. Saying the takeaway on the chart itself is my own judgment (no approved source): it's the one thing that makes this card more than a decoration.

3. **Pricing in dollars on a product "made in Penzance".** "$0" and "$4 a month" sit on a page about Porthgwidden that says "car park" and is made in Cornwall. A UK swimmer, the obvious audience, can read "$4" two ways: a typo for £4, or a real charge in US dollars with a conversion fee on their card. A price is what people hold you to, so either reading costs trust right at the point of deciding. This is a **defect** in the copy. Fix: show the currency the viewer will actually be charged.

4. **The pricing section ends with nothing to click.** The plans are the moment of decision, but neither has a button, so a convinced viewer has to scroll back up to "Add your beach". This is common sense, not a cited rule. Fix direction: give each plan its own action, with the paid one styled lower than the hero's primary button or matching it. That would also partly fill the empty right third beside the plans.

**Smaller things.**
- The Dipper line, "Two beaches and the tide for the next three days", leaves it unclear whether the free plan includes the current, the water temperature and the safe window, which are the things the hero sells. Say what it leaves out, or what it keeps.
- The copy never sets the product against what swimmers use now, such as a printed tide table or a weather app. A comparison of that kind would let viewers judge the value on terms they already trust. The maker has to supply it.

**What's working.**
- The headline: it names a concrete moment ("before you leave the car") from the swimmer's own day. The section heading "From the car park to the water" picks that moment up again, so the page reads as one voice.
- The type pairing: a warm serif for headings and prices, a plain sans for everything else. Levels are told apart by face as well as size, and there are only about four sizes, so the scale is clean.
- Restraint: one dark green, cream text, one amber accent, rules instead of boxes. The page looks like a specific local product, not a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button, nav and "See a sample morning" link. Whether the card is live or a static sample. How the two-column hero reflows on a phone, which is where swimmers in a car park will actually use it.
