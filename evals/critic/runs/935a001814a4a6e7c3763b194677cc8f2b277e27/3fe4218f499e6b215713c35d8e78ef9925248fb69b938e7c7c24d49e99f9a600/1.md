**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Hand out school lockers before the first bell", then jumps right to the one blue locker, B4, in the corridor plan, then comes back to the blue "Start with one corridor" button. Within two seconds it reads as software that lets schools assign lockers, made for school offices. It feels calm, orderly and practical, more like a tool than a pitch. The sage, tan and navy colors suit a school building.

**What it's for.** Assumed: the landing page hero for a locker-management product. It has to persuade school office staff or admins that it solves the start-of-year locker job, and get them to start a trial.

**Verdict.** This works. The headline names a real job, the product picture shows that job being done, and the main button asks for a small, specific first step. Two things hold it back: the stock sparkle icons, which make a specific page look generic, and copy that never compares the product with what the office uses now.

**What would move it most**

1. **Sparkle icons on the main button and on "Science corridor".** Neither sparkle means anything. One sits before "Start with one corridor" and the other before the corridor name in the plan. Nothing here is AI and nothing sparkles. Viewers have seen this icon on many generic products, so it puts this page in that crowd, at the two spots where the page is otherwise most specific: the ask and the product. That costs the most on a page whose strength is plainness. Remove both. If the corridor title needs a marker, use one that means something here, such as a locker or map glyph, or nothing. *Template tell* (Sokolovsky, unslop, "Sparkle icon as decoration"). It also fails "every element does a job".

2. **No comparison with what the office uses today.** The body copy ("Lockerly holds the locker plan for each corridor, the key list, and the deposits…") says what the product holds. It never mentions what the reader is using now: a spreadsheet, a paper key list, a cash box. So the reader has to imagine the difference on their own. The headline is a strong, specific promise, and the paragraph under it is where the reader should feel the contrast with their own start of term. Name the kind of thing the office does now, in the maker's own facts, and let the paragraph land on what the reader stops doing. The feature row does this well already: "so a lost key is a quick lookup" ends on what the reader gets. Bring that move up into the hero. *Taste/copy*.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline leaves "bell" alone on line three. Rebreak it so the last line carries more than one word, for example "lockers before" / "the first bell". This is a convention (Bringhurst 2.4.2), and it matters more in the largest type on the page.
- The plan has no key: green means taken and white with a dashed border means free. "3 free" lets the reader work it out, but a one-word label or tiny key in the panel header removes the guesswork. This is common sense.

**What's working.**
- The product picture shows the actual job, not decoration. It's a real corridor with rows, a landmark ("Stairs to the labs"), free lockers, one selected locker, and a footer that ties it to a pupil, a key number and a deposit. It shows the product's whole idea in one glance, which most heroes don't. Keep it this concrete.
- The ask is specific and low-commitment. "Start with one corridor" names a first step small enough to say yes to. "See a sample corridor" is a clearly secondary text link. Keep one filled button.
- The restraint holds. One sans family, a clear three-level type scale (headline, ~18 px feature headings, body), one saturated color used only for the button and the selected locker, and empty space gathered into the margins and the gap above the feature row.
