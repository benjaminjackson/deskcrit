**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, crosses to the stock panel, catches on the one red row ("Baby formula, 8, 4 days"), then drops to the green button. It reads straight away as stock-keeping software for food banks, and it feels calm, plain, and practical. Nothing shouts and nothing is in the way. Then the bottom row, with its three emoji, looks like many other landing pages.

**What it's for.** Assumed: the top of a marketing page that gets food-bank coordinators to sign up and set up their pantry.

**Verdict.** This is a good page and it does its job. The headline says exactly what the product does, and the sample table shows it. What holds it back is the feature row: it's built from a stock template and is the only part that could belong to anyone. The table also hides its own best moment.

**What would move it most**
1. **The feature row is the stock "emoji plus bold title" card set** (template tell). The three tinted cards across the lower third each have an emoji (📦 🚚 🥫), a bold title, and one line of text. Everything above them is specific to this product: the Harbour Road pantry, a timestamp, real items and quantities. Then the page switches to a pattern viewers have seen on countless SaaS sites. That makes the product look generic just where it lists what makes it different. The emoji also don't match the page: they're glossy and full-color on an otherwise flat, quiet page (Sokolovsky, unslop: "Feature cards with an emoji and a bold title", "Emoji as icons or status"). Direction: take the emoji out or use one set of simple line icons, and stop the row reading as three identical boxes. For example, attach each feature to the part of the stock panel it explains. The copy in the cards is already concrete, so keep it.
2. **The sample table hides the shortage it exists to show** (defect, with a convention underneath). The intro promises that "the coordinator sees what will run short this week," and the third card says urgent items "move to the top of the list." But in the panel the one item running short, baby formula, sits in row 3 of 5 among items that last for weeks. Only red marks it. A viewer who can't tell red from the dark text, or who just skims, sees five ordinary rows and misses the point of the whole demo. Showing meaning by color alone also breaks WCAG 1.4.1. Direction: sort the rows by "Lasts," shortest first. That's my judgment, not a published rule: Few and Wilke's sorting rule is written for bar charts. Also give the flagged row a second cue besides red, such as a word or a mark, so the demo shows the claim the copy makes.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks "…before the van / arrives," which splits subject from verb and leaves "arrives" alone on line three. Rebalance it so the lines read as phrases (for example, break after "on the shelves") (Bringhurst; the heading-break part is common sense).
- The intro paragraph ends with "week." alone on its last line. A small change to the width or a tie between the last two words would fix it (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The stock panel's left edge (about x 652) lines up with nothing in the card row below: the hero is split in halves and the cards in thirds. Take both from one set of columns so the panel's edge continues down the page (Müller-Brockmann).

**What's working.**
- The headline and intro do the job in words. "Know what is on the shelves before the van arrives" names a moment this audience lives through. The intro then says who does what: volunteers scan, the coordinator sees shortages. Keep that concreteness.
- The stock panel is a real piece of product, not a decorative mock-up. It has a named pantry, a count time, units ("Pasta, 500 g"), and right-aligned numbers with a "Lasts" column, so the viewer gets the value without reading any claims.
- The page is restrained: one green for the single primary action, an underlined secondary link, warm neutral tints, and a short type scale with clear steps. That restraint is why the red row can stand out at all.
