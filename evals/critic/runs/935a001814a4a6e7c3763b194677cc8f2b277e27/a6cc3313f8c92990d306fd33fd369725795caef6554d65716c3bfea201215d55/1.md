**First read.** My eye goes first to the row of big numbers ($31,270, 2,214, $14, 18%), then jumps to the one blue bar, Saturday, and then to the blue November line. It reads as a monthly sales dashboard for a small farm shop's owner. It feels calm, tidy, and trustworthy. What I don't get on first read is whether November was a good month or a bad one.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a monthly overview for the shop owner. It should tell them in a glance how November went against October, and what drove it.

**Verdict.** It's well built and easy to read, but it leaves the main job to the viewer. The data says November was worse than October: revenue fell about 8% and orders about 7%, while visitors went up. Nothing on the page says so. The drop is hidden in small gray "in October" lines that the owner has to subtract in their head.

**What would move it most**
1. **The comparisons are there but say nothing** (KPI row, top). Every card shows last month's figure ("$34,105 in October"), but in the same gray as a caption, with no change and no direction. To learn that revenue fell, the owner has to compare two five-digit numbers. They have to do that for every card. A quick scan reads "$31,270" as neutral, or even as good news. Few's dashboard guidance is to give each headline number a comparison such as the change from the last period, so the reader can tell good from bad. Here the earlier value is shown, but the change isn't. Fix: show the change and its direction next to each number (a signed amount or percent, colored by good or bad and not by color alone). That way "down" can be seen without doing math. *Defect.*
2. **Nobody puts the story together** (whole page). The visitors chart shows November visitors well above October's all month. The KPI row shows fewer orders and less revenue. Together that means more people came and fewer bought, which is the most useful fact on the page. But the two facts sit in separate cards, and every title only names a topic ("Shop visitors", "Orders by weekday"). The owner has to connect them alone. Fix: let the titles or a one-line summary state what each chart shows. Don't add a new metric unless the maker already has it (for example, a conversion rate). *Taste.* The source for finding-stating titles isn't on my list, but on a monthly review page it's what the owner came for.
3. **Three numbers have no unit or reference point.** Each one can be read two ways:
   - **"Farm box subscribers 18%"** (fourth card). 18% of what: of customers, of orders, or growth? A count of subscribers would be the expected reading of the label.
   - **"148" on the Saturday bar.** Is it total Saturday orders in November, or the average per Saturday? Judging the other bars against it by eye, neither reading obviously adds up to 2,214 orders. The chart also has no y-axis, so the other six days have no values at all.
   - **The visitors y-axis** (0 to 900) has no unit or interval. About a dozen points over 30 days don't look like days or weeks, so the owner can't tell what 700 means.

   Tufte's rule applies: the reader should never have to guess what a number measures. Fix: name the base, the period, and the interval on each, such as "per Saturday" or "visitors per day". *Defect.*
4. **"Best-selling produce" can be read two ways** (table). The rows are ordered by revenue, but the units sold go 164, 203, 231. That puts the cider, which sold the most units, in last place. An owner restocking by units reads it wrong. Fix: rename the heading to match the sort ("Top produce by revenue"), or sort by Sold. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- **Sidebar labels step in and out.** "Overview", "Orders", "Produce", "Customers", and "Settings" start at about x=60, 66, 62, 68, and 64, because each label follows its icon's width. Put every icon in a box of the same width so the labels share one left edge. This is a stock template move; the source is unslop (Sokolovsky).
- **The Settings icon** is a sun or crosshair shape, not the gear people know. Use the standard icon (Apple HIG and Material conventions).
- **The visitors chart uses a legend** (top right) for its two lines. Label "November" and "October" at the right end of each line instead (Tufte; Wilke).
- **The split between the two chart cards** (about x=900) lines up with none of the KPI card dividers (511, 756, 1002). Take the split from the four-column KPI grid, for example three columns and one (Müller-Brockmann).

**What's working.**
- **One blue means "this one".** Blue marks November against gray October, and Saturday against gray weekdays. The highlight reads instantly, and the gray context recedes without vanishing. Keep this one-accent rule when you add the change indicators.
- **The structure is in the right order:** headline numbers, then trend, then breakdown, then the item-level table. It all fits on one screen with plenty of space and no chart clutter. The table uses right-aligned tabular figures, so Sold and Revenue scan cleanly.
- **The type is quiet and consistent.** There's one family, a handful of sizes, and secondary text that recedes without turning faint.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the date range can be changed.
- Whether the charts show values on hover. That would ease finding 3 for the weekday bars but not for the KPI cards.
