**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, then jumps to the one dark teal tile (S4) in the rack map, then down to the teal button below the paragraph. It reads as software for whoever runs a building's bike room: calm, practical and quietly confident. The map does most of the explaining. Then a stranger's questions start: what do the green tiles mean, and who pays $6 a month?

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero of a marketing landing page for building managers or residents' committees. It should get them to map their own bike room or look at the sample.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The layout is disciplined, the product picture is specific, and the ask is clear. What gets in the way is that the map and the price can be read two ways, in the one place that has to convince people. Green racks look free when they're taken, and "$6 a month" could be either of two different prices.

**What would move it most**
1. **The map's colors say the opposite of what they mean.** Fifteen racks are sage green and three (R3, S6, T5) are white with a dashed border. "18 racks, 3 free" tells you the white ones are the free ones, but only if you count. Green is the usual color for available or OK (Apple HIG, Material: green marks success), so a first glance reads the map backwards: lots of green means plenty of space. That's the reverse of the scarcity the waiting list exists to handle. Nothing on the card labels the three states either (green, white dashed, dark teal selected), so the meaning rests on color plus a faint dashed edge (WCAG 1.4.1). *Defect.* Fix: make taken racks a neutral or muted fill, let free racks carry the eye-catching treatment, and add a small key ("taken / free / selected") or put a word like "free" in the empty tiles.
2. **"$6 a month" can be read two ways.** It sits in the S4 detail row next to "Flat 12, held since May". It could be the rack fee that Flat 12 pays the building, or Rackspot's price per rack. A building manager who reads it as Rackspot's charge multiplies by 18 racks and leaves. A manager who reads it as a fee they can collect sees a feature the page never mentions. People remember a price and hold you to it, so the ambiguity is a defect. Fix: say who pays whom in the line itself, for example "Flat 12 pays $6 a month", or whatever the true fact is. Only you know which reading is right.
3. **"Bike store" means a shop to a US reader.** The price is in dollars, but the copy uses British terms: "bike store" (the headline paragraph, the button "Map your bike store", the link "View a sample bike store", "A notice for the store door"), "Flat 12", and "Larkfield House". To an American, "Map your bike store" sounds like a store locator for bike shops, and the main button is where that confusion costs most (Nielsen heuristic 2: use the audience's own words). *Defect* if the audience is in the US. Fix: pick one market and match the words and the currency to it, e.g. "bike room" or "bike storage" with "$", or "bike store" and "flat" with "£".
4. **The pitch describes the product but doesn't compare it to anything the reader has now.** The paragraph says what Rackspot does: maps the room, keeps the list, sends rack numbers. It never says what the manager stops doing, or what they use today that this replaces. "Residents swap or give up a space themselves" comes closest, because it implies less work for the manager, but it stops at the fact. *Taste.* Fix: end the paragraph on what changes for the reader, said to them ("you no longer…"), measured against whatever they use today. That fact has to come from you.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline's third line is the single word "find", so a three-line heading ends on a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rebreak it or balance the lines so the last line holds a phrase.

**What's working.**
- The rack map is a real, specific picture of the product. The ramp band ("Ramp to the street door"), the cargo-bike detail row and "held since May" show the actual job in one glance, where a generic dashboard mock would not. Keep it as the hero image.
- The primary action is clear. One solid teal button sits next to a plain underlined link, and the button's teal matches the selected rack S4, which ties the action to the product without a word.
- The restraint holds up. There's one typeface, a muted three-color palette, a left-aligned hero, and a feature row with no icons. Its headings are concrete ("Racks for cargo bikes"), so the page reads as made for this product, not stamped from a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus styles on the button and link, and how the two-column hero and the three-column feature row stack on a phone.
