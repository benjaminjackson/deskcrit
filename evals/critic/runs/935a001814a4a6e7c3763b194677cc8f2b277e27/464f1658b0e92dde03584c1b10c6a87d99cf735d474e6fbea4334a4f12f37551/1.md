**First read.** My eye goes to the robot first. Its dark face panel is the heaviest patch of value on the page. Then it jumps left to the headline, which is larger but lighter in weight. Once I read the headline it's clear right away: a meeting-notes tool for people who sit on a lot of video calls. The page feels calm, plain and competent, apart from a toy robot in the middle of it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero of a SaaS marketing homepage. Its job is to get team members who run frequent video calls to try the product, either by adding it to their calendar or by reading sample notes first.

**Verdict.** The words do most of the job, and they do it well. The picture works against them. The stock robot with circuit traces is what a viewer sees first, and it says "generic AI product" when the copy has worked hard to say "notes you can trust, nothing kept."

**What would move it most**
1. **The robot is the wrong picture, and it takes the focal point** (defect + template tell). The right half of the hero is a stock smiling robot with circuit lines running out to hollow dots. Viewers have seen this image on countless AI products, so the page looks like anyone's at exactly the moment it should show what is different here (unslop: "stock robots… circuit lines… for anything AI → remove, or show what the AI actually produces"). It also costs three more things:
   - It splits the eye with the headline. The dark face panel is the strongest contrast on the page, so the eye starts on the picture and has to come back for the message (Arnheim on a single dominant element).
   - It doesn't join the headline to make one idea. The headline is about *notes before you hang up*; the picture is about *a bot*. Nothing in it shows a note, a decision, or a name next to a task. This is common sense, not a cited rule.
   - It undercuts the tone. A cartoon bot that "joins your calls" sits badly next to "Nothing kept you did not ask for." For a product that listens to meetings, a mascot makes the listening more noticeable, not less.

   The circuit traces and their end dots do no work at all; take them away and nothing is lost.

   Fix: replace the robot with the thing the product makes. The page already promises it in the secondary link, "Read a sample set of notes." Show a real-looking notes excerpt with decisions at the top, tasks with a name and a day, and the one-click correction. That gives the viewer the product to judge with their own eyes rather than on trust. It lets the headline and the picture make one point, and it keeps the headline as the dominant element. Supply the real sample content yourself; don't fill it with lorem.

**Smaller things.**
- Lone last-line words in the feature columns: "and knows." in column 1 and "who." in column 2 each end a paragraph by themselves (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrap them by adjusting the measure or a non-breaking space so at least two words carry the last line.

**What's working.**
- **The copy.** "Notes from every call, written before you hang up" is specific, has a verb, and ends on the moment that matters to the viewer. The body says who does what to what ("writes down what was decided and who will do what, and sends the notes to everyone who was there") and answers the obvious fear ("You correct a line in one click"). The three feature lines each land on the viewer. "Someone who missed the call reads two lines and knows" is a consequence, not a feature label. Keep this voice.
- **The ask is concrete.** "Add it to your calendar" names one exact action in the viewer's own world, and it is the only filled button. The underlined "Read a sample set of notes" is clearly secondary and lets a skeptic judge the output before committing.
- **The structure is disciplined.** A clear three-level type scale (headline, section headings, body) uses weight as well as size. One blue accent is kept for the action. The hero text column and the three feature columns all share the left edge at 80 px, and there is real white space instead of boxes and cards.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the button, the sample-notes link, and the nav.
