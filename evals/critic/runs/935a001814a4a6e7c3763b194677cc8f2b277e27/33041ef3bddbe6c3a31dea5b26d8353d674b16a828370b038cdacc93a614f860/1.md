**First read.** The eye lands on the big cream serif headline, moves right to the shelf card, and stops on the amber "Book" on Shelf 3. Then it drops back to the amber "Set up your studio" button. Within five seconds it reads as booking software for pottery studios, made by people who know the kiln room. It feels calm, warm and handmade rather than techy. Below the fold the page goes quiet and drifts to the left, and it ends without asking you for anything.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing landing page that gets studio owners or technicians to sign up, and lets them check the price on the way.

**Verdict.** This is a good page. The headline, the shelf card and the copy make one clear, specific promise. What holds it back is the second half: the page brings the reader to the price and then gives them nothing to do. The lower sections also lose the grid and balance the hero sets up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page has no ask at the point where the reader decides.** "One price per studio" shows $12 and $29 a month with no button under either plan, and the footer is next. Someone who scrolled to the price is the most ready to act, and they have to scroll back to the hero to find "Set up your studio". Put an action under each plan, or one ask after pricing that repeats the hero's wording, so the price and the step that follows it sit together. This is a defect. It costs sign-ups, and it's common sense: the source behind this checklist line isn't on the approved list.
2. **Below the hero, the page loses its grid and leans left.** The hero is a clear two-column split, and "From the wheel to the shelf" uses three equal columns (left edges at about 80, 467 and 853 px). The two price columns then start at about 80 and 424 px and stop near 720, so they match neither layout, and they look placed by eye. "For studios that teach" and the pricing both leave the right half empty, so the bottom third of the page tips to the left (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). The rhythm between sections also varies: about 96 px from the divider to "From the wheel…", but only about 55 px above "For studios that teach" and "One price per studio". Put the price columns on the three-column grid (two of three columns, or three if a plan is ever added). Give the teaching section something in its right column that belongs to it, such as a view of a class shelf in the same style as the hero card, or else let the text run wider. Use one gap above every section (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*). This is a convention, and here the cost is real: the lower half looks unfinished next to the hero.
3. **The case never sets Kilnroom against what studios do today.** The copy is concrete ("a number to write on the foot", "who to tell when it comes out"), but every line describes the product. Nothing names the way the studio manages the kiln now, or what goes wrong with that way. "See a sample firing" is a good ask because it lets the viewer look before they commit. But a technician would judge the value fastest against their own current method and its familiar failures. You, as the maker, know what studios use now and where it breaks. One line in the hero or in "From the wheel to the shelf" that names it would turn a description into a reason to switch. This is taste, and I'd argue it hard for a niche audience like this one.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline leaves "firing" alone on its third line. Break it so the last line holds a phrase, for example "booked before / the firing" (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). The lead paragraph in "For studios that teach" ends on a lone "teacher.", so adjust its width.
- The "Studio notes" link in the top navigation doesn't say what's behind it, whether that's a blog, guides or a changelog. Use the word for what it holds (Nielsen, heuristic 2).

**What's working.**
- **The shelf card as the hero picture.** It's a real slice of the product: a cone 6 glaze firing loading Friday at 16:00, named people, "Half free" next to an amber "Book". It makes the headline's promise concrete, and it uses the same amber as the main button, so the eye links the two. Keep it, and keep it specific.
- **The copy speaks the potter's language.** "Bisque or glaze firing", "what cone it needs" and "write on the foot" tell the audience the product was made by someone who knows their world. The two hero asks are one clear primary button and one honest underlined link.
- **The palette and type.** A deep green ground, warm cream text, a literary serif for headings and a plain sans for reading. It feels like a studio, not a SaaS template, and the amber is kept for the things you can act on.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the hero's two columns and the three-column row reflow on a phone. Check that the shelf card stays readable and sits below the headline, not above it.
