**First read.** The eye goes to the "Output" title, then along the four big numbers starting with 38.4 MWh, then to the blue line and the single blue bar F. It reads as a calm, tidy monthly operations dashboard for a small solar co-op. "Arrays needing a visit" is the only part that asks anyone to do anything, and it sits last and looks like everything else, so the eye reaches it after it has already decided the month looks fine.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a co-op operator uses this to check the month's output and decide which arrays to send someone to fix.

**Verdict.** It looks clean and competent, but you can't trust it yet. The charts contradict the headline numbers, and an operator who notices will stop believing the whole screen. Once the numbers agree, the next problem is that the fault list, which is the reason to open this screen, comes last and has the least weight.

**What would move it most**
1. **The charts disagree with the stat cards.** *Defect.* The top cards say November made 38.4 MWh against 51.2 MWh in October, which is 25% down. The "Daily output" line chart shows November above October on every point, by roughly 30–40%. "Output by array" also doesn't match the total: the bar for F is 2.3 MWh, and the seven bars together look like about 13 MWh against 38.4 MWh for the month. The viewer gets two opposite stories about the same month and can't tell which is true. On a screen people use to decide where the money and the truck go, that breaks trust in every other number. This is Tufte's graphical integrity (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2): the picture has to show the same effect as the data. Fix: make every chart and card come from the same source and period. If the bars measure something other than the monthly total (a daily average, say), the chart has to say so (see 3).

2. **The page has no first thing, and the thing to act on comes last.** *Defect.* The four stat cards share one size, weight and color, so nothing is seen first (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Hofmann, *Graphic Design Manual*). Meanwhile "Arrays needing a visit" is a plain table at the bottom. It holds 765 kWh of lost output and an inverter that has been offline for two days, but it has the same weight as the context charts above it. Faults are also invisible in the charts: D, G and B, the three faulted arrays, are ordinary gray bars, and the one blue bar goes to F, the best performer. Fix: make the open faults the first thing seen, for example by moving the table up or adding a clear count of arrays down and output lost. Mark the faulted arrays in the bar chart, not the healthy one. Which one leads is your call, but it should be the thing the operator acts on.

3. **"Output by array" can't be read without guessing.** *Defect.* It has no value axis, no unit except on one bar, and no time period. The title doesn't say whether the bars are the month, a day, or an average. Only F has a value, and nothing says why F is blue. The viewer can rank the bars but can't size the gaps between them, so a weak array looks like a mild dip (Tufte; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*: label axes and units). Fix: put the period and unit in the title, and either label every bar or add a scale. If the blue marks something, say what in words.

**Smaller things.**
- "Daily output (kWh)" plots about 11 points, roughly one every three days, not 30 daily values. Either plot every day or rename the chart to match what it shows.
- The line chart uses a legend in the top right. Labeling "November" and "October" at the right-hand ends of the lines saves the trip back and forth (Tufte; Wilke).
- Neither chart title says what the chart shows. "Daily output" and "Output by array" name topics. A title that states the finding, such as how November compares with October, would make the charts read faster. This is common sense; no approved source states it.

**What's working.**
- The cards all follow one format: a label, a big figure, then last month's figure in small gray type. Every headline number has its own comparison, and none of them needs a sparkline or an arrow to make the point. Keep that pattern.
- The fault table's copy is specific and in the operator's own words: "Inverter offline 2 days", "Soiling on lower rows", a place name for each array, and lost output in kWh, right-aligned and sorted worst first. That is exactly what someone needs to plan visits.
- The palette is restrained: grays plus one blue. That leaves room for a real status color when faults get their own treatment.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" can be changed, and whether a row in the fault table leads to an action such as assigning or scheduling a visit. If it leads nowhere, the screen ends at "here is a problem" with no next step.
