**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Hand out parking bays without the arguments". It then crosses to the bay plan on the right and stops on the navy Q4 bay, which shares its color with the "Map your car park" button. It reads as software for whoever runs the parking at a block of flats, sold to the managing office. It feels calm, plain and competent, with nothing shouting.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage hero for a parking-bay management product. The audience is estate offices and managing agents, and the goal is to get them to start mapping their car park.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, honest hero. The picture is the product doing its job, and the copy is specific. The biggest problem is the bay plan's color coding, which works against the page's own promise. The copy says the office "sees which bays are free", but the free bays are the faintest thing on the plan, and the taken ones are in the color most people read as "available".

**What would move it most**
1. **The bay plan's colors read backwards and come without a key.** In the plan on the right, 14 bays are filled sage green and the three free bays (P3, Q6, R5) are white with a pale dashed border. Green usually means "go" or "available", and parking signs and apps use it that way. A viewer glancing at the plan will likely read the green bays as the free ones. They only learn otherwise by counting the white bays against "18 bays, 3 free" in the corner. Nothing on the plan says what green, white or navy mean.
   - **Why it matters here:** this plan is the proof of the body copy's claim that "the estate office sees which bays are free". The free bays should be the easiest thing to spot, and right now they recede.
   - **Kind:** convention (green for success or availability, per Apple HIG and Material color roles) and defect (WCAG 1.4.1: meaning carried by color with no label).
   - **Direction:** give the taken bays a neutral fill and let the free ones carry the visible state. A muted gray-green for taken bays and a clearly marked style for free ones would do it. Also add a small key, or put "Free" on the free bays themselves, so the plan explains itself at a glance.
2. **The price clashes with the rest of the copy.** The detail strip says "$15 a month", but every other word on the page is British: "estate", "Flat 7", "near the lift", "car park", "warden". A UK estate office will wonder whether the product is really for them or is a US product with UK words pasted in. A US viewer meets "flat" and "estate" and has the same doubt in reverse. A price is something a viewer remembers and later holds the maker to, so mixed signals here cost trust.
   - **Kind:** defect (copy).
   - **Direction:** pick one market and make the currency and vocabulary match it. If the product sells in more than one market, show the price in the visitor's own currency.

**What's working.**
- **The hero picture is the product, not decoration.** It shows a real-looking car park ("Ashgrove Court"), a lane to the barrier, and one bay selected with its resident, permit date and price. A viewer understands what the software holds before reading a word. Keep it specific like this.
- **The copy names real things and real actions.** "Without the arguments" names the pain the buyer already has. "Map your car park" says exactly what the first step is, and "Look at a sample car park" gives a low-commitment second option. The three features below each say who does what ("A resident sends a visitor a pass for one day, and the barrier lets them in") instead of naming a category.
- **The hierarchy is restrained.** There is one primary button, the secondary action is set as a plain underlined link, there are about four type sizes with clear steps between them, and the palette is one neutral range plus navy and sage. The single navy accent ties the selected bay to the button, which is a nice touch.

**Smaller things.**
- "18 bays, 3 free" and "Flat 7, permit until April" are small gray text on the beige card. They look like they pass contrast, but measure them to confirm 4.5:1.
