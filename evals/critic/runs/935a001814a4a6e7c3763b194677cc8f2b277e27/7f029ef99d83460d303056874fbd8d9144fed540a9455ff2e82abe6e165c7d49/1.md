**First read.** My eye lands on the big black headline, drops to the dark red "Put on a show" button, then jumps right to the one red seat, B4, in the seat map. Within about three seconds it reads as booking software for small venues, like a village hall or an amateur theatre group. It feels calm, warm and plain-spoken. Nothing on it is shouting.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing page hero for a seat-booking product, aimed at volunteer-run theatres and halls, and meant to get them to start setting up a show.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The page is clear, the copy is specific, and the product shot makes the case. The biggest problem is the seat map's colors. They mean the opposite of what anyone who has bought a ticket online expects, so the one picture meant to show "a full hall" can be read as "a mostly empty one."

**What would move it most**
1. **The seat colors read backwards.** In the seat map (right half), the 15 sage-green seats are the sold ones and the three dashed white ones (A3, B6, C5) are the free ones; only the small "18 seats, 3 free" count tells you so. On most booking sites a colored seat means "you can pick this," and red means "taken." So a quick look reads a hall full of available green seats with one blocked red seat. That's the opposite of the headline's promise, "Sell every seat in the hall." Meanwhile B4, the seat this example booking is for, is in the same red the brand uses for "act here." *Defect:* it breaks the usual status-color meanings (Apple HIG, Material Design: Color), and the viewer gets the demo's main point backwards. **Fix:** make sold seats look sold, using a muted or neutral fill, and keep the clear, open look for free seats. Mark B4 in a way that can't be confused with "unavailable." A tiny key ("sold / free") inside the card would remove any doubt.
2. **Three buttons in the hero.** "Put on a show," "Try a sample plan" and "Talk to us" sit in a row, and two of them have the same outlined style. *Template tell* (Sokolovsky, unslop): a row of three buttons appears on many landing pages, so this one looks like anyone's, and the visitor has to choose between three paths. The strongest ask on the page is "Try a sample plan," because it lets visitors judge the product with their own hands instead of taking the claim on trust. Yet it has the same weight as "Talk to us," which could be on any site and doesn't say what happens when you click it. **Fix:** keep one primary and one secondary button, and make "Try a sample plan" the secondary. Move "Talk to us" to the nav or footer.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline leaves the word "row" alone on its third line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). That breaks up the "front row" phrase the line depends on. Rebreak it or adjust the measure so the last line carries more than one word.
- "Seat B4, row B" in the card footer says the row twice. "Seat B4" is enough (common sense).

**What's working.**
- **The copy.** The headline names a real problem in the viewer's own terms: the back rows don't sell. The paragraph says exactly what the product does ("draws your hall… prints the door list") and compares it to what they do now ("the box office stops taking orders by phone"). The three feature notes below are just as specific: concessions, door lists, seats held for the cast. Keep that voice.
- **The product shot is the real thing.** It shows a believable venue ("Fernhill Village Hall, Friday"), a real show and a price paid, not an abstract illustration. The picture and the headline make one argument together.
- **Restraint.** The palette is small (sage, oxblood red, beige, grays), there's a clear type scale, the feature row is plain text with no icons, and the empty space is gathered generously. The red shows up in only two places, so it works as a signal.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the three buttons and the nav, and how the two-column hero stacks on a phone. Check whether the seat map stays readable at narrow widths.
