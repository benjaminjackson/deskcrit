**Verdict.** This is a strong, unforced hero, with one clear focal point, a product picture that shows real content, and specific copy. The biggest thing in the way is the run map. It's the piece's best argument, but its colors say the opposite of what they mean, and no key settles it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The run map's colors read backwards, and nothing explains them.** In the panel at the right, 15 runs are filled sage green and 3 (K3, L6, M5) are white with a faint dashed edge. The header says "18 runs, 3 free", so green must mean taken. Green is the usual color for "available", though, so a first read takes the green runs as open and the white ones as empty or disabled. The viewer only gets it right by counting cells against "3 free". L4's brown is explained only by the "Run L4" line underneath. This is a convention problem: green means success or available (Apple HIG, Material color roles). It is also a defect against WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Color, because run state is carried by fill alone. On the page that costs a moment of doubt about the very picture meant to make the product look clear. In the product itself, staff would misread it. The fix is to give the states a key or labels, such as a tiny legend in the panel header next to "18 runs, 3 free", or the word "free" inside the empty cells. Then either change the occupied fill to something that doesn't say "go", or make the free state the colored one. *Defect / convention.*

2. **The headline is told from the wrong side of the counter.** "Book every kennel run without a phone call" reads as an instruction to the person reading it to book a run. That fits a dog owner, not the operator that "Set up your kennels" is speaking to. The lede then explains that "Owners book a run", so the operator has to reread the headline as "your runs get booked without you taking a call." That benefit is good, and setting it against the phone, the tool they use today, is exactly the right comparison. The headline just makes the reader rebuild it. The fix is to say whose phone call goes away, aimed at the operator ("you"), in an editorial edit of these words rather than new claims. *Defect, copy.*

**Smaller things.**
- The lede ends on "noon." alone on its last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rebreak it or trim the sentence so the last line holds more than one word.
- The lede says staff see who goes out "at noon", while the third feature prints the walk rota "for the morning shift". Pick one so the two lines don't seem to describe different features. (Common sense.)

**What's working.**
- **The product picture is real, specific content.** It shows named runs, an exercise yard between the rows, and a booking with a dog's name, dates and a paid amount, instead of a generic dashboard. Keep the specificity when you fix the colors. It's what makes this look like a tool built for kennels.
- **There's one accent, and it does one job.** The brown is kept to the logo, the primary button and the selected run, so the eye has exactly three stops, in the right order.
- **The structure is clean.** One left edge at 80 px carries the logo, headline, button and first feature column, and the right edge at 1200 px carries the nav, panel and rule. The three feature blocks have headings that say what they hold, with no icons or decoration propping them up.
