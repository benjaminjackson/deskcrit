**First read.** The eye goes to the "Plan and billing" title, then to the teal outline around the "Salon" card and the teal "Save changes" button. It reads as the settings page of an app for pet-grooming salons: tidy, calm and quiet. It also looks like many other admin templates, and the emoji in the sidebar are the only thing that sounds out of tune.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this: a salon owner or front-desk person uses this screen to see which plan they're on and what it costs, change that plan, keep billing details up to date, and get past invoices.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and easy to read. The problem is that the page doesn't do the billing job well. It never says which plan you're on now, when you'll next be charged, or what a change will cost. A plan upgrade is committed by the same generic "Save changes" button that saves an email address, and the invoices can be looked at but not opened. Each of those is a money question the page leaves the user to guess at.

**What would move it most**
1. **A plan change is a purchase, but here it's just another field on the form.** The three plan cards sit above a divider and the "Billing details" heading. The only commit button, "Save changes," sits below the billing fields, so it looks like it belongs to them. A user who clicks "Salon group" can't tell whether the change saves right away, saves with the email edits, or not at all. They also can't tell that pressing "Save changes" moves them from $45 to $95 a month. That's a **defect** where the user commits money:
   - It breaks natural mapping: the control isn't near the thing it changes (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*).
   - The divider and the heading cut the plan group off from its button.

   **Fix direction:** give the plan its own explicit action next to the cards, worded for what it does (for example "Change plan"). Show what happens before the user commits: the new price and when it takes effect. Leave "Save changes" with the billing fields only.
2. **The page doesn't tell you what you're on now or what happens next.** The "Salon" radio is filled in, but no label says it's the current plan. You'd only infer it from the invoice list. There's also no next charge date or amount, and no payment method, which is the first thing most people open a billing page to check or change. Users have to remember or work this out instead of reading it, which breaks Nielsen's sixth heuristic, recognition rather than recall. That's a **defect**. **Fix direction:**
   - Mark the current plan in words on its card.
   - Add a short status line near the top: current plan, next charge, and the card it goes to. Those facts are yours to supply.
   - Put a one-line note by the plan cards saying how a change is billed (Nielsen's tenth heuristic, help and documentation).
3. **You can see the invoices but can't get to them.** "CL-3307" and the other invoice numbers are plain gray text, and no row has a view or download action. People come to an invoice list to get a PDF for their accountant. Here they can only read a summary. That's a **defect**, and the argument is common sense, not a published rule. **Fix direction:** make each invoice number a real, clearly marked link, or add a "Download" action to each row.
4. **The emoji icons are a template tell.** The emoji in the sidebar (🐾 👥 💳 🔔 🔌) and the ✅ before "Paid" are a stock move users have seen on countless generated dashboards (Sokolovsky, unslop). They bring in five or six unrelated colors and a cartoon tone next to otherwise plain type, and the plug and bell are emoji standing in for real icons. The cost is that the screen looks like anyone's, and the one detail that could feel like Copperleaf, the paw, gets lost among the rest. **Fix direction:** use one set of line icons in the text color, or no icons at all. Show "Paid" as text, or as text with a plain status mark.

**Smaller things.**
- **Billing email and currency share a row.** They're unrelated, so they read as one paired field. Give currency its own row, or move it next to the plans it prices. This is common sense, not a cited rule.
- **No field is marked as required.** Mark the required ones, or put "optional" after the optional ones (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group).
- **Dates may not match the user's convention.** "5 Nov 2026" is day-month format on an account with a North Carolina address billed in USD. A US user would expect "Nov 5, 2026" (Nielsen's second heuristic, match with the real world).

**What's working.**
- **The page sits on one clean grid.** The title, dividers, plan cards, fields and table all share the left edge at about 300 px and the right edge at about 1120 px. Keep it when you add the status line and plan action.
- **Teal is used with restraint.** It appears only on the selected plan, the checkbox and the primary button, so the eye knows where to act. Don't spread it further.
- **The invoice table is set properly.** Amounts are right-aligned with figures lined up, and the dividers are light. It's quick to scan.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether choosing a plan card saves instantly, shows a confirmation, or waits for "Save changes". The size of the problem in finding 1 depends on this.
- Focus states on the cards and fields.
