**First read.** My eye goes first to the one saturated blue bar (Sat, 19) in the right chart, then back to the four KPI numbers across the top, then down the November line. It reads at once as an operations dashboard for a small ferry operator, covering one month, for office staff. It feels calm and orderly, a little generic, and the soft blue halo around every card gives it a stock SaaS sheen.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a monthly overview for harbor-office managers: they need to see at a glance how November compares with October and where capacity is tight.

**Verdict.** It's a competent, readable dashboard that mostly does its job: the KPIs, the comparison and the table are clear. What holds it back is that the two charts tell the manager less than they appear to. The "daily" line can't be daily and hides the weekly rhythm. The sold-out chart gives a number for only one of its seven bars.

**What would move it most**

1. **The "Daily passengers" chart doesn't show daily data, and that hides the pattern the next chart reports.**
   - **Where:** the line chart, left middle.
   - **What the viewer meets:** the title says daily, but each line has about 11 points across 1 to 30 November, so roughly one every three days. The November line climbs smoothly with no weekly shape. Right next to it, "Sailings sold out by weekday" says Saturday is by far the peak. A manager who reads both is left asking which one is true: does traffic peak every weekend, or rise steadily through the month?
   - **Why it matters:** sampling every third day, or averaging, flattens the weekly cycle, and that cycle is the operational story here. Comparing October and November by day of the month also lines up different weekdays, so the gap between the lines partly reflects the calendar, not demand.
   - **Fix:** plot all 30 days, or retitle the chart to say honestly what it shows (for example, a 3-day average). If October stays, line it up so the weekdays match.
   - **Kind:** defect (the title doesn't match the data). The point about matching weekdays is common sense; no approved source states it.

2. **The sold-out chart gives one number and leaves six bars unreadable.**
   - **Where:** the right chart.
   - **What the viewer meets:** only Saturday is labeled (19). There's no value axis, so Friday and Sunday, which look about 12 to 15, can't be read. These are the days a manager would look at when deciding whether to add sailings.
   - **Why it matters:** the title also names only the topic, so the viewer has to work out the finding ("Saturday sells out most, Friday and Sunday close behind") alone. Tufte and Few both ask that a chart label what its marks measure so the reader never has to guess.
   - **Fix:** put a value on every bar, which is cheap with seven bars, and keep the blue highlight on Saturday. Consider a title or short caption that states the finding in words already on the chart. That last part is taste; no approved source states it.
   - **Kind:** convention (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few).

3. **Every card sits on a pale blue colored glow.**
   - **Where:** around the KPI strip, both chart cards, the routes table, and the active "Overview" nav item.
   - **What the viewer meets:** a stock template effect they've seen on countless SaaS dashboards. The piece looks like anyone's product rather than a harbor office's own tool. Across the whole layout, the blue haze also muddies the clean gray ground.
   - **Fix:** take out the colored shadow. Separate the cards with the surface step you already have (white on warm gray) plus a hairline, or at most a neutral shadow. That frees blue to mean "November / this item" alone.
   - **Kind:** template tell (Sokolovsky, unslop).

**Smaller things.**
- The "Overview" page title and the four KPI numbers look the same size (~24 px). That's two levels on one size, so the title doesn't read as above the numbers. Make it clearly larger or set it in a different weight or color (Bringhurst on working from a scale).
- The line chart uses a legend in the top right. Put "November" and "October" at the right-hand ends of the lines so the eye doesn't travel back and forth (Tufte; Wilke).
- "Busiest routes" doesn't say busiest by what. The order shows it's by sailings, so say so in the heading or the column header (WCAG 2.4.6).
- "1 to 30 November 2026" looks like plain text, with no visible way to change the period or compare others. For a review tool, that's the one control a manager would reach for. Make the date range a visible picker (Nielsen #7).
- The KPI comparisons ("8,870 in October") make the viewer do the subtraction. A signed change, worked out from the figures already shown, would read faster. This is taste.
- The Settings icon looks like a sun or brightness control more than settings. Use the conventional gear.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI strip:** each headline number comes with last month's figure in a smaller, quieter line. Context without clutter is exactly what a monthly review needs, so keep that pattern.
- **Restrained color:** gray for context and blue for the thing that matters (the November line, the Saturday bar). That's the right logic for this piece. Keep it, and stop the glow from diluting it.
- **The routes table:** numbers right-aligned in tabular figures, light rules, generous rows. It scans cleanly and needs nothing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover tooltips on the charts would partly cover the missing bar values and the sampling question, so check whether they exist. Also check focus states in the sidebar.
