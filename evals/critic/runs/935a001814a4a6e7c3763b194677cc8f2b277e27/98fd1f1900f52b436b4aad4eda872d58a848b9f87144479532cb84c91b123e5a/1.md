**First read.** The eye goes to the row of four big numbers, then drops to the blue line and the blue Saturday bar. Saturday is the only thing on the page that is clearly picked out. It reads at once as an operations dashboard for a small ferry operator, for staff in a harbor office. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. It also feels a little mute: nothing tells me whether November was good.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a monthly overview that lets the operator's staff see quickly how ticket sales, revenue and capacity went this month compared with last, and where demand is concentrated.

**Verdict.** This is a clean, competent dashboard that mostly does its job: the layout is quiet, the charts are the right kinds, and the right things are emphasized. What holds it back is that it shows numbers but never says what they mean. Every comparison is left to the viewer's mental math, and the sold-out chart gives one value out of seven.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI cards make the viewer do the subtraction.** In the top row, each figure sits over a raw October value ("8,870 in October", "$131,200 in October"). To learn that tickets, revenue and cars all rose, the viewer has to subtract four pairs of numbers in their head. Those four rises are the main news of the page, and nothing on it says so. The comparison is there, so this meets the letter of Few's rule (a headline number needs context) but not its purpose: the viewer still can't tell good from bad at a glance. **Fix:** show the change itself, as a signed difference or percentage with its direction, next to each figure, and keep the October value as the secondary reference. *Defect: it slows the main read of the page.*
2. **"Average fare" says nothing, and may hide a real change.** The card shows "$15" over "$15 in October", so it reads as flat. But the page's own figures give about $15.15 for November ($142,630 / 9,412) and about $14.79 for October ($131,200 / 8,870). Rounding to whole dollars erases a rise of roughly 2%. As set, the card uses a quarter of the most valuable row and tells the viewer nothing. **Fix:** show cents, or the change, on this card so it carries information. Otherwise reconsider whether it earns its slot. *Defect.*
3. **"Sailings sold out by weekday" gives one number out of seven.** Only Saturday is labeled ("19"), and there is no value axis. Friday and Sunday are clearly high, but the viewer can't tell whether that means 12 or 16 sold-out sailings, and can't confirm that the bars start at zero. For a chart whose practical use is deciding where to add capacity, the near-misses matter as much as the peak (Tufte, Few: label what the marks measure). **Fix:** put the value above every bar, in the same quiet gray as the axis labels, and keep Saturday in blue. That also makes it clear the bars start at zero. *Defect.*
4. **"Daily passengers" does not plot daily values.** The lines have about 11 points across 30 days, roughly one every three days. Either the data is sampled or averaged and the title is wrong, or days are missing. A viewer who reads "daily" will trust peaks and dips that are really averages. **Fix:** plot all 30 days, or retitle the chart to say what each point is (for example, a three-day average). *Defect. This would be fine if the points really are daily and the line is only drawn sparsely, but the chart can't look like this.*
5. **The currency looks wrong for this operator.** The routes (Oban to Craignure, Tobermory to Kilchoan) and "Harbour office, Oban" in the sidebar place this in Scotland, but every money figure is in dollars. To this audience that reads as unfinished or as a currency error, and it is exactly the kind of figure people take at face value. **Fix:** confirm the currency and use it throughout. *Defect, if the dollar sign is not deliberate.*

**Smaller things.**
- The y-axis "0" in the line chart touches the "1 Nov" label at the bottom-left corner. Lift the zero or nudge the first date label so they don't collide (common sense).
- The line chart uses a legend in its top-right corner. Labeling "November" and "October" at the right-hand end of each line would remove the back-and-forth (Tufte).
- The chart titles name topics, not findings. "Saturday sells out most" already shows through the blue bar, so stating it in words is cheap and makes the takeaway explicit (taste).
- The chart row splits at about x = 891/908, which matches none of the KPI column edges above (757 and 1002), so the two rows don't share column lines (Müller-Brockmann). Align the split to the KPI grid, either three columns to one or two to two.

**What's working.**
- **Restraint with color.** Everything is gray except the two things that matter: November's line and the Saturday bar. Blue reliably means "look here", and that's why Saturday is the first thing seen after the KPIs. Keep the single accent.
- **The right chart for each question.** It uses a line for change over time with last month as a gray ghost, bars in natural weekday order for a categorical comparison, and a table for exact route figures. In the table, the numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures, so they compare cleanly down the columns.
- **The whole month fits on one screen** with no scrolling, and the card structure maps one card to one question.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The "1 to 30 November 2026" date range in the top right is set as plain text. If it is the control for changing the period, it doesn't look like one; check in the live product.
- Hover tooltips on the charts may supply the missing bar values. Even if they do, the at-a-glance read still lacks them.
