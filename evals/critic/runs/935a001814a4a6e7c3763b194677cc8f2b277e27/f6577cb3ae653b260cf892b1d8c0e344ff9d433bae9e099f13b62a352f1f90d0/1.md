**First read.** The eye lands on the KPI strip, $24,816 first, then jumps to the one solid blue bar (Saturday, 148), then settles on the line chart. It reads as the monthly back office of a small wine shop (Two Rivers Wine) that sells online and through a wine club. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy: gray, white, one blue, nothing shouting.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner opens this to see in a few seconds how the month is going against last month, and which days and wines are bringing in money.

**Verdict.** This is a solid, restrained dashboard, and the layout and charts mostly do their job. What holds it back is the numbers. Several of them don't say what they measure, and one chart looks like it doesn't add up to the KPI above it. On a screen whose whole value is trust in its figures, that's the biggest problem.

**What would move it most**
1. **Some figures can be read two ways, and one may not reconcile with the KPI above it.** *Defect.*
   - **"Wine club share 27%"** (top right card) doesn't say what it's a share of: revenue, orders, bottles or customers. Each reading leads the owner to a different decision about the club.
   - **"Web shop visitors"** has a y-axis running 0–900 with no unit or period. It's most likely daily visitors, but the viewer has to guess.
   - **"Bottles by weekday"** doesn't say whether the bars are totals for the month or averages per weekday. By eye, the seven bars add up to roughly 700 bottles, about half the 1,384 shown in "Bottles sold". If they're totals, they contradict the KPI. If they're averages, or online orders only, the chart has to say so. Please check the real numbers.

   Tufte and Few both say the reader should never have to guess what a number measures. The fix is editorial, not visual: restore the missing word in each label, using only what the data really is. For example, "share of revenue" or "share of orders", "visitors per day", "total bottles" or "average bottles per day".
2. **The KPIs make the owner do the arithmetic.** *Taste.* Each card gives October's raw value ("$21,970 in October"), which meets the convention that a number needs a comparison (Few). But to learn whether the month is good, the owner still has to subtract in their head, four times. Revenue up about 13% is the most important fact on the screen, and nothing says it. Show the change, and its direction, next to each figure, so "better or worse than last month" reads in one glance. Keep the October value as the detail line.

**Smaller things.**
- The line chart uses a separate legend in the top right. With only two lines, label "November" and "October" at the right-hand ends of the lines and drop the legend (Tufte, Wilke).
- "Overview" (top left) and the four KPI figures look like almost the same size, so the page title and the data compete for the same level. Make one clearly smaller, or give it a different weight (Bringhurst on scales; Refactoring UI).
- Blue means "November" in the line chart and "peak day" in the bar chart. That's taste, but a viewer moving between the two charts may look for a link that isn't there. Consider a different emphasis for the peak bar, or keep blue to mean only "this month".

**What's working.**
- **Restrained color.** Everything is gray except the current-month line and one bar. So the blue Saturday bar, with its direct "148" label, is the one thing on the screen that tells a story without a word. Keep that discipline.
- **The best-sellers table.** It's plain, the number columns are right-aligned on even figures, and it has hairline rules and no extra boxes. It's easy to scan and compare.
- **The grid.** Gutters are even (about 18 px between every card, both across and down), the card edges line up, and the page sits on one clear structure with the empty space gathered at the bottom. That's why it feels calm.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" (top right) is a date picker. If it is, it looks like plain text right now and needs a control's shape. If it's only a label, it's fine.
