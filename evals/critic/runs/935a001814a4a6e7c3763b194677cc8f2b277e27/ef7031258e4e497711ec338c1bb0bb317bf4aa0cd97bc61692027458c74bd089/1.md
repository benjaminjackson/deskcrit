**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Book every kennel run without a phone call", then jumps right to the one brown cell, L4, in the run plan, and then drops to the matching brown "Set up your kennels" button. Within a few seconds it reads as booking software for boarding kennels, aimed at the people who run them. It feels calm, practical and warm, closer to a well-kept farm office than a tech startup.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the top of a marketing homepage. Its job is to get kennel operators to start setting up their kennels, or at least to open the sample plan.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the product picture is specific, and the copy is mostly concrete. The biggest weakness is the headline: its verb describes what dog owners do, so the kennel operator has to work out the benefit to them. After that, the glossy orb logo is the one thing that looks like it came from a template.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline's action belongs to the wrong person.** "Book every kennel run without a phone call" says *book*, but the operator doesn't book runs. Their customers do. The button below ("Set up your kennels") speaks to the operator, so the headline and the button address two different people. An operator reading quickly could take the headline as a tool for owners. They have to reach the paragraph ("Owners book a run…") to learn that the gain to them is a phone that stops ringing. The fix is to restore the missing subject so the line lands on the viewer. For example, "Let owners book every kennel run without phoning you" changes nothing but who does the booking. *Defect (copy).*
2. **The asks don't let the operator compare against what they use now.** "Browse a sample run plan" is a good, specific second ask. But both asks only invite the viewer to look at the product. Nothing sets it next to what the operator uses today, whether that's a phone diary, a paper chart or a spreadsheet. That comparison would let them judge the value on their own terms. The fix is to name what they're replacing, near the asks or in the sample, using whatever the maker knows their customers actually use. *Taste.*
3. **The glossy orange orb logo** (top left). It's the one shaded, 3D object on a page that is otherwise flat and muted. Viewers have seen this orb on many other products, so the brand looks like anyone's at the exact spot where it should be most its own. Every other piece of the page already has a clear character: the sage runs, the brown accent, the "Exercise yard" band. The fix is a flat mark in one solid color, or the wordmark "Kennelry" on its own (unslop, Sokolovsky). *Convention (template tell).*

**Smaller things.**
- **Hero paragraph:** "noon." sits alone on its last line. Rebreak the paragraph or adjust its width so the last line carries more than one short word (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- **Grid:** the hero splits at about x=560/652, but the feature columns below start at about 80/469/859. The panel's left edge lines up with nothing beneath it, so the two sections sit on different grids. Put the panel's left edge on the third column or the halfway line so the columns run all the way down the page (Müller-Brockmann).
- **Feature text:** "An owner uploads the vaccination card before the stay is confirmed" and "Print the day's walk rota…" describe how the product behaves, but stop short of what that means for the operator. Add the consequence each line already implies, said to "you" (for example, what a check at booking spares you). Don't add new claims. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- **The run plan is the product, not decoration.** Lettered runs, an "Exercise yard" band, three free runs that match "18 runs, 3 free", and a selected run with a named dog, dates and "$210 paid" show the viewer their own day. Keep it this specific.
- **One accent color with one job.** Brown marks only the selected run and the primary button, so the eye moves from example to action. Sage and warm gray carry everything else. Protect that restraint.
- **The copy is concrete.** "Feeding notes on the run", "who goes out for a walk at noon" and "run by run, for the morning shift" use the operator's own words and name real tasks. Nothing here could sit on another product's page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the run plan is interactive or a static picture. If it's clickable, the free runs' pale dashed borders would need checking for contrast.
