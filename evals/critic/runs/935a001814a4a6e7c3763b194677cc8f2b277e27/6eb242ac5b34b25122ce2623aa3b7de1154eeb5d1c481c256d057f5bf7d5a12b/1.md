**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Hand out parking bays without the arguments," then drops straight to the one dark bay, Q4, in the car park plan below. From there it goes to the detail row and "$15 a month." It reads as a calm, plain SaaS landing page for whoever runs parking on a residential estate. It feels trustworthy and unhurried, with no hype.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing page for Bayline: it has to get estate or building managers to see that it ends parking disputes and to click "Map your car park."

**Verdict.** This is a good page. The headline, the ask and the product view are specific to this product, and the hierarchy is clear. Two things in the product view work against it: green marks the *taken* bays, and "$15 a month" could be read as Bayline's own price.

**What would move it most**
1. **The green bays mean the opposite of what people expect.** In the plan, 14 sage-green bays are occupied and the 3 white dashed ones (P3, Q6, R5) are free. For parking, green usually means "free" or "go," so a first read of the plan is "most bays are free," which "18 bays, 3 free" then contradicts. You can work it out by counting the white bays against that line, but nothing on the plan says what green, white or navy mean. This is a defect, and it breaks a convention (Apple HIG and Material Design on semantic color: green means success or go). The whole point of the plan is "see which bays are free," so it should read that way in one glance. Fix: take green off the taken bays, using a neutral fill for occupied, or else make the free bays the colored ones. Then add a short key or a label in place, such as "free" on the open bays (Tufte: label at the data, not in a separate legend).
2. **"$15 a month" can be read two ways, and the currency doesn't match the words.** In the detail row, "$15 a month" sits right-aligned and on its own, under a nav that has "Pricing" in it. A stranger could read it as the resident's permit fee for Q4 or as what Bayline costs. Prices are what people remember and hold you to, so if they read it as Bayline's price and then find a different number on the Pricing page, it costs you trust. Fix: tie the number to the bay with a word already implied, for example "Permit: $15 a month," or put it in the "Flat 7, permit until April" line. Also, the copy uses UK terms ("estate," "flat," "car park," "lift") with a dollar sign. A UK estate manager will wonder whether the product is for them, and a US one will stumble over "car park." Pick one market and make the currency and the words agree. This is a defect: two readings of a price.
3. **The ask doesn't compare against what the viewer uses today.** "Map your car park" is a strong, concrete ask, and the headline names a real pain. But nothing sets Bayline next to how the estate office handles bays now (a spreadsheet, a paper permit list, a noticeboard), so the viewer has to take "without the arguments" on trust. This is taste, argued: the claim would land harder if the viewer could see their current method lose. Fix: say what kind of tool it replaces, in the intro or under the plan. Only you know what your customers used before, so that fact has to come from you.

**Smaller things.**
- The hero is the stock centered stack of headline, intro, two buttons and a picture below. The specific plan saves it, but the layout itself looks like anyone's. If you want the page to feel more its own, try an off-center split with the plan beside the copy (template tell: "Hero stacked on one centered axis").

**What's working.**
- **The product view is the hero picture, and it's a real one.** A named car park (Ashgrove Court) with rows P, Q and R, a one-way lane to the barrier, and one bay picked out with flat, permit term and price. It shows the product doing its job, with no abstract illustration. Keep it.
- **The copy is specific and spoken.** "Hand out parking bays without the arguments" names a pain only this audience has. The intro says who does what: residents apply, and the office sees which bays are free. Both buttons name a concrete action.
- **The hierarchy is clean.** There is one dominant headline and a small, clear set of type sizes. One navy is used for both the primary button and the selected bay, and the rest of the palette is quiet and warm.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the bays can be clicked in the plan, and what "Look at a sample car park" opens. If it only scrolls to the plan already on screen, it's a duplicate. It should open an interactive demo.
- Exact contrast of the gray small text ("18 bays, 3 free," "Flat 7, permit until April") and of the lane label on the tan band. They look fine, but they should be measured.
