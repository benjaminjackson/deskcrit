**First read.** The eye lands on the row of four big numbers, then drops to the blue line and the one blue bar (Tuesday). Within two seconds this reads as a monthly operations dashboard for a farm-box delivery business. It feels calm and tidy, closer to a well-kept spreadsheet than a sales pitch. The next question, "so is November good?", has to be worked out by hand.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you didn't say: a small box-delivery operator (Greenacre Farm, going by the footer) opens this to see how the month went and what needs attention.

**Verdict.** This is a competent, clean dashboard with a solid grid and restrained color. What holds it back is that it reports numbers without saying what they mean. Every comparison is left for the viewer to do in their head. One chart's title also doesn't match what it plots, which undermines trust in the numbers.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI row gives last month's figure but not the change.** Under each big number, "4,512 in October", "$93,880 in October" and "236 in October" make the viewer subtract to learn that boxes are up about 5%, revenue is up about 5%, and paused subscriptions are down. The comparison is there, which is right (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). But it's raw, so the row shows four facts instead of four verdicts, and nothing says which way is good. "Paused subscriptions" going down is good news, and it looks exactly like the others. Direction: lead with the change (direction and size) and keep October as the reference. Make good and bad readable without relying on color alone. "Average box $21 / $21 in October" is the case to check: whole dollars may be hiding a real change. If it's truly flat, saying so is the finding. *Defect: it slows the main job of the page.*
2. **"Boxes packed per day" doesn't plot per day, and the page uses three words for what may be one thing.** The line chart has about 11 points across 1 to 30 November, so each point covers roughly three days, or some days are missing. Either way the title promises something the chart doesn't show, and a reader trying to find a specific day will misread it. The page also says "packed" (line chart), "delivered" (KPI) and "Deliveries" (bar chart and page title). A stranger can't tell whether a packed box and a delivered box are the same count. If they're different, that difference matters to an operator and needs to be visible. Direction: make the title match what each point is (or plot every day), and use one word for one thing across the page. *Defect: it makes the data look untrustworthy.*
3. **"Deliveries by weekday" can only be read for Tuesday.** There's no value axis and only Tuesday carries a number (1,140). Thursday looks almost as tall, but its value can't be read, and the reader can't tell whether Tuesday is meaningfully ahead. Sunday has no bar and no label, so "no deliveries on Sunday" and "missing data" look identical. Every chart should name what its marks show with a readable scale (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few). Direction: label every bar's value (seven numbers fit easily), and mark Sunday explicitly as zero or "no route". *Defect.*
4. **The chart titles name topics, not findings.** "Boxes packed per day" and "Deliveries by weekday" say what's plotted, not what happened. November running steadily above October and climbing through the month is the best news on the page, and the viewer has to discover it alone. The same goes for the Tuesday highlight: the blue says "look here" but not why. Direction: let each title or a short annotation state the takeaway the data already supports. *Taste, but strong on a page whose whole job is to report.*

**Smaller things.**
- One blue, two meanings: in the line chart blue means "November", in the bar chart it means "the busiest day". A viewer scanning across may read Tuesday as "November" too. Keep blue for one role, or make the bar chart's highlight read differently (Apple HIG, "Color").
- The line chart uses a legend at the top right for two series. Labeling "November" and "October" at the right end of each line removes the look-away (Tufte; Wilke).
- The light gray October line and the gray weekday bars look under 3:1 against the white card, and they carry data. Measure them against WCAG 1.4.11 and darken them slightly if they fall short.
- The page title "Deliveries" and the KPI figures look nearly the same size, so the two levels blur. Make the title clearly bigger or the figures clearly smaller (Bringhurst, scale).
- "1 to 30 November 2026" is static text with no visible way to change the period or compare other months. For a page someone checks monthly, a period control is the obvious shortcut (Nielsen 7).

**What's working.**
- The grid and spacing: the four KPI cells, two charts and table share one set of column edges and even gutters, so the page reads as one system at a glance. Keep it while revising.
- Color restraint: gray for context and one blue for what matters is the right model. It only needs the blue to mean one thing.
- The "Most added extras" table: numbers right-aligned in tabular figures, sorted by "Added", with the grower named. It answers a real question with no extra ink.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the line chart's points show tooltips with exact daily values on hover, and whether the date range is clickable. Either would change how much findings 2 and 3 cost.
