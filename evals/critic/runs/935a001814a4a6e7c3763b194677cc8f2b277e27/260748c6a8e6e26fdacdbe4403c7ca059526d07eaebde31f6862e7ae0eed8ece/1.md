**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Fill every market pitch before Saturday", then jumps right to the one rust tile, F4, in the pitch map, then back down to the rust "Plan your next market" button. Within three seconds this reads as booking software for whoever runs a street market. It feels calm, practical and local: muted sage and rust, real pitch numbers, a footway to the car park. It looks like a tool made by people who have walked the rows.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the landing page for market managers who now take pitch bookings and fees by text and cash. Its job is to get them to start planning a market or look at the sample.

**Verdict.** This works. It's clear, specific and well built, and the only thing that really holds it back is the color code in the pitch map, which is the page's proof of what the product does. A couple of copy fixes would land the pitch more directly on the manager.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pitch map's colors read backwards for a payments tool.** Where: the Canal Street Market panel. Booked pitches are green, and the one pitch you're shown in detail, F4, is rust red. In a tool about who has paid, a manager will read red as "problem: unpaid or blocked." The panel then says "$32 paid," the opposite. Green, meanwhile, is the color most people read as "available," but here it means taken, and the free pitches are white. Nothing on the map explains the code, so the viewer only gets it right by counting "3 free" against the three dashed boxes. And because F4 is the same rust as the main button, it also reads as something to click. Status colors should keep their usual meanings and never be reversed (Apple HIG, Color; Material Design, color roles). **Convention, with a real cost here:** this panel is where the manager decides whether the product understands their market, and it makes them decode it first. Direction: show the selected pitch with something other than alarm red, such as a heavier outline or a darker sage. Keep the rust for the button. Consider a two- or three-item key, or labels like "free" in the dashed boxes. If the brand wants rust in the map, use it for unpaid pitches, which is what a viewer would read it as anyway.

2. **The pitch talks about the manager instead of to them.** Where: the hero paragraph. "…and the market manager stops taking bookings by text." The person reading is the market manager, so the payoff lands on someone else. Taste, but it's the line that carries the relief the product sells. Direction: an editorial edit only, for example "…and you stop taking bookings by text." The feature lines below already do this well ("so nobody walks the rows with a cash bag"), so bring the hero up to their level.

**Smaller things.**
- Copy check, common sense: the price shows "$32", while the words ("pitch", "footway", "car park", "council") lean British. If the target market is the UK, a dollar sign will look like the product wasn't made for them. Make the currency match the market you're selling to.
- The small gray text on the beige panel ("Booked by Rye and Rind Bakery", "18 pitches, 3 free") looks close to the 4.5:1 limit. Measure it (WCAG 1.4.3) and darken it a step if it falls short.

**What's working.**
- **The headline is a deadline, not a slogan.** "Fill every market pitch before Saturday" names the job, the unit and the time the manager actually cares about. Keep that specificity in every revision.
- **The product shot is a real, believable market, not a generic dashboard.** Pitch numbers, a footway band, "3 m with power", a named bakery. It shows the product's model in one glance and pairs with the headline instead of repeating it.
- **The features measure the product against what the manager does today:** taking bookings by text, walking the rows with a cash bag, the council's quarterly list. That lets them judge the value on their own terms. There's one clear primary button with a quiet text-link second action, and the restraint in type and palette lets those few rust accents lead the eye.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the pitch tiles are interactive in the live page (if so, their hover and selected states need checking against finding 1). Also how the two-column hero stacks on a phone.
