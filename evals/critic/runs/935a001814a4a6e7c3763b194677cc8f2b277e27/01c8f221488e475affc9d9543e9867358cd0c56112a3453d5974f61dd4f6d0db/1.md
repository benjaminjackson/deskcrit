**First read.** My eye goes straight to the blue Saturday bar, the only color on the page. Then it goes to the big "2,480" beside it, and only after that up to the row of four figures across the top. It reads as a monthly circulation dashboard for library staff at Ashcombe Public Library. It feels calm, orderly and competent, maybe too quiet, because nothing tells me whether this was a good month.

**What it's for.** My assumption: library managers or staff check this to see how November is going for loans, members and visits, and what needs attention, like holds waiting to be picked up.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, well-built screen that mostly does its job. But the loudest thing on it is a weekday pattern, while the month's actual news, how it compares with October, sits in 12 px gray and you have to do the math yourself. The fixes are about emphasis and meaning, not a rebuild.

**What would move it most**

1. **The emphasis lands on the weekday chart, and the month's results are something you have to work out yourself.** The blue bar and the 2,480 callout are the strongest contrast on the screen, so the first thing anyone learns is "Saturday is busy." Staff already know that and it barely changes month to month. The question a manager opens this to answer is "how are we doing?" The four top figures do have a comparison ("10,480 in October"), which is good. But it's small gray text with no direction or size of change, so the viewer has to subtract four pairs of numbers in their head to learn that every figure is up. This is a defect: it slows down the main job. Direction: make the change itself readable at a glance in the top row, as a difference or percentage next to each figure, with a direction marker that doesn't rely on color alone. Then decide on purpose whether the Saturday highlight should still be the brightest thing on the page. Bringing the blue down, or giving the top row comparable weight, would let the eye start where the month's story is.

2. **The chart can't be read honestly as it stands.** There are two problems.
   - Only Saturday has a value. The other six bars have no labels and no axis, so you can't say what Friday or Sunday was. You can only see that they were "less." Tufte and Few both say a reader should never have to guess what a mark measures. This breaks a convention, and here it costs something, because comparing weekdays is the chart's whole purpose.
   - "Loans by weekday" and "loans on Saturdays" don't say whether these are monthly totals or averages per day. That matters this month. November 2026 starts on a Sunday, so it has five Sundays and five Mondays but only four of every other weekday. If the bars are totals, the Sunday and Monday bars each include an extra day and the comparison is skewed. This is a defect: the conclusion "Saturday is the busiest day" may be stated on uneven terms.

   Direction: label each bar with its value in the small gray style, and say in the title or callout which measure it is (average per day if the counts differ). The callout also repeats "2,480" right next to the label on the bar itself. One of the two should go, and the space it frees could carry the clarified wording.

3. **The Returns and Holds cards are a second, different system for the same kind of figure.** The top row shows bare numbers ("11,206"). The right-hand cards put the unit inside the number ("10,870 returns", "1,620 holds"), use a different label style (dark title instead of a gray label), and come in a different card shape. The Holds card also says "holds" twice ("Holds placed this month / 1,620 holds"). Each card holds one figure and one line, and the bottom half of each is empty. So the right column looks like filler stretched to match the chart's height, and the empty areas are scattered rather than deliberate (Ruder on gathered white space). This is a convention issue (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind follow one program) and a template tell (unslop: numbers in mixed formats). Direction: give every headline figure one format and one card style. Then either give these two cards the same treatment as the top row, including the October comparison, or let them carry the content that justifies their size. For example, "86 waiting to be picked up" is the most actionable number on the page and is currently its smallest text.

**Smaller things.**
- The top row's column dividers (about x = 511, 756 and 1002) don't line up with the split between the chart and the right column below (about x = 931/948). Take both from the same columns (Müller-Brockmann).
- The callout caption breaks so that "day" sits alone on its last line. Rewrap or widen the block (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The Holds icon in the sidebar reads as a shopping cart. That's a store metaphor in a library tool. Common sense: use an icon that suggests reserving or setting aside.
- "Overview" is set at about the same size as the figures. It's fine on a dashboard, but it means the page title isn't a separate level. Distinguish it by weight or let it drop back.

**What's working.**
- One accent color used as a highlight, with everything else gray. When it lands on the right thing, it works exactly as it should. Keep the restraint and change the target.
- The "Most borrowed" table is quiet and well set: right-aligned figure columns, light row rules, no zebra striping or boxes. Data doesn't get cleaner than this.
- Every top figure already comes with a previous-period reference. The data you need is there; only how it's presented needs work.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the "1 to 30 November 2026" range in the top right is a control. If you can change it, it currently looks like plain text and needs a visible cue that it's clickable.
- Hover, focus and keyboard behavior on the sidebar and table rows.
