**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Let the harbour book its own berths," then jumps right to the one navy tile, S4. It drops to "Berth S4, up to 9 m … $380 paid" and then back to the navy "Set up your harbour" button. It reads as booking software for small marinas, sold to the harbour office. It feels calm, plain and practical, a bit like a well-kept noticeboard, which suits the audience.

**What it's for.** No brief was given, so I'm assuming this is the top of a marketing homepage. Its job is to get harbour offices to set up Moorly, mainly by showing them what boat owners will see.

**Verdict.** This is a solid, honest hero that mostly works. The headline, body copy and the pontoon plan all tell one story. The biggest problem is inside the plan: green marks the taken berths, and the free ones are pale dashed outlines. A viewer reads the map backwards, and the free berths that the pitch is about are the hardest thing to see.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pontoon plan colors free and taken berths the wrong way round.** 15 of the 18 tiles are sage green and these are the taken berths. The three free ones (N3, S6, W5) are white with a thin dashed edge. Almost everywhere else, green means "available / go," so at first glance a harbour office sees a pontoon with 15 berths free. The "18 berths, 3 free" line in the corner then contradicts the picture, and they have to work it out. The free berths also look like gaps in the grid rather than spaces you can book, even though the body copy promises "boat owners pick a free berth." The map should show that moment, and right now it plays it down. This is a defect, and it breaks a convention (Apple HIG, Color; Material Design color roles: green means success or availability, and these meanings shouldn't be reversed). Fix: make the free berths the ones that stand out, and put taken berths in a quiet neutral. If green stays, it should mean free. Add a short key (free / taken / your booking) so the navy S4 doesn't depend on the footer line to explain it.
2. **The free-berth outlines are too faint to see.** The dashed borders on N3, S6 and W5 look well under 3:1 against both the white fill and the beige panel. On a dimmer screen these three tiles nearly disappear. This is a convention issue (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.11, non-text contrast). Fixing #1 mostly fixes this too. Whatever marks a free berth needs at least 3:1 against what's around it. Measure the dashed stroke against the panel.

**Smaller things.**
- The hero paragraph ends with "where." alone on its last line. Rewrap it, or tighten the paragraph width slightly, so at least two words share the last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The headline says the harbour does the booking, but it's the boat owners who book; the body copy clears this up a line later. This is taste and it's arguable, because "the harbour books itself" is the actual promise to the office. Still, read it as a stranger would to check it doesn't sound like the office is still doing the work.

**What's working.**
- **The product shot does the explaining.** It's a real-looking pontoon plan with berth codes, the slipway walkway, and a concrete booking with a size limit, season and amount paid. It shows the idea instead of a generic dashboard or a blob, and the hero would be much weaker without it.
- **The two asks are specific and ranked correctly.** "Set up your harbour" is the one filled button, and "Open a sample pontoon" is a quieter text link. The second lets a skeptical harbour master try the product on familiar terms before committing. Keep that pairing.
- **The copy is in the audience's own words.** "Pontoon plan," "season fees," and "a plan the harbour master can print… for the office wall." The feature row is three plain heading-and-sentence pairs with no icons or emoji, and each one names a real task. It sounds like someone who knows marinas wrote it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the berth tiles in the sample plan respond when hovered or clicked. That matters here, because "Open a sample pontoon" suggests they do.
