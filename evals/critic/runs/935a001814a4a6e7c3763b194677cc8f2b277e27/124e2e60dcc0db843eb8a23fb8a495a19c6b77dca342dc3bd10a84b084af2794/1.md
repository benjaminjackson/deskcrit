**First read.** The eye goes first to the row of four big figures ($31,270, 2,214, $14, 18%), then jumps to the one blue bar, Saturday, then down to the table. It reads as the monthly admin screen for a small farm shop. It feels calm and well kept, and it could be any analytics template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner opens this to learn in a few seconds how November went and what to do about it.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and well built, but it shows the month's numbers without explaining them. Revenue and orders fell from October while visitors rose, and nothing on the screen says so, so the owner has to work out the month's main story alone.

**What would move it most**
1. **The figures never say whether the month was good or bad.** Each KPI card puts October underneath in small gray text ("$34,105 in October", "2,390 in October"). To learn that revenue fell about 8% and orders about 7%, the owner has to do the subtraction. The "Shop visitors" chart shows November above October on every day, which means more people came and fewer bought. That is the most useful thing on the page, and the title only says "Shop visitors". The comparison is there, but nobody has worked it out for the reader. The fix has two parts. Turn each October line into a change with a direction and a sign (up or down, by how much), and say it with more than color. Then let the chart titles state what they show instead of naming a topic. *Defect* (it slows the owner down and hides the main finding). Turning topic titles into findings is my own judgment, not a published rule.
2. **"Orders by weekday" doesn't say what it counts.** There's no value axis. Only Saturday has a number, "148", with no unit or time period, so the other six bars can only be compared by eye. By my rough estimate, neither reading of 148 matches the 2,214 orders above. If 148 is all Saturday orders for the month, the seven bars add up to about 700. If it's the average per Saturday, the month comes to about 3,000. A shop owner may plan Saturday staffing or stock from this chart, so a number that can be read two ways is a real problem. Without an axis, you also can't confirm the bars start at zero (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 17.1). Fix: say in the title or the label what one bar measures, such as average orders per day or total for the month. Then either add a zero-based axis or put a value on every bar (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few). *Defect.*
3. **"Farm box subscribers 18%" pairs a count label with a percentage.** The label names a number of people, but the value is a share, and the reader can't tell 18% of what: customers, orders, or revenue. Fix: either show the count of subscribers, or keep the percentage and say what it's a share of. Only you know which one is meant. *Defect* (copy).

**Smaller things.**
- The "Shop visitors" y-axis (0–900) gives no unit or period. The line has about 11 points across 30 days, so it isn't clear whether a point means visitors per day or per few days. Say which (Tufte; Few).
- The November/October legend sits in the top-right corner. Put the labels at the right-hand ends of the two lines so the reader doesn't have to look back and forth (Tufte, *Visual Display*).
- The page title "Overview" and the KPI figures look almost the same size, roughly 24 and 26 px by eye. Two sizes that close read as an accident, not as two levels. Make the figures clearly bigger or the title clearly smaller (Bringhurst, §3.1.1).

**What's working.**
- Color is held back. Everything is gray except the November line and the Saturday bar, so blue reliably means "this one". Keep that rule when you add up and down markers, and don't add a third hue just for them.
- The table is set correctly: quantities and revenue are right-aligned in tabular figures, the row rules are thin, and items carry their units ("500 ml", "250 g", "2 l"). The figures also check out: revenue ÷ orders = $14 in both months.
- The layout is calm and clear: four cards, two charts, one table on one grid, with the current page marked in the sidebar. Everything fits on one screen.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a date-range control or plain text. If the owner can't change the period here, they can't compare any other month.
- Hover values on the charts. If hovering shows exact bar values, finding 2 is partly reduced, but not for someone just scanning the page.
