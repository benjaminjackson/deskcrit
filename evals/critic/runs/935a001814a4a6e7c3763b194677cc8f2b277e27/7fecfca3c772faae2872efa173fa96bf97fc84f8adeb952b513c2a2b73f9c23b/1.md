**First read.** The eye goes first to the band of four blue numbers at the top, then to the one dark-blue Saturday bar, which is the only thing on the page with real contrast. Then it wanders, because almost everything else is the same blue: the page title, the date, the card titles, the table headers, the revenue column and the nav. It reads as the monthly bookings report for a pottery studio, checked by the owner or manager. It feels calm, tidy and competent, but also generic.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a studio owner opens this to see in a few seconds how November is going against October, and which days and classes are carrying the business.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. It's clean, honest and fits on one screen, and every headline number comes with last month next to it. What's in the way is that blue means everything here, so it means nothing. A second problem is that the charts name topics but never say what they found, or exactly what they count.

**What would move it most**

1. **Blue is on everything, so nothing reads as clickable or as important.** The same blue is used for the page title "Bookings", the date range "1 to 30 November 2026", all four KPI numbers, every card title, the table column headers, the Revenue figures, the legend text and the whole nav. So the viewer can't tell links from data. Is the date range a picker? Are "Class" and "Seats" sortable? Are "$5,760" and the other revenue figures links? Nothing answers that, and anything that is clickable has only color to show it. This also drains the accent: the Saturday bar works because it's the only strong blue in a field of gray, and the rest of the page throws that away. This is a defect (the viewer can't tell what's clickable; Nielsen Norman Group on flat links, WCAG 1.4.1) and a template tell (accent on nearly every element; text in one hue throughout, unslop). Fix: make headings, the title and data figures dark neutral text. Keep blue for things that act (nav, links, the date control) and for one or two highlights in the data. If the date range is a control, give it a shape or icon that shows it opens something.

2. **The charts name topics, not findings, and their units don't line up with the numbers above.**
   - "Daily bookings" and "Seats booked by weekday" are labels. The story is already in the data: November ran above October on every point, and Saturday is the busiest day by a wide margin. A title that says that saves the owner from working it out (taste/common sense; the source for this rule isn't on my approved list).
   - The line chart has no unit on its y-axis, and the page uses both "bookings" and "seats" without saying whether one booking is one seat (Tufte, *Visual Display*, on labeling units).
   - The numbers also look like they don't add up. The November line averages about 18 a day by eye, which over 30 days is roughly 540. The KPI says 386 seats booked. And the line has only about 11 points for a month labeled "Daily". A sharp-eyed owner will notice and stop trusting the page.
   - This is a defect if the mismatch is real. Check that the line's values, its sampling and the KPI come from the same measure. Then label the axis with that unit and use one word for it across the page.

3. **"Fullest classes" claims more than the table shows.** "Fullest" suggests highest share of seats filled, but the table only shows seats sold (96, 72, 54), with no capacity. A big class that's half empty could top this list over a small class that sold out. The owner may act on this (adding sessions, pricing), so reading it two ways is a defect (WCAG 2.4.6, headings describe their content). Fix: either make the heading say what the list is ranked by (most seats sold), or add the capacity or fill rate so "fullest" is true.

**Smaller things.**
- Page title "Bookings" and the KPI numbers are nearly the same size, weight and blue (about 24 vs about 26 px by eye), so they compete as two first levels (Bringhurst, scale). Make the title clearly smaller or neutral, or the numbers clearly larger.
- The line chart uses a legend in the top right corner. Put "November" and "October" at the right-hand ends of their lines (Tufte; Wilke).
- The weekday chart labels only Saturday (92) and has no axis, so the other days can't be read even roughly. That's fine if the point is "Saturday wins", which the title should then say. Otherwise, put the value on each bar in small type.

**What's working.**
- **The Saturday bar:** one saturated bar, every other bar gray, with its value written on it. That's highlighting done right. Keep this exact restraint, and use it as the model for the rest of the page's color.
- **Last month under every KPI** ("352 in October", "$19,940 in October"): every headline number has its comparison right under it, in quieter type. That's why the top band is useful at a glance.
- **October in gray behind November** on the line chart: the context recedes and the current month leads, with no extra colors.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date range, the column headers and the revenue figures are actually interactive, and what their hover and focus states look like.
