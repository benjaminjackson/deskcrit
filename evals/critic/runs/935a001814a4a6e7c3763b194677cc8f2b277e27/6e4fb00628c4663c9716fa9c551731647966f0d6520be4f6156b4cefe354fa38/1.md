**First read.** The eye goes to the weekday bar chart on the right. It's the only saturated thing on a gray-and-white screen, and its six rainbow pills pull harder than anything else. From there it jumps back to the big numbers in the top row, then down to the blue line. It reads as a clean, competent analytics page for a farm-box subscription business. It feels calm everywhere except that one card, which feels like it came from a different product.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an operator at a small farm-box delivery business opens this to check how November went against October and which days and extras drive the work.

**Verdict.** This is a solid dashboard. The structure, type, and grid are good, and every headline number comes with a comparison. The biggest problem is the weekday chart: its rainbow colors carry no meaning but take the first look away from the figures that answer "how did this month go?"

**What would move it most**

1. **The weekday chart's rainbow pill bars take the focal point and mean nothing.** *(Defect, plus a template tell.)*
   - **Where:** "Deliveries by weekday", right of the middle row.
   - **What the viewer gets:** Each day gets its own hue (red, orange, yellow, green, cyan, blue) even though the day name under each bar already identifies it. The color adds no information but gets the first look, so the eye lands on a secondary chart instead of the month's results in the top row.
   - **Status colors misfire:** Monday is red and Thursday green, so to an operator Monday looks like a problem and Thursday a success. Nothing in the data says that.
   - **Clashes with the rest:** The line chart next to it uses one blue plus gray for context, so the two cards follow two different color programs.
   - **Template tell:** Rainbow bars with fully rounded ends are a stock template-dashboard move that viewers have seen on many products. It makes this the most generic-looking part of a page that is otherwise its own.
   - **Fix:** Make all the bars one neutral or brand color. If one day matters (the peak, or today), give only that bar the accent, the way the line chart treats October in gray. Square off the bar ends. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ties the palette to the data type: days are a positional order here, so the hues add nothing.

2. **The weekday chart doesn't say what its bars measure.** *(Defect.)*
   - **What's missing:** There's no axis and no unit. Only Tuesday has a value (1,140), and Sunday is a blank slot.
   - **What the viewer can't tell:**
     - Is 1,140 boxes or deliveries?
     - Is it a total for the month or an average per weekday?
     - How big are the other days? They have to be guessed from bar heights against an unlabeled Tuesday.
     - Does an empty Sunday mean "we don't deliver on Sundays" or "no data"?
   - **Fix:** Put a value on each bar, or add a value axis. Name the measure and the period in the title or a subtitle. Make Sunday say which case it is, for example "no deliveries" as a label or a zero mark. Tufte and Few both require that the reader never has to guess what a number measures.

**Smaller things**
- **Points don't match the title:** "Boxes packed per day" plots about 11 points across 30 days, so it's either sampled every few days or mislabeled. Plot all 30 days or rename the chart.
- **"Packed" vs "delivered":** The chart says "packed", but the page and the first card say "delivered". If those are different counts, say why; if they're the same, use one word.
- **Legend instead of labels:** The line chart's November/October legend sits top right, away from the lines. Put "November" and "October" at the right ends of the lines and drop the legend (Tufte; Wilke).
- **Titles name topics, not findings (taste):** "Boxes packed per day" and "Deliveries by weekday" say what's there, not what happened. A subtitle stating the change against October would save the viewer the comparison.
- **Changes left to the viewer (taste):** The top cards show October's value but not the change, so the viewer does the subtraction. Paused subscriptions fell from 236 to 212, which is good news, and nothing says so. A signed change, with a clear sign for which direction is good, would let the row be scanned.
- **Date range has no control:** "1 to 30 November 2026" looks like plain text. On a dashboard that's checked month to month, a visible period control is the obvious shortcut (Nielsen, heuristic 7). If that text is already a control, make it look like one.
- **Icons don't match their items (common sense):** Boxes uses a shopping cart, Growers a document, and Settings a sun or target shape rather than a gear. Pick icons that say the item, or the labels have to do all the work.

**What's working**
- **The top row is built right:** Four even cards, label then large figure then October value. The scale is clear and quiet (roughly 12 / 14 / 24 px), every number has a period and a comparison, and it all fits on one screen. Keep this pattern.
- **The line chart shows the restraint the other chart lacks:** One blue for this month, gray for context, a zero baseline, light gridlines. Copy its color logic over to the weekday chart.
- **The table is set well:** Numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures, the rules are thin, and the column headings are plain words. "Dozen free-range eggs" and "Hollow Lane Hens" read in the operator's own terms.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the date range or the chart cards can be clicked, and what hovering over a bar or line shows. That would fix much of finding 2.
- Whether 30 November is real data or the capture was taken mid-month. If the month isn't over, the last stretch of the line is unmarked partial data.
