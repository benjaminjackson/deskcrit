**First read.** My eye lands on the headline "Let the harbour book its own berths", then moves right to the purple-blue S4 tile in the berth plan, then back down to the matching purple-blue "Set up your harbour" button. Within a couple of seconds it reads as booking software for small marinas and harbour offices. It feels calm and practical, with sage, sand and charcoal colors that suit a waterfront. The purple-blue gradient is the one thing that doesn't fit that mood.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the hero of a marketing homepage. It should get harbour masters and marina offices to see that Moorly replaces their berth-booking admin, and then either set up their harbour or open the sample.

**Verdict.** It does its job well. The headline, the product picture and the two actions say what it is and who it's for, and the copy uses the audience's own words. Two things hold it back. First, the berth plan, which is the proof on the page, uses green for the berths that are taken. Most people will read green as "free." Second, the stock purple-to-blue gradient on the button and S4 makes the page look like any other template.

**What would move it most**
1. **Green means "taken" in the berth plan, and there's no key.** In the North Pontoon panel, the 15 sage-green tiles are the occupied berths. The free ones (N3, S6, W5) are white with a faint dashed edge. On seat maps and booking screens, a colored tile usually means you can pick it, and green usually means available or OK (Apple HIG and Material Design on status colors). Many viewers will see a plan that's mostly free. They only get it right if they connect "18 berths, 3 free" to the three white tiles. The tiles are what a boat owner taps, so this misreading carries into the real product if it's built the same way. **Defect.** Fix: make occupied berths a quiet neutral and give free berths the color or the stronger outline, so "free" is the thing that stands out. Also add a two-item key next to "18 berths, 3 free." While you're in there, make the free tiles' edges clearly visible. Right now they're white on a pale sand panel with faint beige dashes, and they look under the 3:1 contrast that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for on the edges of something you can tap.
2. **The purple-to-blue gradient on the primary button and on S4.** This is the stock fill of AI-made templates (Sokolovsky, unslop). Viewers have seen it on countless products, so the most clickable thing on the page looks like anyone's. It's also the only saturated color on a page that's otherwise sage, sand and charcoal, so it fights the mood. Pairing the button with the booked berth is a smart idea: "this is yours." The color carrying that idea is the problem. **Template tell.** Fix: use one solid accent that belongs to this palette, such as a deep harbour blue or a strong version of the sage, on both the button and the selected berth. That keeps the link between them and drops the gradient.

**Smaller things.**
- The hero paragraph ends with "where." alone on its last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Change the measure slightly or rebreak it so at least two words wrap together.
- "Season fees in one run": the heading has no verb, and "in one run" asks the reader to work out what happens. The text below explains it ("Send every berth holder the new season fee on the same day"). Pulling that verb into the heading, along the lines of "Send season fees in one run", would make the claim land without the second line.

**What's working.**
- The product picture is specific and believable: named pontoons, a "Walkway to the slipway" strip, "Berth S4, up to 9 m", "Booked for the 2027 season", "$380 paid." It shows the product in the harbour's own terms instead of a generic dashboard, and it's the strongest proof on the page. Keep this level of detail.
- "Open a sample pontoon" is a good secondary action. It's clearly styled lower than the primary button, and it lets the viewer judge the product by trying it instead of taking the claim on trust.
- The copy throughout speaks the audience's language (berth holder, pontoon, slipway, "the harbour master can print"). "A plan the harbour master can print… for the office wall" ties the product to something the office already uses, and that kind of comparison sells.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the berth tiles in the sample respond to hover or tap and show their state, and whether the gradient button has a visible focus ring.
