**First read.** My eye goes to the headline first, then across to the field map, where the dark green M4 pitch pulls hard. It then drops to the "Pitch M4… $20 deposit paid" line and back to the green button. It reads as booking software for small campsites and farm sites, made for the owner. It feels calm and outdoorsy. The soft green and yellow haze behind the headline and the map is the one part that feels like a general tech startup page rather than a farm field.

**What it's for.** No brief came with it, so I'm assuming this is the first screen of a landing page. It should get small campsite owners to sign up ("Add your campsite") or at least look at a sample map.

**Verdict.** It works. The headline, the product picture and the main button all make one specific promise, and the order of importance is clear. What holds it back most is the blurred color washes, a stock effect that makes a very specific product look like any other software page. After that, the copy never compares the product with what the owner uses today.

**What would move it most**
1. **Blurred color washes behind the hero (template tell).** There's a green glow behind the headline, spreading to the top-left corner, and a yellow glow behind the map card. Viewers have seen this effect on countless software pages, so the first impression is "another software startup" instead of "a tool for my field." It also competes a little with the map card, the one image here that really belongs to this product. Fix: use a plain background and let the field map carry all the color and character (unslop, "Color washes and blurred blobs behind content"). If you want a sense of place, it should come from this product, not from a gradient.
2. **The pitch never sets the product against what the owner does now (copy).** "Keeps your field map, arrivals, and deposits together" doesn't say together compared with what. "See a sample field map" is a good second action because it lets owners judge the product for themselves. But they judge it in isolation, not against their current booking diary, phone calls, or spreadsheet. A comparison with the system they already trust would let them see the value on their own terms. You need to name that current way of working (only you know what your audience actually uses). The headline already hints at the pain ("while you are out on the field"), and the subhead is the place to finish that comparison.
3. **"Deposits that come back" reads two ways, and it's about money (copy, defect).** It's the heading of the middle feature column. Read cold, "come back" could mean the deposit is safely refunded to the camper. It could also mean money that bounces or leaves the owner. Lines about money are the ones owners hold you to, so the wrong reading ("this tool sends my deposits back") hurts. The body text under it is clear ("Refund a deposit in one step when a camper cancels in good time"). A heading that names the action ("Refund a deposit in one step") would remove the doubt.

**Smaller things.**
- In the map, the free pitches (L3, M6, H5) are white boxes with dashed edges, and nothing labels them. Dashed outlines often read as empty placeholders rather than "free to book." The "3 free" count lets a careful viewer work it out. A tiny key, or a word inside the free pitches, would make it instant. (Taste.)

**What's working.**
- The product picture is specific to this business: "Hollow Farm, lower field", "Track to the wash block", "hardstanding", "Arriving Friday, two nights". The map, the selected pitch and the booking summary make one idea together with the headline. Keep the details this concrete.
- The order of importance is clean. There's one dark headline, one filled green button, and a secondary action styled as a plain underlined link. The only strong green on the map (M4) ties to the booking line beneath it.
- The feature row is plain text with the owner's real tasks ("whoever is on the gate after dark"). There are no emoji cards and no icon soup, and it lines up with the hero's left and right edges.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus and hover states on the nav, the button and the link. Also whether the map is interactive and stays legible on a phone, where six pitches per row will get tight.
