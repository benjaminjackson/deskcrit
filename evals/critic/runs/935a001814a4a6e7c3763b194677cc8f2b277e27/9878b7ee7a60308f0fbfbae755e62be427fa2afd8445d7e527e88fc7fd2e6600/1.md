**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, then goes right to the one dark green tile (M4) in the field map, then down to "$20 deposit paid." Within about three seconds it reads as booking software for small campsite owners, and it shows the product instead of describing it. It feels calm, practical, and rural without being twee.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage hero for a booking tool sold to people who run small campsites. Its job is to get them to add their site, or at least open the sample map.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clean, the copy is specific, and the product panel is the best thing on the page. Two things undercut it. The map's colors work against the convention for availability maps. The copy mixes British campsite words with a dollar sign, so part of the audience will feel the page isn't written for them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The map uses green for the wrong state.** *Defect, with a convention behind it.* In the field map, booked pitches are pale green and free ones (L3, M6, H5) are white with a faint dashed edge. On most seat and pitch maps a viewer has used, the colored tile is the one you can take, and green also reads as "available" (Apple HIG and Material treat green as the success or go color). So the first glance says "15 open, 3 blocked," which is the reverse of the truth. The small gray "18 pitches, 3 free" fixes it, but only for someone who reads it and then reconciles it with the map. For an owner, the free pitches are the point of the screen, and here they are the palest, quietest tiles on it. Direction: make free pitches the tiles that stand out, and push booked ones toward a neutral. Or add a two-item key. Either way, the open tiles should be the first thing the eye counts.
2. **The copy mixes two countries.** *Defect.* The panel says "pitch," "hardstanding," "wash block," and "Hollow Farm," all British campsite terms. The same panel says "$20 deposit paid." A UK owner sees the dollar sign and wonders whether the product handles their currency. A US owner meets "pitch" and "hardstanding" as foreign words, and "Pitchbook" itself leans British (Nielsen heuristic 2: use the audience's own words). Since the panel is your proof, a mismatch there makes the whole demo look made up. Direction: pick the market this page is for and make the vocabulary and the currency agree. If you sell in both, the panel should show one consistent site.
3. **The ask is specific, but nothing lets the owner judge the value.** *Taste.* "Add your campsite" is a good, concrete primary action, and "See a sample field map" is a good low-commitment second step. But nothing sets Pitchbook against what the owner does now, whether that's a diary, a phone, a spreadsheet, or a whiteboard at the gate. The page asks them to take "keeps your field map, arrivals, and deposits together" on trust. Direction: one line or detail that sets the panel against their current way of doing this would let them see the gain on their own terms. That fact has to come from you.

**Smaller things.**
- The third feature heading, "Deposits that come back," is written from the camper's side. To an owner, money "coming back" sounds like money leaving. The body ("Refund a deposit in one step") is clearer than its heading. An edit that names the owner's action would fix it.
- The panel's left edge (about 652 px) lines up with nothing in the three-column row below (columns start at about 80, 469, and 859), so the two halves of the page feel slightly placed by eye (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Tie the panel to one of the lower column lines, or to an exact half.
- "while you are out on the field" sounds like a sports idiom more than "out in the field." If the pun on fields is the point, that's fine. Otherwise "in the field" reads more naturally.

**What's working.**
- **The product panel is the hero image, and it's specific.** It has a named farm, a "Track to the wash block" strip, a selected pitch tied to a booking line ("Arriving Friday, two nights"), and a paid deposit. It shows the job instead of decorating the page. Keep this level of real detail when you revise.
- **The hierarchy is clean.** There's one big headline, then a short body, then one solid primary button with a plain underlined link as the secondary action. The eye never has to choose.
- **The feature row is restrained.** It has three columns with plain headings and one-line bodies, no icons, and no cards. Each line names a concrete moment from the owner's day ("whoever is on the gate after dark").

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on "Add your campsite," on the link, and in the nav.
- Whether the free-tile dashed borders and the gray meta text ("18 pitches, 3 free," "Arriving Friday, two nights") reach contrast. They look borderline, so measure them in a browser.
- How the split hero stacks on a phone.
