**First read.** The eye goes first to the one saturated mark on the page, the blue Friday bar at the right. Then it drops back to the row of four large numbers at the top, and then to the blue line in the left chart. It reads at once as a month-end dashboard for a small hostel's manager. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also like a standard admin template with the hostel's numbers poured in.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner or manager of Fellside Hostel checking how November went against October and deciding what to act on.

**Verdict.** It's clean and mostly honest, and a manager can read it, but it doesn't tell them anything. Nothing on the page says what changed or what to do about it. The only emphasis goes to a secondary fact, Friday check-ins. The real news, more beds sold but a smaller share of beds filled, sits in small gray text that looks just like the good news. Some labels and figures also don't line up with each other, which costs the reader's trust.

**What would move it most**

1. **The emphasis is on the wrong thing.** The four KPI numbers and the "Overview" title are all about the same size and weight, so none of them leads. The only strong color on the page is the Friday bar, so that's where the eye goes first. Meanwhile "Down 3 points on October" under Beds filled is set exactly like "Up 9% on October." A manager skimming the page sees four calm numbers and a blue bar, and misses the one figure that went the wrong way. Tschichold's rule applies: show the order of importance through differences in size and strength. Fix direction: decide what this screen's headline is. For a hostel that's probably occupancy against last month. Make it clearly dominant, and give the deltas a treatment that separates better from worse. Use more than color, so it still reads without color vision. Then decide whether the Friday highlight earns the page's only accent. *Defect.*

2. **The labels and figures don't add up, so the reader starts doubting the data.** In "Rooms most booked," Skiddaw dorm shows 212 "Nights" in a 30-night month. That only works if "Nights" means bed-nights, and the column header doesn't say so. The same number, 212, is also the Friday check-in count, which looks like a copy-paste mistake even if it isn't. "Beds sold" (2,846) and "Beds filled" (74%) sound like the same measure, so the reader has to work out why one went up while the other went down. The answer is presumably that capacity changed, but nothing says so. Also, the "Beds sold per night" line looks like it averages roughly 70 to 75 beds a night. Over 30 nights that's about 2,200, not 2,846. Check that the chart and the KPI come from the same source and the same definition. Fix direction: name each unit precisely ("bed-nights" or "room-nights"), add the missing reference point (capacity, or what "filled" is measured against), and reconcile the chart with the headline number. These are the figures a manager will quote to staff or partners, so a label that can be read two ways is a defect, not a style note (Nielsen heuristic 2: use the audience's own terms, in a natural order). *Defect.*

3. **The charts show data but never say what they found.** "Beds sold per night" and "Check-ins by weekday" are topic titles. The reader has to work out the point themselves: November ran above October all month, and Friday is the peak. The weekday chart has no value axis, and only Friday is labeled (212), so you can't read Saturday's or Sunday's value. The line chart puts the series names in a legend in the top-right corner instead of at the ends of the lines (Tufte; Wilke). Fix direction: have each title state the finding, using only facts the chart already shows. Label the two lines at their right ends and drop the legend. Either label every bar or add a light axis. Titles that state the finding are my judgment rather than a cited rule: on a monthly review screen, the finding is the reason the chart is there. *Convention (labels and legend); taste (titles).*

4. **A sparkline on every stat card.** All four KPI cards have a tiny gray sparkline with no scale or dates. The one under "Average stay" is nearly flat and tells you nothing. This is a stock dashboard-template move: viewers have seen it on countless products, so this screen looks like anyone's, and the lines add noise next to numbers that already have their comparison written underneath. Fix direction: remove them, or keep one only where its trend says something the delta line doesn't, and give it enough scale to read. *Template tell.*

**Smaller things.**
- The same blue means "November" in the line chart and "peak day" in the bar chart. Give one color one job across the screen.
- The gutter between the two chart cards (about x 891–908) doesn't line up with any divider in the KPI row (about x 757 and 1002). The table's Sleeps column sits a little short of the 757 divider too. Put the middle row on the same columns as the top row.
- The Bookings nav item uses a shopping-cart icon, which reads as "shop" rather than "reservations." Pick an icon that matches how hostel staff think about bookings. *Taste.*
- The property name, "Fellside Hostel, Keswick," only appears in small type at the bottom-left of the sidebar. If anyone manages more than one property, it belongs near the "Overview" title.

**What's working.**
- The restraint: one neutral range, one blue, plenty of space, and no gradients, glows or badges. Keep this when you add emphasis. Add one strong signal, not several.
- Every KPI comes with a plain-English comparison ("Up 9% on October"). That's the right habit; it just needs to show which direction is good or bad.
- The table is set properly: numbers right-aligned in even columns, quiet row lines, clear headers.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a control that changes the period. A manager will expect to switch months, and nothing in the still shows it can be clicked.
