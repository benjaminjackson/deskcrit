**First read.** The eye lands first on the four big numbers across the top. Next it goes to the one blue bar (Friday), then the line chart, then the table. It reads at once as a month-end operations dashboard for a small hostel. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy. That makes the problems below costly, because the numbers inside it don't agree with each other.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a hostel manager checking how November went against October, at a glance, on a desktop.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job well. The thing most in the way is that the numbers on screen contradict each other, so a manager who looks twice can't trust any of them. After that, the page shows numbers but never says what they mean.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline number and the chart below it disagree (defect).** The first card says **2,846 beds sold, up 9% on October**. The "Beds sold per night" chart says something else:
   - The November line runs from about 53 to about 93 a night, which averages around 70–75. Over 30 nights that's roughly 2,200, not 2,846. 2,846 would need about 95 every night, which is the line's highest point.
   - The November line sits about 25–35% above October on every night shown, not 9%.
   - The chart is titled "per night", but the line has only about 11 points across 30 nights, so it's plotting something other than nightly values.

   A manager who checks one against the other will stop trusting both, and a dashboard's whole value is that its figures can be trusted. Tufte's integrity principles (*Visual Display*, ch. 2) ask that what a chart shows matches the data. Here the chart and the headline show two different sizes of change.

   Fix: make the card and the chart come from the same data. If the chart really shows a sample or an average, say so in its title or axis.

2. **"Nights" in the table can't be read, and the ranking hides the answer (defect).**
   - Skiddaw dorm shows **212 nights** in a 30-night month. That only works if it means bed-nights, not room-nights. But the column just says "Nights", next to "Sleeps 8 beds".
   - Read either way, the revenue per night doesn't hold together: the dorm and the twin both come out at $90, and the family room at $180.
   - Ranking by bed-nights means the 8-bed dorm wins almost by default. Meanwhile the Revenue column is out of order: Catbells, in third place, made more than Derwent, in second.

   The viewer will rebuild a meaning for "Nights", and may get the wrong one. Tufte and Few: never make the reader guess what a number measures.

   Fix: put the unit in the header (the maker knows whether it's room-nights or bed-nights). Then rank by the measure the manager actually cares about, or say in the title which measure the ranking uses.

3. **The charts show data but never state the finding (taste, argued).**
   - "Beds sold per night" and "Check-ins by weekday" are topic labels.
   - The blue Friday bar with "212" on it is the one place the page points at something, and even there it doesn't say why Friday matters.
   - The four delta lines ("Up 9%", "Down 3 points", "Same as October") are all the same small gray type with no sign or arrow. The one bad number, beds filled down 3 points, looks exactly like the good ones, so you have to read every line to find it.

   Fix: give each chart title the takeaway that its own data supports. Give the deltas a direction mark so a drop is visible at a glance. Don't rely on color alone for this; a sign or an arrow does the job.

**Smaller things.**
- Line chart legend, top right: label "November" and "October" at the ends of the lines instead, so the eye doesn't travel back and forth (Tufte; Wilke).
- Check-ins bar chart: there's no y-axis, and only Friday has a value, so the other six days can only be compared by bar height. Label each bar or add a light axis (Tufte; Few).
- Check-ins by weekday: November 2026 starts on a Sunday, so it has five Sundays and five Mondays but only four of each other weekday. Monthly totals push Sunday and Monday up by about a quarter. Plotting the average per day removes that bias (common sense).
- Line chart x-axis: the ticks 1, 10, 19, 30 Nov are unevenly spaced (9, 9, 11 days). Use even intervals (Cairo; Tufte on consistent scales).
- "Overview" and the KPI numbers look about the same size (around 24 px). The page title competes with the figures. Make the title clearly smaller or clearly larger so the two levels separate.
- "1 to 30 November 2026", top right: if this is how the manager changes the period, it's plain text and doesn't look clickable. If it's only a label, it's fine.
- Blue means "November" in the line chart and "busiest day" in the bar chart. That's mild, but one accent doing two jobs side by side blurs both.

**What's working.**
- **Every headline number comes with a comparison to the previous period.** That's exactly what makes a KPI readable; keep it, and just add a direction mark.
- **Gray for context, one blue for the subject**, in both charts. October in gray and the other weekdays in gray lets the eye go straight to the data that matters. It's restrained, and you can see it was done on purpose.
- **A calm structure that fits on one screen**: one KPI strip, two charts, one table, on a clear grid with even gutters. Everything key is visible without scrolling, and nothing is decorative.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the date range opens a period picker.
- Hover values on the charts. Exact values on hover would ease, but not fix, the missing labels on the bar chart.
