**First read.** The eye goes first to the row of four large numbers, then to the one blue bar (Monday), then to the line chart, and last to the table. It reads as a monthly overview for a small support team (Kettlebrook, the support tool for Harrow & Pine). It feels calm, plain and competent, with no decoration. It looks like a report about the month, not a screen that tells you what to do today.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a support lead's monthly check: how the team did compared with last month, and what needs attention now.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the KPI row does its job well. The parts below it can't be fully trusted or read, though. The "open" ticket list includes a solved ticket, the line chart looks like it disagrees with the totals above it, and the weekday chart has only one value you can read. On a dashboard, trust in the numbers is the main thing, and that is what's at risk here.

**What would move it most**
1. **The "Oldest open tickets" table lists a ticket marked Solved.** It's the third row, "Refund for a returned kettle" (June Sato, Solved, 3 h). The heading promises open tickets and the row breaks that promise. This is the only part of the screen a lead would act on, so if the list is wrong they have to stop trusting it and check the inbox themselves. "Waiting 3 h" on a solved ticket can also be read two ways: still waiting, or took 3 h to solve. Defect. Fix: make the list hold only open tickets, or rename it to say what it really holds. Also say what "Waiting" measures, for example time since the customer's last message. Three rows with no route to the rest of the list is thin for the one actionable panel, too. Showing how many open tickets there are in total would tell the lead whether these three are the whole problem or the tip of it.
2. **The line chart doesn't look like it adds up to the totals above it.** "Tickets opened per day" plots about 12 points over 30 days, so it isn't per day as the title says. By eye, the November line averages about 37 a day, which comes to about 1,100 for the month against the 1,284 shown. The October line averages about 28 a day, about 870 against "1,192 in October". When a chart and the headline number next to it seem to disagree, the viewer starts doubting both. Defect (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2, graphical integrity). Fix: check the data. Either plot every day, or rename the chart to match what's plotted (for example a rolling average), so the area under each line matches its total.
3. **The weekday bar chart gives one number and leaves the rest to guesswork.** Only Monday has a value (262). There's no axis, so Tuesday through Sunday can only be read as "a bit less" or "much less". The blue highlight says Monday is the busy day, and that is the point. But if a lead wants to staff by weekday, they need the other numbers too, and they can't get them. Defect, and it breaks a convention: every chart needs values or units the reader doesn't have to guess (Tufte, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). Fix: label every bar with its value, keeping Monday as the highlighted one, or add a light axis. Also say what the bars count: totals per weekday across the month, or an average per weekday? With five Mondays in November 2026, the two give different answers.

**Smaller things.**
- Neither chart title says what the chart shows (taste / common sense). "Tickets opened per day" and "Tickets by weekday" only name the topic. A title that says what the viewer should take away saves them working it out: the climb above October on the line chart, and the Monday peak on the bars.
- The line chart's legend (top right) makes you look back and forth between the key and the lines. Put "November" and "October" at the right end of each line instead (Tufte; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*).
- The line chart's y-axis has no unit. Add "tickets" once, or let the corrected title carry it.
- The blocks don't share one grid. The KPI dividers sit at about x 511, 756 and 1002. The two chart cards split at about 891/908, which matches none of them, and the table's Status column at about 889 just misses the chart edge at about 891. Put the chart split on the KPI columns (for example three columns and one) and line the table columns up with them (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).
- In the table, the Status column is set a size smaller than the other cells, so the row has two sizes for one level of data. Use the same size as the other cells, and use color or weight if status needs to stand out (Bringhurst, 3.1.1).

**What's working.**
- Every KPI tile shows last month's number under the headline figure ("55 min in October", "1,140 in October"). That gives each number something to compare against without sparklines or colored arrows. Keep that pattern.
- Color is used once, and on purpose. Blue marks "this month" on the line and "the peak" on the bars, and everything else is gray. The one strong color goes to what's current or what stands out, and the screen never shouts.
- The labels are written in plain language: "Happy with the answer", "Waiting on customer", "Assigned to me". These are words a support person uses, not system terms.
