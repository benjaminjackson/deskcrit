**First read.** My eye goes to the row of four big numbers, then to the dark Saturday bar with "126" over it, then slides left across the line chart. It looks like the monthly sales view for a small fabric shop, built for the owner. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy: a quiet layout in gray and one blue. That trust is the thing to protect, and two of the three data panels put it at risk.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's monthly sales screen. It should let them see in a few seconds how November went against October, and which days and fabrics drove it.

**Verdict.** The layout and the stat cards are solid. The charts are where it falls short. The weekday chart makes a pattern that isn't in the data, and the "Daily orders" line doesn't match the screen's own totals. A careful owner will notice one of these and stop trusting the rest.

**What would move it most**
1. **The weekday chart compares unequal things and draws a false pattern.** Each bar looks like a total for the month. November 2026 starts on a Sunday, so it has five Sundays and five Mondays but only four of every other day. Sunday and Monday get about a quarter more days than the rest. By eye, Sunday's bar is about 111 orders over five days, around 22 a day. That's below Friday's roughly 93 over four days, around 23 a day, yet Sunday looks like the second-best day. The colors make it worse. The bars run from pale on Monday to dark on Sunday, which follows the calendar order, not the values. That reads as a steady build through the week, and Sunday, which is lower than Saturday, is drawn darkest. Wilke says lightness should follow the values; here it follows nothing. The ramp is also the "every chart in shades of the accent" template move (unslop), so no bar stands out by color. Then there's no value axis and only Saturday has a number, so Monday through Friday can't be read at all (Tufte; Few). **Defect.** Fix: plot the average per weekday, or say plainly that it's a total. Draw the bars in one neutral color with the blue only on the day that matters. Put values on every bar or add an axis.

2. **"Daily orders" isn't daily, and its numbers don't add up.** The November line has about 11 points across 30 days, so each point covers roughly three days. The x-axis ticks fall at 1, 10, 19 and 30 Nov, which are gaps of 9, 9 and 11 days (Tufte asks for even ticks). The line also sits around 20 to 35, averaging about 27, which gives roughly 800 orders for the month. But revenue divided by average order ($27,640 ÷ $46) is about 600, and the weekday bars add up to about 610 by eye. These are my readings off the pixels, not exact values, but the gap is too big for eyeballing to explain. If the line shows something other than orders, the title is wrong. If it shows orders, one of the figures is wrong. **Defect.** Fix: check the data first. Then plot every day or rename the chart to the period each point covers, and put ticks at even steps, such as weekly.

3. **The currency may be wrong.** The shop is "Bolt & Bobbin, Norwich" and the screen spells "Metres" the British way, yet every money figure uses "$". If the shop sells in pounds, all four money figures are wrong, and those are exactly the numbers an owner quotes to an accountant. If it really does sell in US dollars, this is fine. **Defect unless the dollars are intended.** Fix: show the currency the shop actually trades in.

4. **The screen gives figures but never says what they mean.** All four stats are up on October, but the owner has to work out each change by comparing "$27,640" with "$24,110". The chart titles only name the topic ("Daily orders", "Orders by weekday"), so nothing states the month's main result. **Taste**, because no approved source covers takeaway titles, but on an owner's monthly glance the main result is the whole point. Fix: show the change next to each stat, and let a chart title or caption state the finding the data supports.

**Smaller things.**
- The line chart uses a legend in the top right. Label "November" and "October" at the ends of the lines instead (Tufte; Wilke).
- "1 to 30 November 2026" looks like plain text. If it's how you change the period, it needs to look like a control. If it isn't, there's no visible way to look at another month (Nielsen 7).
- "Best-selling fabrics" can mean two things. The table is sorted by metres, but wool melton earns more than cotton lawn. Say "by metres" in the title or the column header.

**What's working.**
- Each stat pairs the number with last month's figure in plain words ("$24,110 in October"). That gives every headline number context without a chart, so keep it.
- The table is well set: numbers aligned right with aligned digits, light dividers, and real fabric and mill names. It reads like the shop's own records.
- The restraint: one neutral gray and one blue, a clear sidebar showing you're on Sales, and an even grid of cards. Once the chart colors are fixed, the blue can mark the one thing that matters on each chart.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states in the sidebar navigation, and whether the date range is interactive.
