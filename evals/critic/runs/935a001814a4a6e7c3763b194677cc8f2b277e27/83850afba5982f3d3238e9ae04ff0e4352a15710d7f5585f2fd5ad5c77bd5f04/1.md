**First read.** The eye lands on the "Plan and billing" title, then drops to the teal-outlined "Full gym" card, then to the teal "Save changes" button. It reads right away as the billing settings of a SaaS tool for climbing gyms, made for the gym's owner or front-desk manager. It feels calm and orderly, like a familiar admin screen. Nothing is shouting, and nothing is unusual either.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a gym owner can check or change their plan, keep billing details current, and find past invoices.

**Verdict.** This is a solid, restrained settings page and most of it does its job. The biggest problem is that changing the plan, the one action here that changes what the gym pays, has no clear commit point and says nothing about what happens when you switch.

**What would move it most**

1. **Nothing shows how or when a plan change takes effect.** The plan cards sit above a rule. "Save changes" sits under a different section, "Billing details." If someone clicks "Gym group," they can't tell whether the switch has already happened, whether Save applies it, or whether Cancel undoes it. The answer decides whether they just committed to $159 a month. The page also doesn't say when a switch starts (now or next cycle) or how the current month is billed. The radio button also does two jobs: it marks both "your current plan" and "what you've picked." Once someone clicks another card, nothing on screen still shows which plan they're actually on. This is a **defect**. The control isn't next to what it changes (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, on natural mapping), and the help this step needs isn't shown at the step (Nielsen, heuristic 10). Fix direction: give the plan section its own explicit commit, separate from the billing form's Save. Label the current plan in words on its card. Next to the commit, state the effective date and billing outcome. Only you know those facts.

2. **The plan cards can't be compared line by line.** "One wall, 200 members," "Unlimited members," and "Up to 5 gyms" each measure something different. A reader can't tell how many members "Gym group" allows, or whether "Full gym" covers one gym or more. The price, the line people decide on, is set in the same size and gray as the description, so the cards don't read as a comparison. This is a **defect** in the copy: the reader has to fill the gaps, and they may fill them wrong on something they'll pay for. Fix direction: give each card the same lines in the same order (gyms, members, price), using numbers only you can supply. Set the price apart from the description by weight or color.

3. **The invoices can't be opened.** "BS-2093" and the other invoice numbers look like plain text. Nothing offers a PDF or a receipt. For many visitors, especially whoever does the books, a copy of an invoice is the reason they came. This is **common sense** (no approved source): make each invoice visibly openable or downloadable from its row. If the rows already link, make that visible.

**Smaller things.**
- The section headings "Plan," "Billing details," and "Invoices" look only a pixel or two larger than the semibold card titles and field labels, and no heavier. They read as one more label, not as headings over a section. Make them clearly different from the text under them in weight or size (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8).
- No field is marked required or optional. Mark the required ones with an asterisk and explain it once, or add "optional" after the optional labels (Nielsen Norman Group, Budiu, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- The text-field borders look like a light gray, possibly under 3:1 against white. Measure them and darken them if they fall short (WCAG 1.4.11).

**What's working.**
- The teal accent is used in only three places: the selected card, the checkbox, and the primary button. That's why the eye goes straight to the selection and the action. Keep it that scarce.
- The invoice table is plain and correct. Amounts are right-aligned with matching decimals, dates use one format, and "Paid" is plain text, not a colored badge.
- Labels sit above every field, and the left sidebar marks the current page with a clear tint. Both are conventions people already know, so the page needs no learning.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** What happens when someone clicks a different plan card: whether it saves immediately, asks to confirm, or waits for Save. Check this live, because it decides how serious finding 1 is. Focus styles on the cards, fields, and buttons.
