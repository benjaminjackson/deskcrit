**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, then jumps right to the locker plan. The navy B4 tile pulls hardest there, and it links down to the "Locker B4, top row" line. Within a few seconds this reads as software for school offices that hand out lockers, keys, and deposits. It feels calm, plain, and believable, like a real tool rather than a pitch.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing page of a SaaS product, aimed at school office staff deciding whether to try it.

**Verdict.** This is a strong page. It says exactly what the product does and shows it working in a specific, believable picture. The biggest thing in the way is the locker plan: its green means "taken" with no key, so a viewer's first guess about what they're looking at is likely to be backwards.

**What would move it most**
1. **The locker plan's colors read backwards and have no key.** This is in the hero card, top right. The taken lockers are green and the free ones are white with a dashed edge. To most viewers green means available, so a first glance reads 15 green lockers as 15 free ones. Only the small gray "18 lockers, 3 free" lets you work out that it's the other way around. The dashed edges, which are the only cue besides color, look very pale against the off-white card. They probably fall below 3:1, so a quick look or a low-contrast screen loses them. Since this picture is the page's proof of what the product does, misreading it costs the most. It is a defect: it inverts the usual meaning of green (Apple HIG: Color; Material Design) and leaves the meaning resting mostly on color (WCAG 1.4.1). Fix direction: make taken lockers a neutral or muted fill and keep the open state as the one that stands out. Make the free outline clearly visible (measure it against 3:1, WCAG 1.4.11), and add a small inline key or mark the free tiles with a word.
2. **The asks don't let the viewer compare Lockerly with what they use now.** "Start with one corridor" is a good ask: it's specific, small, and low-risk. "See a sample corridor" only invites the viewer to inspect the product and take its value on trust. A school office already runs this job somehow, probably on a spreadsheet, a paper key list, or a deposit ledger. Nothing on the page sets Lockerly next to that, so the viewer has to imagine the gain. This is taste, argued on the grounds of what makes someone act. Fix direction: tie the secondary ask or a supporting line to the viewer's current method, such as a way to start from their existing list. Only the maker can say which method and whether the product supports it.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "…before the first / bell", which leaves "bell" alone on the third line and splits the phrase "the first bell" (Bringhurst 2.4.2 on short last-line words). Break it at the phrase instead, after "lockers", so it sets as "Hand out school lockers / before the first bell" in two even lines.
- The heading "Deposits back in July" can be read two ways: Lockerly or the school returns deposits in July, or deposits come back whenever the school year ends. The body text says "at the end of the year", and a date is something a viewer will hold the maker to. Make the heading and body say the same thing. If July isn't true for every customer's calendar, use the body's wording.

**What's working.**
- The hero picture is the product itself, not decoration. The plan has a real corridor name, a "Stairs to the labs" band, and a selected locker that opens into a pupil's year, key number, and deposit. That one card shows all three claims in the paragraph at once. Keep it specific like this.
- The copy speaks the audience's language and states a concrete job: corridor, key list, deposits, caretaker, "a lost key is a quick lookup". There's nothing generic in it. The headline names an outcome with a deadline the reader recognizes.
- The hierarchy is restrained and clear. There's one large headline, one navy primary button that matches the navy selected locker, a secondary action set as a plain underlined link, and three feature columns with no icons or emoji. It doesn't look like a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the locker plan responds to hover or click (selecting other lockers), and how the two-column hero reflows on a phone.
