**First read.** The eye goes straight to the headline, "Notes from every call, written before you hang up". It then crosses to the gray notes card on the right, which shows the promise kept, and comes back down to the navy button. Within about three seconds it reads as an AI meeting-notes tool for small teams. It feels calm, plain and believable, with none of the usual AI-product noise.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero of a marketing homepage, meant to get people who run regular video calls to connect Minutehand and try it.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, honest hero: the headline makes a specific promise and the sample card proves it. The biggest thing in the way is the call to action. "Add it to your calendar" doesn't say what happens when you click it, and it is the one line on the page that commits the viewer to anything.

**What would move it most**
1. **The primary button can be read two ways** (defect; WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.4, link purpose). The navy button under the paragraph says "Add it to your calendar".
   - One reading is "put an event or reminder in my calendar".
   - The other is "give Minutehand access to my calendar so it joins my calls".
   - The second is the real one, and it is a big step: it lets a bot into every meeting. A viewer who expects the first gets an access request they didn't sign up for, and many will back out right there. "It" also has to be traced back to Minutehand.

   Fix direction: put the real action in the button's words, naming the calendar connection as the thing that happens. The approval step also needs support right beside the button. The reassurance that the recording is deleted currently sits in the third column, 400 px away. Whether this is free or a trial is a fact only you have; if it is either, say it near the button.
2. **The second ask asks the viewer to grade a sample instead of judging against their own notes** (taste, argued). "Read a sample set of notes" offers more of what the card on the right already shows, so it adds little. It also only invites the viewer to rate your example. A stronger ask sets the product against something they already have and trust, such as the notes from their own last call or whatever their team does now. Then the value is judged on their terms, not yours. Keep the link if the full sample is much richer than the card. Either way, the direction is an ask that leads to a comparison with their own meetings. Which comparison fits is your call.
3. **The privacy heading takes two reads, and the first feature line drops its point** (defect, copy).
   - "Nothing kept you did not ask for" is the one line that answers "what happens to my recordings?", and its double-negative shape makes the viewer parse it. Reorder it so it says plainly what Minutehand keeps and deletes, as the sentence under it already does.
   - In column one, "someone who missed the call reads two lines and knows" stops before *what* they know. Restore the object (what was decided) so the line lands on the reader.

**Smaller things.**
- The hero card's left edge (~652 px) lines up with none of the three feature columns (80 / 469 / 859), so the two halves of the page sit on different grids. Either line the card up with a feature column or make the split clearly separate.
- The card's bottom edge sits about 8 px above the button's bottom edge. That reads as a near-miss: line them up or clearly offset them.
- Column two, "Tasks with a name and a day", ends on "who." alone on its last line. Rewrap the line or adjust the column width.
- The headline breaks "written before you / hang up", splitting the verb from its subject and leaving "hang up" alone. Breaking after "written" ("written / before you hang up") keeps the phrase whole, or narrow the measure so it falls that way.

**What's working.**
- **The notes card is the hero visual, and it's real content.** It uses a specific meeting, a changed date ("14 March, not 7 March"), and named people with weekdays. It proves the headline instead of decorating it. Keep it specific and typeset; don't swap it for a screenshot or an illustration.
- **The headline names an outcome the viewer wants in their own terms.** The rest of the copy is mostly concrete verbs: joins, writes down, sends, correct.
- **Restraint.** There is one navy accent, used only on the primary button, one sans-serif family, and a clear primary/secondary split between the button and the underlined link. The page avoids every stock template move, so it looks like this product and not like anyone's.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The hover and focus states of the button and the "Read a sample set of notes" link.
