**First read.** The eye goes to the big headline first, then jumps to the one dark-red seat, B4, in the plan, then to the red "Put on a show" button. It reads as a booking tool for village halls and amateur theaters. It feels calm, warm, and unfussy. The page reads clearly in a couple of seconds.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the marketing home page for a seat-booking product. Small community venues and amateur theater groups should understand it and start setting up their first show.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. It's restrained, the copy is concrete, and the layout belongs to this product. The biggest problem is the seat plan, the page's one picture: its colors say the opposite of what seat maps usually say, and it has no key. So the product demo is the part most likely to be misread.

**What would move it most**

1. **The seat-plan colors are backwards, and there's no key.** In the panel on the right, the 15 sage-green seats are the sold ones and the three white dashed seats (A3, B6, C5) are free. B4 is in the brand's dark red and is the buyer's own booked seat. On almost every booking map a viewer has used, green means available and red means taken. So the first read of this plan is "a mostly empty hall with one seat taken", which is the opposite of what it shows. Only the small "18 seats, 3 free" in the corner sorts it out, and only if the viewer counts. Nothing on the plan says what each color means, so the meaning rests on color alone, and that also fails color-blind viewers (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1). Reversing status colors breaks a published convention (Apple HIG, Material), and here it costs something real: the picture is the proof that this product makes booking easy. *Convention, plus a defect.* Fix: make sold seats a quiet neutral or muted tone so the free seats stand out. Keep the brand red for "your seat" only if a label shows it's yours. Either way, add a small key under the plan (sold, free, selected).

2. **The headline makes a promise the page never explains, and the picture doesn't back it.** "Sell every seat in the hall, not just the front row" suggests a specific problem: the back rows go unsold. But the body text is about drawing the hall, taking bookings, and stopping phone orders. Nothing says how Seatplan fills the back rows. The plan doesn't show it either: it's a nearly full hall with free seats in rows A, B, and C alike, so the front-row idea gets no help from it. The headline and the picture each make sense, but they don't form one idea, so the most memorable line on the page goes unsupported. *Taste.* Fix: either let the body or the plan show the front-row-versus-back-row point (for example, by where the free seats sit), or point the headline at what the page does show. Whatever explains why only the front row sells now has to come from you.

3. **The headline breaks onto a lone "row".** The third line is one short word, so the headline reads as three uneven pieces, and the punchline "front row" is split across lines (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). *Convention.* Fix: change the measure or the break so "the front row" stays together, ideally on two lines.

**Smaller things.**
- "Put on a show" sounds nice, but it doesn't say what happens when you click: an account, a setup screen, a cost? Since the next step is a commitment, say what it is, or add a short line under the button. The facts for that line are yours to supply.
- "Seat B4, row B" in the panel footer says the row twice. Cut it to "Seat B4".
- The plan mixes two points of view: a venue summary ("18 seats, 3 free") and one buyer's receipt ("$12 paid"). Decide whose screen this is so the viewer knows which side of the product they're looking at.

**What's working.**
- The body copy names real jobs in the audience's own words: "prints the door list", "the box office stops taking orders by phone". The features below do the same: concessions, a door list for each night, seats held for the cast's families. It sets the product against how these groups work today, by phone, and that comparison is persuasive. Keep this voice.
- The palette is tight: warm off-white, sage, and one dark red, with the red used only on the main button and the selected seat. That's why the eye goes where it should. Keep it to one or two uses.
- The hero is a real two-column layout with an actual product picture, not a centered template stack. It looks like a specific product for specific people.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the seat plan is interactive or animated, and whether the free seats' dashed borders are the only cue a keyboard or screen-reader user gets for "free".
