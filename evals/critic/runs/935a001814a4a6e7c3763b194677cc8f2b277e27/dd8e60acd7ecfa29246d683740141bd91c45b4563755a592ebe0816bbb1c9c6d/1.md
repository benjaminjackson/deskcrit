**First read.** The eye goes to "Overview," then across the four numbers in the top row ($9,460, 1,032, $9, 36%), then to the one blue bar (Saturday, 148). It reads as a calm, tidy monthly dashboard for a small bakery owner. It feels quiet and trustworthy. That trust is the problem: once you set the numbers side by side, they don't agree.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner of Halfmoon Bakery checks how November went against October and sees where the trade comes from: which days and which items.

**Verdict.** The layout and visual restraint are good. The piece still fails its job, because its own figures contradict each other, and an owner who notices will stop trusting every number on the screen. Fix the data and how it's labeled first. Everything else is polish.

**What would move it most**
1. **The charts contradict the headline numbers.** The Tickets card says 1,032 tickets for the month, which is about 34 a day. The "Daily tickets" line runs between about 350 and 710 on a 0–900 scale, which would mean 10,000+ for the month. The weekday bars, with Saturday at 148, add up to roughly 700, not 1,032. "Daily" is also wrong: the line has about 11 points across 30 days. An owner who does any of this math can't tell which number is real, so the page no longer answers "how did November go." This is a defect, and a data and labeling problem more than a styling one. The fix: make every chart come from the same series as the cards and add up to them. Then make each title say what each point is ("tickets per day" or "per 3 days"), so the line chart can be checked against the 1,032 total.
2. **The weekday chart doesn't say what its bars measure.** It has no axis, and only Saturday has a value. "148" has no unit, and nothing says whether it's the total for November's Saturdays or the average per Saturday. The owner can see that Saturday is busiest but can't say by how much, or compare a weekday to the daily line. This breaks a convention: every chart should name its units, so the reader never guesses what a number measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). The fix: say in the title whether it's a total or an average per day, and either label every bar or add a light axis.
3. **The comparisons make the owner do the math, and "Average ticket" hides its own change.** Each card puts October underneath ("$8,815 in October") but never shows the change, so the reader subtracts four times. "$9" over "$9 in October" says nothing moved. But $9,460 ÷ 1,032 is about $9.17 and $8,815 ÷ 987 is about $8.93, so the one card about spending per customer hides a real rise by rounding to whole dollars. This is a defect: it misleads on a figure the owner might base pricing on. The fix: show the change on each card (as an amount or a percentage, in the same format on all four), and show Average ticket to the cent.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Daily tickets" legend sits at the top right, away from the lines. Put "November" and "October" at the right ends of the two lines instead (Tufte).
- The x-axis ticks on Daily tickets are uneven (1, 10, 19, 30 Nov). Use even steps.
- The chart titles name a topic ("Tickets by weekday") rather than the finding. A title that says what Saturday's bar means would make the blue highlight make its point. This is common sense, not a cited rule.
- The Settings icon looks like a sun or crosshair, not a gear. Use the icon people expect for settings.

**What's working.**
- **Color restraint.** Everything is gray except one blue, which marks only "this month" and "the peak day," so the eye finds the important thing at once. Keep the gray-for-context approach when you fix the data.
- **The layout.** The cards in the top row, the charts, and the table share clean left and right edges and even gutters, and the page has no decoration. It reads in a few seconds.
- **The table.** Numbers are right-aligned, there are no heavy lines, and the columns are clear. Sold and Revenue can be compared at a glance (for example, oat flat white sells fewer than the bun but brings in more).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- "1 to 30 November 2026" at the top right looks like plain text. If it's the control for changing the period, check in the live app that it looks clickable.
