**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, and the orange-red gradient on "before Saturday" pulls hardest. From there it jumps to the rust F4 tile in the map, then down to the rust "Plan your next market" button. It reads straight away as a booking and layout tool for whoever runs a weekly street market. It feels calm, practical and grown-up, and the map makes the product concrete before you read a word of the body.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the landing-page hero of a web product for market managers, and its job is to make them see their own Saturday in the map and click "Plan your next market."

**Verdict.** This is a strong, specific page that mostly does its job. What gets in the way most is the map's color coding: green means "booked," rust-red marks a paid pitch, and there's no key. So the picture that should prove "fill every pitch" makes the viewer decode it, and it shows the free pitches, the point of the headline, as its quietest marks.

**What would move it most**
1. **The map's colors mean the opposite of what viewers expect, and nothing explains them.** (Defect, and a convention: WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1; Apple HIG on color.)
   - **Where:** the Canal Street Market panel.
   - **What the viewer meets:** booked pitches are sage green, which most people read as "available, go." The one pitch the panel picks out, F4, is filled in rust-red, which reads as "problem" or "taken." The three free pitches (E3, F6, G5) are white tiles with dashed borders so faint they almost vanish into the beige panel.
   - **Missing cue:** no legend or label says what each state means. Only the "3 free" count lets you work it out by counting.
   - **Cost:** the headline promises filling empty pitches, but the picture's loudest mark is a pitch already sold, and the empty ones are its quietest. The picture and headline pull apart. Image and headline making one idea is my judgment, not a cited rule.
   - **Fix:** recolor the states so booked recedes and free stands out. Mark a selected pitch some other way than a red fill, for example with an outline. Add a second cue beyond color: a small key or a word inside the free tiles.
2. **"Stops taking bookings by text" can be read two ways, and it talks about the viewer instead of to them.** (Defect, copy.)
   - **The line:** in the body paragraph, "the market manager stops taking bookings by text."
   - **Two readings:** "no longer has to field bookings over SMS" (meant) or "stops accepting bookings," which sounds like the market closes.
   - **Wrong person:** the paragraph opens with "your market layout," so the viewer is the manager, but this sentence switches to "the market manager" in the third person. The benefit is said about someone rather than to the viewer.
   - **Direction:** keep the second person and restore the meaning, e.g. "…and you stop taking bookings by text message." That's an edit of the existing words, not new copy.
3. **The gradient fill on "before Saturday" is a stock template move.** (Template tell: unslop, "Gradient text in headings.")
   - **Where:** the second line of the headline, orange-to-pink.
   - **Cost:** viewers have seen this treatment on a great many startup heroes, so the one generic thing on an otherwise very specific page makes it look like anyone's. It also adds a third warm red that competes with F4 and the button.
   - **Fix:** set the phrase in one solid color. The rust already used for the button would tie the deadline to the action. Or let weight alone carry the emphasis.

**Smaller things**
- **Price format:** "$32 paid" uses a dollar sign, but the rest of the copy is British usage: "pitch," "footway," "car park," "council." A UK manager will notice the wrong currency on the one price shown. Check which market this targets and match the currency to the vocabulary.
- **Type scale:** I count about six sizes. The 12 px pitch labels against 13 px captions, and the 15 px panel text against 16 px nav, are too close to read as separate levels (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Merge each pair and let weight and color do the fine work.

**What's working**
- **The product shown as a real, named, specific example:** Canal Street Market, the footway to the car park, Rye and Rind Bakery, "3 m with power." It shows the actual product instead of a stock picture, and lets a manager picture their own market at once. Keep that specificity when you revise the colors.
- **The feature row is written in the viewer's own world:** "nobody walks the rows with a cash bag" and "the layout and trader list it asks for each quarter" name real chores, with no emoji cards or stock icons.
- **A clear hierarchy and a single action:** one dominant headline, one solid primary button, and a plain underlined secondary link. The order of the page is never in doubt.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus and hover states on the button and nav links, and whether the map tiles are interactive (if they are, their state colors also need 3:1 contrast).
