**First read.** The eye lands on "Quote a bathroom from the doorway," moves down to the brown "Write your first quote" button, then crosses to the quote card on the right and runs down to the $1,731.00 total. Within a couple of seconds it reads as quoting software for tradespeople doing tiling jobs. It feels calm, warm and competent, like a well-made tool rather than a startup pitch.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero of a marketing landing page for self-employed tilers, and its job is to get them to start a first quote.

**Verdict.** It works. The purpose, the main point and the next step are all clear on the first screen, and the copy is specific to this trade. What's left is small. The biggest gap is that the product preview stops one step before the moment the page is promising.

**What would move it most**
1. **The preview stops at "opened," but the promise is "accepted."** The body copy ends on "the customer accepts it from the link," and the third column says "You get a text when they do." But the quote card ends at "Sent 14:12, opened 14:30" (bottom left of the card). The viewer sees the quote get sent and read, and has to take the payoff on trust. The timestamps are the best proof on the page because they show the speed instead of claiming it. Carry that one step further: show the quote in its accepted state, with whatever status and time the product really records, so the viewer can see what happens after it's opened. *Taste.* No published rule requires this, but the preview is the one place the page proves its claim instead of stating it.

**Smaller things**
- **Fake window controls:** the three gray dots on the quote card's header bar (top left of the card) are desktop window controls on a document the page says customers read "on a phone." They add nothing and slightly contradict the copy. Drop them, or frame the quote the way the customer actually sees it. *Taste.*
- **Third column repeats the hero:** "Accepted from a link" restates the last sentence of the hero paragraph. Either cut that clause from the hero or have the column add something the hero doesn't say. Here that's the text you get, so lead with it. *Editorial, common sense.*
- **"See a sample quote" doesn't say what it adds:** a sample quote is already on screen, so a viewer may wonder what the link shows. If it opens the customer's view, say so in the link text. *Copy, common sense.*

**What's working**
- **The headline and the preview make one idea.** "From the doorway" claims speed, and the "Sent 14:12, opened 14:30" line shows it, along with a real-looking job (Floor tiles 30 × 60 cm, 6.2 m², 3 days labour). Keep the preview this concrete. A generic dashboard would lose it.
- **The copy speaks the tiler's language and lands on them.** "so you do not run short on day two" and "Set your day rate and the tiles you buy most" say what the tiler gets, in their words. "Write your first quote" asks them to do the real task with their own numbers, which lets them judge the value for themselves instead of trusting a claim.
- **Restraint.** There's one warm accent, used only on the primary button. Type sizes are few and clearly stepped, with the headline as the obvious place to start. Prices sit right-aligned in tabular figures. The three features are plain title-and-text columns with no icons. Nothing on the page is fighting for attention.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover and focus states, and how the two-column hero collapses on a phone. The phone layout matters most here, because tilers will likely open this page on site.
