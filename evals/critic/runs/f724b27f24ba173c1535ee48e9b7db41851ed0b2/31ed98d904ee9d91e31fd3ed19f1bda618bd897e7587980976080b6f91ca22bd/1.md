**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title "River ferry timetable", then gets caught by the table's black outline. Every cell has its own heavy box, so before you read a single time you see the grid. It reads straight away as a public timetable for one river crossing. It feels plain and trustworthy, but more like a spreadsheet than a notice someone made for passengers.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a passenger timetable on the web or a printed notice. It should let a rider find the next sailing for their day at a glance.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The content is clear, the type is clean, and a table is the right form. But the full cage of black borders is the darkest thing on the page and drowns out the times. The rows are boxed and spaced so evenly that the useful distinctions (time of day, which days a boat runs) have to be read row by row.

**What would move it most**
1. **The borders outweigh the data** (convention, with a defect cost). Each of the 32 cells has a near-black outline about 2 px thick, and the outer frame is just as heavy. The lines are darker and bolder than the times, so the eye reads compartments before departures, and scanning down a column means crossing a hard line every row. Lupton (*Thinking with Type*, p. 157) says a table's rules "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell". Tufte calls this non-data ink. Fix: remove the vertical rules and the outer frame. Keep at most a thin pale hairline or a light tint on alternate rows, so the times are the darkest marks on the page.
2. **Service days and times of day are buried in equal rows** (defect). "Daily", "Weekdays" and "Weekends" in the last column all carry the same weight. A Saturday rider has to read all seven rows to learn that the 12:40 and 17:10 don't sail, and that the 19:30 runs only for them. The Period column repeats "Morning", "Midday" and "Afternoon" on separate boxed rows. Each group is split across boxes, and the space between rows is the same inside a group and between groups, so the repetition adds ink without grouping anything (Arnheim on proximity; common region, Nielsen Norman Group). Fix: make the exceptions stand out and drop the repetition. For example, show the period once as a group label, put more space between groups than within them, and set the non-daily entries apart in weight or with a short marker and a key.
3. **"Spring season" has no dates** (defect, copy). The subtitle "North landing to market quay, spring season" doesn't tell a stranger whether today counts as spring. Passengers act on dates and will hold the operator to them. Read one way, it means "valid now". Read the other way, someone turns up for a sailing that no longer runs. Fix: state the exact date range, e.g. "Valid 1 March to 31 May". It would also help to name the return direction, or say where to find it.

**Smaller things.**
- The column headings "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are clipped. "Leaves North landing" and "Arrives Market quay" match the subtitle and remove any doubt about which stop is meant (common sense).
- Every crossing takes 12 minutes. Saying "Crossing takes 12 minutes" once would let the Arrives column go quieter, or let readers trust it without checking (taste).

**What's working.**
- A table is the right form for looking up exact times (Few; Tufte). Keep it as a table, not a graphic.
- The type is restrained. There's one sans family, one bold title, a grey subtitle and bold column headings, with clear steps between levels and no stray sizes. The 24-hour times line up cleanly down their columns.
- The margins are generous and the layout is calm. Nothing crowds the page edge.
