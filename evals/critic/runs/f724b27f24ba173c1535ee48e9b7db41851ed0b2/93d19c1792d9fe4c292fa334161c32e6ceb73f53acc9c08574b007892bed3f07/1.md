**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Save changes to the shared calendar?" and reads it easily. Then it drops onto a row of four identical blue buttons and stops, because nothing says which one is the answer. It reads as a confirmation dialog in a calendar or scheduling app, probably a community or volunteer group. It feels clean and calm until the moment you have to choose.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since nothing was given: a confirmation dialog where a calendar editor decides whether to publish rehearsal-time changes to everyone who follows the calendar. The point is to make the right choice quick and the wrong one hard to hit.

**Verdict.** It does not do its job. The question is clear, but the four answers look exactly alike. That includes the one that throws away the user's work, so the user has to read every label to find "Save" and can lose their edits with one misplaced click.

**What would move it most**
1. **Four primary buttons, so there is no answer.** *Defect.* "Export as file", "Discard changes", "Keep editing" and "Save changes" are the same filled blue at the same size. The dialog asks a yes/no question but gives four equal choices, and the eye has nowhere to go after the headline. Users scan the row, read all four labels, and use position to guess. Material Design and Apple HIG both call for one dominant action per view, with secondary actions in outline or text style and cancel weakest. Two equal filled buttons already break that rule, and this has four. The fix: make "Save changes" the only filled button and keep it at the far right, where convention puts the confirming action. Make "Keep editing" a quiet secondary next to it. The other two drop lower still or move, as in the next two findings.
2. **"Discard changes" looks and sits exactly like the safe actions.** *Defect.* It is second from the left, between Export and Keep editing, in the same blue as Save. Discarding throws away the edits with nothing visible to undo it, and here it looks no different from saving. A user moving quickly, or one who expects "Cancel" in that slot, can lose their work. Apple HIG and Material both say destructive actions should be set apart from safe ones and styled as destructive. They also say a destructive action should not be the default; Raskin argues for undo over warnings. The fix: give Discard a destructive style (red text or outline), and move it away from Save and Keep editing, for example to the far left on its own with clear space before the other buttons.
3. **"Export as file" doesn't belong in this decision.** *Defect.* The question is "publish these changes or not?" Exporting is a different task, and it doubles the choices the user has to weigh. It is also first in reading order, so it is the first label everyone reads. Nielsen on progressive disclosure: put rare options behind a control and show the main content first. The fix: take it out of the dialog and put it in the editor's menu. If it must stay, make it a text link set apart from the decision buttons.

**What's working.**

- **The body line says who is affected.** "Shown to everyone who follows this calendar" tells the user the save is public, which is exactly what a confirmation should do. Keep it.
- **Strong headline, quiet body.** The headline is large and bold, the body is regular weight, and the card has generous, even padding. The type hierarchy is sound; only the buttons are wrong.
