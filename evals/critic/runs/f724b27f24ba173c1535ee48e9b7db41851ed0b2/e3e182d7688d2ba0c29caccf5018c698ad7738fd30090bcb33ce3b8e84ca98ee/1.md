**First read.** My eye goes straight to the headline, "Booking links that fill your calendar", then down to the blue "Create your link" button. Then it lands on the four cards, and stops on the blue "New" pill in the first card, because it is the only saturated thing in the row. It reads as a clean, competent scheduling-tool homepage that looks like a template. The right half of the hero is empty, so the page looks like it is waiting for a product picture that never arrived.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the home page of a SaaS scheduling tool. Its job is to get small appointment-based businesses (the customer row lists a dental practice, a tutoring service, physio, coaching and vets) to create a booking link.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job: it's clear, legible, and has one obvious action. What holds it back is that the hero tells and never shows. Half the hero is blank and the copy could sit on any scheduling product, so a visitor can't see what Slotwise looks like or why it's for them rather than for any other booking tool.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero's right half is empty, and the words are generic.** Defect, plus taste on the copy.
   - **The empty half.** From x≈680 to the right edge, and from the nav down to the cards, there is nothing. All the weight sits on the left, so the page tips (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). This isn't open space set against dense space; it's space left over. The cost to the viewer: they never see a booking page, a time picker, or a calendar filling up. The one thing that would let them judge the product is hidden behind "See a live example".
   - **The copy.** "Booking links that fill your calendar" and "keep every meeting in the right place" could sit on any competitor's page. Read literally, a link doesn't fill a calendar; clients do. The real audience is visible only in the small grey names at the bottom, and the headline never speaks to them.
   - **Direction.** Put the product in the empty half: a real booking page as a guest sees it, ideally for one of those businesses. Then the image shows the mechanism and the headline can state the result. Aim the headline at what this viewer loses today, such as phone tag, no-shows, or double-booked rooms, in "you" terms.
   - **The asks.** "See a live example" is your stronger ask, because it lets the viewer judge for themselves. Consider giving it more presence, or folding it into the hero image itself.

2. **The first feature card breaks the pattern the other three set.** Defect and convention.
   - **The layout.** In "Shared calendars", the right-aligned "New" pill takes up space, so the title wraps onto two lines and splits a two-word name. That pushes the description and "Learn more" about 30 px below their neighbours. The row no longer lines up across the cards (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*, lines align across columns), and the near-miss looks like a bug rather than a choice (Arnheim).
   - **The pills.** "New" is filled in the primary-button blue, while "Auto", "Email" and "Beta" are grey. So the one pill that is only status pulls more attention than anything on the page except the main button, and it looks clickable. The pills also mix two jobs: status ("New", "Beta") and feature descriptors ("Auto", "Email"). "Email" next to "Reminders" tells the viewer little.
   - **Direction.** Give every card the same structure, with the pill placed so the title never wraps. Give all the pills one style, keep only the status pills, and fold "Auto" and "Email" into the descriptions (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: one programme for elements of one kind).

**Smaller things.**
- Two filled blue primary buttons ("Start free" in the header, "Create your link" in the hero) offer what looks like the same action under two names. Make the header one a lower style, or use the same wording for both (Material Design, button emphasis).
- The four "Learn more" links read identically out of context. Name the destination, for example "How shared calendars work" (WCAG 2.4.4).
- The headline leaves "calendar" alone on its second line. Rebreak it ("Booking links that / fill your calendar") or rewrite it (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The customer-name row has no label, so it reads as a footer link list rather than proof. Add a short lead-in such as "Used by", or show their marks.

**What's working.**
- **A clear text scale.** The headline is large and bold, the subhead light, the card titles semibold, the body regular. Each level is distinct at a glance, so keep this scale when you add an image.
- **One action stands out.** The hero button is the strongest thing below the headline, and the secondary "See a live example" is properly demoted to a text link.
- **The card descriptions land on the reader.** For example "so nobody has to do the math" and "to cut down on no-shows". Each one ends on what the viewer gains, not on how the feature works. Write the headline the same way.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Own one customer.** Taste. The customer row shows the audience is local practices, not sales teams. A hero built around one of them (a physio's booking page, with "Book a 30-min assessment") would make the page clearly *for* small clinics and studios, rather than one more generic booking tool.
2. **Let the image show "fill".** Taste. The headline promises a full calendar. A picture of a week filling with bookings would make that promise something the viewer can see, not just read, and it would put weight on the empty right half.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see hover and focus states on the buttons and the "Learn more" links. I also couldn't tell how the cards reflow on a phone: the "Shared calendars" title wrap will likely get worse at narrower widths.
