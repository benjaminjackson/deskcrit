**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline. It drops through the italic standfirst into the body without any trouble. It reads as a long-form magazine feature, calm and plain, and close to a default blog template. Two things snag: the headline ends on one orphaned word, "line", and a byline promising "Photographs by the residents" sits over a page with no photograph.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the top of a feature article on an independent local-interest publication. Its job is to draw a casual reader into a long story about a community allotment.

**Verdict.** The reading basics are sound and the writing does most of the work. But this is a photo-credited feature with no picture at the top, and its text column runs too wide. So it reads as a generic article page rather than a story about a place and its people.

**What would move it most**
1. **The opening has no picture, though the byline promises one.** "Photographs by the residents" sits right under the headline, and the frame below it holds only text, with a bare white band at the bottom. A feature about forty households, bath-tub beds and a Saturday queue at a water tap is about something you can see. Without an image the reader has nothing to fall into and nothing to tell this story apart from any other article on the site. The headline and a lead picture should make one idea together: the end of the bus line and what grew there. If the lead photo is in the live page and just didn't load in this capture, or sits right after the standfirst, this is fine. If it first appears further down, move one strong image up to sit with the headline. *Defect.* (Taste for the pairing; the source for that entry isn't on the approved list.)
2. **The text column is too wide to read comfortably.** Body lines in the 280–1000 px column run about 83–87 characters ("It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to" is 87). The standfirst runs about the same. On a long read that adds up: the eye takes longer to find each new line, and the piece feels heavier than the writing is. Bringhurst gives 45–75 characters, with 66 as the ideal (*Elements*, 2.1.2). Narrow the column to roughly 600–640 px at this size, or raise the body size a step at the same width. *Convention, with a real cost here.*

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" splits the compound "bus line" and leaves one word alone on line two. Break before "at the end" or "the bus line", or let the column width settle it. *Taste/common sense; no approved source covers breaks in headings.*
- Headline tracking: the roughly 40 px bold headline looks set at text spacing and reads slightly loose. Tighten the letter spacing a little at this size (Bringhurst 2.1.1; Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 80).
- Nav: "Features · Food · Places · Subscribe" doesn't mark that the reader is in Features. Give the current section one cue, such as weight, an underline or the green of the kicker (NN/g, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").

**What's working.**
- **The standfirst is real writing.** "The queue for the shared water tap is the best place in the district to hear the news" is specific, human, and could belong to no other story. The opening, "It started with a letter.", is the right hook. Keep this voice.
- **A clear, short hierarchy.** There is a green kicker, one dominant headline, a quiet grey byline, an italic standfirst, then upright body at the same size. Each level has one job, and the standfirst differs from the body by italic rather than by a near-miss size. That is the right way to do it.
- **The one accent colour is held back.** The green appears only on "Feature", so it reads as a section marker and suits the subject.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the residents' photographs set the page.** *Taste.* Open with one full-width or column-breaking photo above the headline or right after the standfirst, rather than a tidy thumbnail. The story's credit line says the pictures are the residents' own, which is the most distinctive thing about this piece. Showing that big is what would make a stranger stop scrolling.
2. **Give the publication a typographic voice.** *Taste.* Everything here, header and article alike, is one neutral sans, so "Groundwork" could be any product blog. A text face with some warmth for the headline and standfirst would mark this as a publication with a point of view and suit a slow, place-based read. Keeping the sans for the nav and byline would hold the system together (Bringhurst, ch. 6, on choosing faces that suit the task).
