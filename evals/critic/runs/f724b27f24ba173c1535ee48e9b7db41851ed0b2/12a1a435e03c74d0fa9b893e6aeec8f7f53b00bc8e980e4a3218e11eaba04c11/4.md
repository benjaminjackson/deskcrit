**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers (3.8, 64.20, 12.4) and then stops, because none of them says what it measures. Is 3.8 a percentage? Is 64.20 in pounds or dollars? Are there 12.4 subscribers, or 12.4 thousand? After that the eye drops to the orange bars, where Saturday stands out as the tallest and Sunday as the shortest. It reads as a calm, tidy admin overview for a small bakery's online shop, and it feels competent but quiet.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the internal dashboard where the owner or staff of a bakery that sells online and by subscription checks at a glance how the shop is doing and plans around delivery days.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are clean and mostly right. But the three headline numbers, which are the first thing anyone reads, have no units, no time period and no comparison. A viewer can't tell what they mean or whether they're good, so the top of the dashboard doesn't do its job.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI cards show bare numbers** (all three cards in the top row). This is a defect.
   - **No units.** "3.8" under "Checkout conversion rate" is almost certainly 3.8%, but the page never says so. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" active subscribers can't be a headcount, so the viewer has to guess between 12.4k, 12.4%, or a typo.
   - **No period.** The cards don't say which period they cover. The chart below says September 2026, but nothing links the cards to it.
   - **No comparison.** Nothing shows whether 3.8 is up or down, or near a target, so the owner can't tell good from bad. That's the only reason to look at these cards.

   **Fix:** put the unit, the time period, and a comparison next to each headline number, such as the change from last period or the target (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). For example: "3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400", plus a small "▲ 0.4 pts vs August" line under each, labelled with words as well as an arrow or colour. This change alone would make the page work.

**Smaller things.** None. The only other failed checklist line, the chart title stating a topic rather than the takeaway, is the first item under "Where it could go further".

**What's working.**
- **A clear order of reading.** The large, heavy KPI figures sit over small regular labels, and a single section title sits above the chart. With one typeface and about four sizes in clear steps, the eye always knows where to go. Keep that discipline when you add units and comparisons: make them smaller and lighter than the figure, not a new competing size.
- **An honest, quiet chart.** Bars start at zero, gridlines are faint, there's one colour, the days run in their natural Monday-to-Sunday order, and a source line gives the system and month. Nothing in it is decoration (Tufte's data-ink principle).
- **The orange does two jobs.** It's both the brand colour (the wordmark) and the data colour, and it's used nowhere else. The page feels like Crumbwell's, not a template.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the chart title say what the data shows** (taste). "Orders by delivery day" names the topic. For a shop planning baking and couriers, the useful thing is the finding: something like "Friday and Saturday carry 40% of deliveries; Sunday is quiet." Highlighting those bars, or labelling their values, would turn the chart from something to study into something to act on. I've made up the 40% to show the shape: use the real share.
2. **Give the page one point of view** (taste). Three equal cards say every metric matters equally. If this overview is about the subscription business, make "Active subscribers" lead (first and larger, or with a small trend line), so the owner's first glance answers the question they actually came with.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the chart shows exact values on hover.
- Whether the KPI cards link through to the Orders and Subscribers pages.
