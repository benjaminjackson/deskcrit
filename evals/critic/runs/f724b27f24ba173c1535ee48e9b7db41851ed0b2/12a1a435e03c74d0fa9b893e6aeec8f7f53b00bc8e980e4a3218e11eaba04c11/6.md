**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, "3.8", "64.20" and "12.4", then down to the orange bars, where Saturday is tallest and Sunday falls away. It reads as a calm, tidy shop dashboard for a small bakery's owner. Then the first question comes: 3.8 *what*? Is "12.4" active subscribers a count of people, or thousands?

**What it's for.** My assumption: the bakery's owner or ops lead opens this home screen to check, at a glance, how the shop is doing this month and how deliveries spread across the week.

**Verdict.** The layout is well built, but it doesn't do its job yet. The three numbers it puts first can't be read: they have no units, no period and nothing to compare against, so the owner can't tell what they mean or whether they're good.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units.** This is a defect. All three cards across the top have the problem:
   - "3.8" under Checkout conversion rate is almost certainly a percentage, but the page doesn't say so.
   - "64.20" under Average order value has no currency.
   - "12.4" under Active subscribers is the worst case. You can't have 0.4 of a subscriber, so it's probably 12.4k, but it could just as well be read as a bug.

   These are the largest, heaviest elements on the page, the ones the eye lands on first, and every one of them makes the viewer guess. Tufte (*Visual Display*, ch. 2) and Few both say the reader should never have to guess what a number measures. Fix: put the unit inside the figure ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400" or "12.4k"). Keep the unit at the same size, or a step smaller in the same weight, so the figure stays the hero.
2. **The headline numbers have no period and no comparison.** This is a defect. Even with units, "3.8%" on its own can't tell the owner whether to worry. Is it up or down on August, and is it above or below target? The cards also don't say what time span they cover. The chart's source line says September 2026, but nothing ties the cards to that month. Few's approved fix ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"): put the unit, the time period and a comparison, such as the change from last period or the target, next to each headline number. A small "+0.4 pts vs Aug" line under each figure would do it, and the cards have empty room on the right to hold it. Without this, the most important part of the page reports numbers but tells the owner nothing.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Orders by delivery day" only names the topic. A finding such as "Saturday is the busiest delivery day; Sunday is a third of it" would give the owner the point without making them read the bars. This is taste, not a sourced rule. Keep the topic as a smaller label if you want it.
- The y axis has no unit name. "Orders" is implied by the title, so this costs little, but a short "orders" on the axis, or "(orders)" in the title, removes the last guess.

**What's working.**
- **One clear entry point.** The KPI figures are big and bold, and everything else steps down cleanly: page title, card labels, chart title, then small grey axis text. There are about four type levels with real gaps between them, so nothing collides. Keep this scale when you add units and comparisons, and set those smaller and lighter, not as another big size.
- **A sound chart.** The bars start at zero, the weekdays run in their natural order, the gridlines are faint, there is one colour and no legend, and the source and month are given underneath. Nearly all the ink is data, which is what Tufte asks for.
- **A restrained palette.** Using the brand's burnt orange only for the logo and the bars ties the data to the shop without shouting. Everything else stays neutral.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Say something about the busiest days.** This is taste. Friday and Saturday together clearly dominate, and Sunday collapses. That is the thing a bakery would plan staffing and baking around. Colour those two bars in full brand orange, set the rest in a muted tint, and add one annotation. The chart then gives an answer instead of only showing data, which is what an owner who glances at this once a day needs (Tufte's emphasis on letting the data speak, applied through contrast).
2. **Use the empty band at the bottom.** This is taste. About 80 px of the first screen sits empty below the chart. A small "vs last month" ghost bar, or a thin line for the same weekday last month, would put the comparison the cards lack into the chart as well. That makes the page about change, which is what a returning owner actually checks.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover tooltips on the bars (exact counts), and the focus states on the top navigation.
