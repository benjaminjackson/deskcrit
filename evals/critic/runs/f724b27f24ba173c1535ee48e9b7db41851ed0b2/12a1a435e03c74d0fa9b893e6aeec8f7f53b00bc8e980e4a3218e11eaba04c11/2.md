**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and gets stuck on the question they raise: 3.8 what? 64.20 in what currency? 12.4 subscribers, when you can't have a fraction of one? Then it drops to the orange bars and gets an easy, satisfying read: the weekend peak and a Sunday collapse. It reads as the admin home for a small online bakery: calm, tidy and competent, but the top row says less than its size promises.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner or a manager of a small bakery that delivers checks this daily to see how the shop is doing and plan the week.

**Verdict.** Most of the dashboard works. The chart is clean and honest, and the layout is orderly. But the top row, which gets the most space and the boldest type, is the part that tells you least. Its numbers have no unit, no time period and no comparison, so the viewer can't tell whether things are good or bad, or even what is being counted.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI row: no units, no period, no comparison.** This is a defect, and the convention is stated by Few. It's in all three cards at the top. "3.8" is presumably a percentage, but nothing says so. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" under "Active subscribers" must mean 12.4 thousand, or else it's a decimal that makes no sense for a count of people. You have to rebuild the meaning yourself, and someone new to the screen may rebuild it wrongly. None of the cards says what period it covers. The chart below says September 2026, but nothing ties the cards to that month, so you have to guess. Nor does any card say whether its number is up or down. A 3.8% conversion rate is good news or bad news only next to last month or a target. These are the biggest, heaviest marks on the page, so the page's first impression is a puzzle. The fix, following Few's "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": put the unit on each figure (3.8%, £64.20, 12,400), add the period ("September" or "Last 30 days"), and give each number a comparison, such as the change since last month or a target, in small type under it.

**Smaller things.**
- Chart title, "Orders by delivery day": it names the topic but not the finding. A line like "Saturday is the peak; Sunday orders drop to about a third" would hand the viewer the planning point. This is my judgment as a designer; no approved source covers it.

**What's working.**
- **An honest chart.** The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, there's one colour, weekdays run in their natural order, and there's a source line with the month. The weekend shape is readable in a second. Keep this restraint when you add annotations.
- **One accent colour the brand owns.** The burnt orange appears only in the wordmark and the bars, and everything else is neutral. That ties the data to the shop without any decoration.
- **A clear structure.** The cards and the chart panel share one left edge and the same gutters. The current nav item is marked by weight. There are few type levels, and each has an obvious job.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the top row answer "how are we doing?" by itself.** This is taste. A daily-check dashboard is for spotting change. A small trend line or a coloured up/down delta in each card would turn three labels into three verdicts, and the owner wouldn't have to open another screen to know whether this month is better.
2. **Mark the planning point on the chart itself.** This is taste. Pick out Saturday (or Sunday's dip) with a direct label, and grey the ordinary weekdays slightly. The chart would then say what to staff and bake for, not only show the counts.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indicators on the nav links, and whether the bars show exact values on hover. The y axis only gives ticks every 300, so hover values may matter.
