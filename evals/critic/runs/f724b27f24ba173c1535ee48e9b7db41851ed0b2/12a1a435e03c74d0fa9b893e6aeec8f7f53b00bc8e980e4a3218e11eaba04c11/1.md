**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers across the top, then down to the orange bars climbing to Saturday and dropping on Sunday. It reads as a calm, tidy overview for a small bakery's online shop. Then it stalls on "12.4 active subscribers": you can't have a fractional subscriber, so the number is in some unit the screen doesn't show. The neighbours "3.8" and "64.20" don't show theirs either.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an at-a-glance health check for the bakery's owner or operations person, answering "how is the shop doing, and when are orders landing?"

**Verdict.** The layout, chart and restraint are solid. But the three numbers the page is built around can't be read as they stand: no units, no period, nothing to compare against. So the top third, which the eye goes to first, tells the owner almost nothing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units and no period.** This is in the three cards at the top. "3.8" is probably a percentage, but nothing says so. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" active subscribers must be 12.4 thousand or a rate, but a reader could just as easily take it for a typo. None of the cards says which period it covers. Only the chart's source line, at the very bottom, mentions September 2026, and the reader has to guess whether that applies to the cards too. These are the numbers the owner acts on, so two readings is a defect: misread 12.4 and they're off by a factor of a thousand. Fix: put the unit on the figure ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400"), set a little smaller and lighter so the big number still dominates, and state the period once, near "Shop overview" (for example "September 2026"), so every number on the page shares it. **Defect** (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").
2. **No figure can be judged good or bad.** Each card shows one bare value with no previous period, target or trend. The owner can't tell whether a 3.8% conversion rate is a win or a warning, so the page reports but doesn't inform. Fix: add the change from the previous period ("+0.4 pts vs August") or a target under each number, and colour it only if the colour is backed by a sign or arrow. **Convention** (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2).

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Orders by delivery day" names the topic, not the finding. Something like "Friday and Saturday carry the week; Sunday is a third of Saturday" tells the owner what to staff for. This is taste and common sense; no approved source covers it.
- The y-axis has no unit label. "Orders" is implied by the title, but "orders delivered" on the axis or in the subtitle removes the guess (Tufte, ch. 2).

**What's working.**
- One accent colour, the brand's burnt orange, used for the wordmark and the bars and nothing else. The page reads as Crumbwell's and stays calm. Keep that discipline when you add comparison indicators.
- The chart is honest and quiet. Bars start at zero, gridlines are faint, days run in natural order, there's no legend because there's one series, and there's a source line. Nothing competes with the data.
- The type scale is clear: huge numbers, a medium page title, small labels, tiny axis text. Each step is big enough that its job is obvious, and the three cards share one grid with the chart below.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Make the page answer "what should I do this week?", not just "what happened?" For a shop owner, the most valuable thing here is that Friday and Saturday carry the week. Mark the peak days directly on the chart, for example by labelling the two tallest bars with their values, so the pattern can't be missed. This is taste, in the spirit of Tufte's direct labelling.
2. Give the top row a reason to exist as a row. Right now the three metrics are unrelated, each in an identical card. A stronger version picks the one number the owner cares most about (likely subscribers, for a bakery with subscriptions) and makes it lead, with the other two in support. The hierarchy would then say what the business runs on. This is taste.
