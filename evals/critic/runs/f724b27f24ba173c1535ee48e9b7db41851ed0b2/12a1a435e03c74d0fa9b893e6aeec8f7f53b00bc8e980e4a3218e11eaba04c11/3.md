**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers (3.8, 64.20, 12.4), then drops to the orange bars, where Saturday stands tallest and Sunday falls away. It reads as a small online bakery's admin home page for the owner. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy. Then the numbers stop me: 3.8 what? 64.20 in which currency? And 12.4 active subscribers can't be right.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a home screen where the shop owner checks, in a few seconds, how the business is doing and which delivery days carry the load.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean, but the numbers don't do their job yet. The three headline figures have no units, no period and nothing to compare against. The one chart counts a month in which Tuesday and Wednesday came up five times while every other day came up four, so its weekday pattern is partly a calendar effect.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline numbers can't be read as written (defect).** In the three cards at the top, "3.8" needs a %, "64.20" needs a currency, and "12.4" under "Active subscribers" can only mean 12.4 thousand. Read cold, it says twelve and a bit people. These are the biggest type on the page and the first thing the owner sees, and each one makes them fill in a gap. A misread here (a 3.8 ratio, the wrong currency, 12 subscribers) is the one that sticks. Put the unit on every number, at the same size or one step smaller: "3.8%", "£64.20", "12.4k" or "12,400" (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2, on clear labelling; Few).

2. **No KPI says whether it's good or bad (convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2).** None of the cards gives a period or a comparison. The owner can't tell whether 3.8% is up or down, or whether 64.20 beats last month. They also can't tell whether these figures cover September, like the chart, or today, or all time. A dashboard number with no reference point is just a fact. Put the time period and a comparison next to each headline number: the change from the last period, or the target. A small line under each figure, such as "▲ 0.4 pts vs August", is the usual way.

3. **The chart's weekday pattern is partly a calendar effect (defect; common sense, no approved source).** The source line says September 2026, which starts on a Tuesday. That month has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of each other day, so those two bars carry 25% more delivery days. Going by the bar heights, per delivery day Tuesday (about 142 orders) is actually the weakest weekday, below Monday (about 160). The gentle Monday-to-Thursday rise the chart shows is mostly the calendar. An owner using this to plan baking or staffing would put people on the wrong days. Plot average orders per delivery day, or use a span of whole weeks, and say which in the source line.

**Smaller things.**
- Chart title "Orders by delivery day" names the data but not the finding. Something like "Friday and Saturday carry almost 40% of orders" would tell the owner what to act on (taste). Write it once the per-day numbers from finding 3 are right.

**What's working.**
- **One grid, kept strictly.** The cards, the chart panel and the page title share one left edge and equal gutters, so the page reads as one calm surface. Keep it when you add comparison lines to the cards.
- **A restrained chart.** One series in the brand orange, faint gridlines, a zero baseline, weekdays in calendar order, no legend, and a source line. Nothing in it is decoration.
- **A clear type scale.** Big bold numbers, small regular labels above them, a bold chart title and small grey axis text. Every size has an obvious job.
