**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and the first thought is "3.8 what?" From there it drops to the orange bars, picks out a Friday–Saturday peak and a Sunday drop, and stops. It reads as the home screen of a small bakery's online shop. It feels calm and tidy, but it looks like a lot of other dashboard templates.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's daily check: how the shop is doing and when orders land, read at a glance.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are well made. But the three numbers at the top, the part people look at first, can't be read as stated. They have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them with. So the most prominent part of the page says the least.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three headline numbers have no unit, period or comparison** (top row of cards). Defect.
   - **No units.** "3.8" is probably a percent. "64.20" is money in an unnamed currency. "12.4" could be 12.4 thousand subscribers, or a figure with a decimal that makes no sense for people. A new viewer has to guess, and a guess like 12.4 vs 12,400 changes the whole story.
   - **No period.** The chart's caption says September 2026. Nothing says whether the cards cover the same month, today, or all time.
   - **No comparison.** Even with units, there's no previous period, target or trend. The owner can't tell whether 3.8% is good news or bad, which is the only reason to glance at it.
   - **Fix.** This is Few's pitfall of the bare headline number ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Put the unit, the period, and a comparison next to each number, for example "3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400", plus a small "▲0.4 pts vs August" line. The numbers can keep their size. They need the extra words around them.

**Smaller things.**
- **Chart title** ("Orders by delivery day"): it names the topic but not the finding. A title like "Friday and Saturday carry the week; Sunday is a third of Saturday" would tell the owner something they can act on, such as staffing or baking volume. This is my taste, but it's what the chart exists to show.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale.** There are really four levels: page title, card label, KPI figure and chart label, with big jumps between them. The KPI numbers clearly lead. Keep these steps when you add units and deltas: the comparison line should be small and muted, not a fifth size competing with the number.
- **The chart.** It's honest and clean. Bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, there's one series in one colour, the days are in natural order, and there's a source line with a date. Nothing needs taking out.
- **The colour.** One warm orange is shared by the wordmark and the bars, set on neutrals. It gives the shop a recognisable colour without shouting.

**Where it could go further**
1. **Let the top row say whether the owner should worry.** The job is a daily check, so each card should answer "better or worse than usual?" before the owner reads the number. Colour the delta, with a word or arrow alongside so colour isn't the only signal. Few's point about context is the reason. (Taste.)
2. **Use the orange to point at the answer.** Right now every bar is the same strong orange, so nothing in the chart stands out. Make Friday and Saturday orange and the other days a muted tint, and the weekly pattern reads in one glance, without even reading the title. This is Tufte's idea of keeping the ink on what matters. (Taste.)

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards or chart offer a period switch or hover values, which would partly cover the missing period.
- How the three cards reflow on a narrow screen.
