**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Pricing" heading, drops to three identical cards, and scans $9, $29, $79. It reads as the pricing page of a small SaaS calendar or booking tool. It feels calm and tidy, and it could belong to any company. After comparing the three prices, the eye has nowhere to go: the cards end, and below them is about 230 px of blank page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a SaaS pricing page that should get a small business or team to pick a plan for a scheduling tool and start signing up.

**Verdict.** The page shows the prices clearly, but it doesn't sell a plan. No card has a button, nothing says which plan suits whom, and "$29 / month" for a team doesn't say whether that's per person. A visitor can compare the numbers but can't act on one, and may not know what they would pay.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plans can't be bought from where they're shown.** The three cards end on a feature list and have no button. The only action on the page is "Start free" at the far top right, which names no plan and doesn't say what "free" means: a trial, a free tier, or a free start on Solo. Someone who has just picked Team has to leave the card, find a header button that doesn't mention Team, and hope the choice carries over. Their eye path stops at the bottom of the cards (Arnheim on the eye path). The action sits far from the thing it acts on (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, natural mapping). Pricing cards without a button also break the pattern people expect from every other SaaS pricing page (Nielsen, Jakob's law). **Defect.** Fix: give each card a button at its foot that names the plan and what happens next, such as "Start a 14-day Team trial" if that's the offer. Make the plan you most want to sell the primary button and style the other two lower.

2. **The prices and features can be read two ways, and the page doesn't help anyone choose.** "$29 / month" on a card called Team, for "Up to 10 calendars", could mean $29 for the whole team or $29 per person. That's a 1× or 10× difference in the bill, and people hold you to the number they remember. The feature lists don't say whether each tier includes the one below it. Does Team still get the Booking page and Email reminders? Does Business get Reports? Read as written, upgrading seems to lose features. "Reports" and "Shared availability" are bare category labels, so the reader has to guess what they get. All three cards are identical, so nothing suggests where a typical buyer should start. **Defect** (the ambiguous price and features). The missing pointer is common sense: a buyer facing three equal options with no guidance is more likely to stall. Fix: state the billing unit outright, e.g. "$29 / month for your whole team" or "per user". Start the Team and Business lists with "Everything in Solo, plus…". Mark one card as the usual choice with a label and a stronger border or fill, not with colour alone.

3. **The words could be on anyone's page.** "Pricing" repeats the nav item that's already underlined. "Simple plans for teams of every size. Change or cancel whenever you like." could be on any SaaS site, so the visitor learns nothing about why this tool, or which size of team they are. The plan names ("Solo", "Team", "Business") describe the buyer, but nothing in the cards connects to a moment from the buyer's own work. **Taste**, but it carries weight here because this is where the visitor decides. Direction: make the headline say what the money buys, in the visitor's terms, e.g. what booking looks like for their clients. Under each plan name, add one line saying who it's for ("For a freelancer taking their own bookings"), so the visitor recognises themselves.

**Smaller things.**
- The subtitle breaks so that "you like." sits alone on its second line (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Narrow or widen the measure so the two lines are closer in length, or shorten the sentence.

**What's working.**
- The price is clearly the largest, boldest thing in each card, and "/ month" is set small and grey beside it. That makes the three numbers easy to compare across the row. Keep this contrast when you add a button and a "recommended" mark.
- The type and colour are disciplined: one sans-serif family, a dark neutral for text, and one blue kept for the logo, the current-page underline, and the single button. Because the blue appears in so few places, it will clearly mark the buy buttons once they exist.
- The cards line up on one grid, with equal gutters and matching inner padding. Plan names, prices, and feature rows sit on the same baselines across all three, so comparing them across a row is easy.
