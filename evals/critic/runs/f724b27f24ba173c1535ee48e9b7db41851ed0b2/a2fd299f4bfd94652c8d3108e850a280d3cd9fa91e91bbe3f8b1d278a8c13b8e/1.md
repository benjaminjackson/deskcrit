**First read.** The eye lands on the title, drops to the tall Growth bar, then scans the bars left to right. The chart says Growth converts about two and a half times as well as Starter. Only after reading the labels do you find the real gap is 4.0% vs 3.1%. It's a calm, clean internal analytics page. That calm makes the chart look more trustworthy than it is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard page that helps a product or growth team compare how well each plan's trials convert to paid in Q3 2025, and decide where to act.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and table are tidy and mostly right. The chart misstates its own data, though, and that is the one thing this page exists to show. The bars start at 2.5%, so the differences between plans look about five times larger than they are. Nothing on the page tells the reader that most of those differences may be noise.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bar axis starts at 2.5%, not zero (defect).** This is the main chart. Its value axis runs from 2.5% to 4.5%, so each bar's length is its distance above 2.5%. Starter's bar stands for 0.6 points and Growth's for 1.5, so Growth's bar is 2.5 times as tall. The real values are 3.1% and 4.0%, a ratio of about 1.3. Tufte calls the gap between what's drawn and what's in the data the "lie factor", and here it's about 5. A reader who glances at the shapes and skips the labels will tell the team Growth converts "way better", and that is the call this page feeds. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, proportional ink) and Few both say bar axes start at zero. Fix: start the axis at 0%, so the bars honestly look close together. If the small gaps are the point, switch to dots on a narrow, labelled scale, where a baseline above zero is allowed.
2. **No sign of sample size or uncertainty, so noise reads as signal (defect).** This is the chart together with the table at right. Enterprise's 3.4% comes from 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. By my rough sum, that's give or take about a point, so the true rate could be anywhere from about 2.3% to 4.5%. Team vs Starter (3.6% vs 3.1%) is also within normal sampling noise. Only Growth vs Starter is probably real, and only just. The chart draws every gap with the same confidence, and the truncated axis then makes them bigger. A team could reprice or redesign a plan because of a gap that comes from 38 accounts. Wilke (ch. 16) says to show it. Fix: add error bars or interval whiskers to each bar or dot, or at least put the paid count (n) under each plan label. Mark which gaps are big enough to act on.

**Smaller things.**
- **Chart title** (top left): it names the topic, not the finding. Once the honest picture is drawn, say what it shows, e.g. "Growth trials convert best; the other plans can't be told apart yet." This is common sense, not a cited rule.
- **Table** (right): it gives Trials and Paid but no rate column. A reader checking a bar has to do the division in their head. Add a "Conversion" column to tie the table to the chart.
- **Comparison** (whole page): there's no previous quarter or target, so you can't tell whether 3–4% is good. Even a faint marker for last quarter's rate on each bar would help.

**What's working.**
- **Direct value labels on every bar, and no legend.** The exact figure sits at the point you're looking at. Keep this when you redraw the chart.
- **The Definition block and the source line.** Together they say what counts as converted, how trials still open at the cutoff are treated, and the date range. Few dashboards do this, and it's why the numbers can be checked.
- **Restraint.** One purple, light gridlines, one typeface with clear weight steps, and the current nav item shown in bold. Nothing competes with the data.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the page answer the decision, not just display the metric (taste).** A growth team arrives with a question: which plan's trial needs work? A one-line finding in the title, plus a highlight on the one plan whose gap is real (strong purple for it, grey for the rest), would turn the page from a report into an answer. Tufte's case for putting the comparison in front of the eye supports this.
2. **Use the empty lower-right area for the missing context (taste).** The space under Definition is the natural place for last quarter's rates per plan, or a small trend line per plan. A dashboard figure means much more next to its own history, and the space is already free.
