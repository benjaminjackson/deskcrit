**First read.** The eye lands on "Pricing", then moves left to right across the three prices, $9 / $29 / $79. Then it runs out of things to do. The last line of each card is a feature, and below that the bottom third of the screen is empty. It reads as a calm, tidy SaaS pricing page for a calendar and booking tool, but nothing on it lets you act.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the pricing page of a scheduling product. Its job is to help a visitor (a solo user or a team lead) pick a plan and start on it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The page shows prices but gives no way to choose one: there is no button on any card, no recommended plan, and the feature lists don't make the differences between tiers clear. As it stands it's a price list, not a place where someone picks a plan.

**What would move it most**
1. **No plan has an action, so the eye path dead-ends.** The three cards end on "Email reminders", "Reports" and "Priority support", and nothing comes after them. The only button is "Start free" in the header, about 300 px above the cards and off to the right, and it doesn't say which plan you'd start. A visitor who has just decided "Team looks right" has nothing to click at that moment and has to guess whether the header button gives them Team. Per-plan buttons are what visitors expect on a pricing page (Nielsen, Jakob's law), and leaving them out gains nothing here. *Defect.* Fix: put a button at the foot of each card that names the result ("Start Team free", or "Talk to sales" for Business). Use the empty band below the cards for the questions that come right after picking a plan: is there a free trial, what does a team seat count as, annual billing.
2. **Three equal cards, so nothing helps the visitor choose.** The cards are identical in size, border, weight and colour, so the page gives no answer to "which one is for me?" The visitor has to work the comparison out alone (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: make the order of importance plain through differences). *Defect* for a page whose job is choosing. Fix: make one plan clearly stand out, probably Team if that's the plan you want most people on. Give it a stronger border or a tint in the brand blue, a "Most popular" label, and the only filled button among the three, with the other two buttons styled lower.
3. **The feature lists don't say how the tiers relate.** Solo lists "1 calendar, Booking page, Email reminders" and Team lists "Up to 10 calendars, Shared availability, Reports". A stranger can't tell whether Team still includes the booking page and reminders, or whether Business has "Unlimited calendars" but no Reports. Each card makes you remember the one beside it, and a cautious buyer will assume the worst (Nielsen, recognition rather than recall). *Defect.* Fix: open the higher tiers with "Everything in Solo, plus:" or similar. Make "Reports" and "Shared availability" say what you get ("See your team's free time in one view").
4. **The copy could be on anyone's page.** "Simple plans for teams of every size" says nothing about Tidewell, and the plan names don't say who each plan is for. The visitor can't tell what is different about this product, or which plan fits them. *Taste, argued.* Fix: give each plan a one-line "for whom" under its name ("For one person taking bookings", "For teams that share a schedule"). Make the subtitle state the promise in the visitor's terms.

**Smaller things.**
- Subtitle: "you like." sits alone on the second line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrow the text box or rewrite so the line breaks evenly.
- Nav: "Pricing" looks exactly like the other links, so nothing marks the current page. Mark it with one cue, such as weight, an underline or the brand blue (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").

**What's working.**
- The price figures are set large and bold, with "/ month" small and grey beside them. Three clear steps (plan name, price, features) make the prices easy to scan across the cards. Keep that scale when you add buttons.
- The palette is restrained: near-black, grey and one brand blue used only on the logo and the CTA. Because of that, a recommended-plan highlight in the same blue will stand out immediately.
- The cards follow a clean grid, with equal gutters and features aligned across all three. Symmetric cards are the right choice for a side-by-side comparison.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the nav and the "Start free" button.
- How the three cards stack on a phone.
- Whether more content (FAQ, comparison table) sits below the fold and this is just the top of a longer page. If it does, the empty band is a cut-off, not dead space, but findings 1 to 3 still stand.
