**First read.** My eye lands on the title, drops to the tall Organic search bar, then steps down the bars from left to right. It reads as an internal growth report: Q3 sign-ups by channel, for the team that spends the marketing budget. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. The note on the right is what the page is really trying to say, but I only got to it after reading the whole chart.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is an internal report page. It should let a marketing or growth lead see which channels brought sign-ups in Q3 and accept the Q4 budget shift that follows from that.

**Verdict.** It does the basic job well: it's honest, sorted, labelled on the bars and easy to read. What gets in the way is that the page's main claims, that organic grew since Q2 and that the events budget should move, rest on things the chart doesn't show. The reader has to take the note on trust.

**What would move it most**
1. **The change the note claims isn't in the chart.** The note says organic search went "up from a quarter in Q2", but the chart shows Q3 alone. The viewer can't see the growth, can't tell whether referral, the channel getting the money, is rising or falling, and can't tell whether 260 from events is new or normal. Every bar is a bare number with nothing to compare it to, so a budget decision is being argued from a single snapshot. This is a defect: it leaves the decision-maker unable to check the case. It also breaks a convention (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a figure needs a comparison such as the previous period). The fix is to put Q2 next to Q3 for each channel, for example a paler bar beside each blue one or a change figure under each value label. Then "up from a quarter" and "moves to referrals" become things the viewer can see for themselves.

**Smaller things.**
- The note's heading "What changed this quarter" (y≈174) lines up with neither the subtitle (y≈145) nor the top of the chart (the 2,000 line, y≈195), so it looks like it just missed. Align it to the chart top or to the title.
- The heading "What changed this quarter" covers a Q4 budget decision as well as a change. Either retitle it (for example "What changed, and what we're doing") or give the decision its own line. Common sense: the decision is the line someone will act on.

**What's working.**
- Value labels sit directly on every bar, and the gridlines are faint enough that the bars are clearly the darkest marks. Keep that; it's why the chart reads in seconds (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*).
- The bars are sorted by value and all one colour, so the ranking is the only story the chart tells and no colour asks to be decoded.
- The copy is exact and adds up: the bars total the 5,400 in the source line, 1,840 really is about a third, and 410 + 260 really is under 700. The source line gives the date range and total. Numbers this reliable are what make the reader trust the note.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the title state the finding** (taste). "New sign-ups by channel" names the topic, and the finding sits in 15 px type in a side column. For a report meant to justify a budget move, a title like "Organic search now brings a third of sign-ups" makes the first thing the eye reads the point of the page. The current title can stay as the subtitle.
2. **Mark on the chart the bars the note talks about** (taste). The note names Organic search, Podcast ads and Events. Picking those bars out, for example by greying the others or adding a short note beside them, ties the text to the evidence. Then the eye doesn't have to cross the page to match a sentence to a bar.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the nav links and the chart have keyboard focus states, and how the two-column layout reflows on a narrow screen. The side note would likely drop below the chart and lose its tie to it.
