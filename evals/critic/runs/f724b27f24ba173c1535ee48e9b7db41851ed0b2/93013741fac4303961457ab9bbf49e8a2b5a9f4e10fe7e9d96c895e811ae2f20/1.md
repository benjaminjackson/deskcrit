**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the serif headline "Throwing on the wheel for beginners", drops into the paragraph, and then reaches the beige card with the price and the rust "Book a place" button. It reads as a small pottery studio's course page for adult beginners, and it feels calm and craft-like. In version 1 the eye goes straight down one left edge. In version 2 it has to find the start of every line again, and the "Kiln Yard Studio" logo at top left seems to belong to a different page from the centred block under it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a course page that tells an adult beginner what the eight-week wheel course involves, what it costs and when it runs, and gets them to book one of eight places.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The two versions have the same content, sizes, colours and card. The only change is that version 2 centres everything, and that makes the two descriptive paragraphs slower to read and breaks the page's one shared edge. Version 1 is a clean, working page with no defect that stops someone booking.

**What changed, and why version 1 wins**
1. **Version 2 centres paragraphs of several lines** (defect and convention). The four-line paragraph ("Over eight Wednesday evenings…") and the two-line one ("Clay, glazes…") are centred, so both edges are ragged and each new line starts somewhere different. This is the text that sells the course: what you'll make, that each session has a demonstration, that clay and firings are included. Making it harder to scan works against the booking. Tschichold (*The New Typography*) and Bringhurst (2.1.3) both keep centring for a few short lines. Version 1 sets these paragraphs flush left at about 72 characters a line, which is the fix already done.
2. **Version 2 splits the page between two axes** (defect). The logo sits on the left edge at x≈88, and everything below it is centred on the page. The two share no line, so the header looks left over from another layout (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). In version 1 the logo, eyebrow, headline, body, card and button all hang from the same x≈88 edge, which ties the studio's name to its offer. The centring in version 2 also looks like habit, not a choice the content needs. Tschichold's alternative is an asymmetric arrangement that follows the order of the content, and version 1 already is one.

The centred card in version 2 is fine on its own, because its lines are short. It just follows the same choice that hurt the paragraphs.

**Remaining findings for version 1:** none that would change how well it does its job.

**What's working** (version 1)
- **One strong left edge.** Everything from the logo to the button starts at the same x, so the page reads as one column in the order a buyer thinks: what it is, what you'll do, what's included, price, when, book.
- **The copy makes the case without help.** "Centre clay, open it, and pull up the walls of a cylinder, then turn those cylinders into mugs and bowls" tells a beginner exactly what they'll be able to do. "You take home everything you make once it has been glazed" answers the question they would otherwise have to ask.
- **The booking card groups the decision.** The price, day and time, start date, number of places and the one primary button sit in one tinted box. The only saturated colour, the rust, appears on the eyebrow and the button, so the action is easy to find.

**Where it could go further** (version 1)
1. **Use the empty right half** (taste). From x≈720 to the right edge the page is blank, so on a laptop the first screen is half empty. For a course whose appeal is physical, a photo of hands on clay or a row of finished mugs would show the viewer what they will make in a way the words can't. Set it to the right of the text column and line its top up with the headline.
2. **Show what makes this studio different** (taste). The headline names the course but not the studio. A line that sets the course against what the viewer already knows (for example small class size, "eight places, eight wheels", or what they leave with) would give a comparison they can judge for themselves, not just a schedule. "Eight places" also reads better as a reason to book now if it says how many are left. Only claim scarcity if it's true.
3. **Make the price the top of the card** (taste). At about 22 px the price is just a step above the details under it. Making it larger or heavier would make the card answer "how much?" at a glance (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*: bolder, not bigger, where size runs out).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** What happens after "Book a place" (a checkout or a form), and how the text column and card reflow on a phone.
