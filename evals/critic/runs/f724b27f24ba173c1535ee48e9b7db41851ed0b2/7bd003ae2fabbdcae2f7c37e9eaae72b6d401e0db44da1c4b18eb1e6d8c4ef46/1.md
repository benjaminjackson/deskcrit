**First read.** In version 1 the eye goes straight to **$184,320**. It is roughly twice the size of every other figure and sits in a card two columns wide. From there it moves along the top row to Orders and Average order, then down the even row of four. In version 2 the eye has no single place to land. Seven figures of the same size sit in a 4 + 3 grid, Revenue comes first only because it is top-left, and the empty slot at the end of row 2 catches the eye like a missing tile. Both read as a calm, clean monthly sales overview for a small retailer.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal dashboard where the owner or sales team checks month-to-date performance at a glance, with revenue as the number that matters most.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. It tells the viewer which number matters, and its grid closes cleanly. Version 2 gives revenue the same weight as items per order and leaves a hole in the layout. In both versions the weekly chart is the weakest part: it has no labels and only fills the left half of its panel.

**What changed, and why version 1 wins**
- **Hierarchy.** Version 1 makes Revenue the one dominant element, with larger numerals and a double-width card. That matches what an owner checks first. Version 2 sets all seven figures at one size, so revenue, returns and items per order look equally important, and the reader has to work out the priority. This is a defect in version 2 (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance through differences of size).
- **Grid closure.** Version 1 fills a 4-column grid exactly: Revenue takes 2 columns plus 2 single cards above, and 4 cards below. Version 2's seven equal cards leave the bottom-right cell empty. The gap looks like a card failed to load, and the right side of the page feels lighter. This is a defect in version 2 (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on balance).
- **Same in both:** header, labels, colours, deltas and chart are identical.

**What would move version 1 most**
1. **The weekly chart can't be read.** In "Revenue by week" the eight bars have no week labels, no value axis and no figures. The viewer can see that revenue rose and dipped but can't tell by how much, which week is which, or whether the last bar is a partial week. The bars also stop at about the panel's midpoint, leaving the right half as dead space, and the chart is cut off at the bottom of the first screen. This is a defect (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2, and Few: label axes and units; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a dashboard fits on one screen). Fix direction:
   - Label the weeks and give either a value scale from zero or a figure on each bar.
   - Spread the bars across the panel's full width.
   - Shorten the panel so its whole height fits above the fold.
2. **The deltas don't say what they compare against.** Only Revenue says "+12% on September". "+5%", "+7%", "+9%" and the rest are presumably against September too, but a stranger has to assume that. This matters most for "+2 points" and "-0.4 points", where the unit is easy to misread. This is a defect, though minor. Fix: put "vs September" once, as a subtitle under "October so far", so it covers every card.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Revenue by week" names the topic, not the finding. A line such as "Up four of the last seven weeks" would do the reading for the viewer. This is taste, not a rule.

**What's working.**
- In version 1, the single oversized revenue figure gives the page an obvious starting point, and the remaining six figures sit at one size below it. Keep this two-level scale.
- Colour is restrained: neutral cards, one muted blue for the chart, and green kept for the deltas. "-0.4 points" on Returns is correctly green, since fewer returns is good news.
- The type has clear levels: small grey labels, big dark figures, small green deltas. Each one has one obvious job.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Let the chart carry the revenue story: mark the current week as partial and show September's weekly level as a faint reference line. Then "+12%" can be seen, not just stated. This is taste, and it serves an owner who wants to know whether the month is on track.
2. Set apart the deltas that need attention. Right now every delta is green, so the colour carries no signal. Holding green back for significant moves, or adding a neutral state, would let a bad week stand out when one comes. This is taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the chart's baseline is at zero, because the bottom is cut off. Check this in the live page.
- Whether the last bar is a partial week.
