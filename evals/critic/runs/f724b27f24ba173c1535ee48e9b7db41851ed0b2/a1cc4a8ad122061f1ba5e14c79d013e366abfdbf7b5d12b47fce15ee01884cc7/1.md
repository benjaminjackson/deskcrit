**First read.** The eye lands on the serif "How can we help?" in the navy band, drops straight to the white search field, then spreads across the eight topic cards. It's clearly a help-centre home page for a software product. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. It also feels like every other help centre: if you covered the words "Harbor Help Center", you couldn't say whose product this supports.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing page of the help centre for a SaaS product called Harbor, and its job is to get a stuck customer to the right article quickly, by search or by topic.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the grid is clean and the search is impossible to miss, so nobody will get lost here. The biggest thing holding it back is that it's generic. Nothing in the copy or the page meets the customer's actual problem, so it routes people well but doesn't help them yet.

**What would move it most**
1. **The words could sit on anyone's help centre (taste, argued).** "How can we help?", "Browse a topic or search the guides." and "Search for articles" are the stock lines of the genre. The subtitle just describes the two controls the viewer can already see. The cost is small but real: a stressed customer gets no sign that this place knows their problem, and the empty search field leaves them to guess the right words. The fix is to make the search ask concrete. Use the placeholder or the subtitle to show real questions in the customer's own words, such as "Try 'reset two-step sign-in' or 'where's my refund'". That tells them what kind of query works and shows the help centre knows what people come for.
2. **The page gives the customer nothing ready-made (common sense; the catalog's source for this is off the approved list).** Above the fold there are only categories, so every visitor has to translate their problem into one of eight buckets and then dig again. A help centre usually knows its top handful of questions. A short "Popular right now" or "Most asked" list, or a live line from Status if something is down, would answer a large share of visits in one click. If that list already sits just below the fold, this finding drops away, but it should be close enough to the search that a quick glance takes it in.

**Smaller things.**
- **One-word last lines.** In five of the eight card descriptions the last line holds a single word ("receipts.", "recovery.", "data.", "where.", "messages."), which makes the grid look ragged. Tighten or rewrite those lines to fit the card width, or balance the line breaks (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- **"Orders and shipping" doesn't match the other topics.** "Track a delivery or start a return" sits next to topics about workspaces, inviting your team and integrations, so a first-time visitor can't tell whether Harbor is software, a shop, or both. If Harbor really ships physical goods, say so somewhere near the top. If not, rename the card (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2).

**What's working.**
- **The hierarchy is clear.** The serif headline is the only display face on the page and the only large size, so the eye always knows where to start. Everything else is in one sans, with levels set mainly by weight and colour rather than lots of sizes. Keep that two-face plan.
- **The search is placed well.** A white field on the navy band is the highest-contrast shape on the page, sits right under the question, and is plainly something you type into.
- **The grid is clean.** Four equal columns share one gutter and line up with the 64 px left edge of the logo and "Browse by topic". The eight outline icons share one stroke weight and style, so the cards read as one set at a glance.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Own one detail of colour (taste).** Navy, white and grey are safe, and that's why the page could be anyone's. One accent colour, used only where the customer acts (search focus, card hover, the "popular" links), would give Harbor a recognisable face and mark what can be clicked, without adding clutter.
2. **Let the hero speak to the customer's situation (taste).** The headline is the largest thing on the page. Spending it on a stock question wastes the strongest spot. A line that names what people usually arrive with, or a plain statement of current system health, would turn the most-seen words into useful ones.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus styling on the search field and the cards: a white field on navy needs a focus ring that shows up against both.
- Whether the cards respond on hover.
- What sits below the fold, which decides whether finding 2 stands.
