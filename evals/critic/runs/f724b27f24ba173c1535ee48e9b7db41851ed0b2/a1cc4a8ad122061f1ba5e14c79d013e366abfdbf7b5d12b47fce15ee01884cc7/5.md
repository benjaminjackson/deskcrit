**First read.** The eye lands on the white serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops to the search field, then scans the eight cards left to right, top to bottom. It reads straight away as a product's help-center home. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, and it could belong to almost any SaaS company.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the front door of a product's support site. A customer with a specific problem should get to the right article, or to a person, as fast as possible.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The search field is clearly where to start and the topics are easy to scan. What holds it back is that it treats every problem as equally likely. The customer has to sort their own problem into one of eight categories of equal weight, and nothing on the page shows the answers people need most.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing points at the problems people actually arrive with.** The area below the hero is eight identical cards, and nothing else is on the first screen. Most people come to a help center with a specific problem, like "I can't sign in" or "my sync broke". This page makes them guess which category it falls under. "Troubleshooting", "Account and sign-in" and "Integrations" overlap, so a sync failure could sit in any of the three. The fix: put the five or six most-read articles or fixes in front of them, as a short list under the search or above the grid. Order the cards by how often each topic is used, not by the product's own outline. A "Status" link in the nav also suggests outages are a real reason people come here; if something is down, say so on this page. *Common sense: the source for this rule (Tognazzini, Anticipation) isn't on the approved list.* Defect: it slows the visitor down.
2. **The copy describes two different products.** "Set up your workspace and invite your team" and "Connect calendars, chat tools" describe collaboration software. "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" describes a shop. A visitor who reads both will wonder whether they're on the right help site. The headline "How can we help?" and the line "Browse a topic or search the guides" could sit on anyone's page, so nothing else tells them either. The fix: make the topic copy describe one product, in that product's own terms. Then use the subline for something specific, such as the most common task or how fast a person replies. Defect, plus a copy note.

**Smaller things.**
- Six of the eight card descriptions end with a word or two alone on the last line ("your team.", "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."), so the grid looks ragged. Edit the copy, or set the measure, so each description fits on one line or fills two (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- The cards are white on a near-white background with a hairline border that looks well under 3:1. They also have no other sign that they are links: no chevron, no "View articles", no colored title. The edge that tells you a card is a clickable thing is nearly invisible (WCAG 1.4.11; Norman, signifiers). Darken the border or the page tint, or add a small arrow or article count to each card.
- The search field has only placeholder text and no magnifying-glass icon or submit button. A lone search field under "How can we help?" mostly explains itself, but a search icon inside the field would confirm what it is and how to run it.

**What's working.**
- The hero is clear: one serif headline, one subline, one wide search field, all on a dark band. There is no doubt about where to start. Keep the search field this wide and this prominent.
- The grid is tight. Card edges line up with the logo on the left and the nav on the right, the gutters are equal, and icon, title and description sit in the same places in every card. That regularity is why eight topics scan in a couple of seconds.
- The serif headline against the sans-serif body gives the page a bit of voice without clashing.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste.* Treat the search field as the product's best feature. Show three or four real example queries under it as clickable chips ("Reset two-step sign-in", "Download an invoice"). On a help center that is the fastest way to prove you have the answer, and it does it in the customer's own words.
2. *Taste.* Give the brand one thing it owns. The navy, Inter and slate line icons are the default help-center kit. One distinctive accent, such as a color for the card titles, an illustration in the hero, or a warmer voice in the headline, would make this feel like Harbor's help center rather than anyone's. People trust support more when it feels like it comes from the people who built the product.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the search suggests results as you type, the hover and focus states of the cards and search field, and how the 4-column grid reflows on a phone.
