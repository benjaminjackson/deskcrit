**First read.** The eye lands on the white serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops straight to the search field, then crosses the eight topic cards left to right. It reads as a help-center home page for a workspace or team SaaS product. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy. It also feels anonymous: take off the "Harbor" wordmark and this could be any company's support page.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: someone who has a specific problem with Harbor should reach the right article as fast as possible, either by searching or by picking a topic.

**Verdict.** As a piece of layout it works. The hierarchy is clear, the grid is clean and the type is well paired. Where it falls short is the job itself. It only gives the visitor two routes: write a search query, or guess which of eight overlapping categories holds their problem. It never shows the answers most people came for.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing on the page goes straight to an answer.** Defect. Everything below the search is a category, so every route takes at least two more clicks: open a topic, then look through its list. Most people arrive at a help center with one of a handful of common problems: they can't sign in, sync broke, they need an invoice, they want to track an order. The page knows those problems, because the card descriptions name them ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Fix sync problems"), but it shows them as grey captions you can't click. The fix should put the top five or so tasks one click away, either as a "Popular articles" row between the hero and the topics or as the descriptions turned into real links. Common sense, not a cited rule: the visitor came for an answer, not for a map of the documentation.
2. **The topics overlap, and the set blurs what Harbor is.** Defect (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). Someone whose calendar has stopped syncing could reasonably pick "Integrations" ("Connect calendars…") or "Troubleshooting" ("Fix sync problems"). Someone who can't log in could pick "Account and sign-in" or "Troubleshooting". Each wrong guess costs a full page load and a back click. "Troubleshooting" is a type of article, while the other seven are areas of the product, so it cuts across all of them. Separately, "Orders and shipping" next to "workspace", "invite your team" and "chat tools" makes a stranger wonder whether Harbor ships physical goods or is software. The fix is to sort the topics on one axis, by product area, and send troubleshooting into each one ("Integrations → sync problems"). If Harbor really does ship things, the "Getting started" description should say so, so that card doesn't look like it belongs to another company.

**Smaller things.**
- Short last lines in five card descriptions ("recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages.") make the cards look unfinished. Rewrite them or let the lines break more evenly (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- "Browse by topic" (about 18 px bold) and the card titles (about 16 px bold), both in the same dark ink, look almost the same, so the section heading barely sits above what it introduces. Make it clearly bigger or set it apart by colour or case.
- The search field's only label is its placeholder, and it has no search icon or submit button. Add a magnifier icon at least, and keep a visible label or the headline tied to the field so the prompt doesn't vanish once typing starts (NN/g on placeholders).
- The hero copy is three generic lines in a row: "How can we help?", "Browse a topic or search the guides." and "Search for articles". The middle line just describes the page the visitor is already looking at. Replace it, or the placeholder, with a real example in the visitor's own words ("Try 'reset two-step sign-in'"), which shows what search can do and what Harbor is.

**What's working.**
- **The type pairing.** A bold serif headline over a neutral sans for everything else gives the page one warm, human moment without adding a third voice. Keep the serif to that one line.
- **The grid.** The wordmark, the "Browse by topic" heading and the first card column share one left edge at about 64 px, and the nav and last card column share the right edge. The page reads as one ordered thing.
- **The icon set.** All eight icons use one stroke weight, one size and one colour, so they help you scan without competing with the titles.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the hero belong to Harbor.** Taste. The navy band and "How can we help?" are the default help-center look, so nothing tells the visitor they're in the right place for this product. One owned detail would do it: the product's name or voice in the headline, a brand colour on the search field's focus, or a line that names what Harbor does. That would make the page feel like part of the product rather than a hosted template.
2. **Make the hero earn its 250 px.** Taste. Right now the band holds a single field. Putting three or four of the most-searched questions under the field as quick links would turn the most valuable space on the page into answers, and it would also solve the first finding without adding a new section.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are fully clickable and show a hover or focus state.
- Whether the search suggests results as you type, which would soften the first finding a lot.
- Whether the content below this view (popular articles, a contact route) already exists.
