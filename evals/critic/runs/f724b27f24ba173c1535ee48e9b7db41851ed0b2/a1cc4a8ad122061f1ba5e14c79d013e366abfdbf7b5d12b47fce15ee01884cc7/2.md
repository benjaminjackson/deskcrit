**First read.** The eye goes straight to "How can we help?" in the navy band, drops to the white search field, then moves to the 4×2 grid of topic cards. Within a second it reads as a product's support home page: calm, orderly and trustworthy, and very familiar. Nothing is hard to find and nothing surprises.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the help-center home for existing customers of a product called Harbor. They arrive with a problem and need to search or pick a topic within seconds.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The focal point is clear, the one action (search) sits right under it, and the topics are scannable and plainly worded. What holds it back is not a defect. Below the hero it uses several text sizes that are too close together, and the page is so close to the standard help-center template that it does nothing Harbor-specific for someone who arrives with a problem.

**What would move it most**
1. **Below the hero, the type sizes are too close to work as separate levels.** I count five sizes within a few pixels of each other: the "Harbor Help Center" wordmark (~20 px), "Browse by topic" (~18), the card titles (~16), the nav links (~15) and the card descriptions (~14). Most of them are also the same dark bold. So "Browse by topic" barely sits above "Getting started" and the other card titles, and the wordmark and section heading look like near-twins. Below the headline the page reads as one flat layer, not "section, then items, then detail." *Convention* (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). The fix is to keep three levels below the hero and move them apart. Make the section heading clearly larger than the card titles, or make it a small, quieter label. Let card title vs. description be carried by weight and colour, as it already is, rather than by another near-size.

**Smaller things.**
- **Short last lines in the card descriptions.** "data." (Privacy and security), "where." (Notifications), "messages." (Troubleshooting), plus "recovery." and "receipts." each sit alone on the last line, which makes the row of cards ragged (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrite the copy to fit one line, or balance the breaks so the last line holds two or three words.
- **The topic list doesn't add up to one product.** "Set up your workspace and invite your team", "Connect calendars, chat tools" and "Fix sync problems" describe a collaboration app, but "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" describes a shop. A stranger can't tell what Harbor is. If both are real, fine. If the shipping card is leftover template copy, replace it (Norman, *Design of Everyday Things*, conceptual model).
- **The subtitle repeats what the page already shows.** "Browse a topic or search the guides" says what the search field and the "Browse by topic" grid already make obvious. Either cut it or give it a job, such as example searches. This is my taste call.

**What's working.**
- **One clear entry point.** The navy band gives the headline and search field all the contrast on the page, and the field is the only white shape in it. There is never a question of where to start. Keep the dark band and the single field.
- **Topic labels in the reader's own words.** "Account and sign-in", then "Passwords, two-step sign-in, and recovery". Each card names the things a user would type, not internal category names. The descriptions are concrete, short verb phrases ("Track a delivery or start a return").
- **Tidy, consistent grid.** Gutters are equal, the cards share one left edge with the section heading and the wordmark, and the line icons share one stroke weight and style. The serif headline against the sans everywhere else is a clean, deliberate contrast, not a clash.

**Where it could go further**
1. **Put the likely answer in front of people before they search.** People come here with a problem. A short "Popular right now" list or a one-line service status under the search field turns "go hunting" into "it's right there." That is the biggest step up from a competent help center. The "Status" link already exists, so surface what it would say. (Taste.)
2. **Make the search field show what to type.** "Search for articles" is the generic placeholder found on every help center. Examples from the customer's own world, such as "Try 'reset password' or 'where's my order'", tell people the search understands their words and get them typing sooner. A visible search icon or button would also help mouse users who don't think to press Enter. (Taste.)
3. **Give Harbor one thing it owns.** The navy band, centered "How can we help?" and icon-card grid could belong to any product. One distinctive move, such as a brand colour for the icons or a headline in Harbor's own voice, would make customers feel they're in Harbor's place rather than a generic help-desk template. (Taste.)

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus styling on the search field and cards, which matters because keyboard users will tab straight to search.
- Whether the empty band at the bottom of the frame leads into more content, such as popular articles or a contact block. Check in a live browser.
