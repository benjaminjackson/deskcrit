**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big white headline, "See where every dollar goes this month", then down the subhead to the white "Try it free" button. Then it drifts right into the empty half of the blue band and finds nothing there. It reads as a landing page for a budgeting app. It's clean and confident, but it could be any fintech startup's page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the first screen of a marketing page for a consumer budgeting app, meant to get people who are curious about their spending to start a free trial.

**Verdict.** The words and the type do their job, and the layout is tidy. What holds it back is that the page never shows the product. The right half of the hero is empty, so a promise to show you where your money goes stays something you have to take on trust.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero makes a visual promise and shows nothing (defect).** The headline promises a view: *see* where every dollar goes. But everything sits in the left 60% of the blue band, and the right 40% (roughly x 800–1280, y 62–462) is flat blue. The viewer has to imagine the product, and the page looks unbalanced, with all the weight on the left and nothing to answer it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). That empty area isn't white space put there on purpose to set off the text, as in Ruder's tension between printed and unprinted areas. It's space left over. Fill it with the one thing the headline promises: this month's spending broken down by category, with one category nearing its limit and the heads-up alert showing. Then the picture and the headline make one argument. If a picture is meant to be there and didn't load in this capture, the fix is to make sure it loads reliably, and to check it in a live browser.
2. **"Try it free" leaves open what the viewer is committing to (defect, copy).** "Free" can mean a free plan or a trial that ends, and nothing nearby says whether a card is needed. People remember that word and hold you to it. If they read "free" as a free plan and then get billed after 14 days, the brand loses the trust it needs to ask for bank access. Say it on or right under the button: "Start free — 30 days, no card", or "Free for one account". Also tie the ask to what the viewer already has, for example "Connect your bank and see last month in 2 minutes". That lets them judge the value against their own spending instead of taking the claim on faith.

**Smaller things.**
- "Read-only access" is the strongest answer to the biggest worry people have about giving an app their bank login, and it sits third in a row of equal features. Move it first, or put a one-line version of it near the buttons.

**What's working.**
- **The headline and subhead.** The headline is concrete and has a timeframe. The subhead spells out the whole mechanism in one sentence with verbs and a "you": connect, set a limit, get warned before you go over. Keep that plain, spoken voice.
- **Button hierarchy.** The solid white "Try it free" next to the outlined "Watch the tour" makes one primary action clear at a glance.
- **One left edge and a clear type scale.** The logo, headline, subhead, buttons and first feature column all start at x = 64. There are about four sizes with large steps between them: headline, subhead, feature heading, body. Bold sets the feature headings apart from their body text. Keep that discipline as you add things.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the alert the hero image (taste).** The product's one distinctive move is the heads-up *before* you overspend. Showing that moment, with a category bar at 90% and the notification, sells the difference from every "see your spending" competitor. A generic dashboard wouldn't.
2. **Give the brand something besides a stock electric blue (taste).** Saturated blue and Inter is the default look of fintech. When someone compares three budgeting apps, nothing here will help them remember this one. Commit to one thing the brand owns, such as a colour, a wordmark with some character, or a way of drawing money. Don't settle for the palette everyone in the category already uses.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half of the hero and the blank band below the features are the design, or a visual and a section that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check both in a live browser.
- Focus states on the nav links and buttons, and how the hero reflows on a phone.
