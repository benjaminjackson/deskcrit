The walk and deep read are done, and I'm writing up the critique. The piece is a short how-to guide, and one issue clearly comes first: the line length.

**First read.** My eye goes straight to the large blue "Planning a vegetable bed", then steps down the three blue subheads. It reads as a short beginner's how-to on a website. It's calm, orderly and easy to take in, but it's also generic: apart from the words, nothing says "garden", and the blue feels like a software help page.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a short web article that tells a first-time gardener how to set up a vegetable bed, read top to bottom on a desktop screen.

**Verdict.** It does its job. Hierarchy, grouping and copy are all sound, and a beginner can follow it on the first pass. The one real drag is that the body lines are too long for comfortable reading.

**What would move it most**
1. **Body lines run too long.** This is a convention, and here it has a real cost. The two-line paragraphs run about 90 characters a line ("Dig to the depth of a spade and mix in compost from last year's heap. Rake the surface level"). That's well past Bringhurst's 45–75 range (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2; about 66 is ideal). On a page this short you won't lose readers, but each return sweep is long, and in a how-to people read one instruction, look away to do it, and come back. Long lines make it harder to find your place again. Bring the text column's max width down to roughly 600–640 px at this size, aiming for about 65 characters. Leave the headline alone; it can stay wider.

**Smaller things.**
- **Heading colour (taste).** Every heading, H1 included, is set in a saturated blue that most people know as the web's link colour. On a gardening page it makes the headings look clickable and sets a corporate tone the subject doesn't call for (Itten on colour supporting the message). Use a deep green or earthy dark, or set the headings in the body's near-black and let size and weight do the work.
- **Straight apostrophe in "year's" (common sense; no checked source covers curly quotes).** Use the true curly apostrophe (’). It's invisible at body size, but it will show once the typesetting is finished elsewhere.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale.** I count three clear levels (roughly 48 / 30 / 17 px), with bold and colour on top of size. That's exactly enough for this page and there are no near-duplicate sizes. Keep it that way.
- **Grouping by space.** Each subhead sits tight on its own paragraph, about 10 px above it, with clearly more space above it before the previous section. So each heading belongs to its text at a glance (Ruder's gathered space, with large open areas rather than even gaps).
- **The copy gives exact, doable numbers.** "Six hours of sun", "the depth of a spade", "leave it to settle for a week", "twice a week, rather than a little every day". Each line tells a beginner exactly what to do, using things they already have (sticks, string, a spade). That's the hardest part of a how-to, and it's done well.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Show that it's a sequence (taste).** The sections are an order of work: plan, prepare, plant, water. They're styled as parallel topics, though, and the opening paragraph has no heading of its own. Numbering the steps and giving the intro a heading such as "Choosing a spot" would tell a beginner there are four steps and which one they're on. That's the main thing a first-timer needs from this page.
2. **Give it one colour that belongs to the subject (taste).** It could replace the link blue or be added alongside the near-black text. A single garden-coloured accent, used on the headings or step numbers, would make the page feel like it's about growing food rather than a template. It would also stop it looking like anyone's help centre.
