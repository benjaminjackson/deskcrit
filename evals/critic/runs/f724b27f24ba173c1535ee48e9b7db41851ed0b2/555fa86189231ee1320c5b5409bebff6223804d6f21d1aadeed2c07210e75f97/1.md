**First read.** Both versions open the same way. The eye lands on the big serif headline "Small-batch ice cream, made on Mill Street", drops to the three red script subheads, and then to the hours bar. It reads as the homepage of a local ice-cream shop and feels warm and handmade. The difference shows up one step later. In version 1 the three paragraphs are quiet, easy reading. In version 2 they are dense, dark blocks of brush script that pull level with the subheads, and reading slows to a crawl.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage of a small ice-cream shop. It has to tell a local passer-by what makes the shop different (local milk, market fruit, returnable tubs) and when it's open.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job and version 2 does not. The only change is that version 2 sets the three body paragraphs in the same script face as the subheads. That makes the reason to visit, which is the actual content, hard to read and flattens the page's order of importance.

**What changed and why it matters**

1. **Script body text (version 2).** This is a defect. The three paragraphs under "Our milk", "Our fruit" and "Our tubs" are four or five lines each, set in a heavy brush script. Joined, slanted letters with tight counters take longer to decode. "Every batch starts with whole milk… eleven miles up the valley" becomes something you work through, not something you skim. This is the copy that sets the shop apart, and readers with dyslexia or low vision will skip it. Script faces are for a few words of display, and paragraphs need a face made for reading (Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1). The fix is to go back to version 1's sans for running text and keep the script for the logo and the three short subheads.

2. **The subheads no longer stand out from the body (version 2).** This is a defect. In version 1 the red script subheads sit clearly above the plain sans body: a different face, a different colour and a bigger size. That gives three clean entry points you can scan across the row. In version 2 subhead and body are the same face, only a step apart in size, and the body is set so heavy and dark that the red subheads barely stand out. The row reads as three grey-brown blocks, not three labelled facts. Splitting the faces again, as version 1 does, fixes this.

Version 2 also loses the typographic plan. In version 1 each face has one job: script for the brand's voice, serif for the headline, sans for reading. Version 2 spreads the script across the content but leaves the nav and the hours bar in sans, so the script looks like a costume, not a voice.

**Remaining findings for version 1**

**What would move it most**
1. **No next step.** This is taste and copy. After reading the page the visitor has nowhere to go. There's no "See today's flavours" link and no address or map beside the hours. "Flavours" and "Our shop" sit only in the small top nav. For a shop page, the hours bar should sit next to where the shop is, and the page should end with one specific ask, such as "See this week's flavours". Even better would be one that ties to what the visitor already knows, like what's on the board today.

**Smaller things**
- The lower third of the screen is empty below the hours bar. That's probably just the viewport, but on a page this short it makes the hours bar look like a footer, not information.
- The tubs paragraph ends with "or drop it in the box by the door", which reads as an afterthought. Say what the box is for ("drop it in the recycling box by the door").

**What's working**
- In version 1 each face has a clear job: script only for the brand and three short labels, a sturdy serif for the one headline, and a plain sans at about 45 characters a line for reading. Keep this.
- The copy is specific and local ("eleven miles up the valley", "fifty pence off your next scoop", "Closed in January while we clean the machines"). It sounds like a real shop, not a template.
- The restrained pink, plum and cream palette matches the product.

**Where it could go further**
1. Show the ice cream (taste). A food shop with no picture is asking the visitor to imagine the product. One honest photo of a scoop or the flavour board would do more for "small-batch" than any line of text.
2. Give the plum-red a single job (taste). It currently colours the logo and all three subheads. Saving it for the logo and one action would give the page a clear place to end.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't tell whether the empty band at the bottom is the page ending or just the viewport.
