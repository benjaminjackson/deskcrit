**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", moves right to the white card, and drops to the blue "Send message". Within two seconds it reads as a small studio's contact page. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, but also anonymous: apart from the name in the top left, nothing on the page tells you what Fernwick makes or what it is like to work with.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: this is the contact page of a small design or creative studio. Its job is to turn a prospective client's interest into an enquiry the studio can act on.

**Verdict.** It works. The page is clear, the order of things is right, and nothing gets in the visitor's way. The biggest problem is that the page asks for the two facts the studio most needs, budget and timeline, but gives them no fields. So most enquiries will arrive without them, and the first reply will be spent asking.

**What would move it most**
1. **Budget and timeline are asked for in the copy but have no fields** (the paragraph under the headline compared with the three fields in the card). The paragraph says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them," and then offers only Name, Email and an empty Message box. Visitors read that sentence once, start typing about their project, and forget it. The ones who remember must decide for themselves how to write a budget. The studio ends up with free-text enquiries it can't sort, and each one needs a second email. Ask for both in the form itself: a short budget picker with ranges and a "not sure yet" option, and a timeline picker. Then the sentence in the paragraph can go. *Defect* against the page's own goal. The checklist has no approved source for this; it's common sense.
2. **The copy could sit on any agency's site.** "Tell us about your project" and "Send message" are the defaults for this kind of page. A visitor comparing three studios learns nothing here that sets this one apart, and the blank Message box leaves them to work out what a useful brief looks like. The reply promise is the one specific line, and it's good: "within two working days." Build on it. Give the Message field a prompt in the visitor's own terms, such as "What are you making, and what's not working about it now?" Add a line on what happens after they send, such as "You'll hear from [a named person], not a sales inbox." Name the button for its result, for example "Send to the studio." *Taste*, argued on what it costs: a visitor who can't tell this studio apart from the others has no reason to choose it.

**Smaller things.**
- **Navigation, top right:** "Contact" looks exactly like Work, Services and About, so nothing shows which page you're on. Mark the current item with one clear cue, such as a bolder weight or a bar (Nielsen 1, via Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- **Form fields:** no field is marked as required or optional. With three fields that all look required, the cost is small. But once you add budget and timeline, mark the optional ones "(optional)" (Budiu, NN/g "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- **Input borders:** the grey field outlines look pale against the white card, possibly under 3:1. Measure the border colour against #fff, and darken it if it falls short (WCAG 1.4.11).
- **Card width:** the card stops at about x=1068, while the navigation runs to about x=1224. This leaves a strip on the right that lines up with nothing, and the card looks placed by eye. Either run the card to the content's right edge or set both columns on a shared grid (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- **Headline break:** "Tell us about your / project" leaves "project" alone on the second line. A little more width, or breaking after "about", would keep the phrase together.

**What's working.**
- **The two-column split.** The pitch is on the left, the action on the right, and the headline is the clear entry point. The eye path (headline → paragraph → form → button) needs no help, so keep it.
- **The form basics.** Labels sit above their fields, are bold, and are clearly closer to their own field than to the next. There's one primary button, solid and plainly pressable. This is the textbook layout, and it's right.
- **"We read every message and reply within two working days."** It's a specific promise the visitor can hold you to, and the strongest line on the page.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Show one piece of work beside the form** (*taste*). A design studio's contact page is also a sample of its work. One small project image or a client line in the empty left column below the paragraph would remind the visitor why they're writing, at the moment they decide whether to write.
2. **Give the studio one thing it owns visually** (*taste*). Right now the page is Inter, link blue and grey. That's competent, but you could swap the name in the corner and it would be anyone's page. One committed choice, such as a distinctive display face for the headline or a colour that isn't default link blue, would make the page memorable without hurting clarity.
