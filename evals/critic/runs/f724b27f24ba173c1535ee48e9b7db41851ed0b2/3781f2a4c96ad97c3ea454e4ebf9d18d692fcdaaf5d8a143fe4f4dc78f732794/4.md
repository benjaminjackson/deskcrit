**First read.** The eye lands first on the bold black headline "Tell us about your project", moves right to the white form card, and comes down to the blue "Send message" button. It reads at once as a contact page for a small studio. It feels calm, tidy and polite. It also feels anonymous: apart from the wordmark, nothing here says what Fernwick Studio makes or why you'd want them.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the contact page of a design or creative studio's website, there to turn a prospective client into a project enquiry. Correct me if that's wrong.

**Verdict.** As a form, it works: one clear headline, one clear action, a calm column of fields, and an intro that makes a real promise. As a studio's contact page it is weak. It looks like a stock framework template, the layout stops short of its own grid, and the one useful prompt (budget and timeline) sits away from the box where people will type.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page says nothing about the studio, and for a studio that is the pitch.** Grey background, white card, system blue, one sans-serif. Swap in any other company's wordmark and nothing else would need to change. Someone deciding whether to send a brief to a studio treats every page as a sample of its taste. This page asks them to trust the studio without showing a single reason to. *Taste*, but it costs real enquiries. Direction: give the page one thing the studio owns, such as a typeface with character for the headline, a colour that isn't default link blue, or a small piece of recent work or a client line in the empty left column under the intro. Keep it one strong move, not decoration.
2. **The layout stops short and leaves its empty space scattered.** The header runs from 56 to about 1224 px, but the content block ends at the form card's right edge, about 1068 px. That leaves a dead strip of roughly 150 px on the right that lines up with nothing. Below the intro paragraph there is a tall empty area, and below the card another band before the footer. None of these spaces is big or deliberate enough to read as a choice, so the page looks under-filled rather than spacious (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*: one grid governs the page; Ruder: white space gathered, not scattered). *Convention, with a visible cost.* Direction: either run the card out to the header's right edge, or make the empty area one intentional field, for example a narrower centred pair that sits in the space with clear margins on both sides. The left column's empty lower half is the natural place for the content from point 1.
3. **The budget and timeline prompt is in the wrong place, and the Message box gives no help.** The intro asks for "a budget range and a rough timeline", but that line sits about 450 px left of the Message field, and the field itself is blank. People read the headline, jump to the form, and type into an empty box. Many will never connect the two, so the studio gets briefs without the two facts it needs to reply properly. *Defect* (common sense; Nielsen's heuristic 10, help near the step). Direction: put the prompt where people type, either as helper text under the "Message" label or as two short optional fields, Budget and Timeline. Keep the intro for the reply promise.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Contact" is drawn exactly like Work, Services and About, so nothing marks the page you're on. Give the current page one clear cue, such as a bolder weight or a bar (Nielsen heuristic 1; Laubheimer, NN/g menu checklist).
- Form: none of the fields is marked required or optional. Put an asterisk after each required label and explain it once, or add "(optional)" to the optional ones (Budiu, NN/g).
- Input borders: the pale grey outlines on white look under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for on field edges. Check the border colour against white and darken it if it falls short.

**What's working.**
- The intro copy makes a concrete promise ("reply within two working days") and tells people exactly what to include. That beats a generic "Get in touch", so keep the specificity when you move the prompt.
- The form follows the rules: labels above each field, one column, a Message box taller than the single-line fields, and one solid primary button with a verb that says what happens. Don't add more fields than budget and timeline.
- The headline is a direct invitation addressed to the visitor, big and heavy enough to anchor the page without shouting.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the fields and button; what happens after "Send message" (confirmation, errors); how the two columns stack on a phone.
