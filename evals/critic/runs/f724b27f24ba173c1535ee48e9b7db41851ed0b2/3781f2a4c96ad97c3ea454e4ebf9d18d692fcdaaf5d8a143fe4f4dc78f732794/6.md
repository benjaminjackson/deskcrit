**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", drops to the short paragraph under it, then jumps across to the blue "Send message" button. It's plainly a studio's contact page. It feels calm, competent and anonymous. Nothing on it says who Fernwick is or why you'd write to them rather than another studio.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: to get a prospective client to send an enquiry the studio can act on, ideally one that includes budget and timeline.

**Verdict.** It works as a form: it's clear, sensibly ordered, and easy to fill in. Its biggest weakness is that it asks for budget and timeline but gives them no place on the form, and its copy could be any studio's. So it will collect enquiries, but not the ones it says it wants, and it does nothing to make anyone want to send one.

**What would move it most**
1. **It asks for information the form has no place for.** The left-column paragraph says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form has only Name, Email and an empty Message box, with no prompt in it. By the time the viewer reaches the Message field, that instruction is in another column about 450 px to the left. Most people will type "Hi, we'd like a website" and leave both out. Then the studio's first reply has to ask for exactly what this page meant to collect, which eats into the two-day turnaround. This is a defect. Help belongs next to the step it's about (Nielsen, heuristic 10). Fix: put the ask where the typing happens. Either add two short optional fields (a budget range picker and a timeline field) or put the prompt as hint text under the Message label. Two fields also tell you which leads are serious before you read a word.
2. **The copy is generic, and the page gives nobody a reason to choose this studio.** "Tell us about your project" plus a name/email/message form appears on thousands of sites. The only line with any character is the promise to "reply within two working days", and that's a good one: specific and checkable. Everything else leaves the viewer with no idea what Fernwick does, who will read the message, or what happens after they send it. A visitor comparing three studios gets nothing here to tip them. There's also no direct email or phone number for someone who doesn't want to fill in a form. Taste. Fix: make the headline or the paragraph say something only this studio could say, such as what kinds of projects you take, who replies (a named person), and what the first step after replying is (for example a 30-minute call). Then show a plain email address under the paragraph as an alternative.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Contact" is drawn exactly like Work, Services and About, so nothing shows the visitor which page they're on. Mark it with a bolder weight or a heavier underline (Laubheimer, NN/g, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Form: no field is marked required or optional. With three fields, all presumably required, this costs little now, but it will matter once you add budget and timeline fields, which should be optional. Put "(optional)" after those labels (Budiu, NN/g).
- Input borders: the pale grey field outlines on white look to be around 2:1. WCAG 1.4.11 asks for 3:1 for the edges that show where a field is. Measure the border colour and darken it if it falls short.
- Right edge of the card (~x 1068): it lines up with nothing. The nav runs out to ~1224, so there's a dead strip of about 150 px on the right, and the layout looks like it stopped short rather than being set on a grid (Müller-Brockmann). Take the card's right edge to the nav's right edge, or narrow the whole page to match the card.
- Headline: "project" sits alone on line 2. Rebreak it as "Tell us about / your project" or widen the column so the second line carries more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2, applied to a heading).

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean: one heavy headline, a quiet paragraph, bold field labels set above their fields, and a single strong blue primary button. There's no doubt where to start or how to finish. Keep it.
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is the best line on the page. It's a concrete promise the viewer can hold you to. Build the rest of the copy at that level of specificity.
- The palette is restrained: one neutral grey ground, white for the card, and one blue for actions and links. Links are underlined as well as coloured, so they read as links without relying on colour.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Make the page sound like a person at the studio. For a small studio, the reason to write in is the people. A named contact, perhaps with a small photo next to the reply promise, turns "submit a form" into "write to Sam", and that's what makes a hesitant client hit send. Taste.
2. Give the left column one line of proof, such as a recent client or project type, linked to Work. At the moment the large empty area under the paragraph does nothing. One short credential there would make the space count and answer "are they right for my project?" before the visitor has to leave the page to find out. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus indicators on the fields and the button.
- What the validation errors and the after-sending confirmation look like. On a contact form, the confirmation is where the two-day promise should be repeated.
