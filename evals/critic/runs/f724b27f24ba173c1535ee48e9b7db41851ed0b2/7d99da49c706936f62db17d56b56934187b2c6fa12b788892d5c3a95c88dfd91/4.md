**First read.** The eye has no starting point. It drops onto "Changing your pickup time" only because it's top-left, then moves down a block of grey-black text that is all one size. These are FAQ-style answers for a bakery's pickup orders. They feel calm and plain, but unfinished, like body copy without its styling, more draft than page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help or FAQ section on a bakery's online ordering site. Customers scan it to find the one answer they need about pickup, collection, allergens, or cancelling.

**Verdict.** It doesn't yet do its job as something to scan. Questions and answers look the same, so the reader has to read everything to find their topic. And the cancellation line, the one people will hold the bakery to, can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings don't read as headings.** This is a defect. "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two headings use the same face, weight and colour as the answers under them. They even look slightly smaller (about 18 px against about 19 px). Someone looking for "how do I cancel" can't run their eye down the headings. They get eight lines of equal weight and have to read them in order. It's spacing alone that pairs each heading with its answer, and that spacing is good. But nothing marks which line in each pair is the question. Dair (*Design with Type*, ch. 8–9) says a level should differ from the text by more than size. Here it doesn't even differ by size. Fix: make the headings a different weight, such as semibold, while keeping roughly the same size, or make the answers a lighter grey. Then the four topics stand out as a list you can scan at a glance. There is also no page title such as "Pickup and orders", so nothing tells a new arrival what this block is. (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: order of importance shown by difference.)
2. **The cancellation rule can be read two ways.** This is a defect, in the copy. "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" could mean 24 hours before the pickup time, or any time before the day of pickup. A customer who cancels at 9 pm for a 10 am pickup will think they're in time under the second reading and be refused under the first. The line also never says *how* to cancel. Fix: give a clock time or an exact rule, such as "until 24 hours before your pickup time" or "until 6 pm the day before". Then name the action, for example "Open your order and choose Cancel". "Along with a name" has the same gap: whose name, theirs or yours? Say "your name".
3. **The allergy answer only helps people who are already in the shop.** This is a defect, in the copy. "Every loaf has a card by the till" tells someone ordering online that they can't check what's in their bread until they've ordered and are standing at the counter. For someone with an allergy, that's exactly backwards. And "every loaf" leaves out anything that isn't a loaf. Fix: say where online the ingredients and allergens are listed before ordering, and keep the card and "ask the baker" as a second step.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the one-line answers ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run about 95 characters, well past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the column to about 600–650 px at this size.
- Runt: "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergy answer, and "after the chosen time." nearly does the same. Narrowing the column or rewording will fix both.

**What's working.**
- The spacing does the grouping properly: about 10 px between each heading and its answer, about 30 px between topics. Each pair reads as a unit. Keep that ratio when you restyle the headings.
- The answers are short and concrete, and "Open your order and choose a new slot from the list" names the actual action. That's the model for the cancellation answer.
- The text is near-black on white in a plain sans, comfortable to read, with no decoration to get in the way.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "Open your order" links to the order. If it's plain text, the reader has to go looking for where their order is.
- How the column behaves on a phone, where most pickup questions will be asked.
