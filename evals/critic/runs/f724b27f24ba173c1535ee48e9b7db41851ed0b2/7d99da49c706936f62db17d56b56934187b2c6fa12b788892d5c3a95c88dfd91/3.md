**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It starts at the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time", and reads down as if it were one long paragraph. Only at the second or third block do you notice that some lines are headings. It reads as a help or FAQ panel for collecting a bakery order: calm and plain, but flat. Everything sits in the left two-thirds of the frame, with a lot of white below.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section on a small bakery's ordering site. Customers who already have an order scan it to find their one question (pickup, proxy collection, allergies, cancelling) and get a clear answer.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The headings look almost exactly like the answers, so a scanning customer can't find their question at a glance. Two answers that customers will act on can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings and answers look the same, and there's no page title.** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two headings look about the same size as the answers, in the same regular weight and the same near-black. Each heading reads as one more line of text. The block is built to be scanned, but scanning doesn't work: someone looking for "cancel" has to read every line. Nothing at the top says what this is either, such as "Collecting your order" or "Questions about your order", so the viewer has to work that out from the content (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Dair's point applies: a heading within a pixel or two of its text's size looks like an accident, not a choice. **Defect.** Fix: make each heading obviously different from its answer in one clear way, such as semibold, or a clear step up in size. Bolder is usually enough here, and it keeps the page quiet (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). Add one page title above the four blocks, clearly the largest thing on the page, so the eye has a place to start (Tschichold, *The New Typography*).

2. **Two answers customers will act on can be read two ways.** *Defect.*
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead": a day ahead of what? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or by the end of the day before pickup. Someone who reads it the second way and cancels the evening before a morning pickup may find they've already paid for bread that was baked. This is a deadline people will hold the bakery to, so give it in plain terms, e.g. "You can cancel until 6 pm the day before your pickup." The section also never says *how* to cancel, while the pickup answer does say how ("Open your order…"). Add the action.
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name": whose name, yours or theirs? If the person collecting gives their own name and staff expect the name on the order, they get turned away at the till. Say which: "…and give your name, as it appears on the order."
   - Smaller point in the same vein: "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" doesn't say what happens after that. The consequence ("after that, we can't keep it for you") is the part the reader actually needs.

3. **The lines are too long.** The single-line answers, "Give the person your order number…" and "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead…", run about 95–100 characters across a column roughly 820 px wide. Bringhurst's comfortable range is 45 to 75 (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). For text this short the eye copes, but it makes the blocks look like strips rather than answers, and it adds to the flat, unscanned feel. **Convention**, with a small cost here. Fix: narrow the column to about 60–70 characters. There is plenty of room for that.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Allergies and ingredients" answer ends with "anything." alone on its last line, a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrowing the measure will probably fix it. If not, rebreak the line.

**What's working.**
- Grouping by space: the gap between a heading and its answer (about 10 px) is clearly smaller than the gap between blocks (about 25 px), so each question stays attached to its answer even though the type doesn't separate them. Keep that ratio when you restyle the headings.
- The voice is plain and human ("After that the bread has already been baked for you"). That line explains the rule instead of just stating it, which makes the policy feel fair. Keep that tone while making the deadlines exact.
- The headings name what the customer wants to do ("Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else") rather than internal categories, so once they stand out, people will find their question.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether this sits inside a larger page with its own title or navigation. If it does, finding 1's missing title may already be handled, but the headings still need to look different from the answers.
