**First read.** I don't land anywhere first. The eye starts top-left at "Changing your pickup time" only because that's where reading starts, not because anything pulls it there. Then it slides down a grey wall of eight lines that all look like the same kind of text. After a moment it reads as a short help or FAQ section for a bakery's order pickup. It feels calm and plain, but flat: you have to read to find the structure instead of seeing it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section on a bakery's ordering site. Customers who have already ordered scan it to find the one answer they need (change the time, send someone else, allergies, cancel).

**Verdict.** It doesn't yet do its job of letting someone scan. The questions are set the same as the answers, and look slightly smaller, so a customer can't jump to their topic. They have to read every line. Two answers also leave the facts a customer will act on (the cancellation deadline, what to show at the counter) open to two readings.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings look the same as the answers, so the page can't be scanned.** *Defect.* "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are in the same regular weight and the same dark grey as the text under them. They look a pixel or two smaller (about 18 px against 19 px). Only the extra space above each one tells you it's a heading. A customer looking for "cancel" has to read down the column line by line, which is the one thing a help page should spare them. This is Dair's weak contrast (*Design with Type*, ch. 8): a difference of one step looks like an accident. It also breaks Bringhurst's call for sizes from a scale (§3.1.1). Having two sizes this close is worse than having one. Fix: make each heading differ from its answer in a way you see at a glance. The usual move is a heavier weight (semibold) at the same or a clearly larger size, and/or setting the answers back in a lighter grey. Never make a heading smaller than its text.
2. **Nothing leads the page.** *Convention (Tschichold, Hofmann).* There's no title such as "Collecting your order" above the four items. So four equal headings compete to be seen first, and nothing tells a visitor arriving from a link or search what this block covers. Add one clearly dominant title, and the page gets the three distinct levels it needs: title, question, answer.
3. **Two answers can be read two ways, and both are about things customers act on.** *Defect.*
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead": a day ahead of what? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot or any time on the day before. A customer who picks the wrong reading finds out at the till that they've paid for bread they can't cancel. State the exact cutoff, e.g. "You can cancel until [time] the day before your pickup."
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name": whose name, theirs or yours? Say "along with your name" if that's what you mean.
   - Smaller cost: "Open your order" doesn't say where. Name the place (the confirmation email, or "My orders") or link to it. The allergy answer only helps someone already standing in the shop. Someone ordering online with an allergy learns nothing before they pay. Either point to where the ingredients are listed online or say plainly that they're only shown in the shop.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the first answer runs about 90 characters before it wraps, and the one-line answers about 95. That's past Bringhurst's 45 to 75 (§2.1.2). Narrow the column to about 640–680 px at this size.
- Runt: "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer. Narrowing the column will probably fix it; if not, rebreak the line or rewrite it.

**What's working.**
- The spacing does the grouping: each question sits close to its answer (about 10 px) and far from the next item (about 35 px). Keep those proportions when you add weight to the headings.
- The answers are short, use "you", and give reasons ("the bread has already been baked for you"). This voice suits a small bakery, and the cancellation answer explains the rule instead of just stating it.
- Dark text on white in one plain sans is easy to read. Nothing needs to change there.
