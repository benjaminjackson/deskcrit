**First read.** Where your eye lands first is the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time", but only because it comes first. Nothing on the page is bigger, bolder, or darker than anything else. It reads as a bakery's pickup and order help page, a short FAQ. The questions don't separate from the answers, so on a first glance it looks like nine plain sentences of the same weight stacked down the left side. It feels calm and tidy, but you have to read it all to find your way around it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the help section of a small bakery's online-order site or confirmation page. A customer who has already ordered comes here to find the one answer they need and act on it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. A page like this exists to be scanned, but the headings are set in the same size, weight and colour as the answers, so there's nothing to scan by. Two of the answers that customers will act on can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings look exactly like the answers.** This applies to "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order". They are the same size (about 17 px by eye), the same regular weight and the same near-black as the answers under them. The only thing marking them out is the slightly larger gap above each pair. A customer looking for "cancel" can't skim down the headings. They have to read each pair, and the page looks like one run of sentences rather than four topics. There is also no page title, so nothing says "Your order: questions" at the top.
   - **Kind:** Defect (they are meant to differ and don't). Dair, *Design with Type* (ch. 8), says a contrast that isn't obvious is worse than none. Tschichold, *The New Typography*, says logical order is shown by differences of size.
   - **Fix:** Make each heading differ from its text in a way you see at a glance, such as semibold or bold, a clearly bigger size, or both. Add one title above them that is clearly the first thing you see. The spacing already groups each heading with its answer, so keep it as it is.
2. **The rules a customer will be held to are vague.** Kind: defect, because these are deadlines and conditions people act on.
   - **"Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead."** Ahead of what? It could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or by the end of the day before. It also never says *how* to cancel. Someone who reads it the generous way and tries to cancel the evening before a morning pickup gets refused. After that, "the bread has already been baked for you" reads as a scolding.
   - **"…along with a name."** It could mean the collector's name or yours. If the person collecting gives their own name and the counter expects yours, they leave without the bread.
   - **"Holds orders for two hours after the chosen time."** This leaves out the consequence: what happens after two hours, and whether you're charged.
   - **Fix:** Name the cut-off exactly, for example "until 6 pm the day before your pickup". Say whose name. Say what happens when the hold runs out. Give the action, for example "Open your order and choose Cancel".
3. **The last line looks like a leftover note to the designer.** The line in question is "Each heading here stands out clearly, so the page is easy to scan." It says nothing about bread or orders, and what it claims is false on this very page (see finding 1). A customer reads it as filler, or as a sign the page wasn't checked.
   - **Kind:** Defect (common sense; the catalog has no entry for unfinished copy).
   - **Fix:** Delete it. If something belongs at the foot of the page, make it the next step a customer needs, such as how to contact the bakery about an order.

**Smaller things.**
- **Lines are too long.** The longest lines, the "Collecting…" and "Cancelling…" answers, run to roughly 95 characters. That's past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the text column to about 600–640 px at this size.
- **A lone word ends a paragraph.** "anything." sits on a line of its own under "Allergies and ingredients". Rewrite the answer or adjust the column width so the last line carries more than one word.
- **The allergy answer may not help the people who need it.** It sends people to "a card by the till", but someone ordering online can't see that card before they order. Taste and content: say where the ingredients can be read online, or give a phone number.

**What's working.**
- **The spacing groups each pair correctly.** There is a tight gap of about 10 px between heading and answer and a gap of about 35 px between pairs. That's the right structure. Keep it when you add weight to the headings.
- **The text is plain and easy to read.** It's one clean sans in near-black on white at a comfortable size and line spacing, with nothing decorative in the way. It suits a small bakery's practical help page.
- **The answers are short and spoken.** "Give the person your order number" reads like the baker talking. Keep that voice when you tighten the rules in finding 2.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Live links.** I can't tell whether "Open your order" or "the list" are meant to link to the order page. If they are, they need to look like links.
