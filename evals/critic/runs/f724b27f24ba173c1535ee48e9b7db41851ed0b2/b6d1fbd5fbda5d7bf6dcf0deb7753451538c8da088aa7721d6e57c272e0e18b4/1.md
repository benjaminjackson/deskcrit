**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the big white headline. It drops to the amber "Get tickets" button, then goes back up to the amber date line. It reads as the hero of a small, thoughtful design/dev conference in Edinburgh, and it feels calm and confident. In version 1 the headline looks crushed: the two lines run into each other, and that is the first thing a designer would notice.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing hero for Fieldwork 2027. It should tell people in software and design what the event is, when and where it happens, and get them to buy a ticket.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job better. The two versions are identical except for the space between the headline's two lines, and the space under the headline. In version 1 the lines collide. Version 2 fixes that, and what's left is a clean, working hero.

**What changed, and why version 2 wins**
1. **The headline's two lines collide in version 1** (defect). Between "Three days on building" and "quieter, slower software", the line spacing is below the type size. The descenders of "y" in *days* and "g" in *building* touch or nearly touch the "q", "l", "t" and "f" of the line below. The headline is the focal point and the main promise, so it now has a visible mistake in it. It reads as a dense black-and-white block rather than two lines of words, and it costs a moment to parse. That is the opposite of "quieter, slower". Bringhurst (p. 37) allows negative leading in a title only "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide". Version 2 opens the spacing until every letter clears the next line, while still keeping the heading tighter than body text. It also pushes the subhead and button down about 24 px, so the headline-to-subhead gap now matches the space between the other groups. Keep version 2's setting.

**Remaining findings for version 2**

**What would move it most**
1. **The ask is generic** (taste). "Get tickets" is clear and the button is well placed, but nothing next to it gives the reader a reason to act now. There's no price, no ticket type and no deadline. The same goes for the subhead. "Tools that others rely on every day" is good and specific, but it stops at describing the audience. It never tells readers what they'll leave able to do. Put one concrete thing beside the button, such as the price, an early-bird date, or a named speaker or session. That way readers can judge whether it's worth it on terms they already know.

**Smaller things.** None I'd rank. The empty bottom third and right half of the screen read as deliberate quiet space that fits the theme, not as missing content.

**What's working.**
- **One amber accent with two jobs.** It marks the date and venue line and the button, so the practical facts and the action pair up and nothing else competes.
- **A real type scale.** I count four sizes with clear jumps between them: the display headline, the subhead, the nav and date line, and the button. Weight and colour (amber, pale blue, white) separate the small sizes rather than near-identical point sizes.
- **One left edge at about 96 px.** The wordmark, date line, headline, subhead and button all sit on it, so the column reads as one deliberate stack.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the headline own more of the screen** (taste). The pitch is "quieter, slower", and a larger headline with even more air around it would act that out. Right now it sits at a size that could be any SaaS hero, and the restraint doesn't show yet.
2. **Give the event one visual signature** (taste). Navy with amber is pleasant but could belong to anyone. One mark, texture or typographic gesture tied to "fieldwork" or to Leith would make it recognisably this conference's page rather than a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hit area and focus state of the nav links "Programme · Speakers · Venue · Tickets". The text looks about 17 px tall, so check that each link's clickable area reaches 24 × 24 px (WCAG 2.5.8).
