**First read.** The eye lands on the bold black headline, drops to the green "Feature" kicker above it, and then settles into the body text. Within a second it reads as an online magazine feature: a long-form story about a community garden, for general readers. It feels calm, clean and competent. It also feels anonymous. Strip out the words and this could be a SaaS changelog. The byline says "Photographs by the residents", but there are no photographs anywhere on the screen.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the opening screen of a long-form feature on a web magazine. Its job is to pull a casual visitor into reading a human story about one particular place, and to bring them back as subscribers.

**Verdict.** The typography is mostly sound and the writing is strong. The first screen does not do its job of making you feel the place: it is grey text on white in a stock UI typeface, with no image of an allotment that has a byline credit for photographers. That gap is what most stands between this and a feature someone stops to read.

**What would move it most**
1. **The opening screen has nothing of the story's world in it.** Everything you see is grey text on white in a default UI sans: the wordmark, the nav, the kicker, the headline and three paragraphs. Nothing shows beans, bath tubs, a bus stop or the people. The credit "Photographs by the residents" sits right under the headline and promises pictures, but the opening never shows one. The reader is left with only the prose to persuade them, and the page gives them no reason to believe this story is any different from any other article on any other site. Colour and type carry no warmth for a story about a neighbourhood growing food together (Itten, *The Art of Color*, on colour supporting the intended emotion). Fix: open with one of the residents' photographs, close to the headline and early in the first screen. That photo is the story's own evidence and its strongest hook. If the lead image is meant to be here and just failed to load, then this finding is about the capture; check it live. *Defect* (a promise left unkept on the opening screen) together with *taste* (the mood).
2. **The body text runs too wide for a long read.** The first line of the first paragraph ("Ten years ago … Today forty") is about 83 characters, and the other lines look like 76 to 85. Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 as comfortable, with 66 as ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). Over a long feature, a wide measure makes the jump back to each new line a little harder, and readers drop off at that kind of friction. Fix: narrow the column to about 620–640 px at this size, or raise the body size slightly while keeping the column. Aim for about 65 characters per line. *Convention.*

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" splits the compound "bus line" and leaves "line" alone on the second line, on the most visible element of the page. Break it at a phrase, for example after "allotment" or before "at the end", or rewrite or resize it so it holds together.
- Headline tracking looks like the body-text default at about 44 px bold. Tighten it slightly so the headline sets as one solid shape (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 80; Bringhurst 2.1.1).
- The nav ("Features · Food · Places · Subscribe") doesn't mark Features as the current section. Give the current item one cue, such as weight or an underline (Nielsen Norman Group, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- "Subscribe" is the site's one conversion action, but it is styled exactly like the section links and lost among them. Set it apart from the sections, for example with a text button or by separating it, so a reader who is won over can find it.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is clean.** I count about four sizes: headline about 44, body about 19, byline and nav about 14, kicker about 13. Each step is big enough to have an obvious job, and finer distinctions come from weight and colour (the bold green kicker, the grey byline), not from more sizes. Keep this discipline.
- **The body setting is comfortable for reading.** The size is generous, the leading looks well judged (about 1.5), paragraphs are marked by space only, and the column is centred and flush left with a gentle rag.
- **The copy is the best thing on the page.** "The queue for the shared water tap is the best place in the district to hear the news" and "on the condition that nothing was built that could not be moved" are concrete, specific and only this story's. The headline names a real place in plain words. Let the design be as specific as the writing.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste:* Give the feature its own display voice. A serif or more characterful face for the headline, against the plain sans body, would signal "a story" rather than "a page". The contrast would come from a different form, not just a different size (Bringhurst on faces in clear contrast).
2. *Taste:* Commit to the residents' photographs as the structure of the piece, not decoration. Let them run large, break the text column, and carry short captions in the residents' own words. The credit already says this is their story, and the layout could prove it.
3. *Taste:* Give the publication one colour of its own and use it with intent. The green kicker is the only colour on the page and it does nearly nothing. Used for the wordmark, the kicker and Subscribe, it would tie the brand to the subject without adding noise.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The bottom quarter of the screen (below "Most of them are still in use.") is empty. Check in a live browser whether the article continues, a lead image failed to load, or the content is waiting on a scroll animation.
- Focus indicators on the nav links.
