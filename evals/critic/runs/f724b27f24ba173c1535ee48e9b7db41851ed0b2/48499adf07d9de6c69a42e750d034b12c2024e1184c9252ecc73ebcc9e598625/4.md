**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline, then drops straight into the four blue-bordered cards, which take up the most area on the page. It finds "Create your link" only on the way back up. The right half of the hero is blank, so the page feels unfinished and tilts left. It reads as a clean but generic SaaS template for a scheduling tool, and almost everything on it is the same blue.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the first screen of a marketing home page for a booking-link tool. It's aimed at small service businesses like the dentists, tutors and physios in the strip, and its job is to get them to create a link or look at a live example.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and readable, but it doesn't show the product or make you want it. The hero has no picture of a booking page, and one blue is spread over the headline, links, borders, icons and badges, so nothing on the page points at the action.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero never shows what you'd be sending people.** The right half of the hero (roughly x 680–1216, y 100–380) is leftover empty space. It isn't a deliberate pause. A booking link is a very visual product: a page of time slots a client taps. The viewer has to imagine it from "Share one link, let people pick a time". The empty space also makes the hero tip left, with the headline, subhead and button all stacked on one side and nothing opposite (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Defect: the viewer gets a claim instead of a look. Fix: put a real booking page in that space, with the slots in the visitor's time zone and a "Harbor Dental" name on it. Then the image and the headline make one point together, and the "See a live example" link has something to deliver on.
2. **The accent blue is on everything, so it marks nothing.** The headline, nav links, every card title, all four card borders, all four badges, all four icons, the "Learn more" links, the divider rule and both buttons are the same saturated blue. The headline is the same colour as the links, so it looks like something you could click. And the four 2 px blue card outlines add up to more blue than the "Create your link" button, which pulls the eye down past the call to action. Convention (Apple HIG, Color; Material 3, Color roles): keep the accent for the main action and at most one highlight. Draw the headline in near-black, the card borders in light grey (or drop them and use space), and the icons and titles in neutrals. The two buttons will then be the only strong blue on the screen.
3. **The headline and the ask could belong to any scheduling tool.** "Booking links that fill your calendar" is the category's stock promise. It doesn't say what's different here, and "fill your calendar" can read as a threat to a busy professional as easily as a promise. The page's real strengths are in the cards: the guest sees their own time zone, and reminders cut no-shows. Those stop at the feature and never land on the viewer. "See a live example" is the stronger ask because it lets visitors judge for themselves. A stronger one would set the product against what they do now: "See what your clients see instead of the email back-and-forth". Defect: nothing gives a dental receptionist a reason to pick this one. Fix: put the concrete payoff in the headline (fewer no-shows, no back-and-forth) and say it to "you".

**Smaller things.**
- Card 1: "Shared calendars" wraps to two lines and the "New" badge is pushed to the right edge, while the other three badges sit right after their titles. Card 1's body text and "Learn more" therefore sit about 30 px lower than in the other cards, and the rows don't line up across the set. Shorten the title (e.g. "Team calendars") or put the badge after the title on its own line, so all four cards match.
- Badges: "New" and "Beta" are statuses, but "Auto" and "Email" just describe the feature, so the same pill means two different things. Keep the pill for status only, or drop the descriptor pills.
- Icons: the calendar is outlined while the clock, envelope and layout icons are filled. Use one style (taste).
- Four links say "Learn more", and each one only makes sense with its card. Name the destination, e.g. "How reminders work" (WCAG 2.4.4).
- "Primary actions": "Start free" in the nav and "Create your link" in the hero are both solid blue buttons for the same action under two names. Use one name, and consider making the nav button outlined.
- Customer strip at the bottom: five grey names with no label and no logos. It reads like a footer menu, not as proof. Add a short lead-in ("Used by clinics, tutors and coaches") or use the real marks.

**What's working.**
- The headline is clearly dominant through size and weight. The type has four clear levels (headline, subhead, card title, body) in one family. Keep that scale when you recolour.
- Each card gives one benefit in one short, plain sentence that ends on the outcome ("so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows"). This is the best copy on the page and should feed the headline.
- The ask pairs a doing action ("Create your link") with a show-me action ("See a live example"), and the second is styled lower. That's the right structure.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the buttons and links, and how the four-card row reflows at tablet and phone widths.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 4 yes, 4 no (visual and headline work together; every element does a job; one kind looks the same everywhere; headings name their sections)
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 8 yes, 5 no (alignment without near-misses; baselines across columns; no accidental likeness; space gathered; balance)
Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
Color: 9 lines, 8 yes, 1 no (strong colour kept to a small share)
Pictures: 12 lines, 10 yes, 2 no (one rendering style; consistent line quality)
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 29 yes, 3 no (one primary action; audience's own words; link purpose), 1 unsure (focus indicator)
