**First read.** My eye goes straight to the white headline, "See where every dollar goes this month", then down to the subhead and the white "Try it free" button. Then it drifts right into a large empty navy space, where it looks like something should be and nothing is. It reads as a calm, trustworthy budgeting app for individuals and households. It also feels unfinished and could belong to any company, because it never shows the product.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the landing page hero for a personal and household budgeting app, and its job is to get someone who is unhappy with their spending to start a free trial.

**Verdict.** The typography and copy are clean and mostly specific, but the page sells a product you can see without ever showing it. The empty right side of the hero is what stops it working.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero has no picture of the product, and its right ~40% is empty navy.** All the hero's weight (headline, subhead, both buttons) sits in the left 60%. Everything right of about x=800 is a flat navy field with nothing in it. The viewer feels two things:
   - **The page leans left and looks unfinished.** The emptiness is just space left over, not composed space, so it reads as "image didn't load" rather than "deliberate calm" (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight; Ruder: empty space works when it's shaped against something dense).
   - **The headline promises something visual that never appears.** "See where every dollar goes" invites the viewer to picture a breakdown, and the page doesn't show one. Taken alone, the headline could be any budgeting app's.

   Direction: put the product's real answer in that space, such as one month's spending by category with one category nearly over its limit. Headline and picture would then make one point: here is where your money went, and here is the heads-up. If a picture is meant to be there and failed to load in this capture, check it in a live browser; the finding stands until something fills that space. **Defect** (the promise goes unshown; the layout looks broken), backed by **convention** (balance).

2. **"Try it free" doesn't say what "free" means.** This is the button that commits the viewer, so it's the line they'll hold the company to. It can be read as "a free plan, forever" or "a free trial that needs a card and starts billing later". Someone who assumes the first and finds the second feels tricked at checkout. Someone who fears the second may not click at all. The ask also only invites the viewer to grade the product. It gives them no measure they already trust. Direction: name the deal on or right under the button ("Free for 30 days, no card needed", or whatever is true). Better still, make the ask concrete in the viewer's own terms: "See last month's spending in 2 minutes". That lets them judge the app against what they think they spend. **Defect** (the commitment reads two ways).

3. **The answer to the biggest objection is buried.** "Connect your accounts" in the subhead is the moment a stranger hesitates: *you want my bank login?* The reassurance, "Read-only access … can never move money", sits third in a small grey feature row below the fold line of attention, at the same weight as "Shared budgets". The viewer meets the fear at the top and the answer much later, if at all. Direction: put the read-only promise next to the call to action, e.g. one line under the buttons. Say it to the viewer: "Penny Path can see your balances but can never move your money". "The app" is vaguer than naming the product, and "you/your" makes the promise land on the person. **Taste**, argued on the order in which the viewer's questions arise.

**Smaller things.**
- **Uneven spacing below the hero.** The feature row sits only about 45 px under the hero band, while the bottom ~220 px of the frame is empty white. Give the feature row the same generous top padding the hero uses (about 64 px), so it reads as its own section and not a strip tacked on.

**What's working.**
- **Specific copy.** The headline and subhead say concretely what you do and what you get: connect, set a limit per category, get warned before you go over. Each has a verb and a result for the viewer. Keep that standard when rewriting the button.
- **A clear type scale.** There are about four sizes with real jumps between them: headline ~56 px, subhead ~20, feature headings ~18 bold, body ~15. Headings change weight as well as size. Nothing collides.
- **Button hierarchy.** One filled white primary and one outlined secondary, on one grid with a consistent 64 px left edge shared by the logo, the headline and the first feature column. The primary action is unmistakable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus indicators on the two buttons against navy. In a live browser, check the focus ring shows at 2 px and 3:1 contrast on the dark band.
- Whether a hero image fails to load on the right. In a live browser, check whether an asset is meant to be there.
