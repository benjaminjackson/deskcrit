**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, then goes to the first paragraph and reads down. It reads as a plain information page for people taking a small river ferry. It feels calm and tidy, like a utility page. The last line drops to a smaller size, and the eye treats it as fine print and nearly skips it.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you didn't say: a web page telling ferry passengers when the ferry runs and how to bring a bicycle, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read. It falls short where it matters most: it never gives a passenger a time they can plan around, and the one warning that could leave someone stranded is set as the least important line on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The winter warning is set as fine print.** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard…" is the only line that tells passengers to do something, and the only one that can cost them a missed ferry. Yet it is set at about 16 px under ~19 px body text, in the same face and weight. So it doesn't look like a caption. It looks like a paragraph that came out slightly smaller, and the eye reads it as a footnote. Bringhurst's point that sizes should come from a deliberate scale (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 3.1.1) applies here: two sizes this close don't read as two levels, only as a demotion. The order of importance is backwards. **Defect.** Fix: set it at body size or larger and make it stand out from the paragraphs, for example bold, with a short label like "Winter timetable", or with space around it, so it reads as a notice and not a footnote. Moving it up to sit next to the timetable sentence would also help.
2. **The copy gives no times a passenger can use.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last ferry leaves, so nobody can tell whether they will make the last crossing home. "Timetables change in winter" doesn't say which months, so readers can't tell whether it applies to them today. "About twelve minutes in calm weather" leaves open what happens when it isn't calm. A stranger has to fill each gap with a guess, and these are exactly the facts people plan around and later hold the operator to. **Defect.** Fix: give the first and last departure times from each landing, the date the winter timetable starts, and where to find it if not only on the noticeboard. If times change too often to print, say so, and say where the current ones are posted.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing separately.

**What's working.**
- The text column is the right width: lines run about 70–75 characters, the line spacing is comfortable, and it is set flush left with an even rag. It reads without effort, so keep the column width when you add content.
- There is one typeface, and the title stands out through weight and size, set slightly tight. It is clearly in charge without shouting, which suits a utility notice.
- Each paragraph covers one topic (schedule, then bicycles). That makes the page easy to scan, and it's a good structure to build on.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Put the schedule in a small table** (taste). People come to this page for times. Two columns (north landing and market quay) listing first ferry, last ferry and frequency would answer the question at a glance, instead of making people pull it out of a sentence. Tufte and Few both favour a table when the reader needs exact values.
2. **Add a scannable label to each paragraph** (taste). Short run-in heads like "Times", "Bicycles" and "Winter" would let a passenger standing at the landing find their answer in one look. Today they have to read everything.
3. **Use one quiet accent for notices** (taste). One muted colour or a left rule saved only for time-sensitive warnings would give the page a consistent way to flag "act on this" across future updates, without breaking its calm.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where passengers read this at the landing.
