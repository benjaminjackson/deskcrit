**First read.** The eye lands on "Review your order", moves to the bold $1,385.00 total and then down to the dark green "Continue to payment" button. The pale amber delivery note in the left card is the one spot of colour, so it is the next stop. It reads as a calm, tidy checkout review for a furniture shop. The two versions are the same pixel for pixel except for that note. In version 2 the note is set in all capitals, so it shouts and takes noticeably longer to read.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the last review step before payment in an online furniture shop. The buyer checks the items and the total and takes in the delivery conditions before paying.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. Both pages work, but version 2 sets the one paragraph that most needs careful reading in four lines of capitals. That makes it harder to read just where the buyer has to take in a condition with real consequences.

**What would move it most**
1. **The delivery note in all capitals (version 2 only).** This is where the two versions differ, in the amber box at the bottom of the left card. The paragraph tells buyers to measure their doorways and stairs and warns that drivers will not take doors off. Get it wrong and a $940 table doesn't fit through the door. In version 1 it reads in one pass. In version 2, four lines of capitals at body size lose the word shapes readers rely on, so the text turns into an even grey block. People skim it or skip it, and the most important sentence, "measure doorways and stairs first", has nothing that lifts it out. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says not to capitalize whole paragraphs, and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) notes that text in capitals is particularly hard to read. The amber box and bar already mark the note as a caution, so the capitals add volume without making anything clearer. *Defect.* Fix: set the note in sentence case, as version 1 does. If it needs more pull, put the instruction first in bold, e.g. "**Measure your doorways and stairs before you pay.**", then add the delivery window.

**Smaller things.**
- No way back from the review. Neither card has a "Back to basket" or "Edit order" link, so a buyer who spots a wrong item has only the browser's back button. Add a quiet text link near the heading or under the button.

**What's working.**
- **The total and the button lead.** The $1,385.00 total is the largest, boldest number, and the button is the only saturated block. A buyer finds "what do I pay" and "what do I do next" with no searching. Keep that pairing.
- **The line items are plain words.** "Ash dining table, 180 cm" and "Two-person delivery to room of choice" say what the buyer gets with no product codes. The prices line up on a clean right edge.
- **Version 1's note** puts the practical warning in plain sentence case, inside a clearly marked caution box, right next to the order it concerns.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste.* Put the delivery condition next to the decision. At the moment it sits in the left card, away from the button. A short line under "Continue to payment" in the summary, such as "Delivery is on a weekday, 8 am to 6 pm. Measure your doorways and stairs first.", would put the condition in front of the buyer at the moment they commit, not in a box they may already have scrolled past.
2. *Taste.* Make the button say what happens next. "Continue to payment" is clear, but "Pay $1,385.00" (or "Continue to payment: $1,385.00") ties the action to the number the buyer has just checked. That removes the last bit of doubt about what they are agreeing to.
