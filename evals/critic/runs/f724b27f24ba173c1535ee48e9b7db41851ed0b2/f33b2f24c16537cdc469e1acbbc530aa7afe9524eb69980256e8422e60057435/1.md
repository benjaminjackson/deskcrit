I critiqued the screenshot against the skill's checklist. The main problem is that the pictures are placeholders; the frame around them is sound.

**First read.** My eye goes to the big black headline, then drops to the large red block, then moves across the six smaller coloured tiles and their small captions. It looks like a photo gallery for a garden show, with one featured picture and six thumbnails. It feels tidy and calm but empty, because none of the pictures shows anything. Each one is a flat colour field with a pale half-circle.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web gallery page for visitors to a garden show, or people who want to visit. It should show what the gardens look like and make them want to see them.

**Verdict.** As a layout it works. The grid, the hierarchy and the captions are clean and well set. As a gallery it doesn't do its job yet, because not one picture shows a garden. The viewer is left with seven coloured rectangles and some captions. The second weakness is that the headline doesn't say which show this is.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures are placeholders, so the gallery shows nothing (defect, common sense).** All seven tiles, from the red "Rose arch" feature to the purple "Orchard path", are a solid colour with the same pale circle cut off by the bottom edge. The captions promise things you'd want to see: "Climbing roses over the east gate", "Reeds and water lilies", "Tomatoes in late July". The tiles never deliver them. A gallery is the one page where the pictures are the content, so here the viewer meets a promise with no proof. If this is a wireframe and real photos are coming, the layout is ready for them. If this is meant to ship, it isn't finished.
   - **Fix:** put real photographs in all seven slots.
   - **Choosing the feature photo:** pick the rose arch photo for how well it fills a wide frame, about 2.5:1, because it gets the most space on the page.
   - **The other six:** crop them to the shared 2.6:1 tile shape around their subject.
   - **Colour:** right now the page's colour, and most of its mood, comes from these flat fills. Once photos replace them, check that the page still has a clear lead image and doesn't turn into seven equally busy pictures.
2. **The headline names a type of page, not this show (copy, taste).** "Garden show gallery" could be any show's page, any year. A first-time visitor can't tell whose show it is, where, or when, and a gallery is usually where someone decides whether to go. Name the show and the date or year in the headline or just under it. Then the size and weight the headline already has would work toward getting someone to the gate.

**What's working.**
- **The grid holds.** There are three columns with an even gutter of about 14 px. The feature spans two columns and two rows, and the bottom row of three tiles lines up exactly with the columns above. The feature's caption shares a baseline with "Pond edge" in the next column. Keep this system when real photos arrive.
- **The type scale has five sizes, and each has a job.** They are: the page title, the feature title ("Rose arch"), the feature caption, the bold tile titles and the grey tile captions. The two smallest are only about a point apart, but bold and dark against regular and grey keeps them clearly apart. That is the right way to make that distinction.
- **The feature clearly leads.** It's about twice the width and height of a tile and has its own larger caption, so the eye knows where to start.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Choose the photos so they make up a walk through the show (taste).** The captions already name places: the east gate, the pond edge, the orchard path. If the photos are sequenced like a route, starting at the gate in the feature slot, the page stops being a grid of nice pictures and becomes a reason to come.
2. **Make the step between the feature and the tiles bigger (taste).** Small images of about 377 px set against one large one make the feature feel more like a lead image and less like a slightly bigger tile. This is the contrast of scale Hofmann's *Graphic Design Manual* builds on, and it would give the page some tension.
