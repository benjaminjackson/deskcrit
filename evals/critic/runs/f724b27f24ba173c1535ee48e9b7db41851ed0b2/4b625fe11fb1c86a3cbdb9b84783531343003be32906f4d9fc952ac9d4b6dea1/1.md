**First read.** My eye lands on "Low-light houseplants", runs along the four cards and stops on the repeated teal "Add to cart" buttons. It reads as a tidy category page for a small online plant shop. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, but it also looks like a template: the four cards are near copies of each other, and the plant pictures are the same clip-art sprout four times.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a product listing page meant to help someone with a dim room choose a plant and add it to their cart.

**Verdict.** The layout is competent and nothing in it is broken. But the page doesn't help anyone choose. The pictures don't show the plants, and the copy doesn't say how the plants differ, so the only real difference on the page is the price.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures don't show the plants (defect).** The image well is the biggest thing on each card. Every one holds the same terracotta pot with two or three flat green leaves. The "Snake plant" has round, floppy leaves, when the plant's whole identity is stiff upright blades. The "Pothos" card says "trails to 6 ft" while its drawing stands upright. On a plant shop, what the plant looks like is most of the buying decision. These pictures take up most of each card and tell the viewer nothing, and in two places they contradict the text. If they're placeholders for photos that haven't loaded or haven't been shot yet, that is the fix. If they're the intended art, each one needs to look like its own species: the snake plant's blades, the ZZ's glossy leaflets on arching stems, the cast iron's broad strap leaves, the pothos spilling over the pot rim. Use photos, or illustrations specific enough to tell apart at a glance. (This is common sense. I'm not citing a source for it.)
2. **The cards leave out the one thing the page promises (defect, judgment).** The subtitle is good and specific: "Four plants that do well more than six feet from a window." But each card then lists only pot size, height and price. The viewer came to answer "which of these will live in *my* dim corner?", and the cards can't answer it. Nothing says which plant handles the darkest spot, which is hardest to kill, which is safe for pets, or which needs the least watering. So the choice falls back on price, or the viewer clicks into all four. Give each card one line that sets it apart, in the viewer's own terms ("Survives a north-facing hallway", "Water once a month", "Safe for cats"). That turns the grid into a comparison instead of four identical tiles.

**Smaller things.**
- Top navigation: "Houseplants" is drawn exactly like "Pots" and "Care guides", so nothing marks the section you're in. Mark the current item with one cue, such as bolder weight or an underline (Nielsen Norman Group, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Card text: the plant name (bold, ~18 px) and the price (bold, ~20 px) look only a couple of sizes apart, so they compete as near-equal levels. Either make the price clearly larger or set the name and price at the same size and separate them by weight or colour (Bringhurst, §3.1.1).

**What's working.**
- The grid is strict. The headline, subtitle and first card share one left edge, the last card ends on the "Cart (0)" right edge, and the four gutters are equal. That's why the page feels calm and trustworthy, so keep it when you add content.
- The subtitle copy is concrete: "more than six feet from a window" gives a real measure the viewer can check against their own room. The card copy should match it.
- The palette is small (neutral greys, one teal action colour, leaf green, terracotta) and the teal is used only for actions. That makes the buttons easy to find without being loud.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the cards a comparison (taste).** The page exists to help someone pick one of four. Put the same differentiating facts in the same position on every card, such as light tolerance on a simple scale, watering frequency and pet safety, so the eye can read straight across the row and the choice makes itself.
2. **Let one picture style carry the brand (taste).** "Fernhill Plants" has no visual identity yet beyond a bold wordmark. A distinctive, consistent way of showing the plants would make the shop recognisable rather than anyone's storefront. That could be photographed against the same pale green ground, or illustrated with real botanical character.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the plant pictures are final art or placeholders standing in for photos. Check in a live browser.
- Hover and focus states on the "Add to cart" buttons and nav links, and what happens to the cart count when an item is added.
