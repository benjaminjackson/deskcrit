**Version 1 vs version 2.** The two versions are the same page pixel for pixel, except for the colour of the "Book drop-off" button. Version 1 uses a deep teal and version 2 a saturated royal blue. Both are the only filled, strongly coloured shape on the page, and white bold text looks clearly readable on both. Either way the button is the obvious action and the second thing the eye lands on. The blue in version 2 is a little louder against the grey panel, but the teal already stands out, so the extra pull gets the user nowhere new. Blue also reads as the browser's default "link colour", so the page looks even more like a template. Teal at least hints at a shop with its own identity, but that is taste and too small to change how well the page books a service. Neither version fixes, or makes worse, the real problems below. On the job of getting a booking, they are the same.

**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Bring your bike in for a service", jumps to the filled "Book drop-off" button, then drifts to the price list. It reads as the booking page of a small local bike shop: calm, clean, trustworthy, and generic.

**What it's for.** Assumption: get a local cyclist to pick a drop-off day and book a service, knowing roughly what it will cost.

**Verdict.** The page is clear about what it is and what to do, but the booking panel doesn't work as a control. The day options don't look choosable, nothing shows which day is picked, and the booking never asks which service you want.

**What would move it most**
1. **The day options look like empty text fields, not choices.** The three white boxes under "Choose a drop-off day" have the same thin border, white fill and left-aligned text as an input field. They have no radio dot, no check mark and no selected state. A visitor can't tell whether to click one, type in one, or whether one is already chosen, and pressing "Book drop-off" without a visible choice feels like a gamble. Defect (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, signifiers; Nielsen heuristic 1, visibility of system status). Fix: give the options a clear choice signal (a radio mark or a selectable-card style) and show one strongly marked selected state. Preselecting the earliest day is a sensible default.
2. **The service you're booking isn't part of the booking.** The three services and prices sit in the left column as a plain list. The booking panel on the right asks only for a day. The visitor picks "Standard service – $70" in their head, but nothing records it. That leaves them unsure what they're actually committing to, and the shop gets a drop-off with no job attached. Defect (Norman, conceptual model; the common-region grouping principle in NN/g's "Common Region" article). Fix: make the service rows selectable and bring the choice into the booking, either by moving it into the panel or by echoing it there ("Standard service, $70, Wednesday 29 October"), so the button commits to one clear thing.

**Smaller things.**
- Top navigation: "Repairs" isn't marked as the current page. Give it a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Date-option borders look about 1.3:1 against white and the grey panel. Raise them to 3:1 so the options read as controls (WCAG 1.4.11).
- The headline breaks as "…for a / service", leaving one word alone on the second line. Rebreak it ("Bring your bike in / for a service") or tighten the measure so the lines balance.

**What's working.**
- The first paragraph is excellent copy. "Drop it off before 10 am and collect it the same evening. We call you before doing any work that costs more than the quote." It gives a concrete deadline, a concrete turnaround and a promise against surprise bills, which is exactly the worry a bike owner brings. Keep it word for word.
- The price list is honest and easy to scan. Prices are right-aligned against fine rules, three tiers, no "from" or "starting at". The visitor knows the cost before committing.
- The hierarchy is clean: one headline, one panel heading, one filled button, so the eye path is never in doubt.

**Where it could go further**
1. Taste: give the shop one colour it owns and use it beyond the button, for the selected day, the selected service and the current nav item. The colour then means "your choice" across the page. Teal suits this better than browser blue, because the page should feel like a particular local workshop and not a booking template.
2. Taste: let the booking summary read like a sentence in the shop's voice ("Standard service, Wednesday 29 October, drop off by 10 am, collect after 5"). The button then confirms a plan the visitor can read back, and the opening paragraph has already shown that this shop writes well.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the day options have hover, focus and selected states in the live page, and whether clicking one changes anything. Check this in a browser, since finding 1 depends on it.
