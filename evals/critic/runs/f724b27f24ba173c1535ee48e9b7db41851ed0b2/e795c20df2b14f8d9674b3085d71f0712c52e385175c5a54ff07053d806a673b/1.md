**First read.** The eye goes straight to the dark band of cells at late morning on Monday and Friday. Then it moves up to the headline and across to the bold "3,412". Within a couple of seconds it reads as a support-operations report on when the ticket load hits: busy on weekday office hours, quiet at night and on weekends. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, which makes any mistake in it more costly.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a support lead uses this to plan staffing by hour and day, and to see how September compared with August.

**Verdict.** The chart is well built and does most of its job. But the summary panel's "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" contradicts the heatmap beside it. That one mismatch undermines the trust the rest of the page earns.

**What would move it most**
1. **The busiest-hour figure disagrees with the chart.** *Defect.* The hour labels sit centred over their cells (00:00 over the first column, 09:00 over the tenth). By that reading, the darkest cells, the only 30+ cells on the page, are at **11:00** on Monday and Friday. The 10:00 column is 25–29 on all five weekdays, and 11:00 is at least as dark on every weekday. The panel says the busiest weekday hour is **10:00**. A manager who checks the panel against the chart will find they don't match. They then can't tell whether the label, the summary or the axis is wrong, and they will doubt "Same as August" and every other number too. Since this figure is the one most likely to drive a staffing decision, this is the top fix. Check which is true. Either fix the stat, or, if 10:00 is right, the hour labels are off by one column and need to mark the hour each cell actually covers. If the 10:00 and 11:00 values are close, consider "10:00–12:00" as the busy window rather than a single hour.
2. **The panel and the chart cover different periods, and the copy doesn't say so.** *Defect (copy).* The subtitle says the chart is "average week, 1 to 28 September". The panel says "New tickets, September 3,412, Up 4% on August". A reader can't tell whether 3,412 covers 1–28 September (the chart's window) or all 30 days. They also can't tell whether "up 4%" compares 28 days against August's 31, or full months, or a daily rate. Read one way, September looks busier; read another, it might not be. This is the figure someone will repeat in a planning meeting, so it should not be open to two readings. Name the period on the number ("New tickets, 1–28 Sep") and say what the comparison is against ("up 4% on the same 28 days of August" or "per day").

**Smaller things.**
- Headline "When new tickets arrive" (top left) names the topic, not the finding. Something like "Tickets peak late morning on weekdays; weekends stay light" would give a busy lead the answer before they read the grid. *Taste, no approved source.*
- The bold stat figures (3,412, 10:00, 42 min) look about 26 px against a 28 px headline. Two bold sizes that close read as one level, not two. Either make the headline clearly bigger or the figures clearly smaller (Bringhurst, §3.1.1 "Don't compose without a scale").

**What's working.**
- **A single-hue light-to-dark blue ramp with binned steps** (Wilke). The order of the colours follows the order of the values, it survives colour blindness, and the weekday "office-hours block" shows at a glance with no numbers in the cells. Keep this when revising.
- **Quiet, data-first chart.** White gaps instead of gridlines, axis labels every three hours, days in natural order, and a legend aligned to the grid's left edge. Nearly all the ink is data.
- **Every summary number has a comparison**, and the source line says up front that times are local and mean when a ticket was opened, not answered. That caveat prevents the most likely misreading.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste.* Annotate the peak on the chart itself, for example with a thin outline around the busiest cells and a short label. The page exists to tell someone when to have people on shift, and pointing at the answer in the data would also keep the stat and the chart from drifting apart again.
2. *Taste.* Put the staffing consequence next to the reply-time stat. "42 min median first reply" sits apart from the load it depends on. A line tying first-reply time to the peak hours would turn a report into a decision.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether cells show exact counts on hover or focus. Exact values matter for staffing, and the 25–29 and 30+ bins are too coarse to settle the 10:00 vs 11:00 question by eye. Also whether the grid is reachable by keyboard and screen reader.
