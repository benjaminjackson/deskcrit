**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", which is plainly the largest and darkest thing on the page. It drops to the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label, then down through three short paragraphs. It reads as a ferry operator's information page for passengers. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, and on looks alone nothing gets in the way.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page that tells someone about to take the river ferry what they need to know to catch it, pay for it, and bring a bike.

**Verdict.** The layout and type do their job. The words don't. A passenger who reads this to plan a trip still can't tell when the next boat leaves, what it costs, or when "winter" begins. The page sends them to a printed timetable at the landing, which is the place they were trying to plan their way to.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out every number a passenger acts on** (defect, common sense; no checklist line covers it). Every paragraph has this gap:
   - "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing. One reader takes "early morning" as 6:00 and another as 8:00, and whoever guesses wrong at the end of the day misses the last boat home.
   - "Every forty minutes" doesn't say at what minutes past the hour, so you can't plan to arrive on time.
   - "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" gives no dates and no departure minutes, so a reader in late October can't tell which timetable applies.
   - The fare for a foot passenger is never stated, though "no extra charge" for bikes implies there is one.
   - The paragraph on the printed timetable lands the reader at the landing, not in front of the answer.

   These are the times and prices people remember and hold the operator to, so any of them that can be read two ways costs a missed boat. Fix: state the first and last sailings as clock times, the departure minutes, the winter start and end dates, and the fare. Put the timetable itself, or a link to it, on this page. The heading promises "before you board", so let the page answer the question people board with: *when is the next one?*

**Smaller things.**
- Body line length in all three paragraphs: the longest lines run about 80 to 85 characters, a little past Bringhurst's 45 to 75 (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). With three-line paragraphs this barely costs anything. Narrowing the column by about 10% would bring it inside the range.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clear. I count three sizes doing three jobs: a small letterspaced caps label, a heavy heading about twice the body size, and a comfortable body size. Nothing collides, and the heading differs from the body in weight as well as size. Keep this.
- The spacing groups correctly. The label sits tight on the heading, the heading has more air below it, and the gaps between paragraphs are clearly larger than the leading inside them. You read three separate notes without any rules or boxes.
- The bicycle paragraph is the model for the rest. It says who does what and when ("Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"), so nobody has to guess.

**Where it could go further**
1. Make the timetable the hero (taste). The page is for catching a boat, and right now the biggest thing on it is a generic heading. A stronger version would put the next sailing times, or the departure minutes past each hour, in large tabular figures right under the heading. That way the one fact a passenger came for is the first thing the eye lands on. This is Tufte's idea of leading with the data rather than describing it.
2. Give each note a bold lead-in such as "Timetable", "Bicycles" and "Winter" (taste). A passenger checking on a phone at the quay scans for their one concern. Short bold run-in labels let them jump straight to it without adding a heading level, which keeps the three-size scale intact (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).
