**First read.** The eye goes first to the heavy black headline, "Plan the week with your whole team", then across to the blue "Create account" bar. It reads at once as the signup page of a team-planning SaaS. It feels calm, tidy and safe, and also anonymous: apart from the word "Tallyboard", it could be any product's signup screen.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a desktop signup page that gets a team lead to create a free workspace, and sends people who already have an account to sign in.

**Verdict.** The page does its job at a glance. The structure is clear and there's one obvious action. But the form itself is the part people actually use, and it's weaker than the page around it. None of the fields has a label, and the password rule disappears as soon as someone starts typing. Fix the form before you polish anything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **Every field uses its placeholder as its only label** (defect). All five inputs on the right card have this problem: "Enter first name", "Enter last name", "name@company.com", "At least 10 characters" and "Company name". Once someone types in a field, its label is gone. If they get distracted, or the browser autofills the fields, they have to clear their own text to check what a box was for. The password field is the worst case. "At least 10 characters" is the only place the rule appears, so it disappears exactly when the user needs it, and nothing on the page even says this field is the password. People have to remember the rule from a moment ago instead of seeing it (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall; NN/g, "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful"). The fix is a short, always-visible label above each field ("First name", "Work email", "Password", "Company name"). Move "At least 10 characters" into helper text under the password field so it stays visible while typing.
2. **"Sign in" doesn't look like a link** (defect). In the line under the button, "Already have an account? Sign in" is all the same small grey type, with no underline and no change in colour or weight. Returning users are probably the second most common visitors to this page. They'll scan for a way in, find nothing that looks clickable, and some will start filling in the signup form by mistake. The rest of the page already uses the brand blue to mean "action" (the button and the wordmark), so this one link is the only place the colour system breaks (WCAG technique G183: links need more than colour, and underlining by default is safest). Give "Sign in" the brand blue and an underline, or at least a heavier weight, so it reads as a control.

**Smaller things.**
- Input borders: the thin grey-blue outlines on white look like about 2:1, below the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 requires for component edges. Measure the border colour against white, and darken it if it fails.
- Required or optional: nothing tells the user which fields they must fill in. "Company name" in particular looks like it could be optional. Either mark it "(optional)" or drop it from signup and ask for it later (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- Button copy: "Create account" names the system's action, not what the user gets. "Create your free workspace" would repeat the promise in the subhead at the moment the user commits.

**What's working.**
- **The subhead is specific and sets the terms honestly.** "Up to five people" and "You can invite the rest of the team later" answer the two questions a team lead has before signing up (what's free, and do I have to bring everyone now) without making them hunt for a pricing page. Keep that concrete number.
- **There's one clear primary action.** The only saturated fill on the page is the "Create account" button, so the eye never has to choose between actions.
- **The two-column split is calm and well grouped.** The promise sits on the left and the task on the right. Inside the card, the gaps between fields are clearly smaller than the gap above the button, so the form reads as one unit followed by its action.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Show the product, not just the promise** (taste). Below the subhead, the left column is empty, so the person is asked for five fields on the strength of one sentence. A single cropped screenshot of a real week plan, or one concrete example ("Monday: launch review, 3 people"), would let a team lead judge from their own experience whether this beats the spreadsheet or whiteboard they already use. That comparison persuades far better than taking the headline on trust.
2. **Give the brand more than a blue word** (taste). Right now "Tallyboard" in blue is the only thing that makes this page this product's. One owned element would make it memorable: a distinctive colour used across a larger area, a recognisable mark, or headline type with a voice. That matters on a signup page because it's the user's first impression of the product.
3. **Ask for less up front** (taste). Five fields, two of them for names, is a lot of commitment for a free trial. Email and password alone, with names and company asked inside the product, would shorten the path from "this looks useful" to "I'm in". That's the job this page has.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether a focused field shows a clear focus ring. Whether the email and password fields use the right input types, so the password is masked and phones show the email keyboard. Whether errors appear next to each field when someone leaves it.
