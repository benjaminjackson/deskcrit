**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes". It then drops through the paragraph to the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the hero section of a small local ferry's homepage. It feels calm, clean and competent, but bare: everything sits in the left 60% of the frame, and the right half and the bottom third are empty white. It looks less like a choice than like a picture that was never placed.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the homepage hero for a local passenger ferry. Its job is to get a would-be passenger to plan a crossing, mainly by opening the timetable.

**Verdict.** The core works: the headline is clear and specific, the hierarchy is clean, and there is one obvious action. What holds it back is that it reads as unfinished and anonymous. Half the frame is empty, and the copy describes the ferry in general terms without giving the facts a passenger would act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half of the frame is empty, with nothing there to justify it.** All the content sits in a column from x≈160 to x≈790. Nothing from about x≈800 to the right edge, or below the button, carries any weight. The page looks about to tip left, and the empty area reads as a missing image or a layout that didn't finish loading, not as calm. In the piece's favour, the space is gathered into one area rather than scattered; the problem is that it does no work. Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight. Tschichold and Müller-Brockmann both treat large empty areas with no evident purpose as a fault. **Defect** for a hero whose job is to make a crossing appealing. Fix: give the right side something that earns its place. A photo of the boat or the bay from the open deck would also show the "calm" the headline promises. Or, if you want the space to stay empty, widen and enlarge the text column so the white reads as a framing margin rather than a hole.
2. **The copy keeps back the facts a passenger came for.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no first or last sailing. Nowhere does it say what a ticket costs, or where across the bay the boat goes (the "north landing" to where?). Read as a stranger, the page answers "is this nice?" but not "can I get the 8:15 and what does it cost?", so every visitor has to click before learning anything they can act on. This is common sense, not a cited rule: the information people need next belongs in front of them. **Defect.** Fix: put two or three hard facts in the copy, such as "First boat 6:40, last 22:20", the fare, and the destination. Let "See the timetable" serve people who need more than that, not everyone.

**Smaller things.**
- The feature list is set at what looks like about 17–18 px, under a paragraph at about 20 px. Those sizes are too close to read as separate levels, so they look like an accident (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1; Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). Set the list at the paragraph size and mark it as a list (icons, ticks, or a short label), or make it clearly smaller and lighter.
- "Bicycles travel free" repeats "Bring a bicycle" from the paragraph just above it. Either cut the bicycle from the paragraph or swap that list line for a new fact, such as the fare or "Step-free boarding".

**What's working.**
- The headline. "Twelve calm minutes" is a concrete number tied to a feeling, which few competitors could claim word for word. The tight two-line set reads as one unit.
- The hierarchy is clean. There's a small blue eyebrow with the company name, then a heavy dark headline, a comfortable paragraph (about 68 characters per line, generous leading), and one unmistakable primary button. Nothing competes with it.
- "See the timetable" names exactly what the viewer gets. It is a better ask than a generic "Learn more".

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Show the crossing (taste).** The piece sells a feeling ("watch the harbour slide past"), but only in words. One strong photo from the deck, water and far shore included, would do half the persuading and fill the frame at the same time. Hofmann's and Müller-Brockmann's tension between dense and open areas only works when the open area holds something.
2. **Let the brand own something besides default blue on white (taste).** As it stands, the type, the button and the palette could belong to any SaaS site. A sea-derived colour used across a band, or a more characterful headline face, would make this look like *this* ferry and not a template. That matters for a local service people choose partly on charm.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover and focus states, and how the empty right side behaves at narrower widths. If the layout collapses to one column on phones, the empty half may not exist there.
