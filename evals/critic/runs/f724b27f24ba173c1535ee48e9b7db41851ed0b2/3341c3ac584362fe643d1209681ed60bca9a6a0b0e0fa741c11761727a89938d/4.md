**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", moves down through the paragraph, skims the three loose lines, and stops on the blue button. It reads as the hero of a small ferry operator's website: tidy, quiet, competent. Then you notice the right half of the frame is completely empty, and the page feels unfinished rather than calm.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing view for a local passenger ferry, meant to make a rider want to go and to tell them when and how.

**Verdict.** It's clean and readable, and the headline is good, but it promises a trip and holds back what a rider needs to take it: when the boats run and what a ticket costs. Half the frame is empty space that does nothing to sell the calm crossing the headline describes.

**What would move it most**
1. **The information a rider acts on isn't here.** The paragraph says boats run "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". That's vague exactly where the rider has to plan: is the first boat 6:00 or 8:00? Is the last one 19:00 or 23:00? There's no fare at all. The only action, "See the timetable", sends them to another page to find what this page could have shown. *Defect:* the rider has to leave and come back to decide anything (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Fix: put the real first and last sailing times in the copy, plus the fare if it's simple. Better still, show the next departure from the north landing near the button. Then "See the timetable" becomes the way to plan a later trip, not the only way to find out if you can go now.
2. **The page tips left, and the right half is leftover space.** Every element hugs a column from x 160 to about 790. Nothing sits in x 800–1280, and the bottom 230 px are empty too. That emptiness isn't shaped against anything, so it reads as a missing image rather than room to breathe, and the composition feels lopsided (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"; Ruder, *Typography*, on form and counter-form). *Convention, plus taste on what fills it.* Fix: give the right side something that carries the headline's promise, such as a photo of the open deck with the harbour going past or a simple map of the 12-minute route. Or, if it's meant to be text only, make the empty space look chosen: a bigger headline, the column centred on the page's proportions, or a colour field.

**Smaller things.**
- Body paragraph and feature lines look about 20 px and 18 px: close enough to look like a mistake rather than two levels. Set the list at the body size and tell it apart with weight or a marker, or make it clearly smaller (Bringhurst, 3.1.1).
- The three feature lines have no bullets, icons, or weight. They read as stray short lines, and two of them repeat the paragraph ("bicycle", "open deck"). Make the list say something new, for example fares, journey length, or step-free boarding, and mark it as a list.
- No logo, nav, or header. "Coastal Ferry Co." in small blue type is the only sign of who this is. If this is the whole page, riders can't reach fares, routes, or contact details (Nielsen, Jakob's law). If it's a cropped hero, ignore this.
- The palette (system blue on white, Inter-style sans) is calm but anonymous. Nothing about it says sea, harbour, or this company. Taste: a colour drawn from the place would do more work than the default blue.

**What's working.**
- The headline. "Twelve calm minutes" gives a specific number and a feeling in five words, and it's what the rider wants to hear. Keep both the number and "calm".
- The type is set well: one face, a tight two-line headline, a comfortable line length in the paragraph (about 70 characters), generous leading, and one clear left edge everything hangs from.
- One unmistakable primary button with a specific label. "See the timetable" names exactly what you'll get.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Show the trip. A ferry is sold on the view from the deck. A large, calm photo of the harbour from the open deck would pair with "twelve calm minutes" and fill the frame with the reason to go, instead of leaving it to the rider's imagination. *Taste.*
2. Let the page answer "can I go now?" A live "Next boat: 14:20 from the north landing" line, set big enough to rank second after the headline, turns a brochure into a tool and gives the calm promise something concrete to stand on. *Taste.*
3. Own one colour. Swap the default blue for a colour taken from the place (a harbour green, a hull red, a deep water tone) and use it for the brand label and the button. Then the page looks like this ferry company and not any template (Albers, *Interaction of Color*, on colour carrying its context). *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half is an image that didn't load in the capture. Check in a live browser.
- How the button looks on hover and with keyboard focus.
