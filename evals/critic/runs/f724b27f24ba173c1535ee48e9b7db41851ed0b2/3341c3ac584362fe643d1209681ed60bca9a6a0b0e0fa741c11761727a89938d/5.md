**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and the three short lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top section of a small ferry operator's website: plain, tidy, and calm in tone. The empty right half and bottom third make it look unfinished, as if a picture failed to load.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of the landing page for a local passenger ferry, aimed at day-trippers and commuters, and its job is to get them onto a boat.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the headline is good. But the page holds back what a rider needs to decide (when the next boat leaves, what it costs, where to board), and the layout is lopsided enough to look broken. Missing practical information is the biggest problem.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts a rider acts on are missing or vague (defect, common sense).** The paragraph says "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". A rider can't tell from that whether there's a boat at 7:10, or one home at 21:00. "The north landing" assumes they already know where it is. There's no fare anywhere, and the only price on the page is "Bicycles travel free". All of this sits behind "See the timetable", so every visitor has to click before they can decide anything. Departure times and prices are what people plan around and later hold the operator to, and "early" and "late" can be read many ways. Put the specifics on this screen: the next departure (or first and last boat), the adult fare, and the landing named so it can be found on a map. Then the button becomes a way to check details, not a gate in front of the basics.
2. **The page tips hard to the left (defect).** All the content sits in the left 60% of the frame. The right 40% and the bottom third are empty, and nothing there balances the dark headline block (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). It doesn't look like deliberate white space. It looks like a missing hero image, and that makes the whole page look broken. If an image is meant to be there, fix the loading and check it in a browser. If not, give the right side something with weight, ideally a photo of the open deck or the harbour. That would show the "calm" the headline promises and give the eye something to balance the text against.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line break: "twelve / calm minutes" splits the number from its unit (Bringhurst §2.4). Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes", or join "twelve calm minutes" with non-breaking spaces.
- The paragraph (~20 px) and the three-line list (~18 px) look almost the same size. The list reads as slightly smaller body text, not as its own level (Bringhurst §3.1.1). Use the same size, or set the list apart with a marker or icon and a clear change in weight.
- List spacing: the gap between the list lines (~33 px) is close to the gap from paragraph to list (~40 px), so the list doesn't read clearly as its own group. Tighten the list or open up the gap above it.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete promise in the rider's own terms: a time ("twelve minutes") and a feeling ("calm"). Keep that, because it's the best line on the page.
- The hierarchy is clean. There's one clear dominant headline, a small blue brand line above it, one primary button, and one typeface used throughout. Nothing competes with the headline.
- The paragraph's "a bicycle, a pram or a picnic" tells families and cyclists that the boat is for them without a separate pitch. That's well-targeted copy.

**Where it could go further**
1. **Make the next departure the page's second focal point (taste).** For a ferry, "the next boat leaves at 14:20" is the most useful line you can show, and it backs up "twelve minutes" with proof. Set it at a size between the headline and the body, and the page answers the rider's real question at a glance.
2. **Let the picture carry "calm" (taste).** The words promise a slow, pleasant crossing, but nothing on the page shows it. A wide, quiet photo of water and deck, with lots of sky, would do more for the mood than any adjective, and it would fill the empty side with purpose.
3. **Own a colour (taste).** The single corporate blue is safe but anonymous, and it could be anyone's SaaS page. A colour taken from the harbour itself (a sea-glass green, a buoy orange for the action) would make the operator recognisable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half is a hero image that didn't load or is meant to be empty. Check in a live browser.
- Whether a site header (logo, navigation) sits above this capture. If the page really has none, visitors lose the usual way to find fares, routes, and contact details.
- Focus and hover states on the button.
