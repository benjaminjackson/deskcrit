**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and the three plain lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads at once as the top of a small ferry operator's website. It feels calm and tidy, but also unfinished. Everything is pressed into the left 60% of the frame, the right side is empty white, and nothing shows the bay, the boat or the harbour the copy describes.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is the top of a local ferry's home page on desktop. It's for locals and day-trippers who want to know whether, when and how to take the boat.

**Verdict.** The type and order are competent, and the headline is good. But the piece makes its case only in words and sends the visitor elsewhere for what they came for. With no picture and an empty right side, it looks like a page whose image failed to load. The timetable and fare, which are the actual answer, sit behind a button.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half of the frame is empty, and nothing shows the trip (defect).** All the content sits in a column from x≈160 to x≈790. The area from about x≈800 to the right edge, plus the bottom third, holds nothing. The page tips heavily to the left, with no counterweight on the right (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). The empty space doesn't read as chosen; it reads as a slot where something didn't arrive. The headline promises "calm" and the paragraph describes "the harbour slide past from the open deck", but the viewer has to imagine both. A photo of the open deck or the bay would do half the selling. If an image is supposed to be there and didn't load in this capture, this finding becomes "check that it loads". If no image is planned, fix it in one of two ways. Put a picture of the crossing in the right column, so the words and the image make one point: twelve minutes, and this is what they look like. Or, if you want to stay type-only, change the layout on purpose: scale the headline up so it spans the width, so the white space is clearly deliberate.

2. **The page sends people away for the one thing they came for (defect).** Someone landing on a ferry site wants to know when the next boat leaves, how much it costs, and where to board. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" is vague. Is early 6:00 or 8:00? Is late 21:00 or midnight? A visitor planning an evening return can't tell whether the last boat suits them, and if they guess wrong they're stranded. The fare isn't mentioned at all. The only action is "See the timetable", which is one more click to get the answer. This is common sense, not a cited rule: put what the visitor needs next in front of them. Give the first and last departure times ("06:40 to 22:20, every 40 minutes"), the adult fare, and ideally the next departure from the north landing. Then the button can do something more specific, such as "Full timetable and fares" or "Buy a ticket".

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "twelve / calm minutes" splits the number from its unit, so the first line ends on "twelve" with nothing to count. Break after "bay" ("Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes") or keep "twelve calm minutes" together (Bringhurst §2.4.6 on keeping a number with its unit).
- Two nearly equal sizes: the paragraph looks about 20 px and the three feature lines about 18 px. They read as a slip rather than two levels. Set the list at the paragraph size, or make it clearly different, for example by giving each line a small icon or a bolder lead word (Bringhurst §3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale").
- The feature list repeats the paragraph: "Bring a bicycle" and then "Bicycles travel free", "the open deck" and then "Seats on the open deck". The list only adds new facts on two of its three lines. Cut what the paragraph already says and use the space for the times or the fare.

**What's working.**
- The headline is concrete and true to the product. "Twelve calm minutes" gives a number and a feeling, which is far better than a stock line like "Your journey starts here". Keep that specificity.
- The hierarchy is clear: a small blue brand label, a large bold headline set tight, readable body text at about 60 characters per line, and one obvious button. The eye never has to hunt.
- The restrained palette (near-black, one blue, white) suits "calm". The blue is used only on the brand name and the button, so the action stands out.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Make the crossing the hero (taste).* A ferry sells a view and a short, easy ride. Let one generous photo of the open deck or the water fill the right half, or the whole background behind the headline, with the twelve minutes stated over it. The picture shows "calm" so the copy doesn't have to claim it.
2. *Let one fact own the page (taste).* "Twelve minutes" is the strongest thing here. Set the number large, as a figure the visitor sees before anything else, and compare it with what they already know, such as "45 minutes round by road". The visitor then judges the value on their own terms instead of taking "calm" on trust.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the page has a header or navigation above this frame. Nothing shows a way to the rest of the site, so check that in the live page.
- Focus and hover states on the button, and how the single column reflows on a phone.
