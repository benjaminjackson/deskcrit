**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph, skims three plain lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top section of a small ferry operator's website. The left side is tidy and calm. The right half of the screen is blank, so it feels like a page that hasn't finished loading.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a ferry company's homepage, meant to get locals and visitors to plan a crossing. The main action is checking the timetable.

**Verdict.** The left column works: a clear headline, a readable paragraph and one obvious button. As a whole, though, it's half a page. There's no picture of the bay, the boat or the crossing, the right side is empty, and it leaves out the facts a rider needs before they bother clicking: where the boat goes, what it costs and when the next one leaves.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half is empty and the piece has no picture.** Everything sits in the left 60% of the frame (x≈160–790), and the whole right side and bottom third are blank. The page tips left, and the viewer can't tell if that's a choice or an image that failed to load. On a page selling "calm" and "watch the harbour slide past", the only thing you see is grey Inter on white with a corporate blue. The headline makes a promise and nothing on the page shows it. *Defect* (balance: a heavy side needs a counterweight, Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). Pairing the picture with the headline is *taste*. Fix: give the right side something with weight that does a job, ideally a photo of the open deck or the bay that shows the "twelve calm minutes". If the empty space really is a picture that didn't load, check it in a live browser. If it's deliberate, bring the column into a centred or wider layout so the white space looks chosen.
2. **The copy holds back what the rider needs to decide.** "Cross the bay" doesn't say to where. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no times. Nowhere does it say what a ticket costs. A visitor has to click before learning whether the ferry suits them, and "See the timetable" sends them off to dig for the answer. *Defect* (common sense: for a transport service, departure times, destination and fare are what people act on and hold you to). Fix: name both landings ("north landing to …"). Turn the vague span into hours ("6:20 am to 10:40 pm"), or better, show the next departure on the page itself. Add the fare next to "Bicycles travel free" so that freebie means something. Then the button can be more specific, like "See today's departures".

**Smaller things.**
- The paragraph (~20 px) and the feature list (~18 px) are only a couple of pixels apart, so they read as a mistake, not two levels (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1). Set them the same size, or make the list clearly different, for example with icons, weight or a tint.
- There's no header: no logo mark and no navigation, only the small blue "Coastal Ferry Co." line. Users expect a site header with the brand and ways to reach tickets, routes and contact (Nielsen, Jakob's law). Add one, even a minimal one.

**What's working.**
- The headline. "Twelve calm minutes" is concrete, specific to this ferry, and sells the feeling and the speed in five words. It also clearly dominates: it's about 3× the body size, heavy and dark. Keep it.
- There's exactly one action, and it's styled to look clickable. Nothing competes with it.
- The paragraph is well set: flush left, about 70 characters per line, generous leading and friendly copy ("a bicycle, a pram or a picnic").

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste:* Let one image carry the emotional half of the pitch, a wide shot from the deck with the harbour passing by, and make it as big as the headline is bold. The words say calm and quick, and the picture can make the viewer feel it. That's the gap between a timetable notice and a reason to take the boat.
2. *Taste:* Give the brand a colour that belongs to the sea and this place, not the default UI blue. A deep harbour green or a weathered navy, used only on the button and the eyebrow line, would tie the page to the bay and make it stop looking like a template (Itten, *The Art of Color*, on colour carrying mood).
3. *Taste:* Turn the three feature lines into short facts with real numbers ("Bikes free · £3.50 adult · Hot drinks on board"). That lifts the list from a loose afterthought into something people scan, and it does the anticipation job right there on the page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the blank right half is an image or a background that didn't load. Check in a live browser.
- The button's hover and focus states.
