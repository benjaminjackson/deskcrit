**First read.** The eye lands on the black headline "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and the three short lines, and stops on the blue button. It reads clearly as the top of a local ferry company's home page. It feels tidy and calm, but bare. Everything is pushed into the left 60% of the frame, and the right half and the bottom third are blank white, so the page looks like the main picture failed to load.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero section of a small ferry operator's website. It is aimed at locals and day-trippers, and its job is to get them to check departure times and take the boat.

**Verdict.** The type and the message are clear, and the hierarchy works. As a page that sells a pleasant crossing it falls flat. There is nothing to see: the harbour, the deck and the calm are all described and none of them are shown. Second, the one thing a rider actually needs, when the next boat leaves, is hidden behind a click.

**What would move it most**
1. **No picture, and a lopsided frame.** Defect. The right half of the screen, about 480 px wide and the full height, is empty. All the visual weight sits in the left column, with nothing on the right to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). At this width it looks like a gap where something should be, not like breathing room. The bigger cost is to the message. The copy promises "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck", and the headline sells "calm", but the viewer is asked to imagine all of it. For a leisure trip, the picture of the crossing is usually what decides whether someone goes. Put a photo of the boat on the bay, or the view from the deck, in that right half, or run it full-bleed behind a calmer text panel. Choose an image that shows what the headline only claims: open water, an easy pace, people with bikes on deck. Done that way, the picture and the headline make one idea together rather than repeating each other. That last point is taste, argued on its own terms.
2. **The timetable is a click away, and the times on show are vague.** Defect. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" makes the rider guess. Is the first boat at 6:00 or 8:00? Is the last at 20:00 or 23:00? The fare is missing entirely. Departure times are what a rider plans around and later holds the operator to, so they are the facts this page most needs to get exactly right. Give real hours ("First boat 6:40, last 22:00, every 40 minutes"). Better still, show the next two or three departures from the north landing right by the button. "See the timetable" then becomes the way to see the full timetable, not the only route to an answer. This is common sense rather than a cited rule: the person landing here has one question, and the page should answer it before asking for a click.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph and feature lines: the paragraph looks about 20 px and "Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board" about 18 px, in the same weight and colour. Two sizes that close read as an accident (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). Either set the lines at the paragraph size and let the spacing and a marker (a small icon or tick per line) show that they are a list, or make them clearly different, for example darker and medium weight. With the 13 px eyebrow, the 52 px headline and the button text, that makes four sizes plus one near-duplicate. Keep three.

**What's working.**
- The headline gives a concrete promise in plain words. "Twelve calm minutes" says how long the trip takes and how it feels, which is far stronger than a generic "Your journey starts here". It is set large, tight and dark, so it clearly leads. Keep the wording and the weight.
- The call to action names an exact thing ("See the timetable"), not "Learn more". There is one primary button, it is large enough, and it is the only saturated block on the page, so the eye ends where it should.
- The text setting is clean. The paragraph lines run about 70 characters, with easy leading, a single left edge, and one family throughout.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Taste: let the colour come from the water, not from a UI kit. The blue here is a stock interface blue, and the page could belong to a bank. A sea or harbour tone shared by the button and the eyebrow, maybe with a pale sand or mist background in place of pure white, would make the "calm" in the headline something you see as well as read. Itten, *The Art of Color*, on colour carrying mood.
2. Taste: make "twelve minutes" the hero number. The fact that sets this ferry apart is that the crossing is short. Pull "12 min" out large beside the departure times. A rider comparing this boat with the drive around the bay would then see the advantage on terms they already know, rather than taking "calm" on trust.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the button has a visible focus state, and how the layout reflows on a phone. On a phone the empty right half disappears, but the missing picture becomes the main gap.
