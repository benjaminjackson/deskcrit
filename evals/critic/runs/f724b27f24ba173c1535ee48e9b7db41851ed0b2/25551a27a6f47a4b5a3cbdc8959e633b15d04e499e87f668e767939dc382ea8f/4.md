I critiqued the single screenshot of the ferry "Before you board" page. The image fits on one screen, so I read it whole without crops, and I didn't edit any files. Here is the full critique.

**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", picks up the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it, then drops into the body and meets three paragraphs set entirely in capitals. The page reads as a ferry operator's practical notes for passengers. The top is calm and plain, but the body feels like being shouted at, or like a legal notice. You have to work through it word by word instead of skimming it.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: a web page that tells riders of a river ferry what to know before boarding (route, frequency, bikes, winter service), skimmed on a phone or laptop by someone planning a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't quite do its job. The structure on top is sound, but the body, which holds the whole message, is set in all capitals and is slow to read. The two details a passenger will plan around, when the first and last boats run and when winter starts, are left vague.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in all capitals.** All three paragraphs, eight lines in total, are capitals at body size. Without ascenders and descenders, every word becomes a rectangle of the same height. The reader loses word shapes and has to decode letter by letter, so the page takes several times longer to skim. Nothing in the copy stands out, because everything is already in capitals. It also changes the tone: on screen, capitals read as shouting, which jars with the friendly "Before you board". *Convention, and here it's a defect.* Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps": capitals suit headings shorter than one line, and you shouldn't capitalize whole paragraphs. Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, notes that text in capitals is particularly hard to read. **Fix:** set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for the one-line "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already handles them well with added letterspacing.

2. **The facts a passenger acts on are left vague.** "Between early morning and late evening" is the line someone reads to catch the first or last boat. One reader takes it as 6 am, another as 8 am. If they guess late, they wait 40 minutes or miss the last crossing. "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" has no dates, so in late October nobody can tell which timetable applies. "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends the reader to the landing for the information they came to this page for. *Defect (copy).* **Fix:** give the first and last departure times from each landing, and the winter dates (for example, "November to March"). Or link to the timetable here, since this page is where the reader is looking for it.

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- **A clear three-level type scale.** The small, tracked, grey eyebrow label, the large bold heading and the body each have one job. The heading clearly dominates without anything competing with it. Keep this structure when the body goes to mixed case.
- **A comfortable column.** Lines run about 70 characters, flush left, with generous leading and paragraph spacing. Once the body is in mixed case, the lines will get shorter, at roughly 60 to 65 characters, which is still within range.
- **Each paragraph covers one subject:** route and frequency, bikes, then winter and timetables. The content is already chunked the way a passenger thinks.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Put the key facts where the eye can catch them** (taste). The page exists so a passenger can answer "when, how long, can I bring my bike" at a glance. Pulling "Every 40 minutes · 12-minute crossing · Bikes free" into a short line or small list above the prose would answer that in two seconds. The paragraphs would then add the detail (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: make pages scannable).
2. **Give the page one quiet identifying colour** (taste). Right now it's grey type on white, which could belong to any service. A single river-blue for the eyebrow label or a rule would tell a returning passenger they're on the ferry's page, without adding noise.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where people read this.
