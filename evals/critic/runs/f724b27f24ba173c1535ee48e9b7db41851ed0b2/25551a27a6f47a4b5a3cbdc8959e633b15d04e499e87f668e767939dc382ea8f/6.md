**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", the bold heading at top left, which is the right place to start. Then it hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, and reading slows down. The block reads like a legal notice or a sign being shouted, not a friendly note to passengers. The page itself looks calm and tidy: one column, a small tracked label, a clear heading. The body type works against that calm.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page that gives river-ferry passengers what they need to plan a trip: how often the boats run, the rules for bicycles, and where to find timetables.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but the body text is hard to read because it is all in capitals, and the copy leaves out the facts a passenger actually needs, such as times and dates. Fixing the case is the single biggest change. Filling in the times is the second.

**What would move it most**
1. **All-caps body text (convention, and a defect here).** All three paragraphs, about eight lines from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING.", are set in capitals at body size.
   - **What the reader meets:** Capitals are all the same height, so words lose the shapes that make them quick to recognise. Every line comes out as an even grey bar, and a passenger has to read letter by letter to find one fact, like whether bikes cost extra.
   - **Tone:** It also sounds like shouting, which clashes with the soft heading "Before you board".
   - **Source:** Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps": capitals are for headings shorter than one line, never whole paragraphs. Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, says text in capitals is particularly hard to read.
   - **Fix:** Set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for the "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which is already tracked and works.
2. **The copy leaves out the numbers passengers will act on (defect, common sense).**
   - **Vague times:** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing. A passenger can't tell whether a 6:30 boat or a 22:00 boat exists, and the last boat is the one people get stranded by.
   - **Vague season:** "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" gives no dates, so in late October or early March nobody knows which timetable applies.
   - **Fix:** Put in the first and last sailing times, the minutes past the hour the boats leave, and the dates of the winter timetable. Turn the closing line, "printed timetables are kept in the shelter", into something the reader can use right now, such as a link to the timetable on this page.

**Smaller things.**
- The bicycle paragraph only describes a rule: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded." It would land better spoken to the reader, e.g. "Wait until foot passengers are on, then wheel your bike aboard and lock it to the rail."

**What's working.**
- **Clear type scale:** There are three sizes, each with a plain job: a small grey tracked label, a large bold heading, and the body. The heading differs from the body in weight and colour as well as size, so the start point is never in doubt. Keep that.
- **Spacing:** The gap between paragraphs is clearly bigger than the gap between lines, and the heading sits closer to the text than to the label. The groups read without needing any rules or boxes.
- **Line length and leading:** Lines run about 65–72 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range, and the line spacing suits that length.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Pull the facts out of the prose (taste).** The page exists so a passenger can answer "when is my boat, and can I bring my bike?" Put the frequency, crossing time, first and last sailings, and winter dates in a short list of times, and leave the prose for the bike rules. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, argues for tables when people need to look up exact values. A list or table lets a rider find the answer in one glance instead of reading three paragraphs.
2. **Use the empty right side (taste).** The 40% of the screen to the right of the column is unused. A narrow sidebar there could hold the one thing people come back for, like the next departure or the timetable link. The column would keep its comfortable width and the key fact would be visible straight away.
