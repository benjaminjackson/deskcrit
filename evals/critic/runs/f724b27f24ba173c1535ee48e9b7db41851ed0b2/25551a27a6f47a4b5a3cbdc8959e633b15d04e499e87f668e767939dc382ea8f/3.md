**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", a bold, dark heading top left. Then it meets three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, and the page goes from easy to hard. It reads as a short notice for ferry passengers. The heading and eyebrow look calm and careful, but the body reads like someone shouting a timetable at you.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page or panel that gives ferry passengers the practical facts they need before a trip: frequency, crossing time, bicycles, the winter timetable.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but setting every body paragraph in capitals undoes it. The one part the passenger came for is now the slowest part to read, so fix that first. The copy also leaves out the numbers a traveller would actually act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **Body text is set in all capitals (defect).** All three paragraphs, about eight lines of running text, are in caps. Capitals give every word the same rectangular outline, so a reader can't recognise words by their shape and has to read letter by letter. They also lose the sentence starts that lowercase gives them for free. That slows down exactly the reader this page serves: someone checking quickly, maybe standing on a quay. It also wrecks the hierarchy. The eyebrow "PASSENGER NOTES" is caps because it's a label, and that's the right use. The body uses the same treatment, so the label is no longer special and the body looks like a long string of labels. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says caps suit headings shorter than one line and paragraphs should never be capitalised. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) says text in capitals is particularly hard to read. Fix: set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case and keep capitals for the eyebrow only. The caps also make each line run wider than the same words would in lowercase, so check the line length again once they're lowercase. It should land comfortably inside 45–75 characters.
2. **The copy is vague where passengers need exact facts (defect).** "Early morning and late evening" is not a time anyone can plan around: does the first boat leave at 6:00 or 7:30? "Every forty minutes" gives no starting time, so you can't work out departures. "In winter" has no dates. "One boat each hour": on the hour, or at some other minute? "About twelve minutes in calm weather" says nothing about what happens when it isn't calm. A traveller will hold the operator to these details, and right now each one can be read more than one way. The last line sends the reader to a printed timetable in a shelter, which is no use to someone reading this before leaving home. Fix: give first and last sailings, departure minutes, and winter dates, as numerals, or link to the timetable itself. Numerals also stand out in mixed-case text, so the times become the part the eye finds first.

**What's working.**
- **Clear three-level structure.** The small, tracked, grey eyebrow, the large bold heading and the body each have their own job, with clear steps in size, weight and colour between them. Keep that once the body goes lowercase.
- **Calm, consistent spacing.** One left edge holds the eyebrow, heading and paragraphs. The heading sits close to the text it introduces, and the gaps between paragraphs are even. The page reads as one tidy column.
- **Short, single-topic paragraphs.** Each paragraph covers one subject (schedule, bicycles, winter), so the page is easy to scan once the type lets you.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the facts the visual hook (taste).** This page is for quick checking, so pull the three numbers a passenger acts on (every 40 min, 12-minute crossing, hourly in winter) into a small run-in or bold lead at the start of each paragraph. Someone glancing at it would then get the answer without reading the sentences. Refactoring UI makes the same argument: emphasise with weight, not size.
2. **Give each paragraph a short label (taste).** The heading could also be warmer, but the bigger gain is labels like "Times", "Bicycles" and "Winter" in the eyebrow style. That turns the column into something you can search by eye, and it reuses a style the page already owns instead of adding a new one.
