**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", which is the darkest and largest thing on the page, then drops into three paragraphs set entirely in capitals that read as one grey slab. It's clearly a ferry passenger-info page. The heading is calm and modern, but the body reads like a notice that's shouting, and it's tiring before you reach the second paragraph.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers what they need to know before a trip (times, bikes, winter service).

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the heading does its job. But the body copy, which is the whole point of the page, is set in all capitals, so it is slow and unpleasant to read. The copy also leaves out the actual times a passenger would plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in all capitals.** All three paragraphs, from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" down to "…AT EACH LANDING.", run to two or three lines each in capitals. Capitals all reach the same height, so words lose the shapes the eye uses to recognise them. The reader has to go nearly letter by letter, and the block feels like it's shouting. That clashes with the quiet sentence-case "Before you board" above it. The capitals are also set at the font's default spacing rather than spaced out (unlike the "PASSENGER NOTES" label), which packs them even tighter, though at this size that matters less than the case itself. **Convention, and a defect here**, because reading this text is the only job of the page. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says don't capitalize whole paragraphs, and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read." Fix: set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for short labels like "PASSENGER NOTES". If the source text really is in capitals, change it with a CSS text-transform rather than retyping it.
2. **The copy leaves out the times passengers will plan around.** The schedule says "between early morning and late evening", and "in winter" has no dates. A passenger can't tell when the first or last boat leaves, or from which month the hourly winter service starts. If one passenger reads "late evening" as 10 pm and the last boat actually leaves at 8:40, they're stranded on the wrong bank. "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter" also sends people to the landing to find out what this page should tell them. **Defect.** No source on the approved list covers this; it's common sense for copy people act on. Fix: give the first and last departure times and the dates of the winter timetable. Turn each paragraph's main fact into its first words ("Every 40 minutes, 6:20 am to 10:40 pm"), and link to the full timetable.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (small spaced-out label, large bold heading, body text) with big steps between them. The label is set apart by weight, tracking and grey, not by yet another size. Keep this.
- The spacing between paragraphs is about the same as one line of text, and the heading sits closer to its text than to anything else, so the three topics separate without any rules or boxes.
- One left edge for everything, a comfortable line length, and a neutral palette: the page is calm and gets out of the way.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste.* Let each paragraph open with its one fact as a short bold lead-in, like "Bicycles ride free.", "Winter: one boat an hour." Someone checking before a trip scans rather than reads, and a bold first phrase lets them find their answer in a second. Refactoring UI argues for creating emphasis this way, with weight rather than size.
2. *Taste.* Pull the schedule out of prose into a tiny two-row table: weekday/summer and winter, with frequency, first and last boat, and crossing time. Times are things people compare, and a table holds them better than a sentence does (Tufte: tables for exact values). It would also give the page an anchor besides the heading.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, and whether the capitals come from the text itself or from CSS (that decides whether a screen reader spells them out).
