**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", which is the right place, and the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it tells you what kind of page this is. Then you hit three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, and the page goes from calm to shouting. It reads as a tidy, plain info page for a ferry service, but the body is tiring to get through.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for ferry passengers who need to know when boats run, how long the crossing takes, and what to do with a bike. They scan it before a trip.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but the piece fails at its one job, which is being read quickly. All the body text is in capitals, and the copy leaves out the times a passenger would plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body is set in all capitals.** All three paragraphs are in capitals at body size, about 70 characters a line, two to three lines each. Capitals give every word the same rectangular outline, so the reader can't recognise word shapes and has to spell their way through. It reads slower and feels like shouting, which clashes with the calm heading. This is a defect, and it breaks a published convention: "Don't capitalize whole paragraphs" (Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps"). Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, says text in capitals is "particularly difficult to read." Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. Keep capitals for short labels like the "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow, which already uses them properly.
2. **The schedule doesn't give the times people act on.** This is a defect in the copy. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell the reader when the first or last boat leaves, and the last boat is the one they'll hold you to. "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" doesn't say which months, or whether the first and last boats change too. "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends readers to a shelter they can only reach once they're already at the river. Fix: name the first and last departures, give the dates for the winter timetable, and put the timetable, or a link to it, on this page.

**Smaller things.**
- Body capitals, left of the 120 px margin: they are set at the default letter spacing, so the lines read as a dense block (Bringhurst, *Elements*, §2.1.6, tracks capitals 5–10%). Setting the text in mixed case (finding 1) removes the problem, so don't fix it with tracking.

**What's working.**
- The three type levels are clearly different: a small tracked grey label, a bold heading at roughly twice the body size, and the body. Each has an obvious job, and the label-above-heading pairing reads at a glance. Keep it.
- Everything hangs on one left edge at about 120 px, and the paragraphs are separated by space rather than indents. The page looks orderly without any rules or boxes.
- The measure is about 70 characters, which is within the comfortable range. Keep that width when you switch to mixed case. Lower case is narrower, so lines will get longer at the same column width, and you may need to narrow the column.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Make the bicycle paragraph easy to scan (taste). Cyclists are a distinct group of readers looking for one fact: free, lower deck, board after foot passengers, lock to the rail. A short heading or a tight list of three steps lets them find it without reading the timetable prose. Krug's point that readers scan rather than read (*Don't Make Me Think*) supports this.
2. Lead with the most important number (taste). "Every 40 minutes · 12-minute crossing" as a short line under the heading would answer the question most visitors arrive with before they read a sentence. The prose below could then carry the details.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone. When the text becomes mixed case and narrower, check that the line length stays within range on small screens.
