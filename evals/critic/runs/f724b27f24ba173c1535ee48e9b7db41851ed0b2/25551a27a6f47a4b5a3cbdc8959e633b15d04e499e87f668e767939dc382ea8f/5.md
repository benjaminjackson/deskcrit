**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", then hits a solid grey wall of capitals underneath and slows right down. It reads as a ferry operator's passenger-information page: tidy, plain and trustworthy in the top third. Then it turns into something that feels like a shouted notice or a telegram, not help for a traveller.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a web page giving ferry passengers the practical facts they need before a trip, meaning when boats run, how long a crossing takes and what to do with a bicycle.

**Verdict.** The frame is sound: a clear heading, a sensible column and good spacing. But setting all three paragraphs in capitals makes the only content on the page the hardest part to read. The copy also leaves out the facts a passenger actually needs, the times.

**What would move it most**
1. **All three paragraphs are set in capitals** (the body block from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING.").
   - **What the reader meets:** Every word becomes a rectangle of the same height. The reader loses the word shapes that ascenders and descenders give, and the capitals at the start of each sentence no longer mark where it begins. They have to read letter by letter, so a 30-second scan takes noticeably longer. The texture also feels like shouting, which works against the calm, helpful tone of "Before you board."
   - **Type of claim:** Convention, and here it is also a defect. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") keeps capitals for headings shorter than one line and says not to capitalise whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read."
   - **Fix:** Set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. Keep capitals only where they already work, in the short "PASSENGER NOTES" label. If this comes from a CSS `text-transform` or a pasted source, fix it there.
2. **The copy gives no actual times** (paragraphs 1 and 3).
   - **What the reader meets:** "Between early morning and late evening" and "every forty minutes" don't let anyone plan a trip. They can't tell when the first or last boat leaves, or which minutes past the hour. "In winter" doesn't say which months. "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends the reader to the quay to find out what this page should have told them. These are the facts a passenger acts on, so the vagueness is a defect, not a style note: someone reading "late evening" as 10 pm when the last boat is at 8 can be stranded.
   - **Type of claim:** Defect (common sense, not a catalogued rule).
   - **Fix:** State the first and last departures from each landing and the winter dates. Point to the timetable on this site, not only the shelter. The bicycle paragraph shows how to do it: it says who does what, and when.

**What's working.**
- **The heading and label.** "PASSENGER NOTES" is small, bold, tracked out and grey above a large, heavy, tight "Before you board". There are three clearly different levels with one obvious place to start. Keep that scale when the body changes case.
- **The column.** At roughly 70 to 75 characters a line it sits inside Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range. Line spacing is comfortable, and the space between paragraphs is clearly larger than the space between lines, so the three topics read as three chunks.
- **The bicycle paragraph.** It is the best copy on the page: it gives a concrete sequence (wheel on after foot passengers, lock to the rail) that a rider can follow without guessing.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Pull the numbers out of the prose (taste).** A passenger skimming before a trip wants the departure times, the 12-minute crossing and the hourly winter service at a glance. Set them as a short list or a small table of times beside the column. A reader could then find "when's the next boat" without reading any sentences, which is what Krug's "self-evident and scannable" asks of a page like this.
2. **Give each topic a short subhead (taste).** "Times", "Bicycles" and "Winter service" in a weight between the H1 and the body would turn three paragraphs into a page people can navigate. The heading already promises practical notes; subheads make the page live up to that.
