**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline "Savings deposits rose every month", drops to the tall June bar, then jumps right to "$4.82M". It reads at once as a credit union's half-year report to its members. It feels calm, careful and believable, though close to a stock dashboard template. The June bar is a visible jump, and nothing next to it explains it.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page in a member report, meant to show ordinary members that savings deposits grew in the first half of 2026 and to build trust in the credit union.

**Verdict.** The page is clear and well built, but its one message is not quite straight. Most of the growth the headline celebrates comes from a one-off June bonus payroll, and that is only admitted in the grey footnote. On a page whose whole job is trust, that gap matters more than any styling issue. The hatched bars are the second problem.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline and the chart tell different stories, and the reason is hidden in the footnote.** Defect. "Rose every month" suggests steady growth. Monthly increases run $43k–$79k from January to May, then June jumps by $282k. That jump is the most noticeable thing in the chart. The only explanation is the source line at the bottom ("June includes the mid-year bonus payroll"), in small grey type below the chart card, where most readers will never look. A member who works it out later will feel the headline oversold the result, and that is exactly the wrong feeling for a report like this. Some of the side numbers probably lean on June too: "Up 6.1%", and the "Jun 2026" average and account count. That is likely but not certain, and that uncertainty is itself the problem. Fix: put the caveat where the eye already is. Add a short label on the June bar ("includes mid-year bonus payroll"), or mark the bonus part of that bar separately. Then make the headline say what is true without the bonus, e.g. "Savings deposits grew every month, before June's bonus boost". This is argued on common sense: the matching catalog entries cite sources that are not on the approved list.
2. **The diagonal hatching on the bars is decoration, and it shimmers.** Convention (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 11). There is only one series, so the stripes don't separate anything from anything. They add no meaning, but the thin blue-and-white diagonals flicker at screen size and make the bar edges look less solid. That cuts against the calm, factual tone the rest of the page sets. Fix: draw the bars in a plain, flat blue. If you later need to set off the June bonus part (see 1), that is the one place a second fill or tint would earn its keep.

**Smaller things**
- The three stat cards on the right each compare in a different way: "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025", "Up $11 on Jun 2025", "Up 402 on Jun 2025". The cards are a set, so pick one style (all absolute or all percentage, with the full comparison period). Also replace "on" with "vs" or "from", since "up $11 on Jun 2025" reads awkwardly to a stranger.
- The June bar label "$1150k" has no thousands separator and doesn't match "$4.82M" in the first card. Use "$1.15M", or label every bar in the same unit with a separator.

**What's working**
- The numbers check out: the six bar labels add up to exactly $4.82M, the axis starts at zero, ticks are even, and each bar is labelled directly. Keep that rigour; it is the base of the page's credibility.
- The type system is clean: a serif for the wordmark and headline, a sans for everything else, and only about four clear size levels. The headline, the 32 px figures and the small labels never compete.
- The subtitle and chart title say exactly what is being measured ("New money paid into member savings accounts", "US dollars per month"), so nobody has to guess the unit.

**Where it could go further**
1. Taste: let the headline give members a reason to care. "Rose every month" is a fact about the credit union. A line that says what the growth means for the member (the strength of the reserves behind their savings, for example) would turn a status report into reassurance, which is what a member report is for.
2. Taste: put last year in the chart. The cards already compare with 2025, but the chart shows 2026 alone. A pale grey 2025 bar or tick behind each month would let members see the growth for themselves instead of taking the percentages on trust. Tufte's principle of showing comparisons supports this.
