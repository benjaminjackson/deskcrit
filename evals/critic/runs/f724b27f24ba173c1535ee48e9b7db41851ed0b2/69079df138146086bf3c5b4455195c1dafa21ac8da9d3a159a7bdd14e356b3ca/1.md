**First read.** The eye lands on the near-black headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then has to strain at everything under it. All three paragraphs are set in the same pale grey, so the page feels washed out, as if it hasn't finished loading. It reads as a short help page for people about to take a local river ferry. It feels calm and tidy, but faint.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The facts a passenger actually acts on are hard to read, vague, and the most important one is set the smallest. The biggest problem is the pale grey text. After that, the copy gives no real times.

**What would move it most**
1. **All the body text is too faint to read comfortably** (defect, convention). The two main paragraphs and the winter note are a light cool grey on white. That colour looks to be around 2.5:1, well under the 4.5:1 that WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 requires for normal text. At about 19 px these paragraphs are not "large text", so the 3:1 exception doesn't apply. The winter note is smaller still, so it suffers most. A passenger glancing at this on a phone outside at the landing, or anyone with weaker sight, will struggle with exactly the lines that hold the information. The headline is the only thing that reads easily, and it says the least. Measure the grey, then darken the body text until it reaches at least 4.5:1. If you want a softer tone than the headline, a dark grey still gives you that.
2. **The copy describes the service but doesn't let anyone plan with it** (defect). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" has no first or last sailing. A passenger can't tell whether a 6:30 boat or a 22:00 boat exists, and the last boat is the one that strands people. "About twelve minutes in calm weather" leaves open what happens when the weather isn't calm. "Timetables change in winter" doesn't say when winter starts or what changes, and it sends the reader to a noticeboard they can only see once they're already at the landing. These are times and dates, the details people remember and later hold the operator to. Give the first and last departure from each end, the dates of the winter timetable, and a link or place where the winter times can be checked before setting out.
3. **The one warning that changes what a passenger does is set as a footnote** (defect). "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard…" is set smaller than the two paragraphs above it (roughly 16 px against 19 px) in the same grey. It reads as small print. The two sizes are also too close to look like a deliberate step: they look like a mismatch (Bringhurst, *Elements*, §3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). This line overrides the timetable above it, so it should be at least as visible as the paragraphs, for example at body size with a bold lead-in such as "Winter timetable:", or placed directly next to the times it changes. Then drop the extra size so the page has two levels: headline and body.

**What's working.**
- The measure is right. The paragraphs run at about 75–85 characters with generous line spacing, so once the text is dark enough it will read easily. Keep the column width.
- The headline is clear and dominant, and a strong weight change sets it apart, not size alone. The eye knows where to start.
- The copy is short and broken into one topic per paragraph (crossing, then bicycles, then the winter caveat). That's the right structure for people scanning before a trip.
