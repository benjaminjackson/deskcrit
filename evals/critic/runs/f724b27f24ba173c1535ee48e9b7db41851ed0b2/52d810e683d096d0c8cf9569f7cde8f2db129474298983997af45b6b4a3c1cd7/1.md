**First read.** The eye lands first on the purple "Sign in" button, then jumps to the email field, which is outlined in red. The page reads at once as the sign-in screen for a product called Quillpost: calm, tidy, and typical of a SaaS login. The second look raises a question the screen doesn't answer: why is the email field red?

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the sign-in screen of a web app, shown after a failed sign-in or a failed email check, for returning users who want to get in quickly.

**Verdict.** The layout and hierarchy are sound. But the screen fails at the one thing this state exists for: it marks the email as wrong in red and never says what is wrong or how to fix it. Everything else here is minor.

**What would move it most**
1. **The email error is shown only by a red border.** The red outline on the Email field (top of the form) is the only signal. There's no message, no icon and no words. A user who looks at "dana.ortiz@exmaple" won't easily spot the swapped letters or the missing ".com". They may not even know whether the email or the password was rejected, so they'll probably retype the password, which is the wrong field. Colour-blind users may not see the red at all. **Defect**, which breaks WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.1 Error Identification and SC 1.4.1 Use of Color. Under the field, add a short message that names the problem and how to fix it, such as "This email looks incomplete. Check the part after the @ (e.g. example.com)." (SC 3.3.3 Error Suggestion). Keep the red border as a second cue.
2. **"Create an account" doesn't look like a link.** At the bottom of the card it is set in the same grey, weight and size as "New here?", with no underline. Yet "Forgot your password?" two rows above is a purple link. A new user looking for the sign-up path sees a sentence, not something to click. That puts friction on the one route a first-time visitor needs. **Defect** (WCAG 1.4.1; NN/g, Moran, "Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention"), and a consistency break between links of the same kind (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*). Style "Create an account" like the other link, in the purple at least. An underline would be safer still.

**Smaller things.**
- The Password field border is a pale grey on white that looks well under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure it, and darken the resting border of every input until it reaches 3:1.

**What's working.**
- There is one clear primary action. The filled purple "Sign in" button is the only heavy shape on the card, so nobody has to hunt for what to do.
- Labels sit above the fields in bold, each closer to its own field than to the next one. The grouping is unambiguous, so keep it when you add error text: the message should sit tight under its field, with the larger gap kept before "Password".
- The restraint suits a utility screen: one neutral range, one brand purple, one family, and a card that holds exactly one group.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the error state a designed component, not a border colour** (taste). A sign-in screen spends most of its emotional weight on failure. A consistent pattern pays off on every form in the product: red border, small icon, one line of plain-language text, and the focus moved to the field.
2. **Let the brand do a little more** (taste). "Quillpost" in small purple type is the only sign of identity, so the screen could belong to any product. Returning users find their way by recognition. A real wordmark, or one more deliberate brand moment on the grey page, would cost nothing in clarity and make the screen feel like theirs.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus ring visibility and its contrast on the fields, links and button.
- Whether the error message is announced to screen readers, and whether focus moves to the field in error.
