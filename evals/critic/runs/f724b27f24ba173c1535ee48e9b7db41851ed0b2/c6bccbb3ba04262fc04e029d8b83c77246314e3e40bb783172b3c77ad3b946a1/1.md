**First read.** The eye goes to "Bin collection days" first, then the tinted card saying "Next collection: Thursday", then the four blue links in the body. This is a council page for residents checking which bin goes out and when. It feels calm, plain, and trustworthy, like a government service page. The two versions read the same at a glance. The only change is how the links are underlined.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a mobile page that tells a resident which bins go out on what day, and points them to the calendar, missed-bin reports, replacements, and permits.

**Verdict.** Both versions do the job. Version 2 is a little better because of one detail: its links use a normal underline that sits right under the words. Version 1 uses a line that sits well below the text, roughly halfway into the gap between lines. On wrapped links like "report it within two / working days", that low line looks like a rule between lines of text rather than a mark on the words. The link convention is weaker there, and the paragraph gets busier. On both versions, the biggest remaining problem is the wording of the card, not the styling.

**What would move it most**
1. **The card gives a day but says nothing about the date or whose address it means (defect, in the copy).** "Next collection: Thursday" is the line a resident acts on. It can be read two ways: this Thursday or next Thursday, and for my street or for some default area. The body text then says "Find your full calendar for the year by entering your postcode", which suggests the page doesn't yet know where the reader lives. If someone puts the green bin out on the wrong Thursday, or for the wrong round, it sits on the street for a week. The fix: give the full date ("Thursday 26 November") and say whose address it is ("for 12 Mill Lane" with a "change" link). If no address is known yet, ask for the postcode inside the card before showing any day.

**Smaller things.**
- In version 1 only, the link line sits low, near the next line of text. Use a standard text underline, as version 2 does. Link to the version 2 approach is taste, not a rule: both versions mark links with more than colour, which is what WCAG technique G183 asks for.
- "$25" on a page that says "bins" and "council" may be the wrong currency for the audience. Check it is the right one. Readers will hold the council to the fee.

**What's working.**
- **The card.** Putting the next collection in a tinted card straight under the title answers the question most people came with before they read anything else. Keep it first.
- **The link text.** It says what happens: "report it within two working days", "replaced for a fee of $25". Each link makes sense out of context and carries the deadline or price with it.
- **The type scale is tight.** There are three levels (title, card heading, body), with clear steps between them and weight doing the work in the card.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the card a complete answer** (taste). Show a small marker for each bin (a green dot or icon next to "Green bin", a blue one next to "blue box") alongside the words. A resident standing by their bins could then match them at a glance. Keep the words, so the colour isn't carrying the meaning alone.
2. **Show the week after too** (taste). Most councils alternate bins. A second, quieter line such as "Following week: black bin" answers "which one next time?" without sending people to the full calendar.
