**First read.** The eye goes to the bold serif headline, then to the tallest bar (705, Q3 2026), then across the row of value labels. It reads at once as a quarterly board-deck slide: calm, orderly, corporate blue. Next you notice that the bars are chunky 3D blocks and that all eight look the same, including the last one.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one slide in a board sales review. It should make one point about shipment volumes that directors can take in within seconds and repeat later.

**Verdict.** The slide mostly works. The headline states the finding, the axis starts at zero, and the values are labelled on the bars. Its biggest problem is that the Q4 2026 forecast is drawn exactly like the seven real quarters, so a director can read 560 as something that already happened. The 3D extrusion is the second issue: decoration a board audience will read as a dated office-chart default.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast looks like an actual (defect).** The rightmost bar, Q4 2026, has the same solid blue, the same 3D treatment, and the same bold "560" as the seven bars to its left. The only signs that it's a forecast are a parenthetical at the end of the subtitle and a clause in the source line. At a board meeting the figure people remember is the one they later hold management to. If anyone reads 560 as booked, the slide has misled the room. Projections must look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Mark that bar as an estimate: use a hatched or outline-only fill, a paler tint, or a dashed edge, and put "Forecast" directly on or under it. If the forecast has a range, show it as an error bar. Once the bar carries the label, the parenthetical in the subtitle can go.
2. **The 3D bars (convention, and it costs something here).** Every bar has a lighter top face and a darker right-hand side about 12–14 px deep. That depth shows no data. It puts each bar's true height in question: is the value at the front edge or the back? It also adds a second blue on every bar, so the chart looks busier than its data. Wilke (ch. 26.1, "Avoid gratuitous 3D") and Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") both rule it out, and Tufte counts it as chartjunk. To a board, it also signals a default template rather than considered work. Draw the bars flat, in one colour, so each value is read from one length.

**Smaller things.**
- Grid: the y-axis labels (800…0) sit indented at about x≈115 instead of on the 80 px left margin that the logo, headline, subtitle, chart title, and source line share. The plot also ends at about x≈1140 while the header text, footer rule, and page number run to 1200. Hang the axis labels on the left margin and let the plot run to the right margin, so the chart sits on the same columns as everything else.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a claim ("Shipments peak each third quarter") rather than naming a topic, and the data backs it: 610 and 705 are the high points of each year. Keep headlines that state the finding.
- The type plan is clean and sparing: one serif for the headline and one sans for everything else. The chart title ("Units shipped by quarter") is set apart from the subtitle by weight and colour, not by yet another size, and it is followed by a separate units line ("Thousands of units"). Few sizes, each with a clear job.
- The bars start at zero, the gridlines are hairline pale grey, and each value is labelled on its bar. Nothing has to be read off the axis. Once the 3D goes, the chart will hold almost no ink that isn't data.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the chart's layout show the claim (taste).** The headline is about a repeating seasonal peak and, by implication, growth. With eight bars in a row, the reader has to match bar 3 against bar 7 and bar 4 against bar 8 to see either one. Pairing the two years quarter by quarter (2025 in a muted grey, 2026 in the brand blue, Q1 to Q4 along the axis) would put the Q3 peak and the year-on-year rise side by side. The board would see both in one glance instead of working them out. This is Few's point that the chart should be built around the comparison it is meant to make (*Show Me the Numbers*).
2. **Land the headline on what the board cares about (taste).** "Peak each third quarter" is a pattern. The number a director acts on is that this year's peak (705) is about 16% above last year's (610), or whatever that growth means for stock and staffing. A subtitle that says what the pattern means for them, not a description of the data set, would make the slide argue rather than report.
