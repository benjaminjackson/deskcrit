**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three serif paragraphs and reads straight down. It reads as a plain notice page for a river ferry. It feels calm and easy to read, but quiet and a little unfinished, because the whole page sits in the left half of the screen.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells ferry passengers how the service runs, so they can plan a crossing. Correct me if it's for something else.

**Verdict.** The typography does its job well. The words don't. A passenger reads the whole page and still doesn't know when the first or last boat leaves, so the page can't help them plan, which is what it's for.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out every fact a passenger would act on.** The page says "between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour", "before dusk", and then sends the reader to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing". Read it as a stranger: what time is the first boat? Which minutes past the hour does the winter boat leave, and does "before dusk" mean 3:30 or 5:00 in December? These are the times people plan around and later hold the operator to. A line that can be read two ways here is a defect, not a style note. If someone reads "late evening" as 11 pm and the last boat actually leaves at 9:20, they're stranded at the market quay. Sending them to a shelter at the landing means they have to be at the landing already to find out whether they should have gone. **Defect.** Fix: give the first and last departure times for summer and winter, and the minutes past the hour the boats leave. A small timetable block would make that the one thing on the page a passenger can scan. Keep the friendly paragraphs around it for the soft details: the tide, bicycles, where the shelter is.

**Smaller things.**
- **Balance:** at 1280 px wide, the text column ends at about 810 px. The right ~470 px and the bottom third of the page are empty, so the page looks tipped to the left (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Keep the line length. Either centre the column in the window, or put something worth having on the right, such as the timetable from finding 1.

**What's working.**
- **The measure and leading:** body lines run about 65–70 characters with generous spacing between lines, right in Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Three short paragraphs read without effort. Keep this exact setting when you add the timetable.
- **The type pairing:** a bold sans headline over a serif text face differs in form, weight and size all at once. The levels can't be confused, and you only need two sizes to do it.
- **The tone:** "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" sounds like a person who runs the ferry, not a transit authority. That warmth is worth keeping once the hard facts are in place.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the timetable the visual anchor (taste).** The page is for planning a trip, so the departure times should be the thing the eye finds second, right after the headline. Set them as a short grid with tabular figures that line up in columns. That gives the page its focal point and fills the empty right half with something useful (Tufte: data as the content, not decoration).
2. **Lead each paragraph with its subject (taste).** "Times", "Bicycles", "Winter": a short bold lead-in or small heading on each of the three paragraphs lets a passenger who only cares about their bike go straight to that paragraph. It doesn't add bulk.
