## Critique: Coastal Ferry Co. landing hero

**First read.** My eye goes straight to the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes". Then it drops down the centred stack: the paragraph, the three unmarked lines, and the blue button. Within a second I know it's a ferry company's home page. It feels tidy, quiet and safe. It also feels like any website starter template with the words swapped in.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a ferry operator's home page on desktop. It's for locals and visitors deciding whether and when to take the boat.

**Verdict.** It's clear and calm, and the headline is good. But it fails at the one thing a ferry visitor came for: when the next boat leaves, from where, and what it costs. All of that sits behind a click, while the page spends its space on generic reassurance.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page hides what the visitor came for** (defect). The paragraph says boats leave "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". That has no first or last sailing, no next departure, no fare, and "the north landing" has no address or map. Someone deciding whether to walk to the pier now can't tell. They can only click "See the timetable" and remember what they read on the next screen (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Times and prices are what people act on and later hold you to, so "late evening" is a real problem. It could mean 8 pm or 11 pm, and someone who guesses wrong misses the last boat. Fix: put the next two or three departures from the north landing right under the headline, plus the actual first and last times and the fare. Keep "See the timetable" for the full schedule. Using today's departures as the main content is common sense, not a cited rule.
2. **Nothing here belongs to this ferry** (defect from the look and copy; the checklist has no line for this). Centred Inter-style sans, a small blue eyebrow, a stack of short lines and a rounded blue button could open any SaaS product's page. The bay, the open deck and the harbour are all things you can see, yet you only see words. So the visitor gets no picture of the trip the headline promises, and nothing marks this ferry as different from the bus. Fix: let the place carry the page. A photo of the deck or the bay, or a simple route line from landing to landing, would make "calm" something the visitor sees instead of something they're told.

**Smaller things.**
- **Too many close text sizes.** I count about five: headline (~56 px), paragraph (~20 px), list (~18 px), button (~18 px bold), eyebrow (~14 px). The paragraph and list are two near-identical sizes, which looks accidental (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Set the list at body size and separate it some other way.
- **The three features read as more paragraph.** The gaps between "Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board" are about the same as the paragraph's line spacing, and they have no markers. They look like a second centred paragraph, not a list of benefits (Arnheim on proximity). Group them with clearly bigger space above and below, or set them in a row with small icons.
- **Everything is centred on one axis.** Headline, paragraph, list and button all stack centred, which makes the page static and templated (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). If you add departure times, a left-aligned layout that follows the content order would read faster.
- **The headline breaks badly.** "Cross the bay in twelve / calm minutes" splits the number from what it counts. Break before "twelve" so "twelve calm minutes" stays together. This is common sense; the source for the rule isn't confirmed.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete promise in the viewer's terms: a time ("twelve minutes") and a feeling ("calm"). Keep that wording when you add the schedule.
- "See the timetable" is a specific ask, and it's the only button. There's no doubt about what to click.
- The type scale has one clear top: the bold headline far outweighs everything else, so the eye always knows where to start.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the next departure the hero number** (taste). A large "Next boat 10:40, then 11:20" from the north landing would turn the page from a brochure into a tool people check every day. "Twelve minutes" then has a time to sit next to.
2. **Own one colour from the place** (taste). The current blue is the default web-link blue. A sea or hull colour taken from a real photo of the boat would tie the brand to the harbour rather than to a UI kit (Albers, *Interaction of Color*, on colour taking its character from what surrounds it).
3. **Name the route, not just the bay** (taste). "North landing to [south town]" lets a stranger place the trip on their own map. Right now they have to already know where the boat goes.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The button's hover and focus states.
- How the centred stack reflows on a phone, where most people at a pier will open this page.
- The bottom of the screen is empty, so this may be only the top of a longer page. Check what sits below in a live browser.
