**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the homepage of a local home-services company. Its job is to get a homeowner to pick a service and book a visit.

**Verdict.** It's clean and well built, but it doesn't do its job yet. The subhead tells people to "pick a service", then gives them nothing to pick. The page shows what Fernway sells and stops before the visitor can act.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cards don't look like you can pick them, and the page offers no next step where the visitor's attention ends up.** This is a defect. "Pick a service, choose a day…" is an instruction. But the four cards have no button, link, arrow or hover cue, and the green price text looks like a label, not a link. A visitor who has decided on "Deep cleaning" has to guess that the card can be clicked, or go back up to "Book a visit" in the far top-right corner. That button sits about 600 px from where they made their choice, and it doesn't carry that choice with it. The eye path runs headline → illustrations → price → nothing. Fix: make each card plainly a control. Give each one its own action, such as "Book window cleaning", or style the whole card as a link with a visible cue. Carry the chosen service into the booking flow so "choose a day" is really the next step. This is common sense rather than a sourced rule, because the clickability entry's sources don't confirm it for this case.
2. **The prices leave out what they're for.** This is a defect: people remember prices and hold you to them. "From $24" for laundry pickup could be per bag, per pound, per week or per pickup. "From $89" for windows could be per visit or per house, and "From $65" for garden care could be per mow or per month. A visitor who reads $24 as "a week's laundry" and then sees a per-pound quote at checkout will feel tricked. Fix: put the unit and the basis next to every price, for example "From $24 per bag" or "From $89 per visit, up to 10 windows". This follows Nielsen's "match between system and the real world": say it in the terms the customer will be billed in.
3. **The headline could sit on any competitor's page, and one card contradicts it.** This is a copy finding. "Help around the house, on your schedule" says nothing only Fernway can say. Then the laundry card says "We collect on Monday and bring it back folded on Wednesday", which is *your* schedule, not the customer's. The subhead has the more distinctive claims: a vetted crew, and they bring their own supplies. Fix: lead with something concrete the visitor can check against their current cleaner or their own Saturday, such as the service area, the earliest booking slot, or what "vetted" means. Also make the laundry card show some flexibility, or drop "on your schedule".

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line break: "on your / schedule" splits the phrase and leaves one word alone on the second line. Break after "house," or widen the measure so it holds on one line. This is common sense; the Bringhurst entry doesn't verify heading breaks.

**What's working.**
- **The illustration set.** It sticks to a small palette of teal, coral and mustard on one pale mint tint, with the same flat style and amount of detail in all four. The four cards read as one family, and each picture names its service at a glance. Keep it.
- **The grid and spacing.** The headline, the subhead and the first card share the left edge, the last card lines up with the right edge of "Book a visit", and the gutters are even. Inside each card, the title sits close to its description, and the price is pinned to the same baseline in all four.
- **One primary action.** Only "Book a visit" is filled dark, so when people do find it, there's no doubt it's the main action.
