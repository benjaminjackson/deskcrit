**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three paragraphs of dark grey text in one column on the left. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry. It feels calm and trustworthy, but nothing in the text stands out, so you have to read every sentence to find out when the boat goes.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page where a passenger checks when the ferry runs, how bikes work, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** The page is cleanly set, but it doesn't give a passenger what they came for. The facts they would plan around have no actual times, and they're buried in running prose. The type is fine. The content and its structure are what's wrong.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts a passenger acts on are too vague to act on** (defect, copy). The first paragraph says the boat runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", but doesn't give a first departure, a last departure, or which minutes past the hour. Paragraph three says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk", which moves through the season, and "the quay" could mean the market quay only or both landings. "A little longer when the tide turns" doesn't say how much longer. A passenger can't plan a trip from any of this, and the page then sends them to a printed timetable in a shelter, which only helps once they're already standing at the landing. The fix is to give the times in words a passenger can hold the operator to: first and last boat from each landing, in summer and in winter, and the actual winter cut-off or how it's worked out. If the times change, link to the current timetable here rather than only pointing to the noticeboard.
2. **Three topics are set as one run of prose, so nothing can be skimmed** (convention: Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Frequency and crossing time, bikes, and winter changes each sit in a paragraph that looks just like the others. Someone who only wants to know "can I bring my bike?" or "is it running tonight?" has no heading to jump to and has to read all three. The paragraphs are short, so this isn't a wall of text, but each fact is buried mid-sentence. Give each topic a short subhead ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter"), and pull the hard numbers out into a short list or a small table so they're the first thing the eye picks up.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Notes for ferry passengers" is generic. Naming the route ("North landing ↔ Market quay ferry") would tell a passenger straight away that they're on the right page.

**What's working.**
- **The reading setting is good.** The body text is roughly 19 px, lines run about 70 characters, the line spacing is open, the text is flush left with a calm rag, and paragraphs are separated by space alone. Keep all of this when you add structure.
- **The hierarchy is restrained.** There are two sizes and one weight change, near-black on white, and nothing decorative. That fits a public-service notice and earns trust.
- **The copy sounds like a person talking.** Sentences like "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" say who does what and when. Keep that voice when you add the times.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Make the timetable the centre of the page (taste).** Passengers come for times, so a stronger version would lead with a compact departures table under the headline and turn the prose into notes under it. Tufte's argument for showing data directly applies: a grid of times beats a sentence about frequency.
2. **Use the empty right half (taste).** The right side of the page is unused. Moving the key facts into a narrow side column there (next boat, crossing time, bikes free) would give a skimmer an answer at a glance without touching the reading column.
