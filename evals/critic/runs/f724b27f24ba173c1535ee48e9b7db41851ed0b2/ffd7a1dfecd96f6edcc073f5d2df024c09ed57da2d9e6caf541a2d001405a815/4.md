**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops straight into the first paragraph. Nothing else competes for it. It reads as a plain information page for a small local ferry, and it feels calm, tidy and a bit anonymous: the default sans-serif, near-black on white, with a lot of unused space to the right and below.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs and what the rules are, so they can catch the right crossing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read, but the page doesn't do its job. A passenger who wants to know when the boat leaves can't find a single departure time, and the facts they need are buried mid-sentence across three unlabeled paragraphs.

**What would move it most**
1. **There are no times you can act on (defect, copy).** The first paragraph says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". The third says the winter service runs "one boat each hour" and the last crossing leaves "before dusk". A passenger can't plan around any of that: forty minutes from when? Is "late evening" 9pm or 11pm? Does "before dusk" mean 3:30pm or 4:30pm in December? These are the lines a reader will hold the operator to. The vague versions can be read two ways, and the cost of the wrong reading is a missed last boat. The page then sends the reader to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", so they have to be at the pier to learn what the page should have told them. Fix: give the first and last departure from each side, the interval, the crossing time, and the winter dates and last-boat time as actual clock times. If they change, say where the current ones are online.
2. **The page has to be read in full when it should be scannable (defect).** Three topics run as unlabeled prose: the schedule, bicycles, and winter changes. Someone checking "can I bring my bike?" or "when's the last boat?" has to read every paragraph to find their answer. Nielsen's "How Users Read on the Web" says text people skim needs headings, short chunks and lists. Fix: put a short label on each topic. Pull the times out of the sentences into a small, scannable block, such as a few lines or a two-column table, so the numbers sit on their own and the rules stay as prose.

**Smaller things.**
- Balance, taste: the text column fills the left ~60% of a 1280 px frame and the rest is blank, so the page looks unfinished rather than set. Either center the reading column, or let the timetable block from finding 2 sit in that right-hand space.
- Copy: "Notes for ferry passengers" names the audience but not the ferry, the river or the route. A stranger landing here from search can't tell which ferry this is. Name the route in the title.

**What's working.**
- The measure is about 65 to 72 characters with comfortable line spacing, so the prose reads easily. Keep it when you add structure.
- There are only two clear type levels, a bold title and regular body, with even paragraph spacing and a crisp left edge. Any subheadings you add should come from those same two sizes, set apart by weight, not by a third size.
- The tone is plain, concrete and polite ("The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle"). Keep that voice when you rewrite the times.

**Where it could go further**
1. **Make the next departure the hero (taste).** For a ferry page, the first thing a reader wants is "when's the next boat?" Leading with a large, glanceable block of departure times, above the prose, would make the page useful in two seconds instead of thirty.
2. **Give the page one identity mark (taste).** Right now it could be anyone's notes page. One colour taken from the boat or the signage at the landings, used only on the times and labels, would make it feel like this ferry's page and would point the eye at the numbers that matter (Refactoring UI argues for using colour to emphasize rather than decorate).
