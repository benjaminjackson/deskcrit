**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading, then straight down into three grey-black paragraphs of the same weight, and stays there. It reads as a plain, calm information page: tidy, quiet, a little bit like a parish newsletter. After the heading, nothing helps you find anything. To learn when the boat runs, you have to read.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for people planning a river-ferry crossing. They come with one question, usually "when's the next boat?" or "can I bring my bike?", and want the answer quickly.

**Verdict.** It's cleanly set and easy to read, but it doesn't do its job. The facts a passenger plans around are vague, and they sit in the middle of sentences where nobody scanning will find them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule can't be acted on (defect, in the copy).** In paragraph 1, "between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing. In paragraph 3, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" gives no time either. It also doesn't say which quay: the page names a north landing and a market quay. "Every forty minutes" has no starting time, so a reader can't work out departures. "A little longer when the tide turns" doesn't say how much longer. These are exactly the lines a passenger acts on, and each one can be read two ways. Someone who reads "late evening" as 10 pm, or who waits at the north landing for a winter boat that only leaves the market quay, is stranded. The page sends people to printed timetables in a shelter at the landing, which only helps once they're already standing there. Fix: give the first and last departure from each side, summer and winter. Give winter's last crossing as a clock time, not "dusk". Give the tide delay in minutes. Then say where to find the current timetable online, not only on a noticeboard.
2. **The facts are buried in prose (convention: Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** The page holds three separate topics: crossings, bicycles and winter. All three are set as unlabelled paragraphs of the same weight, and the key figures sit mid-sentence: "forty minutes", "twelve minutes", "no extra charge", "one boat each hour". A passenger skimming for the winter timetable has to read about bike locks first. Fix: give each topic a short subhead in the viewer's words ("When the ferry runs", "Bringing a bike", "Winter service"). Pull the times into a short list or a small table so they can be found at a glance. Keep the prose for the softer guidance, such as "bags stay on the bicycle".

**Smaller things.** None worth listing.

**What's working.**
- The body text is set well. Lines run about 65–72 characters, the line spacing is generous, paragraphs are separated by space with no indents, and the rag is soft. Keep this setting when you add structure.
- The two-level scale is clear: the heading is bold and large, the text is regular and plain, and nothing sits half-way between them. Any subheads should be a third, clearly distinct level (body size or a bit larger, in bold), not another size close to the heading's.
- The copy has a calm, human voice ("the crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle"). Keep that tone while you make it exact.

**Where it could go further**
1. **Make the next departure the first thing on the page** (taste). For a ferry, the time is the whole reason someone opens the page. A short block of times set large under the heading ("North landing: :00, :40, :20 … first 06:40, last 22:00") would answer the question before anyone reads a sentence. That would make the page a tool rather than a notice.
2. **Use the empty right half** (taste). The reading column on the left is a fine choice. But the open space on the right could hold a small at-a-glance panel: departure times, crossing length, "bikes free". That would serve skimmers while the prose stays on the left for people reading properly (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, on giving different kinds of information their own column).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, and whether the page carries anything below this view, such as a timetable link.
