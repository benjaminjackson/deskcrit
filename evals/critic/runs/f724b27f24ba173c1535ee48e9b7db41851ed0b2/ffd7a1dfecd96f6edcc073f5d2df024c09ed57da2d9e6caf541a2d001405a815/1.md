**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three paragraphs that all look the same, in a left column. The right half of the frame and the bottom third are empty. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and anonymous, and after the heading nothing tells the eye where the useful part is.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page for people planning a crossing. It should tell them when the boat runs, how long a crossing takes, how bikes work, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is competent, but the page doesn't do its job well. The facts a passenger needs (first boat, last boat, the winter cut-off) are either missing or buried mid-sentence in vague prose. Someone who opens it to answer "can I still catch a boat tonight?" can't.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts people act on are vague or missing (defect, copy).** Paragraph 1 says the ferry runs "between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk". "In winter" has no dates. Those are the lines a passenger plans around and will hold the operator to, and each one can be read more than one way. If one reader takes "late evening" as 9 pm and another as 11 pm, one of them misses the last boat. "Before dusk" moves by an hour across the season, so it isn't a time anyone can plan to. Even "every forty minutes" gives no departure times to count from. The page then sends readers to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing", so a web reader has to go to the quay to find out when the boat leaves. Fix: give the first and last departures as clock times, give the winter dates, and show the departure times themselves or link to them. Then "every forty minutes" and "one boat each hour" are facts the reader can check, not descriptions.
2. **You can't scan it for the one thing you came for (defect; Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** The three paragraphs are three separate topics: timetable and crossing time, bicycles, and winter and where to find changes. But nothing labels them, and they're identical in size, weight and shape. A passenger looking for the last boat or the bike rule has to read every line, because the answers sit in the middle of sentences ("forty minutes", "no extra charge", "one boat each hour"). Fix: give each paragraph a short subhead that names its topic ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter"). Pull the departure times out of the prose into a small block or list the eye can find in a second. The bike rules (board after foot passengers, lock to the rail, bags stay on) also work better as a short list than as a sentence.

**What's working.**
- The text setting is solid. It's a plain, sturdy sans at a comfortable size, with lines that look about 65–75 characters long and leading that suits that length. The rag is gentle and there are no lone words on last lines. Keep all of this when you add structure.
- The two-level type scale is clear. The heading differs from the body in both size and weight, so it reads as a title without shouting. Add subheads as a third level, set apart by weight, not a size only slightly bigger than the body.
- The copy is plain and human ("a little longer when the tide turns against the boat"). The voice fits a local ferry. Keep it, and put the hard facts next to it.

**Where it could go further**
1. **Give the page one fact that dominates: the next departure, or the timetable (taste).** This page is for planning a crossing, and right now the heading, a generic label, is the only strong element. Make the times the visual centre: a clear block of departures, with first and last boat set in heavier type. Then the page answers its main question at a glance, and the prose becomes the supporting notes its heading says it is. This is the "one element dominates" idea in Müller-Brockmann and Refactoring UI, used for the thing the viewer actually came for.
2. **Use the empty right half for the information you look up (taste).** At this width the right half does nothing. That's acceptable as a margin, but it's wasted on a reference page. A narrow side column for the timetable, the landings and the winter dates would keep the reading column as it is and put what people look up beside it, in a grid with a clear job for each column (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
3. **Give the page some identity (taste).** Nothing here says river, ferry or this town. It could be anyone's notice. One quiet signal, such as a single colour the service owns used on the subheads or the times, or a small route line from north landing to market quay, would make the page look like it belongs to this ferry and help regular passengers recognise it.
