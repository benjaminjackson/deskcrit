**First read.** My eye goes straight to the bold headline. Then it hits three grey blocks of prose that look the same, and I start reading from the top because nothing else offers a way in. It reads as a plain information page for people using a small river ferry. It feels calm, clean and a little bureaucratic, like a notice pinned up in a shelter. The empty right half and bottom third give it room, but they also make it feel unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for people planning a crossing, who want to find out quickly when the boat goes, how long it takes, and whether they can bring a bike.

**Verdict.** It's well set and doesn't do its job. Every fact a passenger acts on is buried mid-sentence, and the facts that matter most (when the boat runs and when the last one leaves) are never actually given.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times you'd plan around are vague or missing.** This is a defect in the copy. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat leaves. "In winter" doesn't say which months. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" is a time that changes every day, so two readers will pick two different departures, and the one who guesses late misses the boat. "One boat each hour" doesn't say on the hour or at half past. Timetable facts are what a passenger holds the operator to. Fix: give actual first and last departure times, the dates winter service starts and ends, and the winter last-boat time, or a clear link to the timetable. At the moment the page sends people to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which they can only read once they've already walked to the landing.
2. **The facts are buried in prose.** This is a defect. All three paragraphs are the same weight with no subheads, so someone looking for "can I bring my bike?" or "how often in winter?" has to read every line. People skim pages like this rather than read them (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). The page is short, which helps, but it's built like an essay when it should work like a reference card. Fix: give each paragraph a short subhead that says what it covers ("How often the ferry runs", "Bringing a bicycle", "Winter timetable"). Pull frequency, crossing time and the first and last boats out as a few lines someone can take in at a glance, then let the prose cover the details (tide delays, boarding order, bags).

**What's working.**
- The type is set for reading. The body is around 18 px in a plain sans, lines run about 65–72 characters, the leading is generous, the text is flush left, and the near-black on white has strong contrast. Keep all of this when you add structure.
- The two-level hierarchy is clear. The headline differs from the body in both size and weight, so you always know where to start.
- The paragraphs are separated by space only, with no indents and no rules. It's quiet and correct.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste:* Use the empty right column. A small "At a glance" panel there (next boat, the frequency now and in winter, crossing time, bikes free) would answer most visits without any reading, and it would fill the space that currently looks unfinished.
2. *Taste:* Rewrite the headline to say what the page gives you, for example "Crossing times and what to bring". "Notes for ferry passengers" is a category label, and it reads like small print people are free to skip.
3. *Taste:* Write the bicycle rules to the rider as "you". "Wheel your bike on after foot passengers… keep your bags on it" is easier to follow than the third-person "Riders wheel them on… The crew asks that…", and it puts the reader in the scene.
