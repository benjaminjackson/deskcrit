**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three paragraphs of the same grey-black weight and stays there. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, but nothing pops out to scan. The times, the bike rule and the winter change are all buried mid-sentence.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells someone about to take this ferry when it runs, how long it takes, what to do with a bike, and how winter changes things.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and competent, but the page doesn't do its job. A passenger comes for a time and a rule and gets an essay with no real times in it. The biggest problem is the content and its structure, not the styling.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts a passenger acts on are vague or missing.** *(Defect.)* "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk", "in winter": none of these lets anyone plan a trip. Someone standing at the north landing at 18:50 can't tell whether a boat is coming. "Before dusk" moves by more than an hour across the winter, so a reader can easily miss the last boat. "In winter" has no dates. The page never says what a foot passenger pays, only that bikes cost nothing extra. These are the lines people remember and hold the operator to, so loose wording here is a defect, not a style note. Fix: give the first and last departure from each landing as clock times. Give the winter period as dates and the last winter crossing as a time, or "about 30 min before sunset, see board". Add the fare. If the timetable really changes week to week, say so plainly and point to where the current one lives online, not only "in the shelter at each landing". By then the reader is already standing there.
2. **The page is built for reading straight through, not for scanning.** *(Defect. Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*.)* Three different topics (regular service, bicycles, winter changes) sit as three paragraphs that look exactly alike, with no subheads. The numbers are spelled out in words ("forty", "twelve"), so the eye has nothing to catch. A rider looking for the bike rule has to read the first paragraph to find out it isn't there. Fix: give each topic a short heading ("Timetable", "Bicycles", "Winter"). Lead with the departures as a small table or list, with figures as digits. Keep the bike instructions as a short ordered sequence: board after foot passengers, lock to the side rail, keep bags on the bike.

**What's working.**
- The type is set well for reading. Body text looks around 18 px with comfortable spacing between lines, at roughly 60–72 characters a line. One sans family, with only weight and size separating the headline from the text. Keep this setting when you add structure.
- The order of importance is unmistakable. There is one bold headline well above the body size and nothing competing with it. Subheads should sit clearly between the two, not add a fourth near-size.
- The voice is calm and human ("the crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle"). It suits a small local service, so keep the tone when you sharpen the facts.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *(Taste.)* **Put the answer above the explanation.** The one thing most visitors want is the next boat. A stronger version opens with the departure times in large figures, the most scannable thing on the page, and lets the prose follow as context. That uses the empty right-hand half for something a passenger needs, rather than leaving it blank by default.
2. *(Taste.)* **Give it a sense of place.** As it stands it could be any operator's notes page. One small committed detail would tell people it's this ferry: a simple route line from north landing to market quay with the 12-minute crossing marked, or a single river colour used on the headings. Make it one thing, not decoration everywhere.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where most passengers will read it.
