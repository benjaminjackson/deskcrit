**First read.** The eye goes straight to the headline, "Update the card that pays for your subscription", and then down into the body text. Nothing else competes, because the nav and sidebar are small and grey. It reads as a calm, tidy help-centre article for a SaaS billing product. It feels trustworthy and quiet, maybe a little bare.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help article for an account owner or billing admin who has come to swap the card on their subscription. They want to do it now and know what happens next.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are competent, but the article doesn't do its main job. A page titled "Update the card…" never tells the reader how to update the card. The steps and the way into the billing page are both missing, so the reader finishes the article and still has to go looking.

**What would move it most**
1. **The task is missing.** The only instruction is "from the billing page", in the first sentence of the opening paragraph. It isn't a link, and it gives no path (which menu? which button?). There are four links in the body, but they go to billing history, roles, paying by invoice and restoring a plan. None of them goes to where the card is changed. Someone who came to do one thing leaves with background information and a hunt ahead of them. This is a **defect**. It breaks Nielsen's "recognition rather than recall": the page makes the user find a place it only names. Fix: put the action first. Give a direct link to the billing page and two to four numbered steps that use the product's own labels (for example "Settings → Billing → Payment method → Replace card"). Then move the current first paragraph, about which invoice gets charged, under the steps as "What happens next".
2. **The decline rules can be read two ways, and they concern money.** "We try a declined card again after three days and after seven days" doesn't say what the seven days count from. It could be seven days after the first decline or seven days after the first retry, so the account drops to free on day 7 or day 10. "The subscription moves to the free plan" also doesn't tell the reader what they lose: seats, data, features? Dates and downgrades are exactly what a customer will later hold you to. This is a **defect**. Fix: anchor both retries to one event ("3 and 7 days after the first decline"), state the day the plan changes, and add one line in the reader's terms about what stops working on the free plan.
3. **The page doesn't show where you are.** The sidebar doesn't mark "Update a payment card", and the top nav doesn't mark "Billing". The breadcrumb names a level, "Payment methods", that appears nowhere in the sidebar or nav. The sidebar label also differs from the H1. A reader who arrived from search can't tell where this page sits or what its neighbours are. This is a **convention** (Nielsen, visibility of system status; Laubheimer, NN/g menu checklist, guideline 5) and it costs something real in a help centre. Fix: mark the current item in the sidebar and nav with one clear cue, such as bolder weight or a bar. Then make the breadcrumb, sidebar and title use the same names.

**Smaller things.**
- Inline links (for example "roles and permissions" and "paying by invoice" in the second paragraph) are marked by blue alone. Blue against near-black body text looks well under the 3:1 that WCAG technique G183 asks for. Underline them.
- Body lines look like they run about 80–85 characters, a bit past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the column slightly. There's room on the right.
- The headline breaks "your / subscription", which splits a noun phrase. Shorten the title or rebalance the break. This is mostly taste; the catalogue's source for this rule is unverified.
- The help centre header has no search, which is the main way people find help articles. Add it to the header (Nielsen, flexibility and efficiency).

**What's working.**
- One clear type hierarchy: a large bold H1, a bold H2 and a comfortable body size with generous leading. The article reads easily at a glance. Keep this.
- The sidebar's "In this section" label and the breadcrumb sit on the same line, and the content column shares a clean left edge. The page feels ordered without any boxes or rules.
- The links are descriptive ("check your billing history", not "click here"), and the copy is plain and speaks to "you".

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav, sidebar and inline links, which matter all the more because the links have no underline.
