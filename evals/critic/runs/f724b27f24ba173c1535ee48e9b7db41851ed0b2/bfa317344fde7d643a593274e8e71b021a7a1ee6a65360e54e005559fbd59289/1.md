To learn what the coloured wedges are, the eye has to cross the page to the legend and match colours. The page reads as a calm, well-made annual-report page about where costs went. It feels trustworthy, but it doesn't tell you much.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is one page of a printed or PDF annual report. Its job is to show shareholders and staff how fiscal 2026 operating costs split by category, and what that says about the business.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and correct, and it says almost nothing. A quarter of costs sit in an unexplained "Other". The title names the topic, not the finding. With no amounts and no prior year, the reader can't tell whether any of these shares is good, bad or new.

**What would move it most**
1. **"Other" is the second-largest category and the title draws no conclusion.** The grey 25% wedge at the top left is bigger than rent, cloud and marketing. A reader of an annual report will ask what a quarter of operating costs is spent on, and the page doesn't say. The title "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" only labels the chart, so the reader has to find the point alone, and here the point they find is the gap. *Defect:* it leaves out what the reader most wants to know. Direction: break "Other" down until no remaining bucket is bigger than the smallest named category, or say in the caption what it holds. Then rewrite the title as the finding, such as "Salaries took 38% of operating costs; …". The title fix is taste/common sense; the entry for it has no approved source.
2. **There are no amounts and no comparison.** Every figure is a share of one year with nothing next to it. The reader can't tell whether salaries at 38% is up or down, or whether a share went up because spending grew or the total fell. On a page about costs, that is usually the question. *Convention:* Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2, says to give each figure a comparison. Direction: put the amount in currency and the change from fiscal 2025 in the legend rows, which already have room on the right. Or replace the pie with sorted paired bars for 2025 and 2026.
3. **The legend separates names from wedges.** The pie shows only percentages. The category names sit about 300 px away in the legend, so the reader matches teal, orange and purple by hue. Those three are close in lightness, so a colour-blind reader or a greyscale print loses the match. Each percentage also appears twice, on the wedge and in the legend. *Convention:* Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, puts labels on the graphic, and WCAG 1.4.1 says colour can't be the only way to carry meaning. Direction: put the category name on or beside each wedge. Then either drop the legend, or make it a small table that adds what the pie can't show (amounts and change, as in point 2).
