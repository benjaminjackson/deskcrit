**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the bold serif headline, drops to the byline, and moves into the three paragraphs. It reads as a calm, bookish cycling-route article. In version 1 the body text is noticeably washed out: the headline is ink-dark and the paragraphs are a mid grey. That makes the article look like secondary text, or like a page that hasn't finished loading. In version 2 the body is a dark grey that reads as the text itself.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a route write-up on a cycling blog. A rider reads it from start to finish to plan the trip, so the body text is the product.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job better. The only change is the body text colour, and it fixes the one real defect on the page. Version 1's pale grey paragraphs look to be under the 4.5:1 contrast minimum. On a page that exists to be read, that weakens exactly what the visitor came for.

**What would move it most**
1. **Body text contrast (version 1, fixed in version 2).** The three paragraphs at roughly 290–985 px across and 290–630 px down are set in a light warm grey, which I'd estimate at about 3.5–4:1 on white. That is below WCAG 1.4.3's 4.5:1 for normal-size text.
   - **What the reader meets:** six minutes of reading at reduced contrast. Readers with low vision, or anyone reading outdoors on a phone before a ride, lose detail first, and these paragraphs carry the details riders act on: tyres of 32 mm, cafés closing at four, and lights from October.
   - **Hierarchy cost:** the paragraphs end up lighter than the byline above them, which turns the order of importance upside down.
   - **Version 2:** the body is darkened to a grey that looks well above 4.5:1. The text is still softer than the headline, so the type keeps its gentle look while the text becomes readable.
   - **Kind of claim:** defect and convention (WCAG 1.4.3). To confirm, measure the actual hex value of version 1's body colour.

**Smaller things.** Both versions have this one: in the first paragraph, "brickworks." sits alone on the last line. Tighten the measure slightly or rebreak the line so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- **The type pairing:** a bold serif headline with a regular serif body and a small sans byline. Each of the three sizes has an obvious job, and the step from headline to body is large and decisive.
- **The column:** the line length is near the top of Bringhurst's 45–75 range, the leading is generous, and the spacing between paragraphs is even. It's a comfortable reading column.
- **The copy:** concrete and useful ("tyres of 32 mm or more", "both close at four in winter"). Every line tells the rider something they can act on.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Give the route its numbers up top** (taste). Thirty-one miles, flat, gravel after the third swing bridge, lights after October. A rider planning a day wants these at a glance. A small line of facts under the byline would let them decide in five seconds whether to read on, without adding visual noise.
2. **Let the masthead belong to the column** (taste). "Lowland Rides" sits on a different left edge (120 px) from the article (290 px), and nothing else on the page uses that edge. Either align it with the column or make the masthead a clearer piece of identity, so the page feels like a publication rather than a template.
