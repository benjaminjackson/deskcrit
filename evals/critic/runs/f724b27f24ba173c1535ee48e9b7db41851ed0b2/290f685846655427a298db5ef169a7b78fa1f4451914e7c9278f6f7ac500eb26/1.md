I think version 2 is better. The two are the same layout. The only change is that version 2 sets the two body paragraphs in a plain serif instead of the script used for the logo, and that one change decides it.

**First read.** In both versions my eye goes to the big serif headline ("A restored hay barn for weddings of up to 120 guests"), then across to the large tan block on the right, then back down to the brown "Book a visit" button. It reads as a wedding-venue home page: warm, calm and a bit rustic. In version 1 the second look hits a wall of brushy script. It looks like a wedding invitation, but reading it is slow. In version 2 the paragraphs just read.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a small venue's home page, meant to get engaged couples to believe this barn fits their wedding and to book a visit.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job; version 1 makes the copy hard to read, and the copy is what makes the case. That copy is the best thing on the page: 1870, taken apart beam by beam, bring your own caterer, two nights in the cottage. Both versions share one large problem. The space where the barn should be is an empty gradient, so the most important picture on a venue page is missing.

**Why version 2 wins**
- **Version 1 sets running text in a script face (defect).** About eight lines of paragraph copy are in the same heavy brush script as the "Hollins Barn" logo. Letters run into each other and every word has to be worked out, so readers skim or skip exactly the details that set this barn apart. Bringhurst (6.2.1) says to keep decorative faces for a few words of display and set paragraphs in a face made for reading. Version 2 does exactly that.
- **Version 1's type has no plan (convention).** It uses script for the logo and body, a bold serif for the headline and a sans for the nav and button. Using the script for both the brand mark and the text weakens the logo and makes the body look like part of the branding. Version 2 keeps script for the logo only, serif for headline and body (bold against regular), and sans for the nav and button. Each face now has one job (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 7–8).

**What would move version 2 most**
1. **The picture panel is empty (defect).** The right half is a rounded box of tan-to-brown gradient with nothing in it, about 470×520 px. Couples choose a venue by eye. With no photo they can't see the oak floor, the glass doors or the orchard that the copy describes, and the button asks them to book a visit to a place they haven't been shown. If a photo failed to load, this is fine once it loads. If the gradient is the design, put in a real photo of the restored interior or the orchard side.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "120" ends line 2 and "guests" sits alone on line 3. That splits a number from its noun and leaves a one-word last line. Use a non-breaking space between "120" and "guests", or reflow so the break falls after "for" or "weddings" (Bringhurst 2.4.6).
- First paragraph: "morning." sits alone on its last line. Adjust the measure or rewrite a word so the last line carries two or three words (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The copy is concrete and talks to the reader: a capacity, a date, what you get ("the barn, the orchard lawn, and the cottage for two nights") and what you're free to do ("bring your own caterer, band, and florist"). It answers the questions couples actually ask. Keep it.
- The type plan in version 2: script only in the logo, a bold serif headline over a regular serif body, and a quiet sans for the nav and button.
- "Book a visit" is one clear, specific ask in the only strong colour on the page, with plenty of room around it.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Let the photo carry the page (taste). Use one strong image of the inside, with the beams and the open glass doors to the orchard, bled to the top or right edge instead of in a rounded card. On a venue page the room is the product, and a bigger, braver image does more selling than any line of copy.
2. Bring the strongest fact up into view (taste). "Bring your own caterer, band, and florist" is what sets this venue apart from most, and it's buried mid-paragraph. Pulling it out as a short line under the headline or next to the button would let couples see it in a glance.
3. Let the ask carry what the visit includes (taste). "Book a visit" is clear, but the copy already promises "as many times as you need". A line by the button like "Walk the barn with us. Come back as often as you like" turns booking from a commitment into something with no risk.
