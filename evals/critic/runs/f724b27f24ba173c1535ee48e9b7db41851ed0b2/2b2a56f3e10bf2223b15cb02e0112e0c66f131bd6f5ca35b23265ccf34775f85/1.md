**First read.** In version 1 the eye skips past the headline and lands on the three card titles ("Perennials", "Fruit trees", "Workshops"), then the three tinted blocks. "Hardy plants grown on our hillside" reads like a section intro, not the main message. In version 2 the eye lands on the headline first, reads the subline under it, then moves down to the three cards. Both feel calm, earthy and quiet. Version 2 also feels confident; version 1 feels unsure of what it wants to say.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of a small plant nursery. Its job is to tell gardeners in cold areas what makes the plants different and send them on to plants or workshops.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job better. The one change is a much larger headline, and it fixes version 1's biggest problem: there, the brand's main promise is smaller than the category labels under it. Both versions still have no pictures and no ask, so version 2 works only as a calm wireframe, not yet as a shop front.

**What would move it most** (findings for version 2, after the comparison)

0. *What changed (v1 → v2).* In version 1 the headline looks about 26 px bold and the card titles about 28 px bold: same weight, almost the same size, and the titles are slightly bigger. Four items compete to be seen first, and the promise loses. Version 2 sets the headline at about 64 px. That gives one clear entry point and a plain order: promise, subline, categories, card text. This is the fix Tschichold describes in *The New Typography*: show the order of importance through differences of size. That one change decides the comparison. **Defect in v1, fixed in v2.**
1. **The three image blocks are empty tints (both versions).** The three rectangles at the top of the cards are flat sage and olive panels with no photo or illustration. For a nursery, the plants are the product. A viewer who can't see a plant, a tree in fruit or a class in progress has nothing to want, and the cards look unfinished. If these are placeholders for images that didn't load, that explains it, but as shown they fail. The fix is to put real photographs in the blocks, one look across all three, with each one showing what its card sells. **Defect.**
2. **There is no ask anywhere (both versions).** There is no button and no link styled as one. The only way forward is the small nav text at top right, and the cards give no sign that they can be clicked. The promise "ready for a cold garden on the day they arrive" builds intent, then the page gives the viewer nowhere to act on it. Add one primary action under the subline that names a concrete next step in the viewer's terms, for example "Shop plants for cold gardens". Make the cards look clickable too, with a visible "Browse 300 perennials →" or similar link. Material Design and Apple HIG both call for one dominant primary action per view. **Defect.**

**Smaller things.**
- The Perennials card text leaves "pots." alone on its own last line, in both versions (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Shorten the line ("Over 300 kinds in 1 and 2 litre pots") or widen the measure.
- The version 2 headline breaks "on our / hillside", leaving one word on the second line. Make the text box slightly narrower so it breaks "Hardy plants grown / on our hillside", or wide enough to fit on one line.
- The nav doesn't mark the current page, and its links are small text separated by middots. Check that each link's hit area is at least 24 px (WCAG 2.5.8).
- The bottom third of the screen is empty in both versions, more so in version 1. That space would be better spent on the missing pictures or the ask than left over at the bottom.

**What's working.**
- The copy has substance. "Raised outdoors in Fernhill, so they are ready for a cold garden on the day they arrive" gives a specific reason to buy and speaks to the viewer's own garden. It isn't filler that could sit on any shop's site. Keep it.
- The palette is calm and owned: an off-white ground, dark green-black type and three muted greens. It fits a nursery and holds up in grayscale.
- The grid is clean. The three equal columns share a left edge with the logo and the headline, and the gutters are even.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste.* Let photography carry the brand. One strong photo of the actual hillside next to or behind the headline would prove "grown on our hillside" instead of just saying it. That would be the one thing no other nursery's page could show.
2. *Taste.* Push the headline's lead a little further. It is now clearly dominant, so the card titles (about 28 px) could drop a step, closer to the body size, making the scale three clear levels instead of four.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the tint blocks are placeholders for images that failed to load (check in a live browser); hover and focus states on the nav and cards; how the three columns reflow on a phone.
