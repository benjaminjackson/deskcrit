**First read.** The eye goes first to the row of four coloured buttons (teal, purple, orange, blue), then jumps up to "Low-light houseplants", then moves across the four cartoon pots. It reads as a small plant shop's category page. It feels tidy and friendly but anonymous: the plants look like stand-ins, and the buttons look as if each does something different.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a category page where shoppers with a dim room compare four plants and add one to the cart.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but two things work against the job. The four different button colours draw the eye first and suggest a meaning they don't have. And the cards don't help anyone choose, because the pictures don't show the plants and the text doesn't say how they differ in low light.

**What would move it most**
1. **Four colours for one action** (defect). The four "Add to cart" buttons do the same thing but are teal, purple, orange and blue. Three problems follow:
   - They are the strongest colour on the page, so they outrank the headline and the products. The first read is "four kinds of button", not "four plants".
   - The colours suggest categories, as if each plant belonged to a different range or the buttons did different things. A shopper might stop to work out what purple means.
   - Together with the leaf green and pot terracotta, they bring the page to about six hues with no system behind them.

   One kind of element should look one way everywhere (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*; Nielsen 4, consistency), and a palette should be one neutral range plus one or two brand hues (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*). Fix: use one button colour for all four, ideally a hue the brand owns, such as a deep green from the leaves. Then the plants, not the buttons, become what the eye compares.
2. **The pictures don't show the products** (defect). All four illustrations are the same terracotta pot with a few generic green leaves. The "snake plant" has round lobes, not tall upright blades. The "pothos" stands upright even though the card says it "trails to 6 ft". The cast iron plant and ZZ look like variations on one shape. A shopper can't recognise a plant they've seen in a friend's flat or picture how it will look in their room, and on a product page that is the picture's whole job. If these are placeholders, they are fine for a mockup and still need replacing. If they are the intended art, draw each plant's real shape: blades, glossy paddles, broad strappy leaves, trailing vines. Photos would work too.
3. **The cards don't answer the question the page asks** (defect, common sense). The headline promises plants for low light, but the only differences between cards are pot size, mature size and price. The shopper still has to work out which plant handles the least light, which needs the least care, and which is safe for pets. Add one line per card on what makes each plant different, in the shopper's terms, such as "Survives a north-facing hallway; water monthly." Then the comparison happens on the page, not in another tab.

**Smaller things.**
- Top navigation: nothing marks "Houseplants" as the current section, so the shopper can't see where they are. Mark it with one clear cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist"). The headline goes some way toward showing it, which is why this is minor.

**What's working.**
- The subhead "Four plants that do well more than six feet from a window" is excellent copy. It is concrete, measurable and in the shopper's terms. Keep that voice and carry it into the cards.
- Each card has a clear internal order: name, specs, a bold price, then the action. Spacing is consistent, it sits on a clean four-column grid, and the type uses about four sizes with clear jobs. Keep this skeleton as it is.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 5 yes, 3 no (image and headline make one idea; every element does a job; each kind of element looks the same)
What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (one element dominates)
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 12 yes, 1 no (no accidental groups)
Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
Color: 9 lines, 8 yes, 1 no (small palette)
Pictures: 12 lines, 11 yes, 1 no (every image shows its content)
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 30 yes, 2 no (shows where the user is; puts what the user needs next in front of them), 1 unsure (focus indicator)
