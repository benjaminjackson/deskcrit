**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the big serif title, drops to the two columns, and settles on the method. It reads at once as a calm, editorial recipe page from a small food site. The only difference is the ground. On v1's warm cream the page feels like a cookbook. On v2's pure white the same type feels colder and more like a default template.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a recipe page that a home cook reads before and during cooking, to check what to buy and what to do.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job slightly better, because the cream ground suits the warm serif and the homely subject, and the page reads as one thing. Version 2 changes only the background to white, and the piece loses its mood without gaining anything in reading. In both versions the biggest flaw is a spacing problem: the serving and timing line floats alone between the title and the recipe.

**What changed between the versions**
The background went from warm cream (v1) to pure white (v2). Nothing else moved. Contrast and legibility are fine in both, so the change only affects mood and coherence:
- The serif display face, the soft rules, and the subject (leek soup, brown butter, "smells of hazelnuts") all speak warmth. Cream carries that through the whole frame. White removes it, and the page starts to look like any blog template (Itten, *The Art of Color*, on colour temperature carrying feeling). Taste, but it decides the comparison.
- In v2 the hairline rules under the ingredients look faintly yellow-grey against white (crop of the ingredients list, rows ~428–613). They look like they were picked for the cream ground and left behind. In v1 they sit quietly in the paper. This is a small craft point, but it shows that v2's white wasn't thought through.

**What would move it most** (applies to both; written for v1)
1. **"Serves 4 · 45 minutes" belongs to nothing.** It sits about 100 px below the title and about 100 px above the Ingredients/Method headings, so the gaps on either side are equal. The cook can't tell whether it is a subtitle or part of the recipe, and the page above the fold has two big empty bands that do no work. Defect (grouping by proximity: Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II). Fix: pull the line up under the title, about a third of the current gap, so that title and facts make one header block. Then let a single larger space separate that block from the ingredients and method.

**Smaller things.**
- Top navigation ("Recipes · Seasons · About"): nothing marks which section the visitor is in. Give "Recipes" one cue, such as weight or an underline (Nielsen 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Method step 4: "chives." sits alone on its last line. Rewrap the step or tighten the measure slightly so the line doesn't end on a single short word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- Bottom of the page: an empty band of about 150 px under the columns. It is harmless on its own, but together with the gaps around the meta line the space looks spread out rather than gathered (Ruder, "Contrasts").

**What's working.**
- The title clearly dominates, and the scale is clean: title, section heads, and body are distinct levels, with one small size for the meta line and nav.
- The pairing is clear: a sans for the scannable parts (ingredients, labels, nav) and a serif for the method's reading text. The method lines run about 70 characters with generous leading, which makes them easy to come back to with flour on your hands.
- Ingredients sit in a narrow ruled column next to numbered steps, so a cook can scan what to buy and follow what to do without hunting.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Make the cream a deliberate brand colour, not just a background. Give the page one warm accent, such as a brown-butter tone on the step numbers or the nav's current item, so that "Second Helpings" has a colour it owns. Taste; Itten on colour carrying mood.
2. Treat "Serves 4 · 45 minutes" as the useful facts they are. Set them as a small two-item block in the header, so the cook sees right away whether the recipe fits their evening. Taste: these are the first things a cook decides on.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the two columns reflow on a phone, where ingredients and method would need to stack.
