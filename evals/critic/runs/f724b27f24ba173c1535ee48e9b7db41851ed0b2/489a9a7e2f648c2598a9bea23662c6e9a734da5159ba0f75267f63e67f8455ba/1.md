**Version 2 does the job better.** The two versions share the same words, type, colours and column positions. The one thing that changed is the table's rules. Version 1 draws a near-black outline around every cell. Version 2 keeps only pale horizontal lines between rows and lets the column alignment do the rest.

**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the title "Departures from Corn Exchange", drops to the bold times in the left column, then catches the two red words on the right ("4 min late", "Cancelled"). In version 1 the black grid arrives as fast as the data. The page reads as a form of boxes, and the eye has to step over a vertical line at every column to follow a row. Version 2 reads as a calm, scannable timetable. The rows are the shapes you see, and the text is the darkest thing on the page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a live departures screen for one bus stand, so a rider can find their bus, see when it leaves, and see whether it is running.

**Verdict.** Version 2 works: it is clear, quiet and quick to scan. Version 1 does the same job less well, because its heavy grid competes with the timetable and slows reading along each row. In version 2, the biggest thing left is that the disruptions are easy to miss: they are regular-weight words far across the row from the bus they belong to.

**What changed, and why it matters**
- **Version 1: every cell is boxed in black.** That is about 50 compartments with 1 px near-black rules, roughly as dark as the text. The vertical rules cut each row into five pieces just where the eye needs to read across, from 14:40 to Northgate to Cancelled. They add nothing that the shared left edges of the columns don't already show. Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 157, says table rules should "illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell". Tufte's data-ink principle points the same way. This breaks a convention, and here it costs reading speed. Version 2 applies the standard fix: no vertical rules, and pale row dividers that hold each row together without competing with it.

**What would move version 2 most**
1. **Disruptions don't stand out enough** (defect for the people affected). The "Cancelled" for the 19 at 14:40 and the "4 min late" for the 7A at 14:11 are only told apart by red text at the same weight as "On time". They sit in the far-right column, about 400 px from the destination, across the wide and mostly empty Via column. A rider reading "14:40 … Northgate" can stop before reaching the status, which is the one cell that changes what they do. Fix: make the exception the loudest thing in its row. You could make "Cancelled" bold, give the disrupted rows a pale tint, or narrow the Via column so Status sits closer to Destination. Leave the "On time" rows quiet, so the exceptions still stand out from them.

**Smaller things.**
- The column labels (Time, Route, Destination, Via, Status) are small grey bold. That is fine for a desk screen, but they look small if this is ever shown on a display read from a few metres away.

**What's working.**
- **The bold times in a fixed-width column.** They make the left edge a clean scale to scan down, and they are the right anchor for a departures list.
- **Red used only for disruptions, and always with words.** The meaning never depends on colour alone (WCAG 1.4.1), so keep that pairing.
- **"Stand C · Updated 14:02" under the title.** It tells the rider where they are and how fresh the data is, without adding another heading.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the next departure lead** (taste). Riders mostly want "what's next, and is it on time?". Making the first row, or the first row that is still running, a step larger or setting it apart would answer that at a glance and turn a correct table into a board people trust.
2. **Size the Via column to its content** (taste). The large empty band in the middle of each row is space taken from the relationship that matters (bus → status). Tightening it would make each row a shorter, single read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether it refreshes in place, and how a status change is signalled when it happens; viewing distance, if this is a physical display.
