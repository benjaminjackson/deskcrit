**What it's for.** You didn't tell me, so I'm assuming this is the landing screen for a local pool's programmes. Its main job is to get adults who can't swim to sign up for the Tuesday class.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The headline makes a clear, warm promise, but the page gives the reader nothing to do about it: no button, no time, no price, no place. The rest of the page then drifts to offers that aren't for that reader.

**What would move it most**
1. **There is no ask (defect).** Someone who reads "Learn to swim … a coach in the water beside you" and thinks "that's me" has nowhere to go. There's no button, no start date, no time on Tuesday, no price, no class size behind "small", and no pool name or location. The moment of interest passes without anything happening. The fix is one primary action directly under the subtitle that names the exact step, something like "Book your first Tuesday class". Put the facts the reader will hold you to (time, cost, how many people per coach) right beside it. Nervous adult beginners mostly fear being out of their depth. An ask that lets them judge the class on their own terms, such as "first session free" or "you start in the shallow end", is stronger than asking them to trust the copy.

2. **The page doesn't stay on its own promise (defect, copy).** The headline is about learning. The two blocks below are "Join the lanes", which is open lane swimming with "three speeds", and "Family hour". Neither is for a non-swimmer, and lane speeds are exactly the thing that scares a beginner. A reader who came to learn finds the page changing the subject. A lane swimmer, meanwhile, only finds their offer after a headline that told them this page wasn't for them. Decide what the page is. If it's the adult lessons page, the space below should back up the lesson promise (what the first session is like, who the coach is, what you need to bring) and the other programmes should move elsewhere. If it's the pool's front page, the headline needs to cover all three offers and give them equal weight.

3. **The middle of the hierarchy collapses (convention: Bringhurst, scale; Müller-Brockmann, distinct levels).** The size drops from the headline (around 96 px) to the subtitle (around 22 px, grey). The card headings "Join the lanes" and "Family hour" then come in at about 20 px, regular weight, only slightly darker. That puts the subtitle and the card headings within a couple of pixels of each other, so they read as the same level. I count four sizes, and two of them collide. Keep three clear levels: headline, one intro or lead size, and body. Separate the card headings from the subtitle by weight (semibold) rather than by another near-identical size.

4. **The empty space is left over, not placed (convention: Ruder on gathered space; Arnheim, *Balance*).** All of the content sits in the top-left 60% of the screen. The lower half and the right edge are blank, and nothing on that side balances the heavy headline. Ruder's empty space works because it pushes against something dense, but here it just reads as "the page ran out". There are also no pictures at all on a page about water and being looked after, so nothing fills that space or sets the mood. The fix is to give the open area a job: a photo of a coach in the water with an adult learner, or the booking block. Then the space that stays empty is space you chose. If this screenshot was cut short and the page continues, check how the first screen looks at this size anyway, because it's all most visitors will judge.

**What's working.**

- "With a coach in the water beside you" is the best line on the page. It names the fear and answers it. Build the ask around it.
- The text is well set: body lines run about 55 to 70 characters, leading is comfortable, the grey text still looks readable against white, the left edges are crisp and the two columns share baselines.
