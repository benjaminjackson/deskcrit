**First read.** I see the title first, then the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%. My eye goes across the four bars and then to the table on the right. It looks like a page from an internal analytics dashboard for someone in growth or product. It feels calm, tidy and easy to trust.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal SaaS dashboard page. It should tell a product or growth lead how well each plan's trials turn into paid accounts, so they can decide where to put effort.

**Verdict.** The page is well built, but it shows numbers without telling you what they mean. One quarter with no comparison means you can't tell whether 4.0% is good. The bars also suggest a ranking that the counts in the table can't fully support. Those two gaps stop the page from supporting a decision.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing to compare against.** Each bar shows one quarter's rate with no previous quarter, target or trend. A reader can see that Growth beats Starter. They can't tell whether conversion went up or down, or whether 3.1% is a problem. For a performance dashboard that's the key question, and the page doesn't answer it. Few's dashboard pitfalls name this directly: a number with no comparison doesn't tell good from bad. Defect. **Fix:** show last quarter next to each bar, as a ghost bar or a tick mark, or add a target line. Then state the change in the bar labels or the table.
2. **The chart looks more certain than the data is.** The gaps between plans look solid, but the table shows how small some of the counts are:
   - Enterprise's 3.4% comes from 38 conversions out of 1,120 trials. That is roughly ±1 point of sampling noise.
   - Growth's 4.0% (118 of 2,950) and Team's 3.6% (185 of 5,140) are within noise of each other.

   The tallest bar gets attention first, so a reader will likely conclude "Growth converts best" and act on it. The data doesn't support that ranking. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) asks for uncertainty to be shown when you present rates from samples like this. Defect. **Fix:** add interval whiskers, or at least an "n =" under each plan label. Once that's done, give the chart a title that states the finding, at whatever strength the data supports. Right now the title only names the topic.
3. **The date wording contradicts itself.** The subtitle says the page covers trials that converted within 30 days. The source line says the data covers trial events from 1 July to 30 September. But the definition counts conversions up to 30 October, which must come from data after 30 September. "Trials still open on 30 October" also doesn't make sense once every Q3 trial has passed its 30 days by then. A careful reader will wonder whether late-September trials are undercounted, and that weakens trust in every number on the page. Defect (common sense). **Fix:** state one window plainly. For example: "trials started 1 July to 30 September; conversions counted to 30 October". Then make the source line match it.

**Smaller things.**
- The table heading "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" leaves out the Paid column. Use "Trials and paid accounts by plan, Q3 2025".
- There's no control to pick the period or a segment. A dashboard page that only shows Q3 2025 will push people to ask an analyst for any other quarter. Add a period picker by the title (Nielsen 7).

**What's working.**
- Value labels sit right on top of each bar, and the bars start from a true zero. With quiet gridlines, the chart is nearly all data, so the reading is honest and quick. Keep that restraint when you add comparisons.
- The table holds the raw counts in tabular, right-aligned figures. That's what lets a reader check the rates (286 of 9,210 is 3.1%). It's also the basis for the uncertainty fix.
- The hierarchy is clean: one large title, then small bold headings, then body text, then small grey captions. Bold purple marks the current page in the nav. Nothing competes with the title.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover states and tooltips on the bars.
- Whether the nav targets are large enough once they have their real padding.
