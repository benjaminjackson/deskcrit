**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title "Trial to paid conversion by plan", then down to the tallest purple bar, Growth at 4.0%, then across the other three labels. It reads as one clean, calm tab of an internal analytics product, made for a product or growth team. It is tidy and believable, but it is also flat: it presents four numbers and leaves you to decide what they mean.

**What it's for.** My assumption is that this is an internal dashboard view that tells a growth or product team how well each plan's trials convert, so they can decide where to act.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid and the numbers add up (286/9,210 = 3.1%, 118/2,950 = 4.0%, and so on). But the chart presents gaps of a few tenths of a point as a clear ranking without saying whether they are real, compares them to nothing, and leaves the conclusion to the reader. The biggest problem is that a team could make a decision on noise.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bars rank differences the data can't support.** Enterprise's 3.4% comes from only 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. At that size, the rough 95% range is about ±1.1 points, or about 2.3% to 4.5%. That range covers Starter, Team and Growth. Team (3.6%) and Enterprise (3.4%) can't be told apart, and only Growth vs. Starter is likely to be a real gap. The chart draws all four as equally solid bars with bold value labels, so the viewer reads a ranking. The counts that would make them doubt it are in a separate table 100 px away. This is a defect: it can mislead a pricing or onboarding decision. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) asks for samples to be shown with ranges or sample-size notes. To fix it, add interval whiskers to the bars, or at least put "n = 1,120 trials" under each plan label. If a gap isn't significant, say so in the caption.
2. **There is no point of reference.** Is 3.1% good? Up or down from Q2? Above or below target? The page doesn't say, so the reader can't tell a problem from normal. This is a convention break that costs real meaning here (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). To fix it, put a comparison next to each rate: the Q2 value as a faint marker or a ghost bar, or a target line across the chart. The table could also get a "vs Q2" column.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" plus a subtitle that defines the metric means every viewer has to work out the conclusion alone, and different viewers will reach different ones. This is common sense, not an approved rule. Once finding 1 is resolved, the title should say what is true, for example "Growth trials convert best; other plans are within noise of each other." Keep the metric definition as the subtitle.

**Smaller things.**
- **Lopsided weight:** the right column ends around y = 415, so the lower right third is empty while the left carries the dense purple bars. The page feels heavy on the left. Moving the comparison data or a short note on what changed into that space would balance it.
- **Definition is unclear:** "within 30 days" in the subtitle and "still open on 30 October" in the Definition box describe two different cutoffs. A reader can't tell whether a July trial that pays on day 45 counts as converted. Use one cutoff, stated once.
- **"Paid" column header:** it doesn't say paid what. Use "Paid accounts", or add a "Rate" column so the table matches the chart.

**What's working.**
- **Honest chart:** the axis starts at zero, values are labelled directly on the bars, gridlines are faint, and there is one colour with no legend. Nearly all the ink is data. Keep this restraint when you add intervals and comparisons.
- **Clear type scale:** there are about four sizes (title, section head, body, axis and caption), each with an obvious job. Section heads are set apart by weight, not just size.
- **Number table:** figures are tabular and right-aligned under right-aligned headers, so they compare cleanly down each column.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the bars or table rows show exact values or intervals on hover.
- How the two columns stack on a narrow screen.
