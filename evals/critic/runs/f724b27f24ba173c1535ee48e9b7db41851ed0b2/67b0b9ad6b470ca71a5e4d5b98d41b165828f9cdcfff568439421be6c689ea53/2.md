**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline at top left, drops to the tallest bar (Growth, 4.0%), then runs across the other three. Next it jumps to the table on the right. It reads as an internal analytics report: clean, calm, and easy to trust. The page shows four numbers clearly but doesn't say whether any of them is good news, bad news, or a real difference at all.

**What it's for.** No brief came with the image, so I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard page that tells a growth or product team how trial-to-paid conversion differed by plan in Q3 2025, so they can decide where to act.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, honest chart, but as a decision tool it's thin. Four bars between 3.1% and 4.0% are presented as if the gaps were real and meaningful, with no uncertainty, no comparison and no stated finding. The reader can see the numbers but has no basis for acting on them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The differences may be noise, and the chart doesn't say so (defect).** The table shows Enterprise at only 38 conversions out of 1,120 trials. A rough 95% range on 3.4% is about 2.3% to 4.4%, which overlaps every other bar. Even Growth against Starter (118 of 2,950 against 286 of 9,210) is only a borderline difference. Yet four solid bars with bold value labels look like a firm ranking, so a reader will conclude "Growth converts best, Starter worst" and may move budget on that basis. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says estimates from samples should carry ranges or a note about sample size. Add error bars or a light interval band to each bar, or at least put n next to each label. If you keep the plain bars, say in words which differences hold up.
2. **No comparison, so nobody can tell good from bad (convention).** 4.0% alone means nothing without Q2, the same quarter last year, or a target. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2) says each headline number should sit next to a comparison. The usual fix is a faint marker or a paired bar per plan for the previous quarter, or a target line. Then the page answers "did conversion get better or worse, and where?", which is almost certainly the question the reader came with.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding (taste, common sense).** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells you what the chart is, which the axis labels already do. The one sentence a team member would repeat in a meeting is missing. Once items 1 and 2 are in place, write that sentence as the headline, for example "Growth converts best; Enterprise is too small to call". Keep the current wording as the subtitle. That way the first thing the eye lands on is the conclusion, not a label.

**Smaller things.**
- **Source line and definition disagree about dates.** The source line under the chart says the data covers trial events from 1 July to 30 September. The Definition says trials still open on 30 October count as not converted, so payments made in October must be in the data too. A careful reader will ask whether October payments were counted. State the payment window in the source line as well.
- **The 30-day rule is stated twice, in two ways.** The subtitle says paid "within 30 days". The Definition says a trial counts when "the first invoice is paid", with a 30 October cut-off. These are two different rules. Is a trial that started on 1 July and paid on 20 August counted or not? Make the Definition repeat the 30-day window so the two match.
- **The table's "Paid" column is vague.** It could mean paid accounts or paid invoices. "Converted" or "Became paid" matches the chart's subtitle.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are pale, each bar is labelled with its value, there's one purple and no legend, and the bars follow the plan tiers in order. Keep that restraint when you add intervals and comparisons, and make the new marks lighter than the bars.
- The table beside the chart shows the counts behind each rate, with right-aligned figures. That's exactly the right companion to a rate chart, and the arithmetic checks out (286 / 9,210 = 3.1%, and so on for each plan). It's also what makes finding 1 visible, so give it a column of rates or a link to the chart rather than leaving the reader to connect the two.
- The page has a clear order: navigation shows where you are, then the title, subtitle, chart and source line. The right column (table, then Definition) is easy to scan, with each heading sitting close to its content.
