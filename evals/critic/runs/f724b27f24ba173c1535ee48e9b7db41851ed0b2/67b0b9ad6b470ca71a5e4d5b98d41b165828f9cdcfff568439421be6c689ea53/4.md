**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan", drops to the tallest bar (Growth, 4.0%), then moves across the other three bar labels. After that it drifts right to the table, which reads as backup. It looks like an internal analytics page for a product or growth team: calm, tidy and easy to trust. It tells you what the numbers are but not what they mean.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is an internal dashboard page that lets product and growth people compare trial-to-paid conversion across plans for Q3 2025 and decide whether any plan needs attention.

**Verdict.** The page is well made, and the chart is honest and clean. But it presents four rates as a ranking when the data can't support most of that ranking, and it gives no reference point for whether any of them is good. A reader will leave with a firm conclusion, "Growth converts best, Enterprise lags", that the data mostly doesn't back up.

**What would move it most**
1. **Differences shown with no sense of noise (defect).** The four bars and the bold 3.1 / 3.6 / 4.0 / 3.4% labels sit at the same weight, so they invite you to rank them. Use the table's own numbers to work out rough 95% ranges by eye:
   - Enterprise: 38 of 1,120, about ±1.1 points (roughly 2.3–4.5%).
   - Growth: about ±0.7 points.
   - Team: about ±0.5 points.

   Team, Growth and Enterprise overlap heavily. Only Starter against Growth is likely a real gap. A reader who acts on "Enterprise underperforms Team" is acting on 38 conversions. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says to show uncertainty when you show estimates. Add error bars or a shaded range to each bar, or at least a note on sample size beside Enterprise. Then the chart shows which gaps are real.
2. **No comparison, and no takeaway (defect, with taste on the title).** Each rate stands alone: no previous quarter, no target, no change. A viewer can't tell whether 3.1% for Starter is a problem or a record. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") flags metrics shown without context. Add the Q2 value as a tick or ghost bar on each bar, or a change figure next to each label. The title also names a topic instead of a finding. That's common sense rather than a rule from an approved source. Once you have the comparison, let the headline or subtitle state what it shows ("Growth leads; Starter fell 0.4 pts since Q2", or whatever is true). The page should say its point, not leave each reader to find their own.
3. **The definition contradicts the subtitle and the source line (defect).** These are the words people will hold the number to:
   - The subtitle says trials that became paid "within 30 days".
   - The Definition says a trial counts when its first invoice is paid, and trials "still open on 30 October" count as not converted. That is a fixed cutoff, not a 30-day window.
   - A trial started on 1 July and paid on day 60 counts under the Definition but not under the subtitle.
   - The source line says the events run "1 July to 30 September 2025". Conversions up to 30 October can't come from that range.

   Two readers can rebuild two different metrics from this, and the person who wrote it will be asked which one is right. Settle on one rule and state it the same way in all three places. If it really is a 30-day window, say so in the Definition and extend the source dates to 30 October.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: the subtitle (about 15 px) and the table and body text (about 14 px) are almost the same size, and so are the axis labels (about 13 px) and the source line (about 12 px). Merge each pair into one size, and use colour or weight for the difference.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. The axis starts at zero, the gridlines are faint, there's one hue, values sit right on the bars and there's no legend. Keep that restraint when you add error bars or comparisons.
- The table next to the chart gives the denominators (trials and paid counts) that the percentages hide. The figures line up on the right in clean tabular numerals. That pairing is exactly what makes the uncertainty in point 1 checkable, so keep it.
- The bars follow the plan order from Starter to Enterprise instead of being sorted by value. That fits a reader who thinks in tiers.
