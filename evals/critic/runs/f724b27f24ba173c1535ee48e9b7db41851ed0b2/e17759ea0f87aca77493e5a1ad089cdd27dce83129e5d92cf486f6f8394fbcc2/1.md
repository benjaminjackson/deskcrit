**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, then drops to the tallest bar, Q3 2026 at 705, and then runs left to right along eight bars of the same blue. It reads as a calm, well-made board slide about seasonal shipments. It feels trustworthy, maybe more than it should, because the last bar is a guess dressed as a fact.

**What it's for.** My assumption: page 3 of a board deck, telling directors how shipments are going so they can judge the business at the October meeting.

**Verdict.** This is a clean slide that mostly works. The one real problem is that the Q4 2026 forecast is drawn exactly like the seven measured quarters. The second problem is that the headline points at the seasonal pattern, which the board already knows, rather than the year-on-year growth, which is the news.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast bar looks like an actual (defect).** Q4 2026 (560) is the same solid blue, with the same bold label, as Q1 2025 to Q3 2026. The only signs that it is a forecast are a bracket at the end of the subtitle and a line in the source note. A director looking at the chart sees eight results. If they remember "560 in Q4" as something already done, then the September forecast gets held to the standard of a result, or a miss looks like a fall. Projections should look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Draw that bar differently: an outline or a lighter tint, its label as "560 (forecast)", and ideally a range if the forecast has one. Then the subtitle bracket can go.
2. **The headline states the pattern, not the news (taste, on the copy).** "Shipments peak each third quarter" is true, but an outdoor brand peaking in summer is no surprise to its board. The chart's real story is that every quarter of 2026 beat the same quarter of 2025: Q1 +11%, Q2 +13%, Q3 +16% (610 → 705). The forecast Q4 is also up about 17%. Right now the reader has to compare bars four apart to find that out, and nothing on the slide says it to them. A good comparison number is one shown next to the previous period (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). The fix is to put the growth in the headline in the board's terms, for example "Every 2026 quarter is up on 2025; the Q3 peak grew 16%". You could also make the year-on-year pairing visible, by grouping each quarter's two years side by side or by marking the change on the 2026 bars.

**Smaller things.**
- Chart title and unit line ("Units shipped by quarter / Thousands of units"): they repeat what the subtitle already says. Fold them into one line, "Thousands of units".
- Right edge: the plot area stops around x=1140, but the header text, footer text, and footer rule all run to 1200. Run the chart out to the same right margin so it sits on the page's grid (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- Bottom band: everything ends at the footer around y=680, which leaves about 120 px of empty space under it. The slide looks top-heavy, as if content is missing. Either give the chart that height or move the footer to the bottom of the slide.

**What's working.**
- **Direct value labels on every bar.** Directors can quote exact numbers without reading off the axis, and the faint gridlines keep the ink on the data. Keep both.
- **A bold serif headline over quiet sans-serif text.** This gives a clear way into the slide. With one blue and grey text everywhere else, nothing competes with it.
- **Honest scale.** The axis starts at zero, there's one unit, and a source line is included. Keep it that way.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the forecast be the payoff (taste).** This slide goes to a board in October, so the decision in front of them is Q4. A stronger version would draw the eye from the Q3 peak to the forecast bar and say what has to happen to hit 560. That way the slide frames the decision instead of just reporting on the past.
