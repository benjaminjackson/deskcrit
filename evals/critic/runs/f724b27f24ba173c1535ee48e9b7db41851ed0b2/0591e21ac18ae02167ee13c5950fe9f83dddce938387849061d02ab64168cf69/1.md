I've finished the critique. The page fails mainly because the lines of the headline crash into each other.

**First read.** The eye lands on the huge black headline and stalls there. Its four lines are set so tight that they run into each other: the "g" and "y" of "daylight" cut into "through", and the "y" of "early" drops into "summer". It looks like a broken page before it looks like a ferry timetable. Next comes the short paragraph, and after that there's nothing. The right half and the bottom third are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a web page for the spring ferry timetable, for people who want to know when they can cross.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The headline's lines overlap, so the biggest thing on the page reads as a rendering bug. Under that, the copy says "timetable" but never gives a time.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline lines collide** (defect). The four lines of "Quayside ferry crossings by daylight, spring through early summer" sit closer together than the letters are tall. Descenders run into the line below in at least three places: "crossings", "daylight" and "early". The reader has to pull the words apart, and the page looks broken, which is a real trust problem for something people use to catch a boat. Bringhurst allows display lines tighter than their type size "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide" (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, p. 37). Hochuli makes the same complaint about headings set too tight (*Detail in Typography*, p. 28). Fix: open up the headline's line spacing until no letter touches the line below. It can stay tighter than the body text. A shorter headline would also help: it's four lines long because it holds a whole sentence (see 2).
2. **It promises a timetable and gives no times** (defect, from reading the copy). The label says "Spring timetable" and the headline says "by daylight", but the only facts are "every forty minutes" and "about twelve minutes". The reader still can't answer the one question they came with: when is the first and last crossing, and from which date? "By daylight" moves with the season, so it can be read two ways: the reader may guess the last boat is later than it really is. For a schedule that's the costly mistake. Fix: say the start and end dates and the first and last sailing in words near the top, for example "first boat 7:00 from the north landing, last 19:40". Put the details in the table below. With the season and the "daylight" idea moved into the text, the headline can be a short name the eye reads in one go.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks "early / summer" across lines and leaves "summer" alone on the last line. Once the line spacing is fixed, break it at a phrase or shorten it.
- The paragraph ends with "weather." alone on its third line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Change the measure slightly or edit the copy so the last line carries more than one word.

**What's working.**
- Everything hangs off one strong left edge at about x=120: the label, the headline and the paragraph. That gives the page a calm, clear structure. Keep it.
- There are three type sizes with big jumps between them: the small grey bold label, a body size around 19 px, and the large heavy headline. Each size has an obvious job, so the scale is right and only the line spacing is broken.
- The paragraph runs about 70 characters a line in a plain sans with comfortable spacing, so it's easy to read.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Make a time the hero (taste). This page is for someone deciding whether they can still make a crossing. A large "Every 40 min · 07:00–19:40" in the headline position, with the route name as the smaller line, would answer that before they read a word of prose. It also fills the empty right half with something useful.
2. Let the page own one colour (taste). It's all grey on white, so it could be any page. One river or harbour colour used only on times and the landing names would show what to look at and make the schedule recognisable from one glance to the next.
