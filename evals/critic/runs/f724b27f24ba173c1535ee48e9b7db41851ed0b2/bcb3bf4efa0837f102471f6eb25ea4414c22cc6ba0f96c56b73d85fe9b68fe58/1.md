**First read.** The eye goes straight to the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then down the centre through the paragraph, the three short lines and the blue button. It reads as the landing page of a small local ferry. It feels calm, clean and well spaced, but also generic: swap the words and it could be any template hero. The last line, "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge", stops the eye because it doesn't sound like the ferry talking.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a landing page that tells locals and visitors there is a quick, pleasant ferry across the bay and sends them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The basic structure works: there is one clear headline, one clear action, and plenty of space. Two things in the copy hold it back. A stray line at the bottom is about the design, not the ferry, and it is wrong about the layout. And the copy dodges the facts a rider actually came for.

**What would move it most**
1. **Remove the line under the button.** "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge" (lower third, centred) is a comment about the design, not copy for a rider. It is also false: every block on the page is centred and nothing is left-aligned. A rider reads it as a leftover note or a mistake, and after reading it they trust the rest of the page a little less. It is the last thing on the page, so it is also the last thing people read before they leave. Delete it. If the page needs something below the button, use a fact the rider needs, such as the fare or where the boats land. *Defect: unfinished copy (common sense).*
2. **The copy avoids the facts a rider decides on.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" doesn't give a first or last sailing. "The north landing" doesn't say which town or street, and the page never names where the boat goes. The page gives no fare at all. So anyone asking "can I catch one at 7 tonight, from where, and for how much?" has to click through. The paragraph and the three short lines spend their space on mood (the harbour sliding past, hot drinks) instead. Put the departure point, the destination, the first and last sailings and the fare on this page, for example "From North Landing, Harbourtown, to Eastport · 6:20 am to 10:40 pm · £3". Then the button can be more exact too: "See today's departures" names what you will get, where "See the timetable" only names a document. *Defect: the viewer has to go elsewhere to learn what they came for (common sense; it is close to Nielsen's "recognition rather than recall").*

**Smaller things.**
- Everything sits on one centred axis, including the three-line paragraph, so that paragraph has no steady left edge to return to. Set the paragraph flush left, or cut it to one or two short lines that can stay centred (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst 2.1.3).
- The three short lines repeat the paragraph: bicycles and the open deck appear in both. Keep each fact once, and use the space saved for the timetable and fare facts above.
- The three short lines look only slightly smaller than the paragraph (about 18 px against 20 px by eye). That reads as a slip rather than a separate level. Either set them the same size as the paragraph, or make them clearly different by weight or colour (Bringhurst 3.1.1).

**What's working.**
- The headline is specific and promises a benefit: "twelve calm minutes" gives a number and a feeling in four words. It is set large, bold and with tight leading, so it clearly dominates. Keep that.
- There is one primary action in one saturated blue, used again only on the small brand name above the headline. The colour job is clear and the button can't be missed.
- The generous empty space above and below the content suits a "calm" crossing.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste.* Let the page show the crossing, not just describe it: a photo of the open deck, or a simple map line from one landing to the other. A ferry is a place and a view, and an all-text page gives up the strongest reason to take the boat. The picture should go with the headline rather than repeat it, for example the twelve minutes drawn as a short line across the bay.
2. *Taste.* Turn the button into a comparison the rider already understands, such as "12 minutes by boat vs 40 round the bay by car". People already know how long the drive takes, so the value shows on their own terms instead of being taken on trust.
3. *Taste.* Give the brand one thing of its own, a colour or a typeface with some sea in it. Right now the system font and stock blue make Coastal Ferry Co. look like any company.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's focus and hover states, and how the centred blocks reflow on a phone.
