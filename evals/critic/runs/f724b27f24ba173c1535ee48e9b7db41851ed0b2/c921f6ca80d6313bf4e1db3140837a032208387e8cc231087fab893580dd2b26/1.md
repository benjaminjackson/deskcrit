**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the big black serif headline, drops to the dek, then the byline. It reads as the opening of a long-form feature in a quiet, literary magazine called *Northing*. It feels calm and confident. In version 2, though, the headline looks clotted on first sight: its three lines have run into each other, and that is where the eye stalls.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a long-read article page. Its job is to draw a reader into the story and get them to scroll and start reading.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The two versions are the same except for the headline's line spacing. Version 2 tightens it so far that the descenders of each line hit the letters below, and that damages the single most important element on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline's lines collide in version 2 (defect).** In "The quiet people keeping a / forgotten lighthouse lit on / Fair Skerry", the p's and g of "people keeping" run into the "l" and "h" of "lighthouse". The g's of "forgotten" and "lighthouse" hit "Fair Skerry". At full resolution the shapes merge into one black mass. The reader has to pick the letters apart before the words come through, and the opening line of a story about quiet care looks careless. Tight leading on a display heading is right, but only "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide" (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, p. 37). **Fix:** open the line spacing until no letter touches the line below. Version 1's setting already does this: it is tighter than the body text, the three lines still read as one block, and every letter stands clear. Keep that.

Nothing else changed between the versions. Version 2's only gain is that the byline sits about 40 px higher, and that is not worth damaging the headline.

**What's working** (both versions)
- **The type contrast.** A heavy serif headline sits over a lighter serif dek, with a small sans for the label, byline and navigation. That is three plainly different jobs, so the eye never wonders where to start.
- **The dek.** "For forty years a rota of volunteers has rowed out each week to trim the lamp. Now the last boat is for sale." It gives the story's history and its turning point in two sentences. "Now the last boat is for sale" is the hook that makes someone read on.
- **The red "Long read" label.** It is the only colour on the page. It sets expectations without competing with the headline.

**Where it could go further**
1. **Let the opening carry the place (taste).** This is a story about one remote rock and a lamp. The page credits a photographer, yet the first screen is all type and white space. A full-bleed photo of Fair Skerry, or the headline set over the sea, would give the piece the sense of place the words promise. As it stands, nothing in the look sets it apart from any other well-set magazine page.
2. **Make the headline bolder in scale, not just in weight (taste).** The headline fills about three-quarters of the measure and stops well short of the right margin. For a feature opener, setting it larger, or across the full width, would make the first screen feel like an event rather than a standard article template.
