**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", drops to the bold header row, and runs down the departure times. In two seconds it reads as a calm, clean, web-style schedule for a short river crossing. Then the sentence under the table pulls you up: it says "heavy black lines around every cell" make this a mess, but nothing like that is on the page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public timetable that lets a rider find the next ferry from North landing to the market quay on the day they're travelling.

**Verdict.** The table itself is well made: it's quiet, light on lines, and easy to scan. Two things get in the way. The note at the bottom describes something that isn't there. And the one thing a rider has to filter by, which days a boat runs, is pushed into the last column at body weight.

**What would move it most**
1. **The closing sentence contradicts the page.** This is the line under the table: "The heavy black lines around every cell make this timetable a mess." The rules here are pale grey hairlines, horizontal only, with no cell borders. So a reader either distrusts what they see or wonders whether a different version of the table exists. On a public timetable, text that disagrees with the table makes people doubt the table, and that is what they act on. If it's a leftover review note, delete it. If something should sit there, use a line riders need, such as the season dates or what happens in bad weather. *Defect (common sense: the copy isn't finished).*
2. **The day pattern is hidden in the last column.** "Weekdays" and "Weekends" sit far right, in the same regular weight as "Daily". A Saturday rider has to read all seven rows to the end to learn that the 12:40 and 17:10 don't run, which leaves a four-hour gap from 15:20 to 19:30. A weekday rider can easily miss that the 19:30 is weekends only and plan an evening crossing that doesn't exist. These are departure times people act on, so a row that is easy to misread is a defect. The Period column, meanwhile, takes the prime first column to repeat what the clock already says ("Morning" twice, "Midday" twice). Give the leading position to the day information: either split the table into Weekdays and Weekends, or mark the exceptions so they stand out from "Daily", not with colour alone. Drop Period or turn it into group labels. *Defect (the exceptions are easy to miss); moving Period is taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- Time columns: the digits look proportional. "11:00" ends about 10 px left of "06:40", and "11:12" ends short of "06:52". The colons wobble down the column. Use tabular figures so the times line up (Bringhurst, 4.4.3).
- Column spacing: the gap after Period looks clearly narrower than the gaps between the other three columns, which spread across about 1,080 px for very short entries. Set the columns on one interval (Müller-Brockmann), or tighten the table so the eye doesn't travel so far between a time and its "Runs" value.

**What's working.**
- The rules are pale grey, horizontal and thin. The rows hold together without boxing each cell, which is how Lupton says a data table should be ruled. Keep it that way whatever the caption says.
- The hierarchy is clear: one bold title, a quiet grey subtitle that says where the route runs, and a bold header row. You know where to start, and nothing competes.
- A table is the right form for exact departure times, and the rows run in time order.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste.* Make "next boat" the organising idea. Riders look up the nearest departure, so a stronger version would make the times the most prominent thing in each row, larger or heavier than the labels, so the eye finds the time first and the conditions second.
2. *Taste.* Show the crossing time once ("12 minutes") instead of restating it in every Arrives cell. That frees a column for what actually varies, and it tells a first-time rider something useful in plain words.
