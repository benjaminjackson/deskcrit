**First read.** The eye lands straight on the big black headline, which runs to four lines. Then it drops to the paragraph, then to a single loose sentence at the bottom. It reads as a clean, calm landing page for a local ferry service. It feels quiet and competent, but it doesn't give you much. Once you've read it, you still don't know when a boat leaves.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a public web page that tells riders when the spring river ferry runs, so they can turn up for a crossing.

**Verdict.** The page looks good and does almost none of its job. It is labelled "Spring timetable" but shows no times. On top of that, the most prominent text after the intro is a stray sentence about the design itself. The typography is sound. The problem is the content.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable is missing.** The kicker reads "Spring timetable" and the headline promises "crossings by daylight, spring through early summer". The only schedule detail is "every forty minutes". Riders can't plan a trip from that: there's no first or last sailing, no departure times from either landing, and no dates for "spring" or "early summer". "By daylight" changes over the season, so a rider can't work out the last boat either. This is a **defect**: the page's main job fails, and riders either can't plan or have to guess. The label "Spring timetable" also doesn't describe what's on the page (WCAG 2.4.6, Headings and labels). Fix: put the actual departures under the intro, from the north landing and from the market quay. Add first and last sailings and the season's start and end dates. Set the times in tabular figures so the columns line up.
2. **A stray sentence sits where the next content should be.** At the bottom (y≈665) is "The cramped, overlapping heading makes this page hard to read." It is set at body size and weight, so it looks like part of the page. It reads as a leftover note or a test string. A rider will be confused by it, and it makes the page look unfinished. It's also not true: in a full-resolution crop the headline lines are tight, but no descender touches the line below. This is a **defect** (unfinished copy, judged as common sense). Fix: delete it, and use the space for the timetable.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line breaks: "spring through early / summer" splits "early summer" and leaves "summer" alone on the last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2; the heading-break rule is my judgment). Shorten the headline or reset the line breaks so it breaks at "…by daylight, / spring through early summer". With the time span already in the kicker, three lines would probably do.
- The intro paragraph leaves "weather." alone on its last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Adjust the measure slightly or tie the last two words together.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clear: about 70 px headline, 19 px body and 15 px label. Each is a distinct level with a distinct job, and the heavy weight separates the headline from the text by more than size. Keep this when you add the timetable, and don't bring in a fourth size that sits close to one of these.
- One strong left edge at x≈120 that the label, headline and body all share, with the white space gathered on the right instead of scattered around. The page looks calm and deliberate.
- The intro line length (about 68 characters) and line spacing are comfortable for reading.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Make the times the hero (taste). Riders come for the next departure, so a large, glanceable block of times could sit next to or right under the headline, and the headline could shrink to a line or two. Tufte's point holds here: the data is the content, so give it the space. At the moment the largest type on the page is spent on a sentence that doesn't help anyone catch a boat.
2. Have the page say who is speaking and where (taste). The copy is plain enough to belong to any ferry anywhere. Naming the river, the town or the quay, or adding a small map of the two landings, would make it this ferry's page and help first-time riders find the landing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the headline reflows on a phone: four lines at this size could become seven or eight and push the content down the screen. Check this in a live browser.
