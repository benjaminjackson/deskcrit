**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, then drops onto the big navy 38% wedge, then the slivers fanned out along the top-left edge of the pie, then across to the legend to work out what they are. It reads as a page from an annual report: calm, competent, well set. That impression lasts until you try to name a small slice. Then you are flicking between a 2% wedge and four similar colour chips in the legend.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a page in a company annual report that shows shareholders and staff where operating costs went in FY2026, and that they should be able to read at a glance.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and the typography is good, but the chart doesn't fit the data. A 14-slice pie decoded through a 14-colour legend makes the reader do by colour matching what a sorted bar chart would do by position. It also never says what the reader should take away, or how this year compares with last.

**What would move it most**
1. **Wrong chart for 14 categories** (defect). Ten of the fourteen wedges are 4% or smaller. They become slivers along the top-left edge, and their own small labels are the only way to tell them apart. Nobody can compare a 3% slice with a 2% slice by angle. The pie only carries "salaries are big" and "three things are next", and the rest is noise. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1) both keep pies to about five slices. Fix: show the shares as one sorted horizontal bar chart with the category name and the percentage on each bar. This page already has the sorted legend, so it is most of the way there. If the brand needs a pie, keep the four largest slices plus Travel and fold the nine slices of 3% and under into one "Other (17%)" slice, itemised in a small table.
2. **The legend makes the reader decode colour, and the colours don't hold up** (defect). The legend is the only place the names appear. To match a wedge to its name, the reader has to tell apart Rent teal from Equipment-repair pale teal, Software blue from Utilities indigo, and Travel red, Training brown, Office pink and Postage salmon from each other. That is hard with normal colour vision at this wedge size and fails for many colour-blind readers, because colour is the only link (WCAG 1.4.1). Fourteen hues is also more than anyone can keep apart. That point is common sense, not a cited rule: past about eight, categorical colours stop working. Tufte and Wilke both say to label the data directly. The bar chart in point 1 fixes this too, because each name sits on its bar and the whole chart can be one colour, with salaries or one highlighted group as the accent.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding** (taste, common sense). "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" tells readers what the chart is, then leaves them to find the story. In an annual report the story is the point: something like "People and premises took more than half of what we spent". Without a stated takeaway, readers each draw their own conclusion, or none. Fix: make the headline the finding and keep the current line as the subtitle.
4. **No comparison** (convention). Every share is shown alone. A shareholder's first question is "is that up or down?", and the page can't answer it. Few's dashboard pitfall 2 and Tufte (ch. 2) apply here. Fix: put last year's share or the change in points next to each value. That is easy in a bar chart or table column, and impossible to read in a pie.

**Smaller things.**
- White bold labels "12%" on the orange wedge and "16%" on the teal wedge look below 4.5:1. They are about 16 px bold, which is not large text under WCAG 1.4.3. Measure them, and use dark text on light fills if they fail.
- The legend and its rules stop at about x = 1060, but the header runs to 1216. The right-hand block doesn't sit on the page's right margin, so it looks placed by eye. Extend the rows to the margin or set the column on a grid line.

**What's working.**
- The type system: a bold serif headline over a lighter serif subtitle, sans for the data, and a small tracked "4.2 COSTS" eyebrow. That gives four clear levels with no near-duplicate sizes. Keep it.
- The data is sorted descending in both the pie and the legend, and the legend has right-aligned tabular figures and hairline row rules. Carry that straight into the bar chart.
- The subtitle and source line do their jobs: they give the period, the basis, and a note that shares are rounded (they add up to 100). Honest framing like this builds trust in an annual report.
