**First read.** My eye goes straight to the two red cells in the middle of the grid (Monday and Friday, late morning), then to the orange band around them, then the title. Before I read anything, the page has the feel of an alarm: a bright red-orange-yellow hot spot sitting in a field of loud blue. It reads as a support team's report on when tickets come in. The type and layout are calm and tidy. The chart colours are not.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a Reports page in a help-desk tool. A support lead uses it to see when ticket volume peaks so they can plan staffing.

**Verdict.** The layout, labelling and stat column are well made, and the day-by-hour grid is the right chart for the question. But the rainbow colour scale makes the pattern harder to read than it should be, and it makes busy hours look like errors. The "busiest hour" figure also seems to contradict the chart next to it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The colour scale has no order.** *(Defect. Convention: Wilke, Fundamentals of Data Visualization.)*
   - **What's wrong:** The legend under the grid runs blue-violet → cyan → teal → green → yellow → orange → red for 0–4 up to 30+. Those hues don't get steadily lighter or darker. Yellow (20–24) is the lightest swatch, and the 0–4 blue is about as dark as the 30+ red. In grayscale, the quietest overnight hours and the peak would look alike, and the yellow band would look like a gap in the middle of the peak (Albers; Refactoring UI, "don't rely on colour alone").
   - **What the viewer meets:** To find out whether green beats cyan, they have to check the legend, when they should be able to see "darker means busier" at a glance.
   - **Colour blindness:** The middle steps are green, yellow-green, yellow and orange side by side. With red-green colour blindness, the 10–14 to 25–29 range collapses (WCAG 1.4.1).
   - **Wrong signals:** Red and green carry status meanings in an interface, which suggests 11:00 on Monday is a fault and 15–19 is "good" (Apple HIG, Material). That's why the page reads as an alarm.
   - **Wrong dominance:** About two-thirds of the chart is the 0–4 bin, filled with a saturated blue-violet. So the emptiest hours take up the most strong colour on the page.
   - **Fix:** Use one hue that runs from very light (0–4, nearly the page colour) to dark (30+). The order of the colours then matches the order of the values. The quiet hours drop back, the weekday 09:00–17:00 block stands out on its own, and the legend becomes a check rather than a decoder.
2. **"Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" disagrees with the grid.** *(Defect.)*
   - **What the chart shows:** In the grid, the hour labels sit centred over their cells. That puts 09:00 on the yellow column, 10:00 on the orange one and 11:00 on the next. The 11:00 column is red (30+) on Monday and Friday and orange on Tuesday to Thursday. The 10:00 column is orange every weekday. On the chart's own evidence, 11:00 is at least as busy as 10:00 every weekday.
   - **What it costs:** This is the number a lead will plan the rota from. When the headline figure and the picture disagree, the viewer stops trusting both.
   - **Fix:** Either the stat is wrong or the labels are offset by one column. Check which. If the stat is computed differently (for example, as a total across the week), say so in its sub-line.

**Smaller things.**
- **Title:** "When new tickets arrive" names the topic but not the finding. The subtitle or a one-line caption could state it, for example "Weekdays 09:00–17:00 carry almost all tickets; late morning peaks." That's taste, but it's what the reader came for.
- **"Up 4% on August":** September's figure covers 1–28 September (28 days), and August has 31 days. Say whether the comparison is like for like (same 28 days, or per day). Otherwise a reader can't tell whether +4% is real growth or a different window.

**What's working.**
- **The right chart:** A 7 × 24 grid for "when do tickets come in" shows both the weekday/weekend split and the daily rise and fall in one view. Keep this form.
- **The labelling:**
  - The subtitle gives what is measured, its scope and the date range.
  - The legend has a title and bins with true en-dash ranges.
  - The source line spells out that the hours are local time and mean "when a ticket was opened, not when it was answered", which heads off the obvious misreading.
- **The stat column:** Each figure has a plain label, a large number and a comparison with last month. Three levels, clearly separated by size and weight.

**Where it could go further**
1. *(Taste.)* Mark the peak right on the grid, with a thin outline or a small label on the busiest cells. A lead planning cover should be able to read the answer without matching cells to the legend. This would also tie the "busiest hour" stat visually to the cells it describes.
2. *(Taste.)* Put a total for each day at the end of each row and a total for each hour under each column. The grid would then answer "which day?" and "which hour?" with exact numbers, while the colours keep showing the overall pattern (Tufte's small, dense, labelled displays).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cells show exact counts on hover, which a staffing planner would want.
