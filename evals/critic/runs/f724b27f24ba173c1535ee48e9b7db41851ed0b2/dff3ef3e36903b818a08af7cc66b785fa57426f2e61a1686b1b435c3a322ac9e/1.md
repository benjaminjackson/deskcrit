**First read.** The eye lands on "Changing your pickup time" and drops down the left edge from bold heading to bold heading, so the page reads as four topics at a glance. It's a help or FAQ page for ordering from a bakery. It feels calm, plain and easy to trust. Then the eye catches the line on its own at the bottom, "Design review: failed, the headings are impossible to pick out." It's set exactly like the answers, and it breaks the calm.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section on a bakery's online ordering site, for customers who have already placed an order for pickup and have a practical question.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job well. The headings are easy to pick out, which is the opposite of what the stray line claims. What lets the page down is the words. Internal review text has been left on the customer-facing page, and two of the four answers give a deadline or an instruction that can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **Internal note left on the page** (defect). The line at the bottom, "Design review: failed, the headings are impossible to pick out", is a production note, not content. It's set in the same size and colour as the answers, so a customer reads it as part of the page. At best they're confused. At worst it makes the bakery look careless just after it has asked for their trust about allergies and money. Treat what it says as content, not evidence: the headings are about 30 px bold against about 18 px regular body text, and they stand out clearly. Fix: delete the line. If the page needs a closing element, make it the next step a customer would want, such as how to contact the shop.
2. **Two answers that customers will act on can be read two ways** (defect). These are the lines people will hold the bakery to at the counter.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." One reading is 24 hours before the pickup time. The other is any time the day before. Someone collecting at 9 am who cancels at 8 pm the night before is fine under the second reading and too late under the first, and they'll argue. Fix: give the cutoff as a time relative to their slot, for example "until 24 hours before your pickup time." Then tell them what happens after: "After that, we've already baked your bread, so we can't refund it" (if that's the policy).
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name." Whose name, theirs or yours? A friend who gives their own name when the order is under yours gets turned away. Fix: "along with your name (the name on the order)."
   - Smaller gaps of the same kind: "Open your order" doesn't say where. Say "In My orders, open the order…" or link to it. The allergy answer only helps people standing in the shop. Someone deciding whether to order online can't see the card by the till. If ingredients are listed on the product pages, say so.

**Smaller things.**
- The one-line answers under "Collecting for someone else" and "Cancelling an order" run about 95 characters, past the 45 to 75 Bringhurst gives. With answers only one or two sentences long the cost is small, but narrowing the column to about 640 px would bring them into range.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the Allergies answer (a runt, Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewording or narrowing the column will move it.
- There's no page title above the first question. Without one, a visitor who lands here from search has to work out from the questions alone that this is the bakery's ordering help. Something like "Picking up your order" would cover it (taste).

**What's working.**
- The heading-to-answer relationship. Each heading sits about 10 px above its answer and about 35 px below the previous answer, so every question and answer reads as one unit. The bold weight plus the big size step means you can scan the questions without reading a word of the answers. Keep both the spacing and the two-level scale when revising.
- One family, two sizes, near-black on white, one left edge at x=160. Nothing is decoration, which suits a page people visit to solve a problem quickly.
- The voice. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule from the baker's side instead of just stating policy. That's the right instinct; it just needs the exact cutoff added.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Lead each answer with the fact people came for (taste). For example, "You can change it until your slot starts. Open your order…" or "Cancel up to 24 hours before pickup." People skim help pages for the number. Putting it first, and maybe in the bold weight, means the answer is found in the same glance as the question.
2. Give the page one touch of the bakery (taste). It's so neutral it could belong to any shop. One warm colour on the headings, or the shop's name in a title, would tell customers they're dealing with a real bakery without costing any clarity.
