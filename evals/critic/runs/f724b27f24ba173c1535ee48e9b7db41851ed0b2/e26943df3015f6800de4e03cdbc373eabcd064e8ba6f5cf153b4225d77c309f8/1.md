**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Update the card that pays for your subscription", and then down into calm, well-set paragraphs. It reads as a help-centre article for a SaaS billing product. It feels quiet, trustworthy and competent, the way a good docs page should. Then you look for the steps and there aren't any.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a customer, often one whose card just expired or was declined, lands here to change the card on their account and wants to be done in under a minute.

**Verdict.** The visual design does its job: the hierarchy is clear, the type is readable and nothing gets in the way. The article doesn't do its job, though. A page titled "Update the card…" never says how to update the card, and the paragraph about what happens when a payment fails can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**

1. **The how-to is missing (defect).** The only instruction is "You can replace the card on file at any time from the billing page" in the first paragraph. "The billing page" isn't a link, and the page gives no path to it and no steps. The rest of the article covers when the change takes effect, who is allowed to make it and what happens on a decline. All of that is useful, but none of it is the task in the title. The reader has to leave, find the billing page and work out the steps themselves, which is exactly what they came here to avoid. Nielsen's sixth heuristic (recognition rather than recall) asks that what the user needs to act is on the screen, not somewhere they have to go and find. The fix: put a short numbered sequence straight under the headline (where to go, what to click, what to enter, how to confirm). Make "billing page" a direct link, and move the timing and permissions details below the steps.

2. **The decline paragraph is ambiguous at the point where the reader's money and access are at stake (defect, copy).** In the paragraph under "When the new card is declined", "We try a declined card again after three days and after seven days" doesn't say what the days count from: the first decline, or the invoice date? "If the card is still declined after the second attempt" has two readings:
   - The second charge overall, the day-3 retry. The account drops to the free plan about four days earlier than under the other reading.
   - The second retry, on day 7.

   A reader planning when to add a card can easily guess wrong and get downgraded before they expected. One gap also lands right on the most common reason people come here. The first paragraph says an open invoice "keeps the old card until it is paid", but if the old card has expired, that invoice can't be paid, and the page doesn't say what happens to it. The fix: state the timeline with an anchor, such as "We retry 3 days and 7 days after the first decline. If the day-7 retry fails, you move to the free plan." Then add one sentence on how an open invoice gets paid when the old card no longer works.

**Smaller things.**
- **Location isn't marked.** The current page isn't marked in the left "In this section" list: "Update a payment card" looks the same as its neighbours. The breadcrumb calls the page "Payment methods" and the headline names it a third way. Mark the current item with one cue, such as bold weight or a bar (Laubheimer, NN/g, "Menu-Design Checklist"), and use one name for the page in all three places.
- **Lines are too long.** Body lines in the main column look like they run about 80–85 characters, beyond the 45–75 range Bringhurst gives. Narrow the column by roughly 15%; there is plenty of empty space on the right to give.
- **There's no search.** The header holds four section links and no search field. On a help centre, search is the frequent-user shortcut (Nielsen 7), and people arriving from a payment-failure email will want to type "declined" rather than browse.

**What's working.**
- The headline is written in the reader's terms ("the card that pays for your subscription"), not in system jargon. Keep that voice.
- Links are blue and underlined, and their text says where each one goes ("roles and permissions", "paying by invoice"). Each is clear in context and doesn't rely on colour alone.
- The type is restrained: one sans family, a heavy H1, a bold H2 with more space above it than below, and generous leading. The page looks calm, which matters for someone dealing with a failed payment.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Lead with the answer (taste).** For a task article, the strongest version puts the action first: a single "Go to billing →" link or a three-step list right under the title, with the explanatory text demoted to "Good to know" below. Someone who only reads the top of the page is then already finished.
2. **Make the decline timeline a visual (taste).** A tiny day 0 / day 3 / day 7 / free plan sequence, as a short list or inline timeline, would show the stakes faster than a paragraph and would also remove the ambiguity in finding 2.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indicators on the nav, sidebar and inline links, and whether any sticky header covers focused items.
