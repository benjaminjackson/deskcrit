**First read.** My eye goes first to "184,220", then across the three KPI cards, then down to the chart and its three rising lines. Within a couple of seconds it reads as a calm, tidy marketing-analytics overview where everything is going up. Then the chart stops making sense: I can't tell which line is which channel, what the values are, or which weeks the points cover. The page feels finished, but the chart doesn't actually say anything.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an at-a-glance dashboard for a marketing or growth team to check traffic, sign-ups and conversion over the last 12 weeks, and to see which channels are driving the change.

**Verdict.** The KPI strip works. The chart, which takes up two-thirds of the screen, can't be read: it has no channel names, no values and no dates. So the one panel that should explain where the +6.1% came from explains nothing.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart has no labels of any kind** (defect). There are three coloured lines under "Visits by channel" with no legend, no direct labels, no y-axis values and no x-axis dates. The viewer can see that blue is highest and orange is the most jagged, but not which channel either one is. They can't tell whether blue means 8,000 or 80,000 visits a week, or which week the dip at the seventh point falls in. Without values they also can't tell whether the gaps between lines are large or whether the baseline is zero, so even "blue is about twice green" is a guess. The page already left room for these labels: there's an empty gutter about 70 px wide left of the plot and an empty band about 75 px tall below the baseline inside the card. That's why the chart looks oddly inset. A colour-blind viewer is worse off still, because colour is the only thing that tells the series apart. Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2) and Few (*Show Me the Numbers*) both say every chart needs labelled axes or direct labels with units, and WCAG 1.4.1 rules out colour as the only cue. The fix: put the channel name at the right end of each line, where the line ends (Tufte, Wilke). Add a few y-axis values in visits per week and week labels along the bottom, and use the gutter and band that are already there.

2. **The chart doesn't connect to the numbers above it** (taste, but it decides whether the page earns its space). The KPI strip asks a question ("visits up 6.1%, why?") and the chart is the only place that could answer it. But "Visits by channel" only names the topic, and nothing on the plot points out what changed. Even with labels added, the viewer would still have to compare three lines by eye to work out which channel drove the growth and whether the orange channel's swings matter. The title or a short caption should state the finding, for example "Organic drove most of the 6.1% rise; paid is volatile week to week". It could also mark the week-7 dip if it's real. That turns the panel from a picture of data into an answer.

**Smaller things.**
- The green chart series uses the same hue as the green "good" deltas in the KPI cards (`+6.1% vs previous 12 weeks`). Green reads as "positive" on this page, so a channel drawn in it picks up a meaning it hasn't earned. Use a non-status hue for that series. Apple HIG and Material both reserve status colours for their meaning.
- The chart title "Visits by channel" looks inset about 2 px further than the "Visits / Sign-ups / Conversion rate" labels in the cards above (around x=71 vs x=69). Either match the card padding or offset it clearly.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards.** There are three clear type levels (small grey label, large bold figure, small coloured delta), and each figure comes with a comparison to the previous period in plain words. "+0.1 pts" for a rate rather than a misleading "+5%" shows care. Keep the structure and the wording.
- **Restraint.** There's one neutral background, white cards with hairline borders, faint gridlines and no chart junk. Almost all the colour on the page is data, which is what makes the lines easy to tell apart at a glance once they have names.
- **A tight type scale.** I count about five sizes (header, page title, card label, KPI figure, delta) with clear steps between them. None of them collide.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the chart answer the KPI strip's question** (taste). A dashboard is for spotting what changed and why. Pick out the one series that moved the headline number, give it the strong colour, and drop the other channels to grey with direct labels. Then the eye goes straight from "+6.1%" to the cause. Tufte's case for showing data over decoration applies: here the emphasis does the analysis for the viewer.
2. **Give every KPI its own trend, not just its total** (taste). A small sparkline in each card, on the same 12-week x-range as the chart, would show whether "+2.4% sign-ups" is a steady rise or one spike. The cards would then read as summaries of the chart instead of separate facts.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "Last 12 weeks" in the header is a date-range control or a static label. If it's a control, it needs a visible affordance such as a chevron or border. If the page has no way to change the range or filter channels, that's a gap for the people who use it often.
- Whether hovering the chart shows values. A tooltip wouldn't fix the missing labels, because the chart needs to be readable without one.
