**First read.** The eye goes to the title and then down the three headings on the left edge. The page reads as a short, plain how-to for someone planting their first vegetable bed. It feels calm and tidy, but anonymous: it could be any blog's default stylesheet. The headings sit close to their paragraphs, so the four parts read as separate blocks right away.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a short web article meant to get a beginner from an empty patch of ground to a planted, watered bed.

**Verdict.** The layout mostly works: the order is clear, the spacing groups things correctly, and the text is easy to read. The weakest part is the content. A beginner who follows it to the letter is still left guessing at key steps. After that, the lines are too long.

**What would move it most**
1. **The steps leave out what a beginner needs in order to act.** *(defect)*
   - "Six hours of sun" doesn't say "a day". "Mark the corners" doesn't say how big the bed should be. Nothing says when to sow or how far apart.
   - The first step, choosing a spot, has no heading. It reads as an introduction under the title rather than step one. The other three steps do have headings.
   - The person who wrote it fills these gaps without noticing. A beginner can't, and these are exactly the details they will act on.
   - Fix: add a "Choosing a spot" heading so all four steps match, and add the missing numbers: hours per day, the size of the bed and when to sow. If the order matters, number the steps.
2. **The lines are too long.** *(convention)*
   - The paragraphs run about 90 characters per line ("Dig to the depth of a spade … Rake the surface level"), across an 800 px column. Bringhurst gives 45–75 as comfortable and 66 as ideal.
   - With paragraphs only two lines long the cost is small here, but on longer articles the reader starts losing their place on the jump back to the start of each line.
   - Fix: narrow the text column to about 600–640 px at this size, or raise the body size a step.

**Smaller things.**
- **Headings vs body:** the headings use the same regular weight and near-black as the body, so they differ only in size (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8–9). A medium or semibold weight would let them be a step smaller and still lead.
- **Apostrophe:** the one in "last year's" is straight; use a curly one. This is common sense: the reference has no checked source on curly quotes.

**What's working.**
- **Spacing:** each heading sits much closer to its own paragraph than to the one above it, so every section reads as a unit at a glance. Keep that ratio.
- **Type sizes:** there are three of them (about 56, 32 and 18 px), with big steps between, and each has an obvious job. Don't add a fourth.
- **Body text:** it's comfortable to read: dark on white, a generous size, and line spacing that suits it.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Give the page a voice of its own.** *(taste)* A gardening guide set entirely in neutral grey sans on white says nothing about the subject, and a reader can't tell whose advice it is. One warm earth or leaf colour, used only on the headings or a rule under the title, would give the page an identity without adding clutter.
2. **Make the steps physically countable.** *(taste)* This is a sequence, so large step numerals hanging in the empty left margin would let a reader with dirty hands find their place by glancing at the page. Those numerals could also be the weight change the headings currently lack.
