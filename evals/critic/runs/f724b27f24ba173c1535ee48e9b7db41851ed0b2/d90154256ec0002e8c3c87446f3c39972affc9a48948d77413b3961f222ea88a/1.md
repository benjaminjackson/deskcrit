It reads as a council or leisure-centre pool's "what's on" strip: friendly and confident in its type, but loud. Then I notice the bottom half of each card is empty, and the line under the cards ("one offer clearly leads the page") says the opposite of what I just saw.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a section of a public pool's website. Its job is to show prospective swimmers the three ways to swim there, so each can find theirs and go on to book or check times.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do the job yet. The three offers are the same weight with nothing to act on, so the viewer can see what exists but can't tell what to look at first or what to do next. The caption's claim that one offer leads is false: nothing on the page leads.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing leads, and the colours make it worse (defect).** The three cards are identical in size, type weight and layout, and each is a different fully saturated hue: orange-red, green and violet. Three equally loud colours compete for the eye, so it can't settle and the page looks like a row of buttons rather than an offer. Tschichold asks for order of importance to be shown by differences of size. Refactoring UI and the Apple HIG both warn that more than about two competing saturated accents splits attention. The fix depends on the goal:
   - If one programme matters most (often lessons, for new swimmers), make it clearly first by size or position and pull the other two back.
   - If they really are equal, make them equal *quietly*. Use one brand colour, or neutral cards with a small coloured tag each, so the colour stops shouting and the headings do the work.
2. **The bottom half of every card is dead colour (defect).** From about y=330 to y=630, roughly 55% of each card is empty saturated fill. It holds no image, times, price or link, so the viewer gets a big block of paint and the cards look unfinished, like a template waiting for content. This is a fault of common sense, not a cited rule. Either fill that space with what the viewer actually needs (finding 3) or shrink the cards to fit their content. Don't keep the height for its own sake.
3. **There is no ask and too little to decide on (defect).** Nothing says what to do: no "Book a class", no timetable link, no price, and no times except "from six" and "Sunday afternoons". A viewer interested in lessons must guess whether the card is clickable and then go hunting (Nielsen's recognition rather than recall; Krug's "what can I do here?"). The descriptions are good, specific copy. Back them with the facts a swimmer acts on, and give each card one exact action such as "Book a Tuesday class" or "See lane times", styled to look clickable. The page also has no heading or pool name, so out of context a stranger doesn't know whose pool this is.

**Smaller things.**
- The warm orange on the left card comes forward by temperature alone (Itten), giving "Learn to swim" a slight lead nobody chose. Once you fix finding 1, make sure the warmest colour sits on the offer you actually want first.
- In grayscale the three cards fall to similar mid-dark values, so nothing separates them except hue (Albers). This resolves with finding 1.
- The page's right margin (about 96 px) looks wider than its left (56 px). Check whether the row is meant to fill the container.

**What's working.**
- The headings are bold, tightly leaded, and short enough that each one reads as a single unit at a glance. Keep that type treatment.
- The descriptions are concrete and speak to a person: "a coach in the water beside you", "three speeds to choose from", "floats and toys for the children". Each one tells the reader who the session is for. Keep that voice when you add the facts.
- The grid is clean. The cards are equal width with even gutters, and the text aligns on shared baselines across all three.

- White text on the green and violet looks like it passes 4.5:1, but measure the green to be sure.
