**First read.** The eye goes first to the three big numbers along the top (3.8%, €64.20, 12.4k), takes them in as a row, then drops to the block of orange bars. There it sees the Saturday peak and the Sunday drop. It reads as the analytics home for a small bakery's online shop, probably one that sells subscriptions. It feels calm, tidy and competent, but generic: apart from the wordmark and the orange, it could be any shop's dashboard.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the landing screen for the bakery's owner or operator. Its job is to show at a glance how the shop is doing and when orders arrive, so they can plan baking and delivery.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The KPI cards are clean and well set up. The chart is the weak part. It adds up orders across a month whose weekdays don't occur equally often, so it shows a weekday pattern that isn't quite real. Its title also leaves the owner to work out what the pattern means.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart counts days unevenly, and the weekday pattern it shows is partly wrong (defect).** The source line says these are orders delivered in September 2026. That September has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of every other weekday. Tuesday and Wednesday therefore get roughly 25% more days in their totals. Reading the bars by eye and dividing by the number of days: Monday is about 160 orders a day, Tuesday about 142, Wednesday about 152 and Thursday about 205. So the steady climb from Monday to Thursday that the bars show isn't there. Monday is actually busier per day than Tuesday or Wednesday. An owner who sets staffing or baking from this chart will plan wrongly for early in the week. One month is also a thin sample for a pattern someone will plan around. Chart the average orders per delivery day, or count over a whole number of weeks, and say which in the title or source line. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, asks for exactly this kind of note on the sample behind a chart.

2. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding (common sense, no approved source).** "Orders by delivery day" tells the owner what the chart is. It doesn't say what to take from it. The two facts that matter here are that Friday and Saturday carry the week and that Sunday is about a third of Saturday. Once the numbers are per-day, those are what decide baking and delivery capacity, and right now the viewer has to dig them out of the bars. Put the finding into the title, for example: "Friday and Saturday bring in about twice as many orders a day as early in the week." Ideally also mark the peak on the chart itself.

**Smaller things.**
- The time periods don't match. The first two KPI cards say "Last 30 days", the subscriber card says "As of 30 September 2026", and the chart covers September. If today isn't 30 September, the cards and the chart describe different periods. Either tie them all to one stated period or show the date range once under "Shop overview".
- There's no control to change the period (for example last 7 days, a month, or a custom range), so the owner can't check whether September was typical. Add a date-range control next to the page title (Nielsen heuristic 7, flexibility and efficiency of use).

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards.** Each has a label, one big figure and a period. The comparison is shown with an arrow and a sign as well as green, so colour isn't the only signal. The unit marks (%, €, k) are set smaller and lighter, so the number itself is what you read. Keep this pattern for any card you add.
- **The chart's restraint.** The bars start at zero, the weekdays are in their natural order, the gridlines are faint, there's only one colour and there's a source line. Nearly all the ink is data.
- **The palette.** One brand orange, used for the wordmark and the bars, plus green kept only for status. It's small and the colours don't clash.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the chart answer a planning question (taste).** The screen exists so the owner can act on it. Highlight the peak days in the brand orange and grey out the rest, so the busiest days stand out without anyone having to read the axis. Then one glance says where the effort goes. Wilke argues for this in *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*: one highlighted series against grey context.
2. **Make it look like the bakery's own (taste).** Right now the orange and the wordmark are the only signs of who this belongs to. That's fine for a tool, but it's a missed chance for a small brand whose owner opens this screen every day. Bring the brand into the type or the page tone, for example warmer neutrals instead of the cool grey background, so the dashboard feels like part of the business rather than a stock admin template.

- The focus state on the navigation links.
