**First read.** The eye lands on "Overview", runs left to right along the four big figures starting at $31,270, then jumps to the one blue bar (Saturday, 148). It reads as a calm, well-made monthly dashboard for a small farm shop's owner. It feels tidy and trustworthy, but nothing on it tells me whether November was a good month or a bad one.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the shop owner's month-end view: how November went against October, and what is selling.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid: clean grid, a restrained palette and honest charts. But the dashboard reports figures without saying what they mean. The month's real story is that visitors went up while orders and revenue went down, and the owner has to work that out with mental math across three panels.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI row shows last month but not the change** (top row, all four cards). Each card puts October under November as a second raw figure, like "$34,105 in October" under $31,270. To learn that revenue fell about 8% and orders about 7%, the owner has to subtract in their head, four times. Because all four figures are the same size, weight and color, none of them stands out as the month's news. Few's dashboard rule is that a headline number needs a comparison the reader can judge good or bad at a glance, and a second number to subtract doesn't do that. Direction: show the change itself (amount or percent, with an up or down marker) next to each figure, and let the line under it carry the October value or drop it. *Defect* (convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tschichold, *The New Typography*, on making one element dominate).
2. **The two charts leave out units, and the one labeled bar can be read two ways** (middle row).
   - "Orders by weekday" has no y-axis and only one value, "148" on Saturday. The viewer can't tell whether 148 is all Saturday orders this month or an average Saturday, and from the bar heights neither reading obviously adds up to the 2,214 total. If they guess wrong, they staff or stock Saturdays wrong.
   - "Shop visitors" runs 0 to 900 with no unit or period, and its roughly 11 points across 30 days suggest each point covers several days, not one.
   
   Tufte says the reader should never have to guess what a number measures (*Visual Display*, ch. 2). Direction: state the unit and period in each chart's title or axis, for example "orders per Saturday" or "visitors per day", whichever is true. *Defect.*
3. **The screen never connects visitors to orders** (shop visitors chart vs. the KPI row). The chart shows November visitors well above October all month, while the KPIs show fewer orders and less revenue. That gap is the most useful thing on the page, and it's split across two panels with no words on it. The chart titles name topics ("Shop visitors", "Orders by weekday") when they could state what the chart shows. Direction: let the chart title or a one-line caption state the finding, or put visitor-to-order conversion next to the KPIs so the comparison is in front of the owner. *Taste/common sense.* No approved source covers takeaway titles, but on a monthly review page, the screen should hand over the finding rather than leave it to be pieced together.

**Smaller things.**
- "Farm box subscribers 18%": 18% of what? Customers, orders, or something else? Name the base in the label.
- "Best-selling produce" is ranked by revenue, but by units sold the order reverses (cider 231, syrup 164). Either retitle it by revenue or sort by units sold, so the heading names what the list ranks.
- The shop visitors legend sits top right, away from the lines. With two series, label "November" and "October" at the line ends and drop the legend (Tufte; Wilke).

**What's working.**
- Blue only for the current period and gray for context, carried across both charts. That one rule makes "this month vs. last" readable without a key. Keep it.
- The grid is strict: the KPI strip, the two chart cards and the table share outer edges and gutters, and the table's numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures. It looks calm and easy to trust.
- The bar chart keeps weekday order and starts at zero, so the Saturday peak is honest.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a control for changing the period. If it isn't, the owner has no visible way to look at another month.
- Whether hovering on the charts shows the missing values and units. Even if it does, the still view should carry them.
