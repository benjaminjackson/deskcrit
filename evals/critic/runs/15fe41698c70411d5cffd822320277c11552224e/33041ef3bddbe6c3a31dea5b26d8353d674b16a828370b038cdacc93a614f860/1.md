**First read.** The eye lands on the big cream serif headline "Every shelf of the kiln booked before the firing". It moves right to the shelf-booking card, where the amber "Book" pulls it, then back to the amber "Set up your studio" button. Within three seconds I know what this is: booking software for a shared pottery studio's kiln. It feels calm and made by hand: dark green, cream, and a fire-colored accent, with no stock-SaaS gloss. Below the hero the page settles into a quiet text column and ends on two prices and a footer.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the marketing landing page for Kilnroom, meant to get a studio owner or technician to sign up their studio.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, honest page. The copy is specific, the hero mock shows the product doing exactly what the headline promises, and the hierarchy is clean. What holds it back most is the ending: the page reaches the prices and then offers nothing to do, and the heading over those prices contradicts them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page has no ask after the prices.** The only button is "Set up your studio" in the hero. A viewer who reads all the way down is most ready to act at the pricing block ("$12 a month", "$29 a month"), and there they get two prices, a footer and nothing to click. They have to scroll back to the top to act, and some won't. Each tier should end with the action, or one button should follow the two tiers. Use the hero's wording or a tier-specific version of it. This is a **defect**: the eye path ends in a dead end (Arnheim's point about a clear path to the call to action; anticipating the next step is common sense, not a cited rule).
   - The hero ask has a related gap: it never sets Kilnroom against how the studio books shelves today. The page states the benefit but gives no "instead of" that the reader can check against their own week. Whatever the studio currently does (the maker knows what that is) would let the reader judge the value on their own terms rather than take the claim on trust.
2. **"One price per studio" sits over two prices.** A stranger reads the heading, sees $12 and $29 underneath, and stops to work out which is wrong. The heading presumably means "priced per studio, not per member", but it leaves out the word that carries that meaning. Price lines are what people hold you to, so this one can't be readable two ways. Fix it as an edit, not new copy: say what the one price is per (for example, restore "not per member" if that is the point), or name the section after what it holds. This is a **defect**, and it also fails the "headings name their content" rule (WCAG 2.4.6).

**Smaller things**
- **Pricing tiers are off the grid.** They run 80–376 and 424–720 px, while the three-column grid above runs at 80, 467 and 853 px. Put the two tiers on the first two columns of that grid so the column lines carry down the page (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- **The hero card floats.** Its top (about 193 px) lines up with neither the headline top (about 135 px) nor the center of the text block. Align it with the headline's top line or center it on the text block, decisively.
- **Space above section headings is uneven.** "From the wheel to the shelf" sits about 80 px below its rule; "For studios that teach" and "One price per studio" sit about 40 px below theirs. Pick one interval (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*).
- **Runts.** "firing" sits alone on the headline's third line, and "teacher." sits alone at the end of the teaching paragraph. Rebreak the headline so "the firing" stays together, and adjust the paragraph's measure slightly (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- **"Studio notes" in the nav doesn't say what's behind it** (blog? guides? changelog?). Name what it holds. This is my judgment, not a cited rule.

**What's working**
- **The hero mock is the argument.** "Glaze firing, cone 6 · Loads Friday 16:00", three full shelves and one "Half free / Book" show the whole product in one glance with believable data. Keep it real data rather than lorem.
- **The copy speaks the studio's language.** "write on the foot", "When the kiln is cool", "Beginners see when their first bowls come out without asking the teacher." Each line says who does what and lands on the reader. Guard this when you edit.
- **Color is restrained.** Amber appears only on the primary button and the one "Book" action, so the eye goes straight to what can be done. The dark green and cream suit a craft audience without looking like a template.
