**First read.** The eye lands on the big white headline, moves right to the tide card, then drops to the amber "Add your beach" button. Within a couple of seconds it reads as a tide-and-current app for sea swimmers on a particular stretch of coast. It feels calm and sober, the way a tool you'd trust with your safety should, and a little austere.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a landing page meant to get sea swimmers to add a beach on the free plan and later move up to $4 a month.

**Verdict.** The page is solid in structure, type and tone, and the headline and ask are good. Its weak point is the hero card. It's the only proof on the page, and it doesn't show the thing the page sells. The copy promises a marked "safe window." The card shows four unlabeled tide events and a bar chart with no axis.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero card never shows the safe window. Defect.** The paragraph says Slackwater "marks the safe window for each swim," and step 2 is titled "See the safe window." The card shows only point times (06:10, 09:30, 12:25, 18:40) with a one-word verdict each. No span of time is marked as safe, either in the list or on the bars. A swimmer can't answer "from when to when can I go in?", which is the question that brings them here. The headline makes a promise and the picture beside it doesn't keep it, so the visitor has to take the product's main claim on trust. Fix: make the window the most visible thing on the card. Shade or bracket it on the chart, and state it as a time range in the list.

2. **The chart can't be read. Defect (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few).** The 24 gray bars have no time axis, no unit, no baseline and no label saying what they measure. Tide height or current strength? The viewer has to guess. Nothing ties the bars to the four times listed below them. You can't find 12:25 on the bars, and you can't tell whether the low point lines up with it. Right now the chart is decoration that looks like data. On a safety product, that costs trust. Fix: add a few hour marks along the bottom and say what the bars are. Better still, mark the listed events directly on the bars so the list and the chart read as one thing.

3. **The row that matters most loses its verdict. Defect.** "Next" (bold white, at 12:25) replaces Swim/Avoid in the same column, so the one event the swimmer is about to act on is the only one without a swim-or-avoid call. Low-water slack could be either. Nothing shows the current time, and past rows (06:10, 09:30) look the same as future ones. Fix: show "next" some other way (a row highlight, a marker, or dimming past rows), and keep the Swim/Avoid verdict on every row.

4. **The pricing raises doubt and leads nowhere. Defect (prices are what the viewer holds you to).** The product is "made in Penzance," says "car park," and uses °C, but the prices are in dollars. A UK swimmer will wonder what they'll actually be charged, or whether the page was built for them at all. "Two plans, cancel any month" sits over a $0 plan, which has nothing to cancel. The section then ends with no button. The eye path (Arnheim) stops on the last plan's description, and the visitor has to scroll back to the hero to act. Fix: use the currency the audience pays in, have the "cancel" line refer to the paid plan, and give each plan its own action. The free plan's action can repeat "Add your beach."

5. **The ask doesn't set the product against what swimmers already use. Copy.** "Add your beach" is a good ask: it names one concrete thing the visitor can do. But the page never says what Slackwater gives over the tide table or tide app the swimmer already checks. Step 1 even says it "reads the tide tables." So the visitor is asked to trust the value instead of seeing it. What's missing is a comparison with their current routine: what the tide table leaves them to work out, which Slackwater works out for them. That fact has to come from you.

**Smaller things.**
- Pricing columns: the second plan looks like it starts about 5 px left of the second step column (≈464 vs ≈469), and its width doesn't match the step columns either. Put both sections on the same three-column grid.
- "Sea 13 °C" has no point of comparison. For cold-water swimmers, a change since yesterday or a time of reading would make it useful.
- One-word last lines: "days." in the Dipper description and "reading." in step 2. Rewrap them or trim a word.

**What's working.**
- The headline is specific and spoken to the swimmer ("before you leave the car"). It could only belong to this product. Keep the voice.
- The palette is restrained: dark gray, white and one amber accent used only on the primary button. The ask can't be missed, and the page feels calm and serious, which fits a safety tool.
- The type scale is clean: a large headline, one section heading size, one subhead size and one body size, with weight doing the finer work. The three steps scan in seconds.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether "Next" and the Swim/Avoid labels update live, and how the two-column hero stacks on a phone, where the card would fall below the button.
