**First read.** My eye goes to the "Output" title and then across the four big numbers, which all have the same weight, so nothing stops it. Next it goes to the one blue bar (array F), then to the two rising lines, and last to the "Arrays needing a visit" table. It reads as a monthly report for a community solar co-op. It's calm, tidy and well built, but it reads as a general summary, not as "something is wrong and here's what to do."

**What it's for.** My assumption: a monthly view for the co-op's operators and board. It should show how November went against October and which arrays need a technician.

**Verdict.** The layout is well made, but the screen contradicts itself. The cards say November output fell by a quarter, while the daily chart shows November above October on every day. A viewer can't tell which to believe, and that undercuts everything else on the page. After that, the page hides its one urgent message (three arrays losing output) at the bottom, and its strongest highlight goes to the best array instead of the broken ones.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cards and the line chart tell opposite stories (defect).** The top-left card says 38.4 MWh, against 51.2 MWh in October. That's a 25% drop. The "Daily output (kWh)" chart shows the blue November line about 150–450 kWh above the gray October line at every point. By eye, November averages about 1,100 kWh a day, which comes to roughly 33 MWh, not 38.4. October looks like about 850 a day, around 26 MWh, nowhere near 51.2. The chart also plots about 12 points across 30 days, so it isn't daily as its title says. A board member who notices any of this will stop trusting every number on the screen. Check the chart's data source and aggregation against the cards. Either plot all 30 days or rename the chart to match what it plots. Don't ship until the two agree.

2. **Nothing tells the viewer what matters, and the action sits last (convention + defect).** The four KPI numbers are the same size and weight. Each has a small gray "in October" line underneath, with no direction or change shown. The viewer has to do the math to learn that every figure went down. The table of arrays needing a visit is the only part that asks someone to act, and it sits at the bottom with the same weight as everything else. Tschichold asks that the order of importance be shown by difference. Here it's flat. Make one thing clearly first. For an operator, that's the faults or the size of the drop. Next to each number, show the change from October and which way it went, not just October's raw value (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").

3. **The bar chart highlights the wrong array and has no scale (defect).** "Output by array" colors F blue and labels only F (2.3 MWh). Nothing says why F is singled out, so the meaning rests on color alone (WCAG 1.4.1). Meanwhile D, G and B are the arrays the table says are faulty, and they look like plain gray bars. There's no y-axis or other values, so the viewer can't read how far D or G falls short (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). Use this chart to back up the table: mark the faulty arrays, give every bar a value or an axis, and say in words why any bar is highlighted.

**Smaller things.**
- Line chart x-axis ticks are spaced unevenly: 1, 10, 19 and then 30 Nov, a gap of 9, 9 and then 11. Use even intervals (Tufte).
- The line chart uses a legend at the top right. Label the two lines at their right ends instead (Tufte; Wilke).
- The chart titles name topics, not findings. A title that says what the chart shows would help a board reader (taste).
- "Panels online" fell by only 6 (1,190 to 1,184), while the table lists 64 panels behind an offline inverter on array D. The viewer can't tell what "online" counts. Say what the number counts (common sense).
- The faults table names the problems but gives no way to act on them: no assign, schedule or "seen" control is visible. If this screen is where visits get arranged, it needs one. If not, say where (taste).

**What's working.**
- The structure is clear: navigation on the left, KPI cards, two charts, then a table. All of it sits on one grid with consistent padding and alignment. Keep it.
- The type is restrained. There's one family and roughly four sizes with clear jobs, and the gray secondary lines sit back properly.
- The faults table is well set. Its numbers align right in tabular figures, its rules are light, and "Lost output" in kWh turns each fault into a cost.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the table rows or the bars can be clicked, focus states, and the exact contrast of the gray axis labels and "in October" lines. They look acceptable, but measure them.
