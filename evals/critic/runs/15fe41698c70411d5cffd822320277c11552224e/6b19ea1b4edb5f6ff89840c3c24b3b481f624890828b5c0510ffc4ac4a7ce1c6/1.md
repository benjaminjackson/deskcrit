**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Let the harbour book its own berths". It then jumps straight to the purple-blue S4 tile in the berth plan, which is the loudest thing on the page and louder than the button under the headline. The page reads as calm, tidy software for marina offices. The plan mock does most of the convincing, because it looks like a real pontoon.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a booking and billing tool for small harbours and marinas. It should get harbour masters to set up their harbour or to try the sample.

**Verdict.** It mostly works: the job is clear in five seconds, the copy is concrete, and the layout is disciplined. The biggest problem is the berth plan, which is the main proof on the page. Its color code is backwards from what a booking map normally means. Second, the purple gradient is a stock SaaS touch that the rest of the page doesn't share.

**What would move it most**
1. **Green means "taken" in the berth plan.** On almost any booking map, green means free and white or gray means unavailable. Here the 15 green tiles are the booked berths and the three white dashed tiles (N3, S6, W5) are the free ones. There's no key. A harbour master glancing at it first reads "nearly everything is free", and only the small "18 berths, 3 free" label at the top right lets them work it out. The whole pitch is "boat owners pick a free berth", so the picture of free berths needs to be obvious without decoding. This is a defect that comes from breaking a convention (Apple HIG, Material Design: green means success or available, and status colors are not reversed). To fix it, show taken berths as neutral or occupied, and make free berths the inviting state. Showing a boat name on each taken berth would also do it. That would back up your own third feature, "each boat name on its berth", and the mock would explain itself.
2. **The purple-to-blue gradient doesn't belong.** It sits on "Set up your harbour" and on S4. Everything else on the page is muted sage, stone, and gray with plain type. This is the only saturated color, and it's a gradient, the most common SaaS cliché there is. It makes the page look like a template instead of a harbour product. On S4 it also out-shouts the headline and the button, so the eye goes to an example booking before the ask. This is taste, and I'm arguing it on unity: every part should share a visible trait with the rest. It also counts against a small palette (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*). Pick one solid accent from the page's own world and use it for the button and the selected berth. A deeper sea color would work against the sage and stone. Keep S4 a step quieter than the button.

**Smaller things.**
- The hero paragraph ends on "where." alone on its last line. Adjust the measure or the wording so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The plan is titled "North Pontoon", but its rows are N, S, and W, and "18 berths" counts all three. A harbour reader will wonder whether this is one pontoon or three. Make the title and the row names agree (Norman, conceptual model).
- Copy: "Set up your harbour" and "Open a sample pontoon" are specific asks, and the sample lets viewers judge for themselves, which is good. Nothing on the page sets Moorly against what the office uses today, though. Naming the current way of booking berths and collecting fees, so the viewer can see what this replaces, would make the value land on their terms. That fact has to come from you.
- "Visitors book the night before" can be read as "visitors can only book the night before". If you mean "as late as the night before", say so.

**What's working.**
- The berth plan as the hero image: it's realistic, uses real harbour terms (pontoon, slipway, berth, "up to 9 m"), and shows a finished booking with a price. It shows the product instead of describing it. Keep it, and fix its color code.
- The copy is concrete and in the reader's own words. "The harbour office sees who is moored where" and "Print the pontoon plan for the office wall" each say who does what. Protect that when editing.
- The type scale is clean: about four sizes with clear jobs (headline, lead and feature headings, body, captions), a shared left edge, and a three-column feature row on one grid.
