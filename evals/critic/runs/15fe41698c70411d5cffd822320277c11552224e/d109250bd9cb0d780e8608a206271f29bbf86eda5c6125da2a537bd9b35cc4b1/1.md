**First read.** The eye goes first to the one strong color, the blue Saturday bar on the right. Then it goes back to the row of four large figures, and reads $27,640 first only because it sits furthest left. It's clearly a monthly sales dashboard for a small fabric business. It feels calm, tidy and competent, but quite anonymous: nothing on it tells you how the month went.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner of Bolt & Bobbin opens this to see how November went against October and what sold.

**Verdict.** It's well built and easy to scan. But the numbers don't agree with each other, and the page never says what happened this month. The biggest problem is trust. The "Daily orders" chart looks like it shows about a third more orders than the revenue and average order imply, so the owner can't rely on the screen without checking it.

**What would move it most**

1. **The order numbers contradict each other (defect).** Revenue of $27,640 at a $46 average order works out to about 600 orders. The weekday bars, scaled from Saturday's 126, add up to about 615, which matches. The "Daily orders" line in November runs from about 20 to 35 and averages around 27. Over 30 days that would be about 800 orders. The line also has only 11 points, roughly one every three days, so either it isn't daily or the title is wrong. An owner who notices this stops trusting every figure on the screen, and one who doesn't will plan from the wrong number. My figures are by eye, so check two things: the sum the line plots against the order count behind the average, and the bucket size behind each point. Then make the title, the points and the totals agree. If the points are 3-day averages, the title has to say so. Tick labels at 1, 10, 19 and 30 Nov also have uneven gaps (9, 9, 11 days); use even ticks.

2. **The weekday chart is the loudest thing on the page, and it's the least honest (defect).** It's the only saturated color on the page, so it outranks revenue. It also compares totals across unequal numbers of days. November 2026 starts on a Sunday, so it has five Sundays and five Mondays but only four Saturdays. Sunday's bar is partly inflated by the extra day, and Saturday's lead is understated. Only Saturday carries a value. The other six bars have no axis or numbers, so "how much busier is Saturday?" can't be answered (Tufte, *Visual Display*, on labeling; Few). Fix: plot the average orders per weekday, label every bar or add an axis, and keep the accent if Saturday really is the point.

3. **Nothing leads, and nothing says how the month went (convention + taste).** The four KPI cards are equal in size, weight and color, and the page title "Sales" (about 24 px) is nearly the same size as the figures (about 26 px). Your eye has no clear starting point, which goes against Tschichold's advice to show the order of importance through differences in size. Every comparison is there ("$24,110 in October"), but you have to do the subtraction yourself. Each chart title names a topic ("Daily orders", "Orders by weekday") rather than a finding. My view, as taste: make revenue clearly dominant, or show the change from October beside each figure. Let each chart title say what the chart shows, in words drawn from its own data.

4. **The currency looks wrong (defect, if it is wrong).** The shop is in Norwich, the copy spells "Metres", and the mills (Hessle, Ardleigh, Pennine) are English, yet every amount is in "$". If the shop trades in pounds, every money figure on the page is mislabeled. Revenue is exactly the number the owner acts on and quotes to others. If the business really does price in dollars, ignore this.

**Smaller things.**
- Line chart: the November/October legend sits in the top right corner. Label the two lines at their right ends instead (Tufte; Wilke).
- About seven type sizes: 26, 24, 18, 16, 14, 12, 11. The page title and KPI figures collide. Drop the title back or push the figures up so they read as separate levels (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1).
- The date range "1 to 30 November 2026" looks like plain text. If the period can be changed, it should look like a control. If it can't, the owner has no visible way to look at October or the year so far (Nielsen, heuristic 7).
- The Settings icon in the sidebar is a sun/target shape, not the gear people expect, so it reads as "brightness" (Apple HIG; Material).
- "Repeat makers 54%" doesn't say 54% of what: makers, orders, or revenue. Add the missing noun.
- "Best-selling fabrics" is ranked by metres, but wool melton out-earns cotton lawn. Say which measure sets the order.

**What's working.**
- Every headline figure carries last month's value right under it, in quieter gray. That's the right context, set at the right weight.
- The neutral palette with a single blue for "this period" (November's line) is restrained and consistent. Keep that one accent rule when you fix the weekday chart.
- The table is clean: numbers right-aligned in matching figures, light rules, and plain-language names ("Washed linen, oat").

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date range or the table headers can be clicked, and what a hover on the chart shows. Hover values would partly answer the missing numbers on the weekday bars.
