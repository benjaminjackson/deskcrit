I've finished the walk and the deep read of the flagged lines. Here's the critique.

**First read.** My eye lands on the row of four large numbers along the top, then jumps to the one blue bar (Saturday, 19), then drifts to the November line. It reads at once as a ferry operator's monthly summary for office staff: tickets, money, cars, which days sell out and which routes are busiest. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, and also a little stock, like a dashboard template with ferry data poured in. The pale blue glow around every card is the first thing that isn't data, and you notice it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal overview for harbor-office staff and managers. They open it to answer "how did this month go, and where are we under strain?"

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. It's clean and legible, it fits on one screen, and its charts are the right shapes. The biggest thing in the way is that it reports numbers but never draws a conclusion. Every KPI makes the viewer do the subtraction, the bar chart hides six of its seven values, and no title says what happened this month.

**What would move it most**

1. **The KPIs give last month's figure but not the change, so the viewer does the arithmetic.** In each tile in the top row, "9,412" sits over "8,870 in October". To learn whether that's good, the viewer has to subtract and estimate a percentage in their head, four times. They'll do it once at most, so "up about 6%" and "revenue up about 9%" never get across. Including the prior period is right: that's Few's point about context in "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design". But the comparison should arrive already worked out. Put the change itself next to each number, as a signed difference or percentage with a direction, and keep the October value as the detail line. That also fixes "Average fare $15 / $15 in October": a plain "no change" reads faster than two identical numbers. *Defect: it slows the viewer on the main question the page exists to answer.*

2. **The bar chart shows one value and hides six.** In "Sailings sold out by weekday", only Saturday is labeled (19). There's no value axis, so Friday and Sunday, the two days that matter next, can only be guessed at (roughly 12–15?). Highlighting Saturday in blue against gray is the right call. But a manager planning extra sailings needs Friday and Sunday as numbers too. Tufte and Few both say a reader should never have to guess what a mark measures. Put a small value at the top of every bar, or at least Fri and Sun, and keep Saturday as the emphasized one. *Defect.*

3. **No title says what happened.** "Daily passengers", "Sailings sold out by weekday" and "Busiest routes" name topics. The findings are plain in the data: November ran above October every day, and Saturday is the pinch point. But the viewer still has to find them. This is a common-sense point, not a convention from an approved source. On a page someone glances at once a month, a short caption under each chart title saying the finding in the chart's own terms (November above October throughout; Saturdays sell out most) turns a glance into an answer. Write it from the data you have; don't add claims the data doesn't support. *Taste, argued: it's the difference between a report and a briefing.*

**Smaller things.**
- The blue halo around every card and the active "Overview" nav item is decoration with no job. A thin border or a neutral shadow does the separating, and the glow adds a tint that competes with the data's blue (Nielsen 8, aesthetic and minimalist design).
- The x-axis ticks on the line chart (1, 10, 19, 30 Nov) are 9, 9 and 11 days apart. Use even intervals or label every week (Tufte).
- "Daily passengers" plots about 11 points across 30 days. Either plot every day or retitle it to say what each point is. As it stands, "daily" and the shape disagree.
- The line chart has a legend (top right) where direct labels would do. Put "November" and "October" at the right-hand ends of the lines and drop the legend (Tufte; Wilke).
- The edge between the line-chart and bar-chart cards (around x = 890) lines up with none of the KPI dividers above it, so the second row looks placed by eye. Either align the split to the KPI columns (3 + 1, or 2 + 2) or make the offset obviously deliberate (Müller-Brockmann).
- "1 to 30 November 2026" looks like static text, so there's no visible way to change the period or compare another month. If it's a control, make it look like one. If it isn't, a period picker is the obvious shortcut for this page (Nielsen 7).

**What's working.**
- **Gray with one blue for emphasis.** October gray and November blue, Saturday blue against six gray bars. One accent always means "look here", so the eye goes to the right place without a legend. Keep that rule when you add values.
- **The numbers are set well.** Big plain figures in the KPI tiles, and right-aligned tabular figures in the routes table, where the Sailings and Revenue columns line up cleanly for scanning. Labels like "Cars carried" and "Oban to Craignure" use the staff's own words.
- **Everything fits on one screen.** The essentials of the month are visible with no scrolling, and the type sizes are few and clearly distinct.
