**First read.** The eye lands on "Send invoices in minutes, not hours," drops into the gray paragraph, and stops at the blue "Start free trial" button. Then it notices the empty right half of the hero. It reads as an invoicing tool for small creative studios: calm, clean, competent, and interchangeable with a dozen other SaaS homepages. Nothing on the screen shows the product.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage hero of an invoicing product for small design and photo studios, meant to get a studio owner to start a trial.

**Verdict.** The page is tidy and easy to read, but it doesn't make anyone want the product. The headline could sit on any invoicing site, the one thing that sets this product apart is buried at the end of a paragraph, and there's no image of the product, so the viewer has to take every claim on trust. As a layout it's clean; as a sales page it's weak.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero shows no product, and half of it is empty.** The text column stops about 45% of the way across. Everything to the right of the headline, paragraph and buttons is blank white, down to the "Why studios use Quillfern" heading. There's no picture of an invoice, the reminder schedule or the "what each client owes" view anywhere on the screen. Two things go wrong. The page leans left and the void looks like something failed to load (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). And the viewer never sees what a "clear invoice" looks like, so the headline's promise has nothing behind it. For a studio audience that judges visual polish for a living, the look of the invoice they'd send their clients may be the strongest selling point you have. Fix: fill the right half with the product itself, ideally a real invoice as the client would see it, so the image and the headline make one argument. *Defect* (balance), plus *taste* on what the image should be.

2. **The headline is generic, and the one distinct claim is buried.** "Send invoices in minutes, not hours" fits every invoicing product, so the viewer can't tell what's different here and has no reason to stop. The paragraph ends on the only specific, studio-shaped benefits on the screen: "There is nothing to install, and your accountant can see every invoice from one shared link." That's the fifth line of gray body text, which is where scanners stop reading. The "minutes, not hours" comparison also states a number the viewer can't check. It would land harder as a comparison against what they do now, such as their current spreadsheet or Word-template routine. That's for you to supply; I'm not writing it. Fix: lead with whatever Quillfern does that others don't (the accountant link is one candidate, if it really is distinctive), and cut the paragraph down to that. *Taste*, argued: copy that could be anyone's gives the viewer nothing to act on.

3. **The same three points are said three times.** The hero paragraph lists "send clear invoices, follow up on late payments, and see what each client owes." The three cards then repeat exactly those points as titles, and each card's body repeats its own title again ("Chase late payments politely" → "Send a polite reminder when a payment is late"; "Know what each client owes" → "See who owes what"). The viewer reads the same pitch three times and learns nothing new on the second or third pass. Meanwhile the section heading "Why studios use Quillfern" promises reasons and gets a feature list. Fix: give each layer its own job. The hero makes the one claim, and the cards give the specifics behind each feature (what the reminder actually says, what the client sees, why a studio in particular cares). Otherwise cut the hero paragraph to one line. *Convention* (Nielsen heuristic 8, aesthetic and minimalist design; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*, "get rid of half the words").

**Smaller things.**
- Hero paragraph (about 18 px), card titles (about 17 px bold) and card body (about 16 px) are three sizes a point or two apart. Put both paragraph blocks on one size and let weight carry the card titles (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Measure the actual sizes in the browser to confirm.
- "Start free trial" doesn't say how long the trial is or whether a card is required. That's what a studio owner hesitates on before clicking. Put the length and the card terms in a short line next to the button.

**What's working.**
- One clear primary action. A solid blue "Start free trial" sits next to "Book a demo", which is styled as an underlined text link, so there's no doubt which action you want. Keep that difference.
- One left edge runs from the wordmark through the eyebrow, headline, paragraph, buttons, section heading and first card, and the three cards span exactly the nav's width. The page feels orderly without trying.
- The palette is restrained: near-black, gray, one blue, and warm-gray card tints. That leaves the blue entirely to the call to action. When the product image goes in, keep its color from competing with that button.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states for the nav links, the button and "Book a demo".
- How the empty right half and the three-card row behave at narrower widths.
