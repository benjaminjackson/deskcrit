**First read.** My eye lands on the headline, "Know which rack every boat is on," and then goes right to a large, pale gray box with a broken-image icon in it. That box is the biggest shape on the screen and it shows nothing. Next I go to the dark blue button, then the three feature columns. I take it for a software product that rowing clubs use to manage their boathouse racks, aimed at club captains and committees. It feels calm, plain and competent, and fairly templated.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing page of a web tool for rowing-club boathouses, meant to get a captain or committee member to try it with their own boathouse or to look at the sample.

**Verdict.** The words, structure and button do their jobs well. The page fails at the one place it needed to show something: the right half of the hero is an empty placeholder where the product should be. The page promises that "the captain sees every boat at a glance," and that is exactly where the glance should happen.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero image is an empty placeholder (defect).** In the top right, a gray box about 550 by 350 px holds only a generic image icon. It takes half the first screen and is the second thing the eye lands on. A visitor arriving here can't see what a rack plan looks like, so the claim that you can see every boat at a glance has to be taken on trust. The page offers the proof only one click away, in "See a sample boathouse." If this is an image that didn't load in the capture, check it in a live browser. If it's a design placeholder, it is the biggest gap on the page. The fix is to fill it with the product itself: a real rack plan, readable at this size, showing club and private boats and an empty rack a member could claim. Then the picture proves the headline instead of repeating it, and the three features below have something to point back to. Pairing the picture with the headline is common sense, not a cited rule.

2. **The page names no comparison with how clubs manage racks today (taste, copy).** The copy is specific and in the club's own words ("rack holder," "captain," "boathouse wall"). The button is a good ask: "Map your boathouse" names one exact thing to do with something the visitor already has. But nothing sets Slipway against what the club uses now, whether that's a sheet on the wall, a spreadsheet or the captain's memory. The visitor has to judge the value on Slipway's own terms. The closest the page gets is "A plan for the boathouse wall," which hints at the paper plan they already trust. The direction: say in the intro or the feature lines what the club stops having to do, compared with its current method. That should be the maker's real comparison, not one I've made up.

**Smaller things.**
- Intro paragraph: "glance." sits alone on the last line. Rewrap or edit the copy slightly so the last line holds more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- "Rack fees each spring": "with a card link" makes the reader rebuild the meaning. Restore the missing verb, for example "with a link to pay by card," if that's what it means.
- The heading "Club boats and private boats" sits over text that uses a straight apostrophe in "members' own." Use a curly one. It's minor at this size.
- The "Map your boathouse" button commits the visitor to something, but nothing near it says what that costs or involves (free? account needed?). Pricing is only in the nav. Consider a short line under the button saying what happens next, using facts only you have.

**What's working.**
- The headline is concrete, says what you get in the club's own terms, and is clearly the biggest thing on the page. Keep it.
- There's one clear primary button with a secondary underlined link beside it, and the link ("See a sample boathouse") lets a doubtful visitor judge the product before committing. Keep that pairing.
- The type is quiet and disciplined: one family, about four sizes with clear jobs, a readable line length in the intro, and "Product" darkened in the nav to show where you are.
