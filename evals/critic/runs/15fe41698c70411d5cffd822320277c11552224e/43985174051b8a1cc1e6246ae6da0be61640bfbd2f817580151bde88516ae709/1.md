**First read.** The eye lands on "Quote a bathroom from the doorway," then drops to the brown button, then moves right to the quote. It reads as quoting software for tradespeople, specifically tilers. It feels calm, warm, and unhurried, with no stock photos or gradients. The headline does its job in about two seconds.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a marketing site meant to get working tilers to sign up and write a quote, viewed on desktop.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, restrained page. It is clear what it is and who it's for, and it asks for one specific thing. The weak part is the product picture. The quote that should prove the promise floats on the page background with no edges. And the one line that proves the speed claim, "Sent 14:12, opened 14:30," is the quietest text on the screen.

**What would move it most**
1. **The quote doesn't read as an object (defect).** The quote on the right sits on the same cream as the page, with no fill, border, or shadow. Three gray window dots imply a window frame that isn't drawn, so they decorate nothing. Because there's no edge:
   - The card's thin rules read as more of the page's own dividers, like the one above the feature row.
   - The header rule runs wider than the table rows by about 20 px on each side. With a frame that would be padding; without one it looks like a misalignment.

   The viewer has to work out that this is a document the customer receives rather than part of the page layout. That weakens the "customer accepts it from the link" promise, which depends on picturing the quote as a thing that gets sent. Give it a surface: a white sheet, a phone frame, or at least a fill and an edge. Then either make the dots part of a real frame or drop them. This is common sense; no approved source covers it.
2. **The proof of speed is the faintest line on the page, and its words leave a gap (defect, copy).** The headline and lead promise speed: "Send it before you leave the house." The only evidence is "Sent 14:12, opened 14:30" under the total, in small light gray type. It also doesn't say who opened it. A stranger has to work out that it was the customer, and that 18 minutes is the point. Put the subject back ("Mrs Okafor opened it 14:30") and give the line enough weight to be seen after the total. If the product records acceptance, that step is the payoff the lead promises. The card stops one step short of it, and only you know whether showing it is honest.

**Smaller things.**
- Grid: the hero is split near halves (the quote's left edge is at about x 652, close to the page center at 640). The feature row is split in thirds (80 / 469 / 858), so the quote lines up with nothing below it. Put both on one grid, for example by starting the quote at the third column's line, or give it a deliberate offset.
- Column headers: "Line" is a software term (a tiler would say "Item"), and "Amount" usually means money on a quote, right next to "Price." "Quantity" removes the double reading.
- The small gray labels ("Quote 118…", column headers, the sent line) look close to the 4.5:1 contrast minimum at about 13 px. Measure them, and darken any that fall short (WCAG 1.4.3).

**What's working.**
- **The copy speaks a tiler's language.** "From the doorway," "you do not run short on day two," and "You get a text when they do" each end on what the viewer gains. The button names one exact action ("Write your first quote"), and "See a sample quote" gives a lower-commitment second option, styled lower as it should be.
- **The sample quote is real.** It has believable sizes, units in every cell, right-aligned figures, and a total that actually adds up ($248 + $406 + $117 + $960 = $1,731). Keep that care: a tradesperson will check the math.
- **Hierarchy and color are disciplined.** One dark headline, one brown accent used only on the primary button, warm neutrals everywhere else, and a three-column feature row with short headings that sit tight to their text.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the nav, button, and link, and how the two-column hero collapses on a phone. That matters here, because tilers will likely first see this on site, on a phone.
