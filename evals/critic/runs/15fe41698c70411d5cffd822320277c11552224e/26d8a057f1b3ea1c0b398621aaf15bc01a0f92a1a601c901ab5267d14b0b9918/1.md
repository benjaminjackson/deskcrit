**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the hero of a marketing homepage. Its job is to get a studio owner or tutor to click "Add your studio" or look at the sample.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the headline sets the product against the paper list the viewer already uses, and the product picture backs up the claim. The biggest problem is in that picture: the color code reads backwards, and nothing on the map explains it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The easel map's colors read the wrong way round, and there's no key.** In the card on the right, 15 tiles are sage green and three (A3, B6, C5) are white with a faint dashed border. The header says "18 easels, 3 free", so green must mean taken. But most people read green as "available", so at first glance a visitor sees a studio with plenty of open easels, which is the opposite of what the card says. The only way to get it right is to count the white tiles and match them to "3 free", and that's work a hero image shouldn't ask for. This is a convention break (Apple HIG and Material Design both reserve green for success or available), and it costs something here because the map is your proof. Two things to fix. Make taken easels a neutral, muted fill so the free ones stand out as the thing to find. And give "free" a second cue besides fill color, either a small label or a heavier outline (WCAG 1.4.1). The dashed border now looks too faint to carry that by itself. Defect.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline's last line is the single word "list", and the break splits "paper list" across two lines. Bringhurst says a short word shouldn't sit alone on a last line. The phrase split is common sense rather than a sourced rule. Rebreak so the headline ends on "without a paper list", for example by narrowing or widening the text column a little.
- Copy: the hero paragraph describes the product in the third person ("Students book… the tutor sees…") and never says what it means for the person reading. If the reader is the tutor, a line that tells them directly what they no longer have to do would land harder. That's an edit of the existing words, not new content.
- The badge "Easelroom now books model sessions too" is the first text in reading order, but "now… too" assumes the viewer already knows the product. A first-time visitor reads an update before they know what it's an update to. Consider putting it after the headline or making it quieter.

**What's working.**
- The headline names the thing the viewer uses today (a paper list) and says the product replaces it. The viewer judges the offer against their own routine, not against a claim they have to take on trust. Keep that framing.
- One purple used with one meaning: the primary button and the selected easel (B4) share it, and the B4 detail row below names the same easel. Primary versus secondary is clear, too: a solid button next to an underlined text link.
- The layout is calm and holds to a grid: the hero splits in halves, the feature row in thirds on the same column lines. The three feature headings each name a concrete studio task ("A register for the tutor"), not a generic benefit.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the nav, button, and link. How the two-column hero stacks on a narrow screen, especially whether the easel map stays readable.
