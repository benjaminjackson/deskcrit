**First read.** The eye goes first to the row of four big numbers at the top, then splits between two blues: the dot in the middle of the map and the Friday bar. Only after that does it find "0 bikes" in the map callout, and last the restock table at the bottom. This reads as an ops dashboard for a city bike-share. It looks calm, tidy and competent, but also generic: it reports on the fleet without telling anyone what to do about it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home screen for a fleet or operations manager. They use it to check the system's state today and decide where the vans should go next.

**Verdict.** It's clean and well built, but it's set up to be looked at rather than acted on. The one thing that needs a decision, empty and nearly empty stations, is spread across a map that doesn't connect its labels to its dots and a passive table at the bottom. Meanwhile four status numbers of equal weight hold the top of the page.

**What would move it most**

1. **The urgent task is last and has no action.** "Stations to restock" is the only part of the page that asks the manager to do something. It sits at the bottom, below the chart, as a plain table with no way to act on it. Every row looks the same, though Canal Basin has 0 bikes and has been empty for 40 minutes. At the top, four KPI tiles of the same size and weight compete to be seen first, and none of them leads (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance through differences in size). The viewer has to read the whole page to find the one thing that needs doing now. Direction: make the restock problem the first thing seen. Give it a higher position, more weight, or a count in the header, and give each row the next step the manager would take. Rank the rows by urgency so the empty station stands out from the ones that are merely low. *Defect (hierarchy); the missing action is common sense.*

2. **The map callouts aren't attached to anything.** Neither callout has a pointer. "Canal Basin · 0 bikes" floats in the top-left corner, far from the only blue dot, which sits near the center. Meanwhile "Van route 2" sits right beside that blue dot, so the eye pairs the van with the empty station. The van itself has no marker on the map, and the viewer can't tell whether it's at the station, near it, or somewhere else entirely. The map is meant to answer "where is the problem, and how far away is help?", and right now it answers neither. Direction: anchor each callout to its point with a leader line or by placing it next to the point, and give the van its own marker. *Defect (accidental grouping; Arnheim, Art and Visual Perception).*

3. **The map and the table disagree.** The table lists three stations to restock, but the map marks only Canal Basin. Hartley Park gate (2 bikes) and Market Cross (3 bikes) look the same as every healthy gray dock. The only thing marking a problem station is the color blue, which fails anyone who can't tell the blue dot from the gray ones (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1). Direction: mark every station the table lists, with a second cue besides color, such as a label, a different shape, or a bike count on the dot. Make the low stations visibly different from the empty one. *Defect.*

4. **"Last Monday" is the wrong comparison, or at least an unclear one.** The header says today is 24 November 2026, which is a Tuesday. Every KPI comparison, though, reads "last Monday." The manager can read that two ways. If it means yesterday, then part of today is being compared with a full day, and 3,184 against 2,960 doesn't mean what it seems to. If it means the same weekday last week, then the label is just wrong. Either way the manager can't tell whether today is ahead or behind, and that's the only reason the comparison is there (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a headline number needs a comparison the reader can trust). Direction: compare like with like, the same weekday at the same time of day, and label it in words the manager can't misread. *Defect (copy on figures people act on).*

5. **Blue means two opposite things.** On the map, blue means "Empty," which is an alarm. On the chart, blue means "Friday, the best day," which is good news. Because it's the only accent color, the viewer learns one meaning in one panel and misreads the other. Direction: give the alarm state its own color, one that reads as a warning, and keep blue for neutral emphasis. Or the other way around, but don't use one color for both. *Convention (Apple HIG and Material: status colors carry one meaning).*

**Smaller things.**
- "Rides by weekday" doesn't say which week it covers or whether it's an average. Name the period in the title or a caption.
- In the weekday chart, only Friday has a value (3,410), so the other six bars can only be compared roughly. Label each bar, or add a light axis.
- The chart title names a topic rather than a finding. Saying what the viewer should take from it would earn the panel its space. This is taste; no approved source states it.
- In the sidebar, Rides uses a cart or basket icon and Repairs uses a document icon. Neither suggests what the section is. Use icons that match the labels, such as a bike for Rides and a wrench for Repairs.

**What's working.**
- Every KPI comes with a comparison underneath ("of 600 in the fleet," "41 last Monday"). That's the right habit; it just needs the right comparison period.
- The table is set well. Numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures, the dividers are light, and the headers sit quietly. Keep that setting when you give it more importance.
- The page sits on one clear grid with consistent card padding and a restrained gray palette, so whatever you raise in priority will stand out without a fight.
