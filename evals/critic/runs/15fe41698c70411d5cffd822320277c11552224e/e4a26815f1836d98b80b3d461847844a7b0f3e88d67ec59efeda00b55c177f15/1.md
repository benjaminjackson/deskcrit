**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of the home page for Pantrylog. The job is to get food bank coordinators to sign up with "Set up your pantry."

**Verdict.** This is a strong page. The headline, the product preview and the main ask all name one specific job in the audience's own words. The biggest thing in the way is the sample table: the body copy promises that "the coordinator sees what will run short this week," but the table doesn't show that clearly. The one urgent item sits in the middle of an unsorted list, marked only by red.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sample table doesn't prove the main claim.** In the panel on the right, the "Lasts" column reads 3 weeks, 2 weeks, 4 days, 2 weeks, 9 days. The item running out is third of five. Breakfast cereal, at 9 days, isn't marked at all. The only thing that sets baby formula apart is the red text. The panel's title, "Harbour Road pantry," gives the place but not what to look at. A viewer has to scan the column and work out the urgency themselves. Anyone who can't tell red from black can't see the warning at all.
   - **What's wrong:** Rows should follow a meaningful order: Few, *Show Me the Numbers*; Wilke. Color alone shouldn't carry meaning: WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1. Both are conventions that cost something here. The missing one-line summary of what the table shows is my judgment, not a published rule.
   - **Fix:** Sort the rows by "Lasts," shortest first. Give the short-stock rows a second cue besides red, such as a label or icon. Let the panel's header say what the coordinator learns from it, using a fact from your own product, so the preview shows the promise instead of leaving the viewer to find it.
   - Defect plus convention.
2. **The ask doesn't compare Pantrylog with what coordinators use now.** "Set up your pantry" is a clear, specific ask, and the headline names a real moment ("before the van arrives"). But nothing sets Pantrylog against the way a food bank counts stock today. Without that, the viewer has to take the value on trust. The page would be stronger if one line, in the hero paragraph or under the button, named the current method this replaces and what it costs. That fact has to come from you.
   - Taste, on the copy.

**Smaller things.**
- **Feature cards:** the full-color emoji (📦🚚🥫) are glossy and detailed, unlike the flat, muted interface around them, so the card row looks pieced together. Draw the three icons in one flat style that matches the UI. Common sense.
- **Grid:** the table panel's left edge (about x 652) doesn't line up with any column of the three cards below (about x 461 and 843). Set the hero split on the same columns as the cards. Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*.
- **Line breaks:** "week." sits alone on the last line of the hero paragraph, and "arrives" alone on line 3 of the headline. Rebalance the breaks so the last lines aren't single words. Bringhurst 2.4.2.

**What's working.**
- **The copy is specific to this audience:** "before the van arrives," "Baby formula," "Pasta, 500 g," "best-before." Nothing here could sit on another product's page. Keep that voice.
- **The hierarchy is clean:** one dominant headline, a body that's easy to read at a good line length, one green primary button with a plain text link as the secondary. Nothing competes with the headline.
- **The table is set well:** numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures, the row rules are light and a pale tint does the enclosing, and the column headers are quiet. It reads like a real tool, not a mockup.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the button, the link and the navigation, and how the two-column hero reflows on a phone.
