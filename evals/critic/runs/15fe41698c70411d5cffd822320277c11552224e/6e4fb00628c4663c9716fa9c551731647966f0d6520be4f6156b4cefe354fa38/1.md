**First read.** My eye goes straight to the rainbow bar chart on the right. It's the only saturated thing on a gray and white page, so it beats the KPI row, the page title, and the main line chart. Then I go back to the KPI strip and read across four equal numbers without knowing which one matters. This reads as the monthly operations dashboard for a farm-box subscription business, used by the farm that runs it. It feels calm, competent, and a bit generic, until the toy-colored bars break the mood.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the operator of a box-delivery business (here Greenacre Farm) checks how November went against October and where to act, such as which weekdays to staff or which extras to push.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, mostly well-built dashboard, but it tells you nothing on its own: the comparisons are there as raw numbers you have to subtract yourself. The weekday chart, the most eye-catching thing on the screen, is also the hardest to read and the easiest to misread.

**What would move it most**

1. **The weekday chart pulls the eye and then can't be read.** *(Defect.)*
   - **Color with no meaning.** The six bars are one series, yet each is a different saturated hue. The colors encode nothing, but they make this panel the first thing seen. Red on Monday and green on Thursday also read as "bad day" and "good day" to anyone used to status colors.
   - **No scale.** There's no value axis, and only Tuesday has a number (1,140). The other five days can only be guessed against an unmarked height.
   - **Pill-shaped tops.** The rounded ends hide where each bar actually ends, which matters most when there's no axis.
   - **Totals may be skewed.** If these are totals, November 2026 has five Mondays and four of most other weekdays, so Monday's bar is about 25% taller from the calendar alone. The title doesn't say whether this is a total or an average per day.
   - **Fix:**
     - Draw all bars in one color, and keep a second color only for the one day the reader must notice (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*).
     - Square off the tops.
     - Label every bar's value, or add an axis.
     - Say "average per Monday" or "total", whichever is true.
     - Once it's gray, it stops competing with the KPIs, which fixes the eye path as well.

2. **Every comparison makes the viewer do the subtraction.** *(Defect, copy.)*
   - Each KPI card carries "4,512 in October", "$93,880 in October", "236 in October". The context is there, but it stops at the fact. To learn whether November was better, the viewer has to subtract in their head, four times.
   - The direction isn't even good in the same way: boxes going up is good, while paused subscriptions going down (236 → 212) is also good.
   - With all four cards at the same size and weight, nothing tells the viewer which number is the story (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance by differences of size).
   - **Fix:** turn each comparison line into a change the viewer can read at once (direction and amount, marked good or bad), using the numbers already there. Then decide which one or two figures this business watches most and let them lead.

3. **The line chart's title and data don't agree.** *(Defect.)*
   - "Boxes packed per day" plots about 11 points across 30 days, roughly one every three days. So it isn't per day, or it's sampled without saying so.
   - It never dips, while the chart beside it shows no Sunday deliveries. A viewer comparing the two can't tell whether packing really runs seven days a week or the line is smoothed.
   - The y-axis has no unit, and the page is called "Deliveries" while this chart counts boxes packed. Packed and delivered are different numbers, and the viewer can't tell which one they're looking at.
   - **Fix:** plot every day, or retitle the chart to match what it shows. Also say in the title what the chart shows, for example that November ran above October throughout (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, on clear labeling).

**Smaller things**
- **Type sizes:** the page title "Deliveries" and the KPI numbers look about the same size (about 24 vs 26 px), so they read as one level by accident. Make the gap between them clearly bigger or smaller.
- **Chart baselines:** the weekday chart's baseline and day labels sit about 10 px above the line chart's baseline and date labels in the panel beside it. It looks like a near-miss. Align them.
- **Line chart legend:** the November/October legend sits top right, away from the lines. Label the two lines at their right ends instead (Tufte).
- **Style:** the pill bars in six hues are the only playful element in a restrained interface, and they look borrowed from another product (taste). This goes away with fix 1.

**What's working**
- **The line chart's color plan:** October in gray behind November in blue is the right call. It gives context without competition, and both lines can be followed easily.
- **The extras table:** numbers are right-aligned in their own columns, rules are light, text columns are left-aligned, and the column names say what each holds. It reads at a glance. Keep it.
- **The overall structure:** clear left nav with the current page marked, one consistent card style, a calm neutral palette, and comfortable margins. Nothing is crowded and nothing is boxed twice.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- **The date range:** I can't tell whether "1 to 30 November 2026" can be clicked to change the period. On a monthly dashboard it should be.
- **Partial month:** if this was captured before month end, check that the chart doesn't show the remaining days as if they were actual data.
