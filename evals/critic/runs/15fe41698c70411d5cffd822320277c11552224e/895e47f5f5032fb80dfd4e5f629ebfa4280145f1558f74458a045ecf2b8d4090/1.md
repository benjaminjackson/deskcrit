**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Notes from every call, written before you hang up", then goes right to the gray panel and reads the sample notes: a decision with a date, then three people, three tasks, three days. Within five seconds I know this is an AI note-taker for video calls, aimed at small teams. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, close to a template, but the specific copy and the realistic sample keep it from feeling generic.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a meeting-notes product. It needs to convince a team lead that the notes are good and that the tool is safe to let into calls, and then get them to connect it.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The promise is clear, and the panel proves it instead of just claiming it. The one weak point is the button that has to convert: "Add it to your calendar" is vague about what it commits the viewer to.

**What would move it most**
1. **The main button doesn't say what happens when you press it.** "Add it to your calendar" can be read two ways. One reading is "connect Minutehand to my calendar so it joins my calls". The other is "add some event to my calendar". Even with the first reading, the viewer is being asked to give a bot access to their calendar and calls, and nothing near the button says which call tools it works with, whether it costs anything, or whether it starts joining calls straight away. Those are exactly the things someone weighs before they click, so many will go to Pricing or Security to find out, and some won't come back. **Defect** (the link's purpose can't be told from its text; this is the WCAG 2.4.4 idea applied to a commit button). Fixes:
   - Make the verb name the action, for example "Connect your calendar".
   - Put one short line under the button that answers "which tools?" and "what does it cost to start?". Only you have those facts.
   
   "Read a sample set of notes" is the right kind of second ask, because it lets viewers judge the output for themselves. The strongest version would let them compare against something they already trust, such as the notes from their own last call. Say that if the product can do it.

2. **"Nothing kept you did not ask for" is the hardest line on the page to parse, and it carries the privacy promise.** For a bot that sits in your calls, privacy is the deciding worry for many buyers. Yet this heading has no subject and a backwards clause, so it takes two reads. The body below it is clear ("The recording is deleted when the notes are sent…"). The heading should say the same thing plainly, as who does what: for example, restore the verb so it reads "We delete the recording once the notes are sent." **Defect** (slows the viewer at the point where trust is decided).

**Smaller things.**
- Feature 2 ends with "who." alone on its last line, and feature 1 nearly does the same with "and knows." Rewrap or adjust the measure (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The headline breaks "before you / hang up", which splits "you hang up" across lines. Break it as "written before / you hang up" so each line is a whole phrase. This is taste.
- The panel's bottom edge (about y=480) and the button's bottom edge (about y=488) almost line up but miss. Align them or separate them clearly (Arnheim on ambiguous placement).

**What's working.**
- **The sample panel is the argument.** "Goes to print on 14 March, not 7 March" and the Ruth/Dev/Ana tasks with days show exactly what the product does, in a world the viewer recognizes. Headline and visual make one idea. Keep the sample this concrete.
- **The hierarchy is clean.** One headline clearly dominates, there's one filled primary button with the secondary ask as a plain link, and the feature headings differ from their body text by weight as well as size. The eye never has to hunt.
- **The copy speaks to the viewer.** "You correct a line in one click if it heard something wrong" and "someone who missed the call reads two lines and knows" each end on what the fact means for the person reading. That is rare. Keep it as you revise.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the button and links.
- How the two-column hero reflows on a phone, especially whether the sample panel stays near the headline or drops below the fold.
