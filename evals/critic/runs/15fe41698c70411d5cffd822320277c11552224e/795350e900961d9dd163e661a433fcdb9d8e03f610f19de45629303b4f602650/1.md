**First read.** My eye goes to the headline "Book every kennel run without a phone call", then across to the one brown cell, L4, in the run plan, then down to the "Set up your kennels" button. By the second look it's clear this is booking software for a boarding kennel. It feels calm, earthy and practical, like a well-run yard rather than a tech startup.

**What it's for.** Nothing came with the piece, so I'm assuming this is the home page of a SaaS product, and its job is to get boarding-kennel owners and managers to sign up or open the sample run plan.

**Verdict.** It does its job, and it's a strong page. The biggest thing in the way is the headline. It doesn't say who does the booking, so the operator reading it has to work out that "book" means "your customers book" before the line becomes their benefit.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline doesn't say who books.** "Book every kennel run without a phone call" has no subject. A stranger reads it as an instruction to a dog owner: you book a run. The button underneath, "Set up your kennels", is aimed at the kennel operator. So the line meant to sell to the buyer is written from their customer's side. It only turns into the operator's benefit ("no more phone calls to answer") once they've read the paragraph, where "Owners book a run for the dates they are away" finally says who does what. The comparison "without a phone call" is the best idea on the page. It sets the product against something the operator already knows and dislikes, so the reader can judge the value on their own terms. Right now that comparison lands on the wrong person. The fix direction is to make it clear in the headline that owners book, so the line is about the operator's saved phone time. Bringing the subject up from the paragraph would do it, for example "Owners book every run without a phone call". This is a copy defect: it costs the buyer a second read on the line that has to win them.

**Smaller things.**
- The hero paragraph ends with "noon." alone on its last line. Rewrap it or adjust the measure so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- In the detail strip of the run plan, "Biscuit, 3 to 10 August" only works once you guess that Biscuit is the dog. Something like "Biscuit (dog), …" or "Biscuit's stay, 3 to 10 August" closes the gap. Common sense.
- In "Vaccinations checked at booking", the body says the owner uploads the card but never says who checks it or what happens if it's missing. The heading's promise is "checked", and the text stops before it. End the line on what the operator no longer has to do.
- "A walk rota for the day": "rota" is British while the price is in dollars. If the audience is American, "schedule" is their word. If it's British, keep "rota" and check the currency instead (Nielsen heuristic 2).
- "Feeding notes on the run" also reads as the idiom "on the run". It's a small snag, and "on each run card" would avoid it. Taste.

**What's working.**
- **The run plan is the right hero image.** It shows the product doing its one job: K–M rows, the exercise yard, "18 runs, 3 free", and one booked stay with its price. Free runs are marked three ways (white fill, dashed edge, and the count in the corner), so the state doesn't rely on color alone. Keep it a real plan with real-looking data, not an abstract illustration.
- **One accent color does all the pointing.** The same brown appears on the logo, the selected run L4 and the main button, and nowhere else. That's why the eye goes headline → L4 → button without being told. Don't add a second accent.
- **The hierarchy and spacing are disciplined.** There's one large headline, a readable paragraph about 60 characters wide, a clear primary button with an underlined secondary link, and three feature columns on an even three-way split. Each feature heading sits tight to its own text.
