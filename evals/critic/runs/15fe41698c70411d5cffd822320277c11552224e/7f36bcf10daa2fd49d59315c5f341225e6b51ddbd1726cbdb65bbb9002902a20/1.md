**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, then crosses to the field map on the right and stops on the one dark-green pitch, M4. Within a couple of seconds it reads as booking software for small campsite owners, the farm-field kind rather than the holiday-park kind. It feels calm, practical, and rural, and the soft green and yellow glows suggest outdoors.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a landing-page hero meant to get small campsite owners to sign up, by showing that campers can pick and pay for a pitch online while the owner gets one clear view of arrivals.

**Verdict.** This is a good, disciplined hero that does its job. The headline is specific, the mock shows the real product, and there is one clear primary action. The biggest problem is that the field map uses green the wrong way round. That's fixable, but the map is the main proof on the page, so the mistake costs a second read right where the page should be most convincing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The map's colors mean the opposite of what owners expect.** In the panel on the right, 15 pitches are filled sage green and 3 (L3, M6, H5) are white with a faint dashed edge. Most people read green as "available," so the first impression is a mostly open field. Then the header says "18 pitches, 3 free," and the viewer has to work out that green means booked and white means free. There is no legend, and the dashed borders that mark the free pitches look too faint to carry the meaning by themselves. This is a **convention** break (green means success or go: Apple HIG, Material Design) that costs something here. It turns into a **defect** in the product itself, where an owner at the gate needs to see free pitches instantly. Fix: make free pitches the ones that stand out, or move booked pitches to a neutral fill and keep green for the selected or available state. Then add a small two-item key ("booked / free") or label the free pitches directly. Check that whatever marks a free pitch has 3:1 contrast against the panel (WCAG 1.4.11).
2. **The pitch never compares the product with what the owner uses today.** The copy describes the product well ("keeps your field map, arrivals, and deposits together"), but it never sets that against what the viewer already has, such as the phone, the paper diary, or the spreadsheet. That leaves them to take the benefit on trust. The headline gets close with "while you are out on the field," which implies that today a booking means stopping work. But nothing on the page says what changes for them or shows a booking reaching them out in the field, because the mock is a desktop panel. This is **taste**, and it's the main way to make the page persuade rather than just describe. Direction: name, in the owner's terms, the current way of doing things that this replaces and what they no longer have to do. That fact has to come from you, since you know your audience. "See a sample field map" is a good second ask because it lets them judge the product for themselves. Keep it.

**Smaller things.**
- "Deposits that come back" (middle feature heading) reads from the camper's side. To an owner it can sound like money leaving them. The text underneath is about refunding in one step, so make the heading say what the owner does: refunding is easy.
- "Out on the field" sounds slightly like sport. "Out in the field" is the usual phrase, although "on" may be a deliberate pun on campsite fields. Your call, but check it reads the way you mean.
- "Cancels in good time" (middle feature text) is vague on the one thing owners will hold you to: who sets the cutoff. If it's the owner's own policy, say so.

**What's working.**
- **The headline and the mock tell one story.** The headline promises bookings coming in, and the panel shows one that did: "Pitch M4, hardstanding / Arriving Friday, two nights / $20 deposit paid." The details are concrete and believable, like a real farm with a track to the wash block, so it doesn't look like a template. Keep that level of real-world detail.
- **The hierarchy is clean.** There's one dominant headline, a clear step down to the body text, one filled primary button with a plain underlined secondary link, and a quiet row of three features under a hairline. Nothing competes.
- **The feature copy lands on the viewer.** "Print tonight's field map for whoever is on the gate after dark" names a real situation the owner will recognize. That's the voice the whole page should have.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states for the button and links, and how the two-column hero stacks on a phone. That matters here because owners "out on the field" will probably first see the page on a phone.
