**First read.** The eye goes first to the row of four big numbers, then to the blue line and the blue Saturday bar. In two seconds it reads as a small café's monthly sales dashboard: calm, tidy and trustworthy. That sense of trust is the problem, because the numbers don't agree with each other.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an owner or manager of a single bakery-café checks how this month went against last month and what is selling.

**Verdict.** The layout and styling are competent and close to finished. But the dashboard reports three different ticket totals for the same month, so an owner who reads closely can't trust any of it. Fix the data first. Everything else is polish.

**What would move it most**

1. **The ticket numbers contradict each other.** *Defect.*
   - The Tickets card says 1,032 for November.
   - "Daily tickets" plots about 400 to 700 tickets a day, which would be well over 10,000 for the month.
   - The "Tickets by weekday" bars, scaled from the labeled Saturday bar of 148, add up to roughly 700.

   A viewer who compares any two of these has no way to know which one is real, and on a dashboard that loses trust in every number on the page. Whether this is mock data or a real query bug, the three must come from the same source and add up. Separately, Sales ÷ Tickets matches the $9 average, and in the table units × price checks out. So the cards and table hang together, and the charts are the suspect.

2. **"Daily tickets" isn't daily, and its date axis is uneven.** *Defect.*
   - The line has about 12 points across 1 to 30 November, so each point is some 2–3 day bucket, while the title promises one point per day.
   - The x ticks sit at 1, 10, 19 and 30 Nov: gaps of 9, 9 and 11 days at equal-looking spacing.
   - The October line is drawn on a November date axis even though October has 31 days.

   The viewer will read a weekly rhythm or a trend that the bucketing may have made up or hidden. Either plot 30 daily points or retitle the chart for the interval it really uses, and put the ticks at even intervals (Cairo; Tufte, on honest, even scales). Label the comparison line as "same day, October" or by day of month, so it's clear what is being matched.

3. **The weekday chart doesn't say what a bar is.** *Defect.* Under "Tickets by weekday", Saturday's 148 could be the total for the month's Saturdays or the average per Saturday. November had five Saturdays and Sundays but four of the other weekdays, so a total makes the weekend look stronger than it is. Say "per day, average" or "total" in the title or the label (Tufte; Few, on labeling what a number measures). Fixing this also helps with finding 1.

**Smaller things.**
- **Average ticket card:** "$9" next to "$9 in October" reads as "no change" when the store's own figures give about $9.17 against $8.93. Show cents here, since it's the one card where rounding hides the movement.
- **KPI cards:** the comparisons are raw October figures, so the viewer has to do the subtraction. A small change marker (up or down, as an amount or a percent) next to each would make them readable at a glance. This is a suggestion, not a defect.
- **Daily tickets legend:** it sits top right, away from the lines. With two series, labeling "November" and "October" at the right ends of the lines would remove the look-up (Tufte; Wilke).
- **Chart titles:** they name the topic, not the finding. "Saturday is the busiest day" or a note on November running ahead of October would tell the owner what to take away. This is my taste, not a published rule.

**What's working.**
- **Blue only for what matters.** November and the Saturday bar are blue, October and the other days are gray. The current period reads first without a legend hunt. Keep that discipline.
- **The best-selling table.** Figures are right-aligned and tabular, rules are hairline, and there are only four columns. It's quick to scan, and Sold and Revenue disagreeing on order (the flat white earns more than the bun) shows up on its own.
- **One grid throughout.** One clear type scale (page title, card numbers, section heads, body, small labels) and one consistent grid of cards with even gutters. Nothing fights for attention apart from the four KPIs, which is right for a peer row.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the "1 to 30 November 2026" date range can be changed. If it's a control, it doesn't look like one.
- Hover values on the charts. Without them the 148 is the only exact chart figure.
