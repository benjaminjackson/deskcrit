I'm done checking. Final critique:

**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Hand out parking bays without the arguments," drops to the dark "Map your car park" button, then to the bay grid. There the navy Q4 tile is the one strong spot of color. Within about three seconds it reads as software for whoever runs an apartment or housing estate's car park. It feels calm, plain and competent, without the usual startup gloss.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the top of a marketing landing page. Its job is to get estate or building managers to start mapping their own car park in Bayline.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The purpose is clear in one read, there's one primary action, and the product demo sits right under the promise. Two things get in the way, and both are in the demo. Its colors say the opposite of what they mean, and its one price can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **In the bay map, green means "taken."** Fourteen of the 18 bays are sage green, and the three free bays (P3, Q6, R5) are white with a dashed border. Almost everyone reads green as available, so the first glance says the car park is mostly free. Then "18 bays, 3 free" in the corner contradicts it, and the viewer has to work the code out backwards from the count. There's no legend. On a page whose promise is "the estate office sees which bays are free," the picture of that exact thing should read correctly on its own. *Convention* (Apple HIG and Material: status colors are not reversed). Fix: make taken bays a neutral or muted tone, let free bays carry the color that means open, and add a small key (taken / free / selected) next to "18 bays, 3 free."
2. **"$15 a month" can be read two ways.** It sits at the bottom right of the demo, next to "Bay Q4, near the lift / Flat 7, permit until April." A stranger can't tell whether it's what Bayline costs or what the resident of Flat 7 pays for the bay. "Pricing" in the nav makes the first reading more likely. If the viewer takes it as Bayline's price, they leave with the wrong price in mind, or they think the product charges per bay. A price is what people remember and hold you to, so this is a *defect*. Fix: say who pays whom in the line itself. The words are already there to reorder, for example by tying it to the permit ("Flat 7, permit until April, $15 a month").
3. **The pitch never sets Bayline against what the manager does today.** The headline lands well on the viewer ("without the arguments"). But the intro paragraph lists what the product holds and stops at the feature. Nothing sets it next to how the reader handles bays now (a spreadsheet, a paper permit list, a phone in the office), so the viewer has to take the value on trust. The ask itself is strong: "Map your car park" names one exact thing to do with something the viewer already has. *Taste*, about the copy. Direction: let the paragraph or the demo show the manager's current way of doing it next to Bayline. Only you know what that current way is.

**Smaller things.**
- The secondary link "Look at a sample car park" offers the very thing that sits just below it. Either make it clear the link goes to a fuller, clickable sample, or let the demo do that job and drop the link.
- The dashed borders and white fill of the free bays look faint against the beige panel (WCAG 1.4.11 asks for 3:1 on marks that carry meaning). This gets solved if free bays get the strong fill in finding 1.

**What's working.**
- There's a single primary action, a solid navy button, with the alternative set as an underlined text link. The order is clear and nothing competes.
- The navy of the selected bay Q4 matches the button. That quietly ties "your bay" to "your action," and the detail row under the grid ("Bay Q4, near the lift") turns the grid from a diagram into a real case. Keep that link between the grid and the detail row.
- The type is restrained: one family and about four clearly separate sizes. The headline is tight and breaks at the phrase. The intro runs about 70 characters a line. Keep it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bay tiles in the demo respond to clicks. Visitors will try them because they look like controls, so check that clicking one either does something or that the tiles don't look pressable.
