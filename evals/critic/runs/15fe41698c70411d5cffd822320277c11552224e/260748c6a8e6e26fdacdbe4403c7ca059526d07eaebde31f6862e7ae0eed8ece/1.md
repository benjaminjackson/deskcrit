**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Fill every market pitch before Saturday", then crosses to the one red cell (F4) in the map card, then drops back to the rust "Plan your next market" button. The page is for people who run street markets, and the product is a booking and layout tool. It feels calm, local and practical. The muted sage and cream look like a real trade, not a generic SaaS page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of the marketing homepage for a booking tool for market managers, meant to get them to start setting up a market.

**Verdict.** It works. The idea is clear, the hierarchy is right, and the copy is specific in a way most landing pages aren't. The biggest problem is the color code in the map. That map is the product's proof, and its colors read backwards.

**What would move it most**
1. **The map's colors mean the opposite of what a glance says.** *Defect, plus a convention.* In the card, the sage-green cells are the taken pitches. The free ones (E3, F6, G5) are white cells with a dashed border so faint it nearly disappears into the cream card. The one red cell, F4, is a paid booking. In almost every booking map people have used, green means available and red means taken or a problem (Apple HIG and Material on status colors). So the quick read is "lots of open pitches, and something's wrong with F4." Nothing on the card says otherwise. There's no legend, and the only hint is "18 pitches, 3 free," which you'd have to count out to check (WCAG 1.4.1: color alone carries the meaning). The free pitches are the problem the headline promises to solve, yet they're the quietest marks on the card, and their edges look well under 3:1 against the cream (WCAG 1.4.11). The fix: make taken pitches the quiet neutral and make free pitches the marked state, with a clear edge. Keep the red for "the pitch shown below" and link it to the detail line with more than a shared label. A one-line legend would also do it.
2. **The body sentence talks about the reader in the third person, and its last clause can be read two ways.** *Defect (copy).* The reader is the market manager. But the sentence says "the market manager stops taking bookings by text," so it describes them instead of speaking to them. It can also be read as "the manager stops taking bookings" (full stop) or "stops taking them by text" (the point). The fix is an edit to the existing words: address the reader with "you" and put "by text" right after "stop" so the benefit can't be misread.

**Smaller things.**
- "Plan your next market" names a real task, but it doesn't say what happens on click (sign up, a free trial, a demo). Say what the click commits the reader to if the maker has that fact.

**What's working.**
- The headline names a specific deadline from the reader's own week ("before Saturday"). The map card shows exactly that problem as real-looking data: a named market, a footway, a bakery, "$32 paid." The image and the headline make one idea together.
- The three feature lines compare the product to what managers already do, such as texts, a cash bag and the council's quarterly list. They judge the value against their current routine instead of taking a claim on trust. Keep that.
- The type is restrained: one sans family, a large headline, then clear steps down. The rust accent is used only for the main action and the selected cell, so nothing competes with the headline.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the map cells or the sample-layout link respond to hover or focus, and how the two-column hero collapses on a phone.
