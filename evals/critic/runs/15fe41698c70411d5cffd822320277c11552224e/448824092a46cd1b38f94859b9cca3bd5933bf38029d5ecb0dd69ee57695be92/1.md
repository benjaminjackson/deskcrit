The walk is done and I've read the relevant reference entries. Almost everything holds up, so I'm writing a short critique now.

**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Share the kiln without double-booked shelves" and then goes to the one brown cell (M4) in the shelf map on the right. From there it drops to the brown "Add your studio kiln" button. In about three seconds it reads as booking software for shared pottery studios. It feels calm, warm and practical, more like a tool than a pitch.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing landing page that gets studio owners or managers of shared ceramics studios to set up their kiln in Kilnshare.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The headline names a real problem in the viewer's own terms, the shelf map shows the product instead of describing it, and there is one clear action. What holds it back is in the words, not the layout. The feature row mostly repeats the hero paragraph, and the ask never sets Kilnshare against whatever the studio uses today.

**What would move it most**
1. **The feature row repeats the hero instead of adding to it** (defect, mild; common sense, close to Nielsen heuristic 8). The hero paragraph already says "what each member owes for the firing" and "the technician loads the kiln from one list". Two of the three features below, "One list for the technician" and "Firing costs split fairly", say the same two things again. Only "Bisque and glaze apart" is new. A viewer who scrolls to learn more gets the same thing again, so the row seems to have nothing new to say. The fix is to make each feature carry something the hero does not, such as the detail behind a claim or a different worry the studio has. You could also cut the hero paragraph down to the core claim and let the row hold the specifics. Which facts to add is your call.
2. **The ask asks the viewer to trust you, not to compare** (copy). "Add your studio kiln" is specific, which is good: a studio owner knows exactly what it means. "See a sample firing" lets them inspect the product, but only on its own terms. The headline suggests the comparison ("without double-booked shelves") but never names what causes double-booking today, like the sign-up sheet, group chat or spreadsheet the studio already uses. Setting the product next to that tool lets the viewer judge the value on terms they already accept. Name that current method somewhere near the ask or the subhead. You know which one your studios actually use.
3. **The shelf map makes the viewer decode its colors** (taste). Green, white with a dashed edge, and brown have no key. "3 free" and the three white cells let the viewer work out that white means free. Green presumably means booked. Brown M4 could mean "yours", "selected" or "paid", and the "$18 paid" line below doesn't settle it. Because M4 is the same brown as the button, it pulls the eye, so its meaning should be obvious. A short label on M4 itself, or a small key next to "18 shelf spaces, 3 free", would fix it.

**Smaller things.**
- "Bisque and glaze apart" has no verb, so it reads as a category, not as something that happens. Bring the verb back, as the body text under it already does ("Book bisque and glaze firings on their own calendars").
- The gray "18 shelf spaces, 3 free" and "Six mugs, cone 6" on the beige card look close to the 4.5:1 limit. Measure them, and darken them if they fall short.

**What's working.**
- The headline and the mock tell one story. The headline names the problem, the shelf map shows it solved, and the specific details ("Friday glaze firing", "Six mugs, cone 6", "$18 paid") could only come from a pottery studio. Keep that level of detail.
- Restraint. One warm accent used only twice (the button and the highlighted space), muted sage and clay neutrals, and one sans-serif family in about four clearly different sizes. The headline dominates without anything shouting.
- The CTA pair is styled right. There is one filled primary button and an underlined text link for the secondary action, so the ranking can't be misread.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the shelf map is interactive (for example, hover on a cell), and how the two-column hero reflows on a phone.
