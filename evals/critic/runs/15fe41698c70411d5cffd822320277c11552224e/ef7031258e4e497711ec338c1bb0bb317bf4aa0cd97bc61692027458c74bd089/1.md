I've finished the checklist and read the reference entries. Writing the critique now.

**First read.** The eye goes to the headline, "Book every kennel run without a phone call". Next it jumps to the one brown square, L4, in the run plan on the right, and then down to the brown "Set up your kennels" button, because it's the same brown. It reads at once as a SaaS landing page for a dog-boarding business. It feels calm and practical, and it looks like it was made for this trade, not taken from a template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing homepage that persuades kennel operators to sign up, with the sample run plan as the fallback ask.

**Verdict.** This works. The layout, the product picture and the restraint are all good, and the copy is concrete in a way most landing pages aren't. The one thing that holds it back is the headline: it talks to the person who books a run, but the page is selling to the person who runs the kennel.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline and paragraph are unclear about who does the booking.** The headline tells the reader to "Book every kennel run", but the button says "Set up your kennels", so the reader is the operator. Operators take bookings; they don't make them. Read cold, the headline has two meanings. It could mean "your customers book online instead of phoning you", which is the intended one. Or it could read as an offer to the dog owner, or as "fill every run". The paragraph adds a second gap: "Owners book a run…" could mean dog owners or kennel owners, and kennel operators often call themselves owners. A reader who picks the wrong meaning in either place has to read twice to work out whose problem is being solved. The comparison itself is strong: "without a phone call" sets the product against a habit the operator already knows and dislikes. Keep it, and make the people in it clear. The fix is an edit, not a rewrite. Say in the headline that the customers do the booking, so the "no phone call" saving lands on the operator. In the paragraph, change "Owners" to "Dog owners". *Defect (copy).*

**Smaller things.**
- **Logo:** the logo is a glossy orange gradient ball. It's the only fake depth, and the only orange, on a page that is otherwise flat brown, sage and beige. It looks like it came from somewhere else. Make it flat in the page's own palette, or give it a form that has something to do with kennels. *Taste.*
- **Run plan colors:** in the run plan, booked runs are green and free runs are white. In booking grids, green usually means "available", so at first glance the 15 green runs can read as open. The "18 runs, 3 free" count and the dashed outlines correct this, but only on a second look. Use a neutral fill for booked runs, or a gray-green that doesn't read as a status color. *Convention (Apple HIG, Material Design: color).*
- **Hero paragraph:** the paragraph ends with "noon." alone on its last line. Rewrap it or edit it slightly so the last line carries more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- **Apostrophes:** the apostrophes in "dog's" (hero paragraph) and "day's" (third feature) look straight. Use curly ones. *Common sense; minor at body size.*

**What's working.**
- **The run plan does the explaining.** It shows the kennel's own layout (rows K, L, M and the exercise yard), the free count, and one paid booking: "Run L4, large dog / Biscuit, 3 to 10 August / $210 paid". An operator recognizes their own building and sees a paid booking that involved no phone call. Keep it as concrete as it is now.
- **There's one accent color with one job.** Brown marks "this one": the primary button and the selected run. Everything else is muted, so the eye has exactly two places to go. There's one primary button and one quiet underlined link, and "Browse a sample run plan" is a specific, low-commitment ask.
- **The copy is specific.** "See who goes out for a walk at noon", "the vaccination card before the stay is confirmed" and "the morning shift" all speak in the operator's own working day. None of it could sit on a competitor's page.
