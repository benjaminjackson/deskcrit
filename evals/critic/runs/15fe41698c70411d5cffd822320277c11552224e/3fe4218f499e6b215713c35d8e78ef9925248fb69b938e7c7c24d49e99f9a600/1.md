**First read.** The eye goes to the headline, "Hand out school lockers before the first bell". It then jumps across to the one navy locker, B4, in the corridor card, and from there drops to the navy button, which shares its color. Within three seconds this reads as admin software for school offices that hand out lockers. It feels calm, practical and unshowy, more like a well-run school office than a startup.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a marketing site for school office staff, meant to get them to try Lockerly on one corridor.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The purpose, the main point and the next step are all clear in a few seconds, and the copy is unusually specific. What holds it back is the hero mockup, which is the piece's main evidence. Its colors and one caption make the viewer stop and work out which lockers are free and what "top row" means.

**What would move it most**
1. **The locker map uses green for the wrong thing.** In the corridor card, the 15 sage-green lockers are the taken ones, and the three free lockers (A3, B6, C5) are white with a faint dashed edge. Most people read green as "available, go". A first look at the card therefore suggests a corridor that is almost all free, the opposite of "18 lockers, 3 free". The viewer has to read that count, then find the three odd boxes, before the picture makes sense. A family picking a locker would run into the same thing. Green is a status color for success or OK (Apple HIG, Color; Material Design color roles), and here it marks the state that is *not* open to choose. Fix: make taken lockers a quiet neutral, such as a gray or the card's beige, a step darker. Then let "free" carry the color or the strongest edge, so the three choices stand out at a glance. *Convention, with a real cost here.*
2. **"Locker B4, top row" contradicts the map.** B4 sits in the middle of three rows, and A is the top row. "Top row" probably means the top tier of a stacked bank of lockers. The map, though, is drawn as a floor plan with "Stairs to the labs" running across it. A stranger can't tell which one they are looking at, and this mockup is the page's proof that Lockerly understands how a school's lockers work. Norman's point about the conceptual model applies (*The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2): the picture should leave the viewer with one correct mental model. Fix: decide what the grid represents, then make the caption agree with it. If tiers matter, show them in the map. Otherwise drop "top row". Only the maker knows which is true. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "bell" sits alone on the third line, which leaves a ragged block and puts the punch word on its own as a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rebreak it so the last line carries two or more words, for example by slightly widening the measure or adjusting the size.
- The sparkle icons on the primary button and next to "Science corridor" add nothing, and they're the one templated, AI-product tell on an otherwise specific page. Remove them. *Taste.*
- The dashed edges of the free lockers look well under 3:1 against the beige card (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure them. If this is how the real product marks a choosable locker, give it a solid edge or fill that holds up.
- "Deposits back in July" is a date a parent or bursar will hold you to. It can read as "Lockerly only refunds in July" or as "refunds happen when your school year ends". The page also mixes "$" with UK/AU school terms (Year 9, enrol, caretaker), so a reader can't tell which school calendar July refers to. State the rule the body copy already hints at, end of the school year, or give the region. Only the maker knows which is right.
- The ask: "Start with one corridor" is a good small, specific first step. Nothing on the page sets Lockerly against what the office uses today (a spreadsheet, a paper key list), though, so the viewer has to take the value on trust. A comparison to their current method would let them judge it for themselves. That fact has to come from the maker.

**What's working.**
- The copy talks in the office's own words: corridor, key list, deposits, caretaker, "a lost key is a quick lookup". Every line says who does what, so nothing reads like it could be on anyone's page. Keep that voice when revising.
- The one navy locker matches the navy button. That color link draws the eye from the headline to the proof to the action without any arrows or extra emphasis.
- The structure is tight: a two-column hero above a three-column feature row on one shared grid, with generous, gathered white space and a small, muted palette. It looks confident and quiet, which suits school staff.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on "Start with one corridor" and "See a sample corridor"; whether the corridor card is interactive.
