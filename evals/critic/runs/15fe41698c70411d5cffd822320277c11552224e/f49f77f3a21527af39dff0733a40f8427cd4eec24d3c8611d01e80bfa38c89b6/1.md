**First read.** My eye lands on the "Overview" title and then on the row of four big numbers: $9,460, 1,032, $9, 36%. Next it jumps to the one dark-blue bar, Saturday, because it is the most saturated thing on the page. Then it drifts to the line chart. This reads as a month-end sales dashboard for the owner of a small bakery or café. It is calm, tidy and familiar, close to a standard admin template, and nothing on the page tells me whether November went well.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the owner's home screen in a café point-of-sale tool. It's meant to show at a glance how the month is going against last month and what to act on.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and well ordered, but the dashboard fails at its main job because its numbers don't agree with each other. An owner who notices that will stop trusting every figure on the screen. After that, the second problem is that it lists figures without saying what they mean.

**What would move it most**

1. **The charts contradict the headline numbers.** *(Defect.)*
   - The Tickets card says 1,032 tickets for the whole month.
   - The "Daily tickets" line runs between about 350 and 700 tickets a day. That adds up to roughly 15,000 for the month, about fifteen times the card.
   - The "Tickets by weekday" bars, read against the labeled 148 on Saturday, add up to about 700, which matches neither.
   - The daily line also has only about 11 points for a 30-day month, so it isn't really daily.

   An owner who checks one number against another, which owners do, now has three answers to "how many tickets did we sell?" They can't tell which one is wrong, so they stop trusting all of them. This is the graphical-integrity problem Tufte describes in *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2, in its worst form: the chart isn't exaggerating the data, it's showing different data.

   Fix: make every chart come from the same figures as the cards, so the daily points add up to the monthly total. If this is placeholder data, fix it before anyone reviews the design with it, because it hides every other judgment. If it's live data, it's a query bug.

2. **The weekday chart and the daily chart don't say what they measure.** *(Convention: Tufte, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*.)*
   - "Tickets by weekday" has no value axis. Only Saturday's bar has a number, so Monday through Sunday can only be compared by eye, and nothing says whether a bar is a monthly total or an average per day.
   - The daily chart's 0–900 axis has no unit, so you can't tell whether it shows tickets per day or a running total.

   This is how finding 1 can sit there unnoticed: nothing on the charts can be checked. Fix:
   - Label every bar with its value, which removes the need for an axis.
   - Make the titles say what is counted, for example "Average tickets per weekday" or "Tickets per day", whichever is true.

3. **The cards make the owner work out the change themselves, and "Average ticket" hides it.** *(Defect.)*
   - Each card gives November and the October figure ("$8,815 in October") but no difference or direction. To learn that sales rose, the owner has to subtract in their head, four times.
   - "Average ticket" reads "$9 / $9 in October", so it looks flat. The page's own figures say otherwise: $9,460 ÷ 1,032 ≈ $9.17 against $8,815 ÷ 987 ≈ $8.93. Rounding to whole dollars removed the only movement that number had.

   Fix:
   - Show the change itself, as an amount or a percentage with up or down, next to each figure.
   - Show the average ticket to the cent. It's a small number where cents are the whole story.

4. **No chart says what it shows.** *(Taste.)*
   - "Daily tickets", "Tickets by weekday" and "Best-selling items" name topics, not findings.
   - The highlighted Saturday bar says "this matters" without saying why.
   - "Best-selling" is ambiguous: the table is sorted by units sold, but Oat flat white earns more than Cardamom bun ($1,710 vs $1,648). An owner deciding what to push could take the wrong item from "best".

   Fix:
   - Give each title the point the chart makes, in words, once the data is right.
   - Change "Best-selling items" to say what ranks them, for example "Most sold items" or "Top items by revenue".

**Smaller things.**
- **Nav icons:** they use five unrelated colors (blue, orange, green, pink, gray) that mean nothing. The warm orange and pink pull the eye toward the page chrome. The Menu icon is a filled slab while the others are thin outlines. Make them one color and one stroke style, and let the active row's tint mark the current page (*Refactoring UI*, "Define your shades").
- **Daily tickets legend:** it sits top right, away from the lines. Put "November" and "October" at the right ends of the two lines instead (Tufte; Wilke).
- **No visible way to change the period:** there's no control to pick another month or another comparison, and on a reporting screen that's the most frequent action (Nielsen 7). If "1 to 30 November 2026" is that control, see below.
- **"Loyalty card visits 36%":** 36% of what? Say "of tickets used a loyalty card", if that's what it means, so the owner doesn't have to guess.

**What's working.**
- **The page order:** totals, then trends, then items, all on one screen with one consistent card style and generous gutters. Keep that structure when revising.
- **Gray October behind blue November in the line chart:** it's the right way to show context against focus. The two also differ in lightness, so the chart still works without color vision.
- **The table:** numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures, rules are light, and item names are plain. It reads quickly and needs nothing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a clickable date picker. If it is, it looks like plain text and needs a visible control cue.
- Whether the charts show values on hover. That would ease finding 2 for mouse users, but not at a glance or on a touch screen.
