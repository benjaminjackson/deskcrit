**First read.** The eye goes to the four big numbers across the top, then to the blue line climbing across the left chart, then to the one blue bar (Friday, 212). This is a hostel's monthly overview for November, made for the owner or manager. It feels calm and orderly, almost like a template, with nothing loud and nothing out of place.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: a hostel manager opens it to see how November went against October, and where the beds and the money came from.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and does its job. What fails it is the numbers. The main chart contradicts the headline figures above it, and the table's "Nights" column can be read two ways. On a screen whose whole job is to be believed, a manager who catches one of these stops trusting the rest.

**What would move it most**
1. **The line chart and the KPI row tell different stories** (defect). The "Beds sold" card says 2,846 beds, up 9% on October. That works out to about 95 beds a night. The blue November line runs from about 53 to 95 and looks like it averages about 75. It also sits about 20 to 30 beds above the gray October line all month, which looks like roughly +30%, not +9%. The viewer either trusts the card or the chart, and the one they distrust drags the other down with it. Tufte's principle that the size of an effect shown must match the size in the data (*Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2) is broken in either case. If this is mock data, fix it before anyone reviews the design. If it's live, check whether the chart and the card measure the same thing (beds sold vs. guests, or a different date window).
2. **"Beds sold per night" isn't per night** (defect). There are about 12 points across a 30-night month. The x ticks at 1, 10, 19 and 30 Nov are also unevenly spaced (9, 9 and 11 days apart), which breaks the even-ticks rule (Tufte). The heading promises one point a night, and the chart delivers something else without saying what. Either plot all 30 nights or rename the heading to say what each point is, then put the ticks at even intervals.
3. **The "Nights" column has no unit** (defect). Skiddaw dorm shows 212 nights in a 30-night month. That's only possible as bed-nights (8 beds × 30 = 240), but Derwent twin's 58 and Catbells' 41 read naturally as room-nights. The table is sorted on this column, so the ranking is only fair if all three rows use the same unit. Name the unit in the header ("Bed nights" or "Room nights"). The 212 also matches the Friday check-in count exactly, so check it isn't a leftover placeholder.

**Smaller things.**
- Check-ins by weekday: there's no value axis, and only Friday has a number, so the viewer can't read Saturday (clearly close) or any other day. Label each bar with its value, which costs very little here (Tufte; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*).
- Chart headings name topics, not findings. "Check-ins by weekday" could say what the blue Friday bar is there to show. This is taste: the highlight already makes the point, and the heading would make it explicit.
- Line chart legend sits top right, away from the lines. Put "November" and "October" at the right-hand ends of the lines (Tufte; Wilke).
- No period switch or filter is visible. "1 to 30 November 2026" looks like plain text, so the manager can't tell how to look at October on its own or a different range (Nielsen 7). Either make it look like a control or add one.

**What's working.**
- Every KPI card has the same four-part structure: label, big number, comparison line. The comparison is always against October and written in words ("Down 3 points on October", "Same as October"). That's exactly the context a headline number needs. Keep it.
- Color restraint. There's one blue for "this period" or "the point", and gray for everything else, so the Friday bar and the November line pick themselves out without a legend fight.
- The table is set properly: numbers right-aligned under right-aligned headers, light row rules, and nothing extra.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date range is a picker. Hover values on the charts, which might answer the bar-chart labeling point.
