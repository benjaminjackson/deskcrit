**First read.** The eye goes first to the three blue bars, then up to the row of four big numbers (11,206 first). In about two seconds it reads as a monthly activity dashboard for a public library, made for a branch or service manager. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also fairly anonymous: change the labels and it could be any SaaS analytics page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a library manager opens this to see in a glance how November went and when the library is busiest, so they can plan staffing and stock.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to scan, and the four headline figures do their job. The three charts, which take the most space, can't be read beyond the single labeled bar in each, and they don't say whether they show totals or daily averages. In a month with uneven weekday counts, that can lead to the wrong staffing conclusion.

**What would move it most**
1. **The weekday charts give one number each and don't say what they measure.** In all three cards, only the blue bar has a value (2,480 / 2,040 / 412). There's no value axis, so every gray bar is a guess. The viewer can't tell whether Friday loans were 1,900 or 2,300, and can't compare Loans with Returns, because each card is drawn to its own unseen scale. The Holds peak of 412 stands almost as tall as the Loans peak of 2,480. The bigger problem is that nothing says whether a bar is the **total** for that weekday or the **average per day**. November 2026 starts on a Sunday, so it has five Sundays and five Mondays and only four of every other weekday. If these are totals, Monday's "peak" in returns (2,040) and the Sunday and Monday bars in the other two charts are inflated by roughly a quarter just by the calendar. A manager would then staff Mondays for a rush that partly comes from the extra day. **Defect** (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2, and Few: the reader should never have to guess what a number measures). Fix: say the measure in the card ("average per day" or "total"), and if it's totals, even out the unequal day counts. Then make the other bars readable, with a light value axis or a small value on each bar.
2. **The blue bar is the only interpretation on the page, and it's never explained.** The blue marks each chart's highest bar, but nothing says so. Is it the peak, today, or a target? The titles ("Loans by weekday") name the topic, not the finding, so the viewer has to work out the point of each chart themselves. Here the point is actually interesting: loans peak on Saturday, returns on Monday, holds on Wednesday. Those three different days are the operational insight, and right now they look like a coincidence of styling. **Taste / common sense:** the cited source for this rule isn't on the approved list. Fix: say in words what the blue marks, or let each title state its finding, built from the data already on the chart.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count about seven sizes. The page title "Overview" and the KPI figures look only a point or two apart, so they read as one level with two jobs. Make the figures clearly larger, or the title clearly smaller (Bringhurst: use a modest set of distinct sizes).
- Sidebar icons: the Holds icon reads as a shopping basket and the Settings icon as a sun or target. Neither is the shape users expect, so the nav leans on its labels. **Taste:** use more conventional symbols (a gear for Settings).
- KPI cards: the October figures are the right comparison, but the viewer has to subtract to see that loans rose about 7%. **Taste:** show the change, with its direction, next to the comparison.

**What's working.**

- The charts are restrained: gray context, one accent, no gridlines or chartjunk, a zero baseline, and weekdays in natural order. The fix is to add information, not to change the style.
- The table uses right-aligned tabular figures and quiet hairline rules, and it fits the page with no scrolling. The whole dashboard fits on one screen.
