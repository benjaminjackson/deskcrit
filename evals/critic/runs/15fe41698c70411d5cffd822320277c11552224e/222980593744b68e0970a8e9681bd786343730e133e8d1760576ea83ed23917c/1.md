I've finished the checklist and the deep reads. Writing up the critique now.

**First read.** The eye lands on "Quote a bathroom from the doorway," drops to the paragraph and the rust button, then crosses to the quote card, where the rows and the $1,731.00 total read as a real job. It reads as a calm, trade-focused software page for tilers and small renovators. It feels warm, quiet and trustworthy, like a well-made tool and not a startup pitch.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the top of a marketing landing page for tile installers, and its job is to get them to write their first quote.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the copy is specific and the sample is believable. What holds it back most is the hero picture: it shows a desktop window, but the story is about quoting on site and the customer accepting on a phone.

**What would move it most**
1. **The picture tells a different story from the headline.** The quote card (right half) sits inside a desktop-style window, with three gray "traffic light" dots in the title bar. The headline says "from the doorway" and the body says "send it before you leave the house" and "the customer accepts it from the link." So the words promise a phone, on site, with the customer saying yes, while the picture shows a desktop window and a status line that stops at "Sent 14:12, opened 14:30." A tiler can't see the claim that would sell them: that this happens standing in someone's house. The window dots also do no work. They're fake chrome with nothing behind them. Direction: frame the quote in the setting the copy describes (on site, or on the customer's phone), drop the dots, and let the card show the outcome the page promises (accepted), if that is a real state of the product. *Taste, argued on its own terms: the picture and the headline should make one idea, and here they pull apart.*
2. **"From the doorway" can be read two ways.** The headline suggests you quote without going into the room, maybe from a photo or scan. The next sentence says Tilewright works from "the room sizes you measure on site." A tiler who takes the first reading expects something the product doesn't do, and the paragraph corrects them a line later. That's a small letdown right at the promise. Direction: make sure the headline means what the product does. If "doorway" means "before you leave the job," the body already says it better ("Send it before you leave the house"). Keep the image, but make the first sentence of the body confirm it, not walk it back. *Defect (copy).*
3. **The ask is specific, but it doesn't set the product against what tilers do now.** "Write your first quote" and "See a sample quote" are good, exact asks. But the page never names the tiler's current way of quoting (writing it up that evening, a spreadsheet, a paper pad). The time saved is only implied by "before you leave the house," and the tiler has to supply the comparison themselves. Direction: name the current habit in the tiler's own terms, using a fact only you have, so the gain is judged against something they already know. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- The row dividers in the quote card look like two colors: peach under the header, Floor tiles and Labour, gray under Wall tiles and Adhesive. Make them one color, unless the peach rules mark something the reader should see. (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind share one style.)

**What's working.**
- **The sample quote is real content, not lorem.** Tile sizes, m², bags, days, a total that adds up ($248 + $406 + $117 + $960 = $1,731), and a sent/opened timestamp. A tiler sees their own job in it. Keep that level of truth in every mock.
- **The hierarchy is clean, with one primary action.** One large headline, one solid rust button, the second ask as an underlined link, and the current nav item (Product) in a heavier weight. Nothing competes.
- **The feature copy lands on the reader.** "So you do not run short on day two" and "You get a text when they do" end on what it means for the tiler, not on how the product works. That's the hardest thing to keep when copy gets trimmed, so protect it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the two-column hero reflows on a phone, which is where this audience will likely first see it, and whether the quote card stays readable there.
