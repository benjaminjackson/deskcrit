**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Give every resident a bike space they can find," then crosses to the rack map, where the one dark-teal rack (S4) pulls it into the card. It reads quickly as a tool that building managers use to assign bike-store spaces. It feels calm, practical and trustworthy, a bit like a utility bill done well. Nothing shouts, and nothing is confusing.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing-page hero for property managers or residents' committees, meant to get them to start mapping their own bike store.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the product picture shows the product, and the main ask is concrete. What's left is mostly copy: one price in the hero can be read two ways, and the intro paragraph lists features without telling the manager what they get out of them.

**What would move it most**
1. **"$6 a month" in the map card can be read two ways (defect).** It sits bottom right of the hero card in the same weight as "Rack S4, cargo bike." One reading is "the resident in Flat 12 pays $6 a month for this rack." The other is "Rackspot costs $6 a month." Prices are what visitors remember and later hold you to. Someone who takes away the second reading will feel misled when they open Pricing, or may decide it's too cheap or too expensive before they get there. The fix is to say who pays whom, using words already in the card: tie the fee to the rack, for example "$6 a month rent for this rack", or whatever the true fact is. Only you know which reading is right.
2. **The intro paragraph describes the product but never says what it means for the person reading (copy).** "Rackspot maps the bike store…, keeps the waiting list, and sends each resident the number of their rack. Residents swap or give up a space themselves." Each clause says what the software does. The last one hints at the real value for a manager, which is that they're out of the loop, but it stops at the fact. End on that consequence, said to "you": what the manager no longer has to handle when residents swap or give up spaces. The page also never compares Rackspot to what the manager does today, whether that's a paper list, a spreadsheet or emails. If you have a real before-and-after, it would let visitors judge the value on their own terms instead of taking the claim on trust. You'll have to supply that comparison yourself.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline ends with "find" alone on its third line, which leaves a lone short word and weakens the end of the line (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Rebalance the breaks, for example by narrowing the measure slightly so the lines read "Give every resident / a bike space / they can find."
- The second feature promises "Racks for cargo bikes… mark the wide racks," but the map draws S4, the cargo-bike rack, the same width as every other rack. The hero picture misses a chance to show that feature at a glance. This one is taste.
- Gray secondary text in the card ("18 racks, 3 free" and "Flat 12, held since May") looks like it passes, but it's small, mid-gray and on a beige tint. Measure it to confirm it reaches 4.5:1.

**What's working.**
- The rack map is a real product picture, not decoration. It uses a believable building name, the "Ramp to the street door" band, free racks shown both white and with dashed borders (so color isn't the only cue), and a selected rack with its details spelled out below. A visitor understands the product before reading the paragraph. Keep that specificity.
- The calls to action are well judged. There's one solid primary button with a specific verb and object ("Map your bike store"), plus a quieter underlined secondary link to a sample. The sample lets doubters judge the product for themselves.
- The palette is restrained: one neutral range, sage green, and a single dark teal shared by the primary button and the selected rack. That tie quietly links "your store" to the action.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the nav, button and link. Whether the map is interactive, and if so whether its racks are usable targets.
