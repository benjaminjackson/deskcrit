**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, then crosses to the rack map, where it stops on the one dark-blue tile, S4, and drops to "Rack S4, cargo bike … $6 a month." It reads as a software landing page for someone who runs an apartment building and wants to stop handling bike-room spaces by hand. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. The product mockup is specific enough that it doesn't look like a template.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a marketing page meant to get building managers to start mapping their bike room in Rackspot.

**Verdict.** The page mostly does its job. The headline is clear, there's one specific ask, and the picture shows the product rather than decorating it. Two things work against it. The rack map's colors read backwards: the green racks look free when they're the taken ones. And the only price in view can easily be mistaken for what Rackspot costs.

**What would move it most**
1. **Green means "taken" on the map, so the map reads backwards at first glance.** In the Larkfield House card, 15 of the 18 racks are filled sage green, and the 3 free ones (R3, S6, T5) are white with a dashed edge so pale it nearly disappears into the beige card. Most people read green as available. So the first impression is "a building full of open spaces," and only the small gray "18 racks, 3 free" sets it straight. The free racks are what the waiting list and the "comes free" feature depend on, but they're the faintest thing in the card. This is a **defect**: status colors shouldn't be reversed (Apple HIG, Color; Material Design color roles). The dashed edges also look well under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for meaningful graphics. **Fix:** fill the taken racks with a neutral gray and give the free racks the one clear, positive treatment. Keep S4 as the highlight.
2. **"$6 a month" can be read two ways.** It's the only price on the screen, set at the right edge of the product card, with "Pricing" in the nav above. One reading is the fee a resident pays the building for rack S4. The other is what Rackspot costs. A manager who takes it as the product price is anchored on a number that may be wrong, and may feel misled later on the pricing page. This is a **defect**, because prices are what viewers remember and hold you to. **Fix:** say who pays whom, with a word or two such as "rent" or "paid by Flat 12," using whichever is true.
3. **British words next to a dollar sign.** "Bike store," "Flat 12" and "Map your bike store" are UK usage, but the price is in "$". To a US reader, a "bike store" is a shop that sells bikes, so the main button can read as "map your retail shop." This is a **defect**: it misses the audience's own words (Nielsen, heuristic 2). It hits the call to action and the headline-level copy. **Fix:** choose one market and make the words and currency match it. For the US, that means "bike room" or "bike storage" and "unit" or "apartment."
4. **The ask doesn't compare the product to what the manager uses now.** "View a sample bike store" lets the viewer judge for themselves, which is good. But nothing sets Rackspot against how they handle this today. The body copy also stops at the product's behavior: "Residents swap or give up a space themselves" never says what that saves the manager. **Fix:** add the missing kind of comparison, such as their current list or the notice on the door. End the body copy on what the manager no longer has to do, said to them ("…so you don't have to"). This is **taste**, but it decides whether a busy manager clicks.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks so "find" sits alone on the third line. Rebreak it or adjust the measure so the last line holds more than one short word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The map doesn't show which racks are the wide cargo racks, even though the middle feature promises them and S4 is labeled "cargo bike." Marking them would let the picture prove that feature.

**What's working.**
- The rack map shows the product doing its job: a named building, rack numbers, the ramp to the street door, and one resident's rack tied to its detail row. That's why the page doesn't look generic. Keep a picture this concrete.
- There's one primary button with a plain underlined secondary link next to it. The hierarchy is clear, and both asks name an exact action.
- The headline dominates through size and weight. The three feature headings sit tight to their text in an even row, and the copy throughout says who gets what ("the next resident on the list is told by email").
