**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Know which rack every boat is on", jumps right to the one navy tile (M4), then drops to "Rack M4, single scull … $95 paid". Within a few seconds it reads as software for rowing clubs to manage boathouse racks and rack fees. The palette of sage, navy and warm paper fits a boat club, and the page feels calm and competent. It is also restrained enough to look like a lot of well-made SaaS landing pages.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the home page that gets a club captain or secretary to start mapping their boathouse in Slipway.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The layout, type and the main button are clean and leave little to fix. The biggest thing in the way is that the rack plan, the page's only picture, doesn't show what the headline promises. It shows which racks are full, but not which boat is on which rack.

**What would move it most**
1. **The rack plan doesn't show boats, so it doesn't prove the headline.** The headline promises "which rack every boat is on". The plan on the right shows 18 tiles labeled U1 to L6 in two colors, and one boat appears only in the detail line under the plan ("single scull", with no boat name or owner). A captain looking at it learns which racks are taken, which a whiteboard already tells them. It doesn't show what they'd actually want this for: seeing the boats themselves on the plan. Two of the three feature columns promise the same missing thing. "Club boats and private boats … on one plan" isn't visible because every taken rack is the same sage green, and "each boat name on its rack" isn't visible because no tile has a name. Fix: put the boat on the tiles. That means the boat's name, using real-looking names the maker supplies, plus a visible difference between club and private boats. Then the picture shows the headline's promise instead of repeating the idea of "a rack plan". *Taste and common sense:* no approved source states this rule. The cost is still real, because the picture is where a skeptical visitor checks the claim.
2. **"Free" can be read two ways, and one is about money.** The hero paragraph says "the yearly rack fees. Members claim a free rack themselves". Right after a mention of fees, "a free rack" can easily read as a rack that costs nothing. A treasurer or member who reads it that way expects something the product doesn't promise. The plan's "18 racks, 3 free" uses the same word. Fix: use a word that can only mean unoccupied, for example "claim an empty rack" and "3 empty". *Defect:* it can mislead on price, which is the kind of line people later hold the maker to.
3. **The asks invite the visitor to judge the product, not to compare it with what they already use.** "Map your boathouse" is a good primary ask: it names one concrete thing to do. "See a sample boathouse" asks the visitor to judge Slipway on its own terms. Nothing on the page sets it next to what a club already uses to track racks and fees, so the visitor has to take the value on trust. Fix: pin the copy to that current method (the maker knows what their clubs use), so the gain shows on terms the visitor already accepts. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- The free racks (U3, M6, L5) have dashed outlines that look far below 3:1 against the off-white card. They show up only because they differ from their green neighbors, yet they are what a member claiming a rack needs to find. Darken the outline in the real product (WCAG 1.4.11).
- The hero paragraph leaves "glance." alone on its last line. Rebreak or adjust the measure so the last line carries more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- "Send every rack holder the yearly fee" says the club sends the fee, when the club is asking for it. Restore the missing noun, for example "send every rack holder a bill for the yearly fee, with a card link".

**What's working.**
- The hero copy is specific. "Know which rack every boat is on" and "Map your boathouse" could only belong to this product, and the visitor knows exactly what clicking does. Keep that level of concreteness through any rewrite.
- The plan reads like a real place. The "Doors to the slipway" band, the U/M/L rows and the "Held until 31 March 2027 · $95 paid" footer let a captain recognize their own boathouse in about a second. Selecting M4 in navy and echoing it in the line below links color and text cleanly.
- The type is disciplined: one family, a large headline, then 18, 15 and 13 px tiers separated by weight and gray rather than extra sizes. The single navy color is used only for the primary button and the selected rack, so the two things that matter share one signal.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav, the button, the "See a sample boathouse" link and the rack tiles. Whether the tiles are interactive in the sample.
