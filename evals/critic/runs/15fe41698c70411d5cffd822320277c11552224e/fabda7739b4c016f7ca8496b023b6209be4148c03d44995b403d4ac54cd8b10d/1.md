**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, crosses to the stock table, and stops on the red "Baby formula · 8 · 4 days" row. Then it drops to the green button. It reads straight away as a tool for whoever runs a food bank. It feels calm, practical and trustworthy, with no hype.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a marketing landing page that gets food-bank coordinators to set up an account, or at least look at a sample.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The promise is clear, the hero table shows the product instead of describing it, and there is one obvious next step. What holds it back is persuasion more than design. The page never sets Pantrylog next to what the coordinator uses today, and the table's best moment, the item about to run out, is marked by color alone.

**What would move it most**
1. **The ask gives the viewer nothing to compare against.** "Set up your pantry" and "See a sample stock list" are specific and well-judged; the sample link lets the viewer see the product before committing, which is better than "Try it." But nothing on the page puts Pantrylog next to what the coordinator already trusts: their current count, whatever they keep it in, or the last time something ran out before the van arrived. So the value has to be taken on trust. The direction: set the sample, or one line of copy, against the viewer's current way of counting, so they can see the difference on their own terms. Only the maker knows what that current way usually is; name it rather than inventing a claim. *(Defect: weaker conversion. Copy judgment, not a published rule.)*
2. **The red row is the payoff, but only color says so.** "Baby formula 8 / 4 days" in red is the whole promise ("sees what will run short this week") shown in one line, and it is the strongest thing on the page. But no word, icon or label says "runs out before the next delivery". A color-blind viewer, or anyone skimming, sees a row that is merely a different color, and "4 days" next to "9 days" doesn't say where the line is. Add a second cue, such as a short status word or a marker in the row, that ties it to the headline's "before the van arrives." This also links the image to the headline more tightly than the color can on its own (WCAG 2.2, SC 1.4.1 Use of Color). *(Convention, with a real cost here because this row carries the pitch.)*
3. **The headline breaks badly.** "Know what is on the / shelves before the van / arrives" splits "the shelves" across a line and leaves "arrives" alone on the last line. This is the largest and first-read element, so the stumble is the first thing the viewer meets. Break it at the phrase, "Know what is on the shelves / before the van arrives." Get there with a slightly wider measure or a slightly smaller size (Bringhurst, 2.4.2, on lone short last-line words). *(Convention.)*

**Smaller things.**
- In the intro paragraph, "week." sits alone on the last line. Rewrap so the last line has a few words.
- The "Lasts" column gives forecasts ("3 weeks", "4 days") but sets them exactly like the counted "On shelf" numbers. Mark them as estimates (a word like "about", or a lighter style) so no one plans an order around a guess as if it were a count (Wilke, ch. 16).
- The card heading "Orders sized to the gap" makes a stranger work out what "the gap" is until they read the line below. Make the heading say it, for example by putting back the idea of "what will run out" that the card's own text already uses. This is copy judgment.

**What's working.**
- The hero shows a real, believable stock list ("Harbour Road pantry", "Counted Tuesday 10:40", tins, pasta, rice). It proves the headline instead of decorating it, and it is far stronger than a generic dashboard picture. Keep it as the main visual.
- The copy is specific and in the audience's own words: "volunteers scan", "the coordinator", "before Friday", "best-before date." It could only belong to this product.
- The hierarchy is restrained: one dark heading, one green primary button, an underlined secondary link, and a muted palette of warm gray and green. That suits a charity audience and keeps the eye on the headline and the table.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the two-column hero reflows on a phone, and whether the table stays readable at narrow widths.
