**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, then goes to the one navy bay (Q4) in the car-park card, then down to the navy "Map your car park" button, which is the same color. Within a few seconds it reads as software for whoever runs residents' parking on a housing estate. It feels calm, orderly and grown-up. Nothing shouts, and that suits a product sold on stopping arguments.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing-page hero for estate offices and property managers, meant to make them see the product and start mapping their own car park.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The headline makes a promise, the card proves it with a real-looking car park, and the button asks for one specific next step. What holds it back is copy, not layout: the one price on the page reads two ways, the currency doesn't match the vocabulary, and the bay map has no key.

**What would move it most**
1. **"$15 a month" can be read as Bayline's price.** It sits at the bottom right of the card, the only price on the page, under a "Pricing" link in the nav. The layout implies it is what the resident in Flat 7 pays for bay Q4. But a manager scanning for cost can just as easily read it as "Bayline costs $15 a month". If that's the wrong reading, they leave with a figure in their head that the pricing page won't match, and they hold you to it. Defect. The fix is an editorial edit that ties the price to the resident and the bay, for example "Flat 7 pays $15 a month" or "Permit: $15 a month". Then it reads as what the product handles, not what it costs.
2. **The currency doesn't match the vocabulary.** "Estate", "flat", "lift", "warden" and "car park" are British, but the price is in dollars. A UK estate manager notices this at once and wonders whether the product is built for them. A US reader finds "estate" and "warden" odd instead. Either way, the viewer the copy is written for gets a reason to doubt it's for them. Defect (common sense; no catalog entry covers it). Pick the market and make the currency and the words agree. I can't tell which market you mean, so that call is yours.
3. **The bay map has no key.** In the card, green bays, white dashed bays and the navy Q4 mean taken, free and selected. The viewer can only work that out by counting three white bays against "18 bays, 3 free" in the top right. Most will get there, but the image is your proof, and it shouldn't need solving. Taste, argued: the card is a demo, and a demo that explains itself sells faster. The fix: label the states in the card header ("3 free" next to a white swatch, for example) rather than adding a separate legend.
4. **The ask doesn't compare with anything the viewer has now.** "Map your car park" is a good, specific ask, much better than "Get started". But nothing sets Bayline next to what the estate office uses today, whether a spreadsheet, a paper permit list or a waiting list by email. So the "arguments" in the headline stay abstract. Taste. The direction: one line under the body copy or the features that names the current way of doing it and what changes. Only you know what your customers actually use, so I won't write it.

**Smaller things.**

**What's working.**
- The product shot is the argument. It's a plausible, specific car park (Ashgrove Court, a one-way lane to the barrier, Q4 near the lift) rather than a vague dashboard, so a manager can see their own site in it. Keep it this concrete.
- The copy is plain and lands on the viewer. "Residents apply for a bay online, and the estate office sees which bays are free" says who does what, and each feature line has a verb and a consequence ("the barrier lets them in"). Protect this when you edit.
- The hierarchy is clean. There's one big headline, then the intro text, then a clear primary button with a link as the secondary action, and the navy repeated on Q4 and the button ties the demo to the action.
