I'm writing up the critique now. The deep read is done.

**First read.** The eye lands on "Fill every market pitch before Saturday" and catches the orange-red "before Saturday". It jumps right to the single rust tile, F4, in the market map, then comes back to the "Plan your next market" button. Within about three seconds it reads as booking software for whoever runs a street market. It feels calm, earthy and competent, not the usual SaaS template. The map does most of that work.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a landing page that gets market managers to start setting up their market in Stallkeep, with traders as a secondary audience ("For traders" in the nav).

**Verdict.** This is a strong page that does its job: the offer, the audience and the next step are clear on first view. The biggest weakness is that the map, which is the proof, highlights the wrong thing. The eye goes to a pitch that is already booked and paid. The three empty pitches the headline promises to fill are the faintest things on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The map spotlights a solved pitch, not the problem.** This is a defect. The headline is about empty pitches. The map's own label says "3 free". But E3, F6 and G5 are white tiles with a dashed border that is barely visible on the cream panel, so they look like gaps more than pitches. Meanwhile F4 gets the page's strongest color. So the viewer's eye goes to a booked, paid pitch, and the link between "fill every pitch" and the picture is left for them to make. The map also shows no payment state, though the paragraph says Stallkeep shows "who has paid this week". Every booked tile is the same green, so that claim gets no visual proof. Fix: make the free pitches the visible tension in the map, with a clearer outline or fill (WCAG 1.4.11 asks 3:1 for edges that identify a component). If the product tracks unpaid pitches, show that state too, so the picture proves both halves of the promise. The rule that the picture and the headline should make one idea comes from off the reading list, so treat it as my judgment: here the headline and the picture are about different moments.
2. **Red on a green map reads as a problem.** This is a convention (Apple HIG, Material: red means error). F4 is rust-red among sage-green booked tiles. Anyone used to booking grids or status maps will read it as "something's wrong with F4". Then the detail row says "$32 paid", and they have to fix their first reading. Fix: mark the selected pitch with something that doesn't mean "alert", such as an outline, a darker shade of the booked green, or a neutral dark. Then save red, if you use it at all, for a real problem state like unpaid.
3. **Three different reds where one would do.** This is a convention (Material color roles: one role, one color) and partly taste. The headline gradient runs orange to pinkish red, F4 is a brown rust, and the button is a slightly different rust. They do different jobs: urgency, selection and action. Because the colors are close, though, they read as drift rather than as a system. The gradient is also the only glossy, digital-looking touch in a flat, earthy palette, so it's the one place the page looks templated. Fix: pick one accent and use it for the action. Set "before Saturday" in that same solid accent or in the headline color, and take F4 off red, as in finding 2.

**Smaller things.**
- "Look at a sample layout" sits right next to what already looks like a sample layout, so the viewer can't tell whether the link goes somewhere new. Say what's different, for example an interactive version or a bigger market, using whatever is actually true.
- The paragraph switches who it's talking to. It starts with "your market layout" and then says "the market manager stops taking bookings by text". Since the reader is the manager, keep it "you": "…and you stop taking bookings by text."
- The type uses about seven close sizes: roughly 46 for the headline, 19 for feature headings, 18 for hero body, 16 for panel titles, 15 for nav and feature body, then 13 and 12 for the map labels. Each one has a clear role, but you could merge the 15/16 and 12/13 pairs without losing anything (Bringhurst, 3.1.1).

**What's working.**
- The map is the hero image, and it's a real slice of the product with believable content: Canal Street Market, the footway to the car park, a 3 m pitch with power, Rye and Rind Bakery. It shows what the product is faster than any copy could. Keep it real, not decorative.
- The copy is concrete and talks the market's language. "Nobody walks the rows with a cash bag" and "the layout and trader list it asks for each quarter" each name a specific chore the product takes away. That's what keeps the page from reading like anyone's software.
- The layout holds back: one solid primary button, a plain underlined secondary link, a single hairline above the three features, and wide margins kept clear. Nothing competes with the headline.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the map is interactive (clicking a pitch to update the detail row) and whether the free pitches show a hover state. That matters here, because the map is the main sales argument.
