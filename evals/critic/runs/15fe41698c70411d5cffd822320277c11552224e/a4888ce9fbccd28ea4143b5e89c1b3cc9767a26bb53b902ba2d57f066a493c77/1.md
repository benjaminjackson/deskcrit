**First read.** The eye goes straight to the headline, "Let the harbour book its own berths", then across to the berth panel, where the one navy tile (S4) pulls it in, then down to "$380 paid". It reads within seconds as booking and billing software for small marinas. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, like an office tool rather than a startup pitch. The panel does most of the convincing: you understand the product from the picture before you read the paragraph.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a marketing homepage. It is meant to get a harbour master or marina office to start setting up, or to try the sample pontoon.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the copy is concrete and the ask is specific. The main weakness is the berth panel, which is also the page's main piece of evidence. Its colors suggest the wrong berths are free, and its heading doesn't match its own rows. Both are errors the expert viewer this page is for will spot at once.

**What would move it most**
1. **The panel's colors give the opposite answer about which berths are free.** In the berth grid, taken berths are filled sage green, and the three free ones (N3, S6, W5) are white boxes with a faint dashed outline. Green means "go" or "available" almost everywhere, and marina and hotel booking screens use it that way. A viewer scanning the grid will first read the green tiles as open and the white ones as empty or broken. The free berths are what the product sells, but they are the weakest marks in the panel: the dashed outline looks well under 3:1 against white and the cream panel. That makes this both a convention issue (Apple HIG and Material status colors; don't reverse them) and a contrast defect (WCAG 1.4.11: boundaries that identify a component need 3:1). Fix direction: make the free berths the most visible tiles, and stop using green for "taken". A neutral or muted fill for occupied berths and a clear, outlined or tinted "free" state usually does it. Measure the outline contrast once it's changed. *Defect.*
2. **The panel's heading contradicts the panel.** It says "North Pontoon, 18 berths, 3 free", but the rows are N1–N6, S1–S6 and W1–W6, which looks like three pontoons, not one. A harbour master knows their own layout and will read this as either a mislabeled demo or a product that gets pontoons wrong. That costs trust exactly where the page is trying to earn it (WCAG 2.4.6: a heading names what its section holds). Fix direction: make the heading and the row labels agree, either a name that covers the whole plan or row letters that belong to one pontoon. Which one is right is up to you. *Defect.*
3. **The headline names the wrong person.** "Let the harbour book its own berths" makes the harbour the one doing the booking. The paragraph then says boat owners pick and pay, and the office just sees the result. The idea (the office stops taking bookings by hand) is there, but the viewer has to piece it together from the next three lines. The page also never sets Moorly against what the office uses today. The sample pontoon link lets the viewer judge the product, but only on its own terms. Fix direction: make the headline say who books and what the office no longer has to do. Then, somewhere near the ask, name the current method the viewer would compare it with (the kind of thing: their paper plan, spreadsheet or phone bookings). You should supply that from what your buyers really use. *Taste, argued: the headline is the first claim a stranger reads, and right now its subject is wrong.*

**Smaller things.**
- Hero paragraph: "where." sits alone on the last line. Rebalance the line breaks so the last line carries more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- **The panel shows the product instead of describing it.** It has real berth codes, a walkway to the slipway, a boat-length limit, a season and a paid amount. Keep that level of specific detail when you fix the colors and labels.
- **The ask is exact.** "Set up your harbour" names the job and "Open a sample pontoon" offers a no-commitment way to try it. There's one clear primary button and a plainly secondary underlined link. The navy of the button matches the selected berth, which quietly ties the action to the product.
- **The three feature headings each describe a real moment**, such as "Visitors book the night before" and "A plan the harbour master can print". They aren't category labels, so the viewer recognizes their own week in them.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the berth tiles are interactive in "Open a sample pontoon", and if so whether they have a visible focus state.
