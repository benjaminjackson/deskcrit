**First read.** The eye goes to the headline first, "Notes from every call, written before you hang up". It's big, dark, and top-left. Then it jumps right to the robot's dark face, which is the heaviest block on the page. From there it drops back to the blue button. In about two seconds this reads as an AI meeting-notes tool for teams. It feels calm, plain, and trustworthy, until the cartoon robot makes it look like every other AI startup.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a landing page for an AI note-taker. It needs to get team leads or managers who run lots of video calls to connect their calendar, or at least to read a sample.

**Verdict.** The copy and the layout do the job well. The headline is specific, the body says exactly what happens, and there's one clear action. The biggest problem is the robot. It takes half the hero to show a generic "AI bot" when it could show the one thing this page promises, which is the notes.

**What would move it most**
1. **The picture shows a robot when it should show the notes.** The right half of the hero is a smiling robot surrounded by circuit traces. The headline promises a result: notes, ready before the call ends. The picture shows a mechanism, a bot. So the pair doesn't add up to one idea, and the most valuable space on the page proves nothing. The page already names the right picture in its own secondary link, "Read a sample set of notes." The three columns below describe that document too: decisions at the top, tasks with a name and a day. Show it there. It's also the comparison a viewer can judge on their own terms: they can hold it up against the notes they write themselves today. The robot also works against the third column, "Nothing kept you did not ask for." A cartoon bot sitting in your calls is exactly the image that makes people worry about recording. And because a robot is the stock picture for "AI," the page looks like anyone's product. The point that image and headline should form one idea is taste (no approved source); the cost is concrete. **Direction:** replace the illustration with a real-looking, readable excerpt of the product's own sample notes. The decisions block and one task with a name and a day are enough. Keep it at the size the robot has now. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- The circuit traces and their ten end circles around the robot do no work. They lead out toward the edges and stop at nothing. If any illustration stays, drop them. (Taste.)
- The illustration doesn't sit on the page's grid. Its right edge (the circles at about x 1160) stops roughly 40 px short of the 1200 line that "Sign in" and the divider share, and its left edge doesn't match the third column at 859. Either align it to the right edge or move it off clearly (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*; Arnheim on near-miss placement). Convention.
- The headline breaks as "written before you / hang up", which splits the phrase "you hang up." Break it as "written before / you hang up". (Common sense; Bringhurst's rule on breaks covers numbers and units, not headings.)
- In the middle column, "who." sits alone on the last line. Rewrap or edit so the last line carries more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- Copy: "Nothing kept you did not ask for" makes the reader stop and work out the grammar. Putting back the missing word, "Nothing kept that you did not ask for", makes it read in one pass. It's a guarantee, so it should read cleanly on the first try.
- Copy: "Add it to your calendar" commits the viewer to something, but it can be read two ways: put a single event on my calendar, or connect my calendar so Minutehand joins my calls. The second is what it likely means, and it lets a bot into every meeting. Make the button or a line under it say which calls it will join.

**What's working.**
- The body paragraph says who does what and when: "joins your video calls, writes down what was decided and who will do what, and sends the notes to everyone who was there." It ends on what this means for you, "You correct a line in one click if it heard something wrong." Keep that plainness through any revision.
- There's one solid primary button next to an underlined text link that clearly ranks below it. The secondary ask, reading a sample, lets a doubtful viewer judge the output before committing. That's the right pairing.
- The type scale holds. There are about four sizes with clear steps: headline, card heading and lead text, body, and nav. It uses a restrained navy-and-gray palette, with a calm mood that fits a privacy-sensitive product.
