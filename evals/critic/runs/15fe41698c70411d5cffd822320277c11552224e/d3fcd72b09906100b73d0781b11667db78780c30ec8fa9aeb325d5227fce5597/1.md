**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the shop owner's monthly look at sales. It needs to answer "how did November go, and what drove it?" at a glance.

**Verdict.** This is a well-built, clean layout that mostly does its job. Most of what stands in its way is in the data: the weekday chart uses shading that means nothing, two labels don't match what they show, and the page leaves the owner to work out every comparison themselves.

**What would move it most**
1. **The weekday chart's shading suggests a meaning it doesn't have, and it shows no values.** The bars get steadily darker from Monday to Sunday. A light-to-dark ramp tells the reader that darker means more (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*). Here darkness only tracks the day of the week, so Sunday is darkest while Saturday is taller, and Tuesday is lighter than Monday even though its bar is shorter. The reader reads the shading as a second measure and then has to unlearn it. The chart also has no value axis: only Saturday is labeled (126), so the other six days can only be judged against each other. The title is a problem too. "Orders by weekday" doesn't say whether these are totals or daily averages. November 2026 has five Sundays and five Mondays but only four of every other weekday, so if these are totals, Sunday and Monday get a built-in boost. Fix: give all the bars one color, or make only the day you want noticed darker. Put a value on each bar. Name the measure in the title ("total" or "average per day"), and use averages if the point is to compare days. *Defect.*
2. **"Daily orders" isn't drawn daily.** Each line has about 11 points from 1 to 30 November, roughly one every three days, not 30. Either the title is wrong or the data has been thinned out. Either way, the reader will take each peak and dip as one day's orders when it isn't. Fix: plot all 30 days, or rename the chart to say what each point covers. *Defect.*
3. **Every figure has a comparison, but the page never states one.** Each number comes with its October value, which meets the basic rule (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). But the owner still has to do four subtractions to learn that everything went up. The chart titles name topics instead of findings. Fix: show the change and its direction next to each number. Let each chart title, or a short note under it, say what the chart shows. Writing those lines is your job. *Taste*: the approved sources only ask for the comparison, not for the change to be worked out. But on a page meant to be read at a glance, a calculation the reader has to do in their head is a wasted second each time.
4. **The currency may be wrong.** The shop is in Norwich and the copy uses UK spelling ("Metres"), yet every amount is in "$". If the shop trades in pounds, then every revenue figure, the average order and the table are labeled in the wrong money, and those are the numbers the owner will repeat to others. If it really does sell in dollars, this is fine. Check it. *Defect unless intended.*

**Smaller things.**
- "Repeat makers 54%": 54% of what, makers or orders? Say which so the number can't be read two ways.
- The line chart uses a separate legend for its two series. Labeling November and October at the right-hand end of each line would save a glance back and forth (Tufte).
- "1 to 30 November 2026" looks like plain text. A sales dashboard needs a visible way to change the period. If this is a control, it needs to look like one.
- "Best-selling fabrics" is sorted by metres, but wool melton earns more than cotton lawn. Add "by metres" to the heading so the order reads as intended.

**What's working.**
- The structure is right: a row of key numbers, then trend charts, then the detail table. Cards share one grid and one set of gaps, so the page scans in a natural order with no effort.
- The typography is restrained. There are only a few sizes with clear jobs (title, big numbers, card headings, body text, small captions). Hierarchy comes from size and weight, not color. Keep that discipline.
- In the table, the numbers are right-aligned in columns, the rows are separated by light rules only, and the palette is one blue. Keep all three.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a control. What the charts show on hover. Focus states in the sidebar.
