**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Hand out school lockers before the first bell", then crosses to the locker plan on the right and stops on the one blue square, B4. Within a few seconds it reads as a tool for school offices to assign lockers, keys and deposits. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, more like a well-run school office than a startup.

**What it's for.** Assumed: the landing page for a locker-management product, meant to get school office staff to start a trial ("Start with one corridor").

**Verdict.** This works. The page is clear and specific, and it shows the real product instead of describing it. The one thing that gets in the way is the locker plan's color code: green means *taken*, which is the opposite of what most viewers will assume, and nothing on the plan says otherwise.

**What would move it most**
1. **Green marks the taken lockers, not the free ones (convention, and a defect here).** In the Science corridor plan, 14 lockers are sage green and the 3 free ones (A3, B6, C5) are white with a dashed outline. Green almost always means "go" or "available" (Apple HIG and Material, on status colors). A parent or office worker glancing at the plan will read the green lockers as open and the white ones as empty slots or missing data. The plan has no key either, so green, white-dashed and blue each have to be guessed, with only "18 lockers, 3 free" to check against. Color alone carries the meaning (WCAG 1.4.1). On the product, families will actually pick lockers from this plan, so a wrong guess turns into a wrong pick. Fix: make "taken" a neutral or muted fill and give "free" the color that invites a click, or keep the colors and add a small key next to "18 lockers, 3 free" (taken / free / selected).
2. **Neither ask compares the product with how the school does this today (copy).** "Start with one corridor" is a good ask: it is specific, low-commitment and in the viewer's own terms. "See a sample corridor" lets the viewer look before trusting. But nothing on the page sets Lockerly against what the office already uses for the locker plan, the key list and the deposits. So the value rests on trust, not on a comparison the viewer can make for themselves. Direction: name the current way, whatever the maker knows schools actually use, in the subhead or the feature lines, so the viewer measures the product against a habit they already have.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "bell" sits alone on the third line, a runt (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Break it as "Hand out school lockers / before the first bell" by widening the text column a little or setting the break by hand.
- Detail row "Locker B4, top row": on the plan, B4 is in the middle row (A, B, C). If "top row" means the upper tier of a stacked locker, a parent may still walk to the wrong locker. Say which one is meant, e.g. "top tier", or drop it.

**What's working.**
- The product panel is the picture. A real corridor with lockers, a "Stairs to the labs" landmark, and one selected locker linked to its key and deposit. It shows the whole product in one glance and makes the headline concrete. Keep it as the hero image.
- The copy is specific and in the school's own words: "before the first bell", "key 2214", "$10 deposit held", "Plans for the caretaker", "Deposits back in July". None of it could sit on anyone else's page.
- Restraint: one blue for the primary button and the selected locker, quiet neutrals everywhere else, one clear headline size. Because of this, the CTA and B4 are the only things that call for attention.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The small gray text in the panel ("18 lockers, 3 free", "Year 9, key 2214") looks close to the 4.5:1 limit on the beige panel. Measure it.
