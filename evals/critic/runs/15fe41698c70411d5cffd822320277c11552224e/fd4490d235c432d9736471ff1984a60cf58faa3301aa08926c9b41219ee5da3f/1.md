**First read.** The eye goes first to the headline, then right to the maroon seat B4, which is the only saturated block on the screen. From there it drops to the maroon "Put on a show" button, because the two share a color. In a couple of seconds it reads as seat-booking software for small community theatres and village halls. It feels calm, local and unfussy, with nothing in it that looks like a ticketing giant, and that suits the audience.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the top of a marketing homepage. It has to get volunteer-run theatre groups to understand what Seatplan does and either start a show or try the sample plan.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The structure, the copy and the restraint are well above average for this kind of page. The biggest problem is in the seat map, the picture that is meant to explain the product: its colors mean the opposite of what most people expect from a seat map, so the first glance gets it wrong.

**What would move it most**
1. **The seat map's colors read backwards.** This is in the panel at upper right. Sold seats are filled green, free seats are white with a faint dashed border, and the booked seat B4 is dark red. People who have booked seats online usually read green as "available" and red as "taken." So at a glance this hall looks almost empty, with one seat blocked. The "18 seats, 3 free" line in small gray type says the opposite, and the viewer has to stop and work it out. This picture is your proof of the headline's promise ("sell every seat"), so if it reads as an empty hall, it argues against the headline. Apple HIG and Material both say not to reverse status colors. **Defect.** Fix direction: make sold seats a neutral or muted fill. Let free seats be the ones that stand out, or at least not the green ones. Keep maroon only for "your seat," and add a three-item key ("sold / free / yours") so the meaning doesn't depend on color or a count in the corner.

2. **The headline ends on a single word.** "Sell every seat in the hall, not just the front / row" leaves "row" alone on a third line, which breaks the phrase "front row" in two. This is the first thing anyone reads. It makes the block look lopsided and makes the sentence's turn land weakly. Bringhurst (2.4.2) says a paragraph should not end with one short word alone on its last line, and this is the most visible place that can happen. **Convention.** Fix direction: change the width of the heading's text box, or add a manual break or a non-breaking space between "front" and "row", so the heading sets as two balanced lines or three even ones. Recheck it at the other breakpoints.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline copy (taste): the contrast in "not just the front row" asks the reader to fill in why only the front row would sell without Seatplan. The body copy never says so, so some readers won't make the link. If there is a real reason, such as people who phone in asking for the front, say it in a few words in the paragraph below.
- "Seat B4, row B" in the panel footer says the row twice. "Seat B4" is enough.
- "For theatres" in the nav names the audience of the whole site, so as a section label it tells the viewer little. Say what is behind it.

**What's working.**
- The body copy says who does what: "draws your hall, takes bookings for each night of the run, and prints the door list." It also ends on the outcome the reader cares about: "the box office stops taking orders by phone." That compares the product to the reader's current way of selling tickets, which is the strongest kind of claim to make. Keep that sentence.
- There is one clear primary action, and "Try a sample plan" offers a real low-commitment way to judge the product for yourself. It is properly styled as an underlined secondary link.
- The three features at the bottom are specific to this audience (concessions, door lists for volunteers, seats held for the cast's families). The quiet palette of off-white, sage and one maroon accent keeps the page warm without decoration.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the seat map is interactive in the live page. If it is, hover and focus states for the seats matter.
- How the headline breaks at other widths.
