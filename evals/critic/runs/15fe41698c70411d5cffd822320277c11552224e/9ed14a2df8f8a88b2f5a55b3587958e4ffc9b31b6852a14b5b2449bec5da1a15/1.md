**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Let members book a desk before they leave home," then crosses to the floor plan, where the one dark teal tile (E4) pulls it in, then drops to the matching teal button. Within three seconds it reads as desk-booking software for coworking operators. It feels calm, competent and warm, with the sage and sand tones of an actual workspace rather than a generic SaaS blue.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page for Deskwell. Its job is to get coworking space owners or managers ("hosts") to set up their first floor.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, disciplined page and it does its job. It has a clear focal point, a product picture that proves the headline, and a specific ask. What's left is small: the headline breaks badly across its lines, and the navigation blurs who the page is talking to.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline breaks in the wrong places.** It sets as "Let members book a / desk before they leave / home." That splits "a desk" across a line and leaves "home" alone on the last line. This is the line every visitor reads first, so it reads in three jerky pieces instead of two phrases, and the single word at the end looks like an accident. Bringhurst covers both problems: a short word stranded on a last line, and breaks that don't fall at phrase boundaries. Change the headline's width or size so it breaks after the phrase: "Let members book a desk / before they leave home." Two balanced lines will also hold together better against the floor plan. *Convention; the cost is a choppy first read of the most important line.*
2. **The navigation contradicts the page's audience.** Everything on the page speaks to the operator: "your coworking space," "the front desk stops keeping a paper list," "Add your first floor." But the navigation has a separate "For hosts" link, which implies this page is for someone else, presumably members. A host who sees it has to stop and wonder whether they're on the wrong page. A member who lands here finds copy written to their landlord. Either rename that link to what it actually holds, or give members a clearly labeled place to go so this page can stay the host page (Norman: the navigation should show how the parts of the product relate). *Defect; the people the button is meant for hesitate before they click.*

**Smaller things.**
- Third feature column, "Quiet rows kept quiet": "calls and meetings book somewhere else" has the calls doing the booking. Put the person back in, along the lines of "members booking calls and meetings are sent somewhere else," so it says who does what.
- Floor plan footer: "Booked for Tuesday" next to "$25 day pass" can be read as Tuesday costing $25, or as the desk's general day rate. A visitor won't act on it, but one connecting word would remove the doubt.

**What's working.**
- **The floor plan is the argument.** It isn't decoration. It shows exactly what the headline promises: a named floor, desks laid out in rows, a kitchen bar, "18 desks, 3 free," and one booked desk with a person-sized caption ("Desk E4, by the window"). Free desks are marked by a white fill and a dashed border, not by color alone. Keep it this concrete.
- **Color links the plan to the button.** The teal appears in only two places, the booked desk and "Add your first floor," so the eye goes from the outcome to the action. Keep the teal that scarce.
- **The ask is specific and grounded.** "Add your first floor" names one real first step. "Walk through a sample floor" is a properly lower-weight alternative. The body copy measures the product against something the host already has, the paper list at the front desk, so the host can judge the value on their own terms.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Keyboard focus styles on the navigation and buttons, and how the two-column hero reflows on a phone, especially whether the floor plan stays legible at narrow widths.
