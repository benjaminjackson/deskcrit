**First read.** The eye goes first to the one saturated block on the page, the blue Friday bar, then up to the row of four big numbers, then across to the line chart. It reads right away as a monthly overview for a small hostel's owner or manager. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, with a quiet, competent SaaS look. Nothing on it tells me what to think about the month.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner or manager of Fellside Hostel opens this to see at a glance how November went against October and what needs attention.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, well-built dashboard that reports numbers without saying what they mean. The biggest problem is trust. The labels don't make clear what several numbers count ("per night", "Nights"), and the figures don't obviously agree with each other. On a screen whose only job is numbers, that matters more than anything about the look.

**What would move it most**
1. **The numbers don't obviously agree, and the labels don't say what they count. (Defect)**
   - **The line chart.** "Beds sold per night" plots about 11 points across 30 nights, not 30, so it looks like it's not per night. Its November line runs from roughly 50 to 95 and averages around 73. Over 30 nights that's about 2,200, but the card above says "Beds sold 2,846".
   - **The table.** "Rooms most booked" gives Skiddaw dorm 212 "Nights" and Derwent twin 58 in a 30-day month. Neither can be room-nights, so "Nights" must mean bed-nights, but nothing says so.
   - **What the viewer gets.** An owner who adds these up gets two answers and stops trusting the whole screen. They also can't tell whether "most booked" means most occupied or most bookings.
   - **The rule.** Tufte (*Visual Display*, ch. 2): the reader should never have to guess what a number measures.
   - **Fix.** Check that the chart really plots every night and that its sum matches the card. Then put the unit in each label: say which kind of night the "Nights" column counts ("bed-nights" or "room-nights"), and add a unit to the line chart's y axis.
2. **The emphasis lands on facts that don't need it, and the one figure that's down gets none. (Defect / taste)**
   - **Friday.** The only strong color on the page goes to Friday check-ins, and the bar chart doesn't say why Friday matters. It has no axis and only that one value, so the other six days can't be read at all (Tufte, labeling).
   - **The month's one bad number.** "Beds filled 74%, Down 3 points on October" sits in the same quiet gray as "Same as October". It's also puzzling next to "Beds sold… Up 9%": more beds sold while occupancy fell implies capacity went up, and nothing on the page says so.
   - **Chart titles.** "Beds sold per night" and "Check-ins by weekday" name topics, not findings. Writing the finding in the title is my judgment (taste), not a published rule.
   - **Fix.** Spend the emphasis on what the owner should notice or act on. Make the title or a short caption say what the highlight shows, using the facts the maker has. Make "down" read as different from "up" by more than the wording. If capacity changed, say so next to the occupancy figure.

**Smaller things**
- Line chart: the legend sits at top right away from the lines. Put "November" and "October" at the ends of the lines (Tufte; Wilke).
- Line chart: the x ticks at 1, 10, 19 and 30 Nov are unevenly spaced (9, 9, 11 days). Use even intervals.
- Blue means "November" in the line chart and "highlighted day" in the bar chart. Keep one meaning for the accent (Apple HIG, Color).
- The chart row splits at about two-thirds, which doesn't line up with the KPI card dividers above it. Line up the gutter with a KPI divider, or make the offset look deliberate (taste).
- Sidebar icons: Bookings uses a shopping cart and Settings a sun-like glyph. Use a calendar or bed for Bookings and a gear for Settings, which users already know (Apple HIG / Material).

**What's working**
- Every headline number comes with an October comparison in plain words ("Up 9% on October"). That's exactly the context a KPI needs, and the sparklines add the trend without clutter.
- The palette is restrained: neutral grays plus one blue. Gray is used for context (October, the other weekdays), so the data stays the loudest thing on the page.
- The table is well set: tabular figures, numbers right-aligned under right-aligned headers, light rules, and room to breathe. Keep that setting if you change the data.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" can be clicked to change the period. It looks like plain text. If it's the period control, it needs a visible cue; if it isn't, the screen has no way to look at another month.
- Hover values on the charts. If hovering shows the Monday–Sunday values, the missing bar axis matters less.
