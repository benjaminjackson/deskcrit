**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes," drops through the paragraph, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a small ferry operator's website. It feels calm and clean, but also bare and generic. Apart from the words, nothing on the screen suggests water, a boat, or this particular bay. The bottom third of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the top of a ferry operator's home page, aimed at locals and visitors deciding whether to take the boat, with the timetable as the next step.

**Verdict.** It works as a clear, readable hero with one obvious action. But it leaves out the facts a rider is actually deciding on (where the boat goes, when it runs, what it costs). It spends its second block repeating the first. And because every block is centered by habit, it could be anyone's page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy is missing the facts a rider needs.** Defect, judged as common sense. The headline says "cross the bay" but never names the other side; only "the north landing" appears. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" can't be planned around. A reader can't tell whether early means 6 or 8, or whether late means 9 or midnight. There's no fare anywhere. Because of this, the reader has to click through before they can decide anything, and some won't bother. Direction: name the destination landing, and replace "early morning" and "late evening" with the real first and last sailing times. If price matters to the decision, add the fare. Only you have these facts. The ask, "See the timetable," is specific and good; keep it. Also, nothing invites the reader to compare the boat with the way they cross now. The "twelve minutes" claim would land harder next to something they already know, such as the trip around the bay by road. Add that only if you have the real figure.
2. **The three-line list repeats the paragraph.** Defect (Nielsen 8: aesthetic and minimalist design). "Bicycles travel free" and "Seats on the open deck" echo "Bring a bicycle" and "the open deck" from the paragraph just above. Only "Hot drinks on board" is new. The list also has no bullets or icons, and its text is nearly the same size as the paragraph. So it reads as a second, weaker paragraph that the eye has to scan again for very little. Direction: cut the overlap from one place or the other. Either keep the paragraph to the schedule and the feeling, and let the list carry the practical perks, or fold the one new fact into the paragraph and drop the list. A list of real perks (fare, bikes, accessibility) would earn its place.
3. **Everything is centered, including a three-line paragraph.** Convention (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst 2.1.3). The brand name, headline, paragraph, list, and button all sit on one axis. The paragraph's centered lines ragged on both sides, so each line starts in a different place and the reader has to find the next one. Centering every block is also what makes this look like a template. Direction: set the paragraph flush left. Ideally, set the whole stack on a left edge that follows the order of the content, and keep centering, if you want it at all, for the short headline.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "twelve / calm minutes" separates the number from its unit. Break before "twelve" or after "bay" so "twelve calm minutes" stays together.
- Type scale: I count five sizes (brand line, headline, paragraph, list, button). The paragraph (~20 px) and the list (~18 px) are close enough to look like a mistake rather than two levels. Set the list in the body size and tell it apart by structure, or by weight or color (Bringhurst 3.1.1).
- No site header or navigation is visible. If this is the full top of the page, a reader can't get to the timetable, fares, or contact details except through the one button. Add the usual top navigation, unless it sits outside this capture.

**What's working.**
- The headline. It makes a specific, sensory promise ("twelve calm minutes"), it's set big, bold, and tight, and it's clearly the place to start.
- One primary action, clearly styled, with a label that says exactly where it goes. Keep that discipline as the page grows.
- Restraint in color: near-black type and one blue shared by the brand name and the button tie the action to the company without noise.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus and hover states on the button, and how the centered stack reflows on a phone.
- Whether the empty band below the button is waiting for content further down the page.
