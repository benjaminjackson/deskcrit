**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title, then to the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%, then over to the table. It reads as an internal analytics dashboard: a quiet, competent page for a product or growth team. It looks calm and trustworthy. What it doesn't do is tell you what to conclude, so the first takeaway is "Growth wins," and the page gives you no way to check whether that's true.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an internal report page that helps a growth, product, or pricing team see how each plan's trials convert to paid, and decide whether any plan needs action.

**Verdict.** The craft is clean and the chart is honest, but it shows four rates that differ by less than one point and gives no help reading them. It has no finding, no earlier period to compare against, and no sign that the Enterprise rate rests on only 38 conversions. A reader can't tell signal from noise. The metric's definition can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart's gaps are small, and nothing tells the reader whether they're real.** In the chart, the bars run 3.1%, 3.6%, 4.0% and 3.4%. Bar height makes those gaps look decisive. But the counts that would tell you how much to trust them are only in the table on the right: Enterprise is 38 paid out of 1,120 and Growth is 118 out of 2,950. A reader who looks only at the chart will treat "Growth converts best, Enterprise lags" as fact. With 38 conversions, a handful of deals either way moves the Enterprise bar by several tenths of a point. That's enough to change its rank. Defect: it misleads the person deciding. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization* (ch. 16), calls for showing uncertainty or sample size with sampled rates. Fix: put the sample next to each bar (the trial and paid counts under the plan name are enough). Add an interval or a plain note wherever the counts are too small to rank on.

2. **No comparison, so "good" and "bad" have no meaning.** The page covers one quarter. It has no previous quarter, no target, and no trend line. A reader can't tell whether 3.1% for Starter is a problem, an improvement, or normal. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") flags this as a convention break: a figure without a comparison to a previous period or a target can't be judged. Fix: show each plan's rate against the previous period or the target. A faint marker per bar or a change column in the table would do; the maker knows which comparison the team actually uses.

3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** The heading "Trial to paid conversion by plan" restates what the axis and labels already say. The reader has to work out the point from the bars, and as finding 1 shows, the bars point to a conclusion the data may not support. Taste, and common sense for a report page: the title or a caption should say what the maker believes the data shows, including "no plan differs meaningfully" if that's the honest answer. Leave the claim to the maker, but say it in words above the chart.

4. **The definition can be read two ways, and it's the line people will hold you to.** The subtitle says trials count if they "became paid accounts within 30 days." The Definition paragraph says trials "still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." Reading A: each trial gets its own 30-day window from its start date. Reading B: anything paid by the fixed 30 October cutoff counts, so a trial started in early July had about four months to convert, and one started on 30 September had 30 days. If Reading B is the truth, the rates mix very different windows, and plans whose trials bunch late in the quarter look worse. Defect. Fix: an editorial edit that says outright which rule applies and when the clock starts. Also make the source line match: it gives event dates of "1 July to 30 September 2025," but conversions are counted until 30 October.

**Smaller things.**
- Layout: the left column runs to the foot of the page, while the right column stops under the Definition paragraph at about a third of the way down. The page tips left and the lower right reads as unfinished. Taste; Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance") on a heavy side needing a counterweight. Fix: move the comparison or sample context from findings 1 and 2 into that space, or narrow the page to the content.

**What's working.**
- An honest chart: bars start at zero, ticks are evenly spaced, gridlines are pale enough that the purple bars are clearly the data, and values are labeled directly on the bars. Keep all of this.
- One hue and one neutral gray, with plans in tier order rather than sorted by value. That suits a report a team reads every quarter, because positions stay put from one quarter to the next.
- The table is set properly: numbers are right-aligned and appear to use tabular figures, with light row rules. The current section in the nav is marked by both weight and color.
