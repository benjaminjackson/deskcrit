**First read.** My eye goes to the headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan", then to the tallest bar (Growth, 4.0%), then across the other three value labels. It looks like a tidy SaaS analytics report page, made for a product or growth team. It feels calm and competent, but it doesn't tell me what to conclude: I see four bars of nearly the same height and I'm left to decide whether the differences mean anything.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal analytics page that tells a growth or product team how each plan's trials convert to paid, so they know where to act.

**Verdict.** The craft is clean and the chart is honest, but the page reports numbers without telling the reader what they mean. It never says whether 3–4% is good or worse than last quarter. It also implies differences between plans that the sample sizes probably can't support. On top of that, it defines "converted" in two ways that conflict.

**What would move it most**

1. **The conversion rule is stated two different ways (defect).** The subtitle says trials "that became paid accounts within 30 days." The Definition block on the right says a trial converts when "the first invoice is paid" and that "trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." Those are different rules:
   - Reading 1: a trial counts only if it pays within 30 days of its own start.
   - Reading 2: anything paid by 30 October counts. Under this rule, a trial that started on 1 July has nearly four months to convert, and one that started on 30 September has one month.

   This number is the one the team will quote and set targets against. If they read it the wrong way, they will compare it with other quarters or plans on a different basis. The source line ("trial events, 1 July to 30 September") adds to the confusion, because payments up to 30 October must come from somewhere. Fix: decide on one rule and state it the same way in the subtitle and the definition. If the 30 October cutoff is how 30 days is enforced, say that in one clause. Only the maker knows which rule is correct.

2. **The plans are shown as different when the data may not support it (defect).** The bars run 3.1, 3.6, 4.0 and 3.4%, a spread of under one point. The bold value labels make each gap look like a fact. The table shows how thin some groups are: Enterprise has 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials and Growth has 118 out of 2,950. At those counts, a few accounts either way would reorder Growth, Team and Enterprise. A reader will come away with "Growth converts best, Enterprise underperforms" and may act on it. Fix: show how much each rate could move given its sample, as a range on each bar or at least a note about sample size, so readers can tell real gaps from noise (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16).

3. **The numbers have nothing to compare against (convention).** Each rate is shown with no previous quarter, target or trend, so the reader can't tell whether 3.1% for Starter is a problem or normal. On a page whose job is to prompt action, that's the gap that matters most (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Fix: put a comparison next to each rate, such as last quarter's figure or the target, as a marker or a faint paired bar. Which comparison to use is the maker's call.

4. **The headline names the topic instead of the finding (taste).** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" describes what the chart is, and the reader has to work out the point. Once 1–3 are fixed and you know what the data really says, put that in the headline or the subtitle. Then the chart has one idea to back up, and the page is worth glancing at. I'd argue this on common sense, not on a published rule.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" sits over a table that also holds Paid. Name both, or add a rate column so the table and chart obviously match.
- The lower half of the right column is empty below the Definition block. It's acceptable as it is, but it's the obvious place for the comparison or sample-size note from items 2 and 3.

**What's working.**
- The bar chart is built right: the axis starts at zero, gridlines are pale and step evenly, there's one color for one series, labels sit right at the bars, and the plans run in tier order rather than being re-sorted. Keep all of that.
- The table puts the raw counts next to the rates, and they check out (286/9,210 = 3.1%, 38/1,120 = 3.4%). Right-aligned figures and light row rules make it easy to scan.
- The type scale is tight: a large headline, one text size, and small gray labels. The current nav item (bold purple "Conversion") shows clearly where you are.
