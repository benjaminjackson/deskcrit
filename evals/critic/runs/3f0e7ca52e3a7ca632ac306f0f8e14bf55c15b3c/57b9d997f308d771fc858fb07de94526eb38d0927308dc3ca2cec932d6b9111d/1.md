I've finished the walk and the deep read, and I'm writing up the critique now.

**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Planning a vegetable bed" and then steps down the three section headings like a ladder. It reads as a plain how-to article for a first-time home gardener. It feels calm and unfussy, and a little thin: everything is one light gray-black and one weight, so the page reads as a quiet document rather than a guide you'd keep open in the garden.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short web how-to that walks a beginner through setting up their first vegetable bed, read on a laptop or desktop screen.

**Verdict.** It works. The structure is clear, the copy is concrete, and nothing gets in the reader's way. The main thing holding it back is the overly wide text column, which makes every paragraph harder to read than it needs to be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The lines are too long.** The paragraphs run about 790 px wide, which is roughly 90 to 95 characters per line. "Dig to the depth of a spade and mix in compost from last year's heap. Rake the surface level" is one line. At that length the eye has to travel a long way back to find the start of the next line, and on a longer article than this one readers start to lose their place or re-read lines. Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 characters as comfortable and about 66 as ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). The empty space on the right is already there, so narrow the text column to about 66 characters at this size (roughly 560 to 600 px), or set the text larger in the same column. *Convention, with a real readability cost.*
2. **Headings differ from the body only in size.** "Preparing the soil", "Choosing what to plant" and "Watering" are the same light weight and the same near-black as the body text, just bigger. They do read as headings, but only because they're big, and the thin strokes at about 32 px look faint next to the denser body text. Dair's point (*Design with Type*, ch. 8–9) is that a smaller size step combined with a change of weight or color carries more contrast than size alone. Make the section heads one weight heavier, and then they can be a step smaller, which would also tighten the gap down to the title. *Convention, moderate cost.*

**Smaller things.**
- The apostrophe in "year's" (Soil section) is a straight typewriter mark. Use the curly one (’). This is common sense rather than a sourced rule, and it's barely visible at body size.
- Copy: "gets six hours of sun" works because most people fill in "a day", but this is a quantity the reader will act on. Adding "a day" removes the guess.

**What's working.**
- **The spacing groups each heading with its text.** Each heading sits a few pixels above its own paragraph and well clear of the paragraph before it, so every section reads as one unit without rules or boxes. Keep these ratios when you change the measure.
- **The type scale is clean.** There are three clearly different sizes (title about 56 px, section heads about 32 px, body about 18 px), with no near-duplicates. The title dominates without shouting.
- **The copy is specific and usable.** "Six hours of sun", "the depth of a spade", "radishes, lettuce, and bush beans", "twice a week, rather than a little every day": each instruction tells the reader exactly what to do, not just what to think about. That concreteness is what makes the page worth reading. Don't let a redesign trade it for something vaguer.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone. At phone widths the measure problem probably goes away, but check that the heading spacing still holds.
