**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then drops down the column to the blue button. In about two seconds I can tell it's a ferry service for day-trippers and locals. It feels calm and competent but empty. The whole right half of the screen is blank, and nothing on the page shows the bay, the boat or the harbor the copy talks about.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a small ferry operator's website on a desktop screen. Its job is to get someone planning a crossing to decide to go and to open the timetable.

**Verdict.** The words and the type order are clear and nearly finished. As a page, though, it doesn't make anyone want the crossing. The right half is empty and nothing shows the experience the copy is selling. The one fact a traveler needs, when the boats actually run, is left vague and hidden behind the button.

**What would move it most**

1. **Empty right half, and no picture of the crossing (defect).** All the content sits in a column that ends around x≈790 of 1280. Everything to the right, top to bottom, is bare white. Arnheim's point on balance is that a heavy side needs a counterweight. Here nothing balances the text, so the page leans left and looks unfinished, like an image that never loaded. It also costs the sell. "Watch the harbour slide past from the open deck" is the most persuasive line on the page, and the viewer has to imagine it. Fix: give that space to one strong picture of the bay or the open deck, so the image shows the calm the headline promises. If an image is already meant to load there, check it in a live browser. In that case this is a loading problem, not a design one.

2. **The copy hides the one fact a traveler acts on (common sense; the catalog's source for this is off-list).** "From early morning until late evening" has no times in it. A stranger can't tell whether the first boat leaves at 6 or at 9, or whether the last one leaves at 8 or at 11. So the only way to plan is to click through. Times are exactly what people remember and later hold you to, so a vague phrase here is a defect, not a style note. Fix: replace the phrase with the actual first and last sailing times, which only you have. Do the same with "every forty minutes" if the gap changes during the day. The button then becomes the place for the details, not the only way to learn when the boats run.

3. **Headline breaks between the number and its unit (convention).** "Cross the bay in twelve / calm minutes" puts "twelve" on one line and "minutes" on the next, so the key claim has to be put back together across the break. Bringhurst (2.4.6) says to keep a number with its unit. Fix: break at the phrase, as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes", or let the column width force that break.

**Smaller things.**
- **Two text sizes that almost match.** The paragraph looks about 20 px and the three feature lines about 18 px. Side by side they look like a mistake rather than two levels (Bringhurst 3.1.1). I count roughly five sizes in total (eyebrow, headline, paragraph, list, button). Set the list at the paragraph size, or make it clearly different by weight or color, not by 2 px.
- **The feature list has no visual cue that it's a list.** The three lines read like stray short sentences. A light marker or icon, or more contrast in weight, would show them as a set of perks. This one is taste.

**What's working.**
- **One clear dominant element.** The headline is the obvious place to start, and the type order below it is easy to read.
- **The headline promises something specific.** "Twelve calm minutes" gives a duration and a feeling, which most transport pages never manage. Keep it.
- **The ask is concrete.** "See the timetable" names exactly what you get. It's the only filled shape on the page and the only saturated color besides the eyebrow, so it stands out without shouting. The paragraph runs about 60 characters per line and is comfortable to read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half is meant to hold an image that didn't load. Check in a browser.
- How the column reflows on a phone.
