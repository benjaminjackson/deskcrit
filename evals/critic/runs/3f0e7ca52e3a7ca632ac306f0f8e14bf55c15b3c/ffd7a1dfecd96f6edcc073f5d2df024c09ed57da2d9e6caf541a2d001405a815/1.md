**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops straight into the first paragraph. It reads as a plain information page for people using a small river ferry, set as a single reading column. It feels calm and tidy. There is nothing to scan: you have to read sentence by sentence to find anything.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming it's a web page that tells passengers when and how to catch the ferry, often read by someone deciding whether they can make a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean, and nothing on the checklist fails. But the page doesn't do its main job: it never tells a passenger when a boat leaves. The facts they came for are vague and buried in prose.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times are missing, so the timetable can't be used** (defect). The first paragraph says boats run "between early morning and late evening". The third says the last winter crossing leaves "before dusk" and that "winter" thins the service. None of these is a time or a date. A passenger standing on the landing at 6:40 can't tell whether a boat is coming, and "before dusk" can be read two ways: the last boat leaves at a fixed time that happens to fall before dusk, or it leaves whenever the light starts to go. If they read it the wrong way, someone is stranded on the wrong bank. Put the actual first and last departures in the copy, along with the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, plus a departure minute or anchor ("every forty minutes, from …") so the interval means something. Only you have those figures. The line about printed timetables in the shelter currently sends the reader somewhere else for the answer this page should give.
2. **The facts that matter are written as narration, not set out for lookup** (taste, argued). The first visit is a read, but every later visit is a lookup: "when's the last boat?", "can I bring my bike?" Right now each answer sits mid-sentence in a paragraph of the same weight as everything around it, so the eye has nowhere to land after the heading. Pull the decision-making facts (frequency, crossing time, last boat, winter change, bikes) up front where they can be scanned, for example under short subheads or as a compact set of lines. The current prose can stay underneath as the explanation. The paragraphs are short, so this isn't a wall-of-text problem. The facts are just hidden inside sentences (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").

**Smaller things.**
- "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what the page answers. A heading that says it covers times and bikes would tell the scanning reader they're in the right place (copy edit, taste).
- "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" gives no figure. If the delay can be 5 minutes or 20, a passenger making a connection needs to know which (copy).

**What's working.**
- The reading column is set well: line lengths look about 65 to 72 characters, the leading is comfortable, the rag is gentle, and there are no widows. Keep that measure when you add structure.
- The hierarchy is two levels with real contrast. The bold heading is clearly separate from the regular-weight body, and there's no extra size competing with it. Any subheads you add should come in between, set apart by weight rather than another near-identical size.
- The prose is plain and friendly ("The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle"), and it's clear who does what. Keep that voice in the explanatory text.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the single left-hand column reflows on a phone, and whether the right half of the 1280 px frame stays empty by design or something is meant to sit there (a map or timetable panel, for example). Check that in a live browser.
