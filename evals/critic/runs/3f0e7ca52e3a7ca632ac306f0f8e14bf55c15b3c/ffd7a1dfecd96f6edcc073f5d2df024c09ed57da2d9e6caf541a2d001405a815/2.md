**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops straight into the first paragraph and reads down. It looks like a plain information page from a local transport operator or a town website. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also generic: a default sans-serif column on white, with nothing that says "ferry" or "this river".

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for people who are about to take the river ferry. They come to it to find out when boats run, how long a crossing takes, and what to do with a bicycle.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound. The page lets the passenger down in its words: every fact they would plan a trip around is vague or hidden inside a sentence. A reader can't use this page to catch a boat.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on aren't given, and the timing they do get is vague** (defect). The first paragraph says boats run "between early morning and late evening". The third says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger can't plan around either. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00. "Before dusk" moves by an hour over the winter, and it doesn't say how long before. Whoever reads it the hopeful way misses the last boat. "Every forty minutes" has the same problem: with no first departure, the passenger can't work out when any boat leaves. The fix is to give the actual first and last departure times, plus the date the winter timetable starts and ends ("In winter" doesn't say when that is). Only the operator has those facts. If the times change too often to print, say so and send the reader straight to where the current times are, rather than only saying printed timetables sit "in the shelter at each landing". Someone reading this online is usually not at the landing yet.
2. **"The quay" in the last-crossing line can be read two ways** (defect). The page names two ends, the north landing and the market quay. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" could mean only the market quay. It could also be loose wording for both ends. If the last boat back from the north landing leaves at a different time, a passenger stranded on the wrong bank is the cost. Name the landing, or give the last departure from each end.
3. **Everything is in running prose, so the reader has to read it all to find one fact** (taste, informed by Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). By the rule, the three short paragraphs aren't a dense block. Still, the page answers about four separate questions: when, how long, bikes, winter. Each answer sits mid-sentence, under one headline. A short label above each paragraph, or pulling the times into a two- or three-line summary at the top, would let a passenger find "bikes" or "last boat" at a glance. The current wording would mostly stay as it is underneath.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean, with two levels doing two jobs. The headline is clearly larger and bold, and the body is one comfortable reading size. Keep it that simple when you add structure: section labels should come from weight, not a third size close to the others.
- The paragraphs are well set. The lines run about 65–72 characters, the leading is comfortable for that length, the right edge is ragged and gently uneven, paragraphs are marked by space only, and the contrast is strong. Don't touch any of this.
- The voice is plain and human ("the crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle"). Keep that tone when you add the hard facts.
