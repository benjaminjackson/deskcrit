**First read.** The eye lands on the bold black headline "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three paragraphs of gray-black prose of the same weight. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and well set, and also anonymous: nothing on the page stands out once you're past the headline.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page for riders who need to know when the ferry runs, how long it takes and whether they can bring a bike.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound, but the page doesn't do its job. A rider can't get a single clock time from it, and the winter timetable is hidden in the last paragraph, where someone who only reads the first will miss it. The words are the problem, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **None of the times a rider acts on is a time (defect).** The first paragraph says "early morning", "late evening" and "every forty minutes" but gives no departure times. The third says "before dusk", and dusk moves through the winter. "A little longer when the tide turns" gives no number. These are the facts riders plan around and will hold the operator to. A rider aiming for the last winter boat has to guess when dusk is, and a wrong guess strands them on the far bank. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can also be read two ways: the market quay only, or both landings. The fix is to put in the actual first and last departure times, the winter last-boat time and the dates winter hours apply, and to say which landing each time is for. Only you have those facts.
2. **The timetable is split, and the exception comes last (defect).** Summer hours are in paragraph 1 and winter hours in paragraph 3, with the bike rules in between. A winter rider who stops after the first paragraph, as skimmers do, leaves thinking boats run every forty minutes until late evening. Keep everything about when the ferry runs in one place, with the seasonal change right next to the normal pattern. Move the line about printed timetables and the noticeboard to sit with them.
3. **The page can't be scanned by topic (convention: Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** Each paragraph covers one topic: the schedule, bicycles, then winter and where to find notices. Nothing marks which is which, so a cyclist has to read the schedule first to find the bike rules. Short subheads named after what each section holds, at the next level down from the headline, would let readers jump straight to their part. The page already has clean type levels to support this.

**Smaller things.**
- "Notes for ferry passengers" is a generic headline that could sit on any operator's page. If the ferry has a name or a route name, putting it in tells the visitor they're in the right place.
- "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" is clear, but it doesn't say why. If there's a reason, such as space on deck or safety, one clause would make riders more likely to follow it.

**What's working.**
- The text is set for reading. Body type looks about 19 px, lines run about 65–75 characters, the leading is generous and the rag is even. Keep all of this when you add subheads.
- The hierarchy has two clear levels. The bold headline is plainly above the regular-weight body, through both size and weight, so the eye knows where to start.
- The bike paragraph is the best copy on the page. Every sentence says who does what and when: riders wheel the bikes on after foot passengers board and lock them to the rail. Bring the rest up to that standard.
