**First read.** The eye lands on the white serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops straight to the white search field, and then moves to the 2×4 grid of topic cards. In about two seconds it reads as the help center for a software product called Harbor. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. It also feels familiar enough to be a template, but on a help center that familiarity helps the visitor.

**What it's for.** My assumption: existing Harbor customers who are stuck land here and need to reach the right answer fast, either by searching or by picking a topic.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy, the grid and the palette are sound. The one thing that could actually misdirect a visitor is the topic list: one card describes a different kind of product from the other seven. Everything else is polish.

**What would move it most**
1. **One topic card doesn't fit the product the other seven describe** (defect, if it's not intended). Seven cards describe team software: "Set up your workspace and invite your team," "Connect calendars, chat tools, and storage," "Fix sync problems." The top-right card, "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return," belongs to a store. A newcomer can't tell what Harbor is. Someone with a sync problem may stop to wonder whether they're on the right help center, and someone looking for a delivery may wonder the same. The topic grid is the page's map of the product, and this card breaks it (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). If Harbor really does ship physical goods, make that relationship clear in the card copy. If not, the card is leftover template content and should be replaced with a real topic.

**Smaller things.**
- **Search field:** it has no magnifier icon and no submit button. It reads as a field because of the white box on navy, but nothing says "search" until you read the placeholder. Add the icon inside the field (Norman, signifiers).
- **"Guides" or "articles":** the subhead and the nav say "guides," and the placeholder says "Search for articles." Pick one word so visitors know they're searching the same thing the nav links to (Nielsen heuristic 2).
- **Type scale:** the sizes look bunched. "Browse by topic" (about 18 px bold) and the card titles (about 16 px bold) are almost the same, and the logo, nav, subhead and descriptions all sit between 14 and 20 px. The section heading reads as just another card title. Either give it a clear step up in size or make it a quieter label, and let weight and color separate the rest (Bringhurst 3.1.1).
- **Card descriptions:** several end with one word alone on the last line ("your team.", "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). Across a row of equal cards these ragged endings look careless. Trim the copy or adjust the text width so each line breaks on a phrase (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- **One shared grid:** the logo, "Browse by topic" and the left column of cards share the left edge at about 64 px, and the nav and right column share the right edge at about 1216 px. The 20 px gutters match in both directions. That's why the page feels settled. Keep it.
- **One clear first move:** a single serif headline, the one strong color (the navy band) and one white field give the visitor an obvious place to start. Nothing else on the page competes with search.
- **Topic cards:** each pairs a plain-language title with a short line naming real tasks ("Passwords, two-step sign-in, and recovery"). Visitors recognize their problem without having to guess what a category covers. The thin, consistent line icons help scanning without getting in the way.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see the focus ring on the search field or on the cards, or whether the whole card is clickable (it should be). The capture also ends just below the cards, so I can't tell whether a "Contact us" path or popular articles follow lower down.
