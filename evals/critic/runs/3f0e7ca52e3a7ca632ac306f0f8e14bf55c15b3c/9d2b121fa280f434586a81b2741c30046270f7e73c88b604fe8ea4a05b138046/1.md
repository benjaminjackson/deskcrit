**First read.** The eye goes straight to the white headline, "Plants for shade and small spaces," on a dark wall of leaves. Then it drops to the gray standfirst and across three plain cards, and stops. Nothing comes after the cards. It reads as a small local garden center's homepage: calm, green, tidy, competent. It's also generic enough to belong to any nursery.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the home screen of a local garden center's website. Its job is to get nearby people to come in, book a workshop, or order plants for delivery.

**Verdict.** It's clean and readable, but it fails at its main job: it tells people what's for sale and then gives them nothing to do. The biggest problem is that there's no next step anywhere on the screen. The second biggest is a hero that spends 400 px on generic wallpaper instead of showing the plants it's selling.

**What would move it most**

1. **No next step anywhere on the page.** This is a defect. The eye goes headline → standfirst → cards and then hits a dead end. There's no button and no link in the content, and nothing in the cards looks clickable.
   - **Workshops card:** it says there are only "ten places in each class" but gives no way to book one, no date, and no price. Scarcity with no way to act on it just frustrates people.
   - **Hours and delivery cards:** the hours card says when you're open but not where, and the delivery card says "free within ten miles" without saying ten miles from where. The address only lives behind "Visit" in the nav.
   - **The fix:** make one action clearly primary, tied to what this page sells. If the headline's shade plants are the offer, that action is browsing them; if the workshops matter more, it's booking a seat.
   - Each card should end in its own specific next step (book a workshop place, see the address or directions, start an order) rather than a vague "Learn more."
   - Basis: Material Design and Apple HIG button guidance call for one dominant action per view. Putting the hours next to the address is common sense (Tognazzini's anticipation principle covers it, but it isn't an approved source).

2. **The hero shows wallpaper, not the product, and the headline has to fight it.** This is taste, plus a borderline contrast defect.
   - **Image and headline aren't one idea.** The headline makes a specific promise (shade, small spaces), but the image is an even, all-over leaf pattern. It shows no fern, no hosta, no balcony and no shady corner, so it doesn't support the promise. It also looks like stock, which costs the "grown at our nursery" claim its proof.
   - **The pattern competes with the headline.** It's busy at the same density everywhere, with pale gray-green dots and olive leaves crossing behind the letters, most visibly around "spaces." White bold type at this size mostly holds up. But WCAG's failure technique F83 judges text against the lightest part of the image directly behind it, not the average. Check the worst point behind "spaces" against 3:1.
   - **The standfirst is cut off from its headline.** "Ferns, hostas and compact shrubs…" is the line that actually explains the headline, but the hero's bottom edge puts it in a separate band. It reads as the start of the card section, not the second half of the headline.
   - **The fix:** use a real photo of the plants in a shady or small space. Leave a calm area for the headline, or add a scrim. Either bring the standfirst into the hero under the headline, or shrink the hero so the two read as one unit.

3. **The headline misses the page's left edge by about 16 px.** This is a convention. The logo, standfirst, and all three cards share one left edge at about x=56, while the headline starts at about x=72. The gap is too small to look deliberate, so it reads as a mistake, and it's the one thing that makes this tidy page look unfinished. The rule comes from Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, and Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on placements that are neither clearly here nor clearly there. Fix it by putting the headline on the same edge as everything else.

**Smaller things**
- **Headline line break:** it breaks "small / spaces," splitting the key phrase and leaving one word alone on line two (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Widen the headline box or tighten its spacing so the phrase stays together, or break before "for."
- **Hours card:** "until 8 / pm" splits a time from its unit, in the one fact people will rely on (Bringhurst 2.4.6). Use a non-breaking space.

**What's working**
- **The copy is specific and concrete.** "Ferns, hostas and compact shrubs," "Repotting, pruning and balcony herb beds," "orders over $60, with a plant care card in every box." It sounds like a real shop talking, not a template. Keep that voice when you add the action copy.
- **The type scale is clear.** A serif wordmark, a bold sans headline, a larger standfirst, and bold card titles over regular text. Each level reads at a glance and the faces don't clash.
- **The palette is tight.** One forest green for the wordmark and hero against white and gray, so the brand reads instantly.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the cards or nav items are links, and how they look on hover and focus. If the cards are meant to be clickable, they need to look clickable.
