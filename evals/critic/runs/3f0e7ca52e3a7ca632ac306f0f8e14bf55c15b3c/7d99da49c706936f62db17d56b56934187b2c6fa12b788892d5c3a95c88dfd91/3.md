**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It starts at the top-left line and reads down eight lines that all look the same: same weight, same near-black, and the question lines are if anything a touch *smaller* than the answers. You only figure out it's a Q&A list from the line lengths. It reads as a bakery's pickup FAQ. It feels calm and plain, but flat, like the styles never loaded.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help block on a bakery's online-order or confirmation page. A customer comes looking for one question (change time, someone else collecting, allergies, cancel), finds it, and acts on the answer.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The questions aren't set apart from the answers, so a customer who came for one topic has to read the whole block to find it. Two of the four answers also leave the customer unsure about the one detail they'll be held to.

**What would move it most**
1. **The questions don't read as headings** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are set in the same regular weight and the same dark gray as the answers. They look about a pixel *smaller* (roughly 17 px against 18 px). Spacing is the only thing grouping each pair, so the block scans as one run of text and a customer looking for "cancel" has to read every line to find it. Dair: a difference meant to be seen must be obvious at a glance, and one step is worse than none (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). The fix is to make each question differ clearly from its answer, in weight first. A bolder question at the same size, or a slightly larger one with a weight change, is enough (Dair, chs. 8a–8g and 9). Don't fix it with a big jump in size. The block also has no title saying what it is ("Pickup and orders" or similar, from whatever the page calls it). Without a title, nothing on this screen marks it as help before you start reading.
2. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways** (defect, copy). "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say ahead of what (the pickup time or the bake) or what "a day" means (24 hours, or the end of the day before). This is a deadline a customer will act on and hold you to. Someone who reads it as "the day before, any time" and cancels at 9 pm when you meant 24 hours will lose their money and blame you. Name the exact cutoff against the pickup time. The answer also never says *how* to cancel, unlike the first answer ("Open your order…"). Add the action the same way.
3. **"Along with a name" leaves the collector guessing** (defect, copy). Whose name: the person collecting or the person who ordered? The collector is the one standing at the counter, and if they bring the wrong one, they get turned away. Say which name. A smaller gap of the same kind: "holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" doesn't say what happens after that (is the order resold? refunded?). A customer running late needs to know what they lose.
4. **The allergy answer sends an online customer to the shop** (defect, copy/content). "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps only someone already in the bakery. The person reading this is ordering, or has ordered, online and can't see the card. If ingredient lists exist online, point to them. If they don't, the answer should say so plainly and tell them how to ask the baker *before* collecting. That's a fact only you have.

**Smaller things.**
- The one-line answers ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run about 95–100 characters, above the 45–75 range (Bringhurst 2.1.2). It costs little here because the answers are one or two lines, but narrowing the column by about a quarter would fix it.
- "anything." sits alone as the last line of the allergy answer (Bringhurst 2.4.2). The edits above will likely change the break anyway; check it after.

**What's working.**
- The grouping is right. The small gap between each question and its answer and the much bigger gap between items mean the pairs hold together even with no type contrast. Keep these spacings when you add weight.
- The voice is plain and human, with short sentences and no jargon ("the bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule rather than just stating it). Keep that tone when you add the missing details.
- One face, one color, a clean left edge. With bolder questions, this restraint will look deliberate instead of unfinished.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether "your order" in the first answer is a link (it's styled as plain text here), and whether the empty lower half of the frame is just the end of a short page or a gap in the capture.
