I've assumed the job here, since you told me nothing about the piece. The big problem is that the four headings look the same as the answers under them.

**First read.** I see an even gray block of text with nothing to start from. The eye lands on the top-left line and reads straight down, as if it were one continuous passage. Only on the second pass do the short lines turn out to be questions and the longer ones answers. It feels calm, plain and friendly, but flat.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help or FAQ section on a bakery's online ordering site. Its readers are customers with a pickup order who scan for their one question and want a clear answer.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. Each heading looks the same size, weight and color as its answer, so a reader can't scan for their question, and a scan is the whole point of an FAQ. The cancellation deadline can also be read two ways, and that deadline is the line people will hold the bakery to.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings don't read as headings.** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" look like the answer text: same regular weight, near-black, and within about a pixel of the same size. The only signal is the extra space above each one. So the page reads as eight paragraphs rather than four questions. Someone looking for "cancel" has to read every line instead of glancing down the left edge. Dair calls this weak contrast that reads as an accident, not a choice (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). Make each heading obviously different from its answer in one way: a clearly heavier weight is the usual fix and is enough here. A step up in size works too. Don't change both by a little. **Defect.**
2. **"Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say a day ahead of what, or what "a day" means.** One reader takes it as 24 hours before the pickup time. Another takes it as any time the day before. A customer who picked the second reading may try to cancel at 9 p.m. for a 10 a.m. pickup, get refused, and feel cheated. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" explains why, but not what happens: is the order charged? Name the cutoff against the chosen pickup time, and say what happens after it. Only you have those facts. **Defect.**
3. **"Along with a name" doesn't say whose name.** It could be the person who ordered or the person collecting. If the collector gives their own name and the counter expects yours, they leave without the bread. Say whose name, for example "your name" if that's what's meant. **Defect.**
4. **The lines run too long.** "Give the person your order number…" and "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead…" each run about 95 characters on one line. That's well past Bringhurst's 45 to 75 (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). Measure the column. Narrowing it to roughly 65 characters makes the answers easier to take in at a glance, and there's plenty of empty space on the right to give up. **Convention.**

**Smaller things.**
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer. Narrowing the column will probably fix it. Otherwise edit it to "if you're unsure about anything", or rebreak the line (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- There's no title or intro line above the list, so nothing says what this is or whose page it is. A short heading such as "Your order" or "Pickup questions" at a clearly larger size would give the eye a place to start. This is taste if the page around it already has one.

**What's working.**
- The space shows the groups correctly. The gap from each heading to its answer (about 10 px) is much smaller than the gap between pairs (about 35 px). Keep those ratios when you make the headings bolder.
- The answers are short and practical, and they tell the reader what to do: "Open your order and choose a new slot", "Give the person your order number", "Ask the baker". Keep that tone.
- One typeface, one color, flush left and generous margins. It's restrained and easy on the eye, and stronger headings won't upset that.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the headings are real headings in the markup, or expand and collapse, which would change how a screen reader user scans the list.
- How the column reflows on a phone, which is where most pickup customers will read this.
