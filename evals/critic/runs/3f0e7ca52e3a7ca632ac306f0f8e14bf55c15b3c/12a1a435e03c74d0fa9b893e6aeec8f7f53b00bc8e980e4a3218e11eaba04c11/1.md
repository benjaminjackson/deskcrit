**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers and stops there. "3.8" what? "64.20" in what currency? "12.4 active subscribers" can't be literal, so is it thousands? After a moment it drops to the orange bars, which read right away: Saturday is the peak and Sunday falls off. It's calm, tidy and competent, and the top row raises more questions than it answers.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the overview screen of a small bakery's shop admin. The owner opens it to see quickly how the business is doing and when orders land.

**Verdict.** The layout and chart are well made, but the headline numbers don't work. They have no units, no time period and nothing to compare against, so the part of the screen that gets the most attention tells the owner almost nothing. Fix the three cards and this becomes a good dashboard.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three headline numbers have no units** (top row): "3.8" needs a "%", "64.20" needs a currency, and "12.4" needs a "k" or the real count. As shown, a stranger can't read any of them correctly, and "12.4" subscribers looks like a bug. A conversion rate of 3.8 could be a percent or a ratio, and an owner who guesses wrong draws the wrong conclusion. This is a defect. Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": put the unit next to each headline number. It's a copy edit to the values the cards already show.
2. **The numbers have no period and no comparison** (same cards). Even with units, the owner can't tell whether 3.8% is good or bad. Was it higher last month? Is there a target? The cards don't say what time they cover either. The chart's source line says September 2026, but nothing ties the cards to it, so the owner has to assume or remember the period (Nielsen, heuristic 6: recognition rather than recall). Add the period to each card, plus a comparison such as the change from the previous period or a target (Few). There's also no visible control to change the period, which an owner checking this often would expect. A date-range picker by "Shop overview" is the usual answer. This is a defect.
3. **The chart title names the topic but not the finding** ("Orders by delivery day"). The chart's real story is plain: orders build all week to a Saturday peak, and Sunday is the weakest day. The owner can see that, but a title that says it turns the chart into a decision about staffing and baking. This is taste. My source entry for it isn't on the approved list, so I'm arguing it on its own terms. Rewrite the title to state the pattern the bars already show, in words the owner would use.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and clean: bars start at zero, ticks are even, gridlines are faint, there's one hue for one series, and days run in their natural order. Keep all of it when you add the takeaway.
- The source line under the chart ("Crumbwell order system, orders delivered in September 2026") is exactly the context the cards lack. Use that same pattern for the cards.
- The type scale and spacing are disciplined: one family, a clear jump between label and value, and one grid governing the cards and the chart panel. The burnt-orange wordmark and bars give a small brand touch without noise.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover, focus states on the nav, and whether the bars show exact values on hover. The last one matters if owners need exact counts.
