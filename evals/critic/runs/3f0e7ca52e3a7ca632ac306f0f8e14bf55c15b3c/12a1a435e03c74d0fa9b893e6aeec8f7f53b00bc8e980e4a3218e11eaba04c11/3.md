**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and stalls, because none of them says what it is measured in. It then drops to the orange bars, where Friday and Saturday stand tall and Sunday is short. It reads as the owner's dashboard for a small bakery's online shop: calm, tidy and well built, but the top row raises questions instead of answering them.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's at-a-glance view of how the shop is doing, used to check health and plan delivery days.

**Verdict.** The layout, the type and the chart are solid, but the dashboard fails at its first job. The three headline numbers have no units, no time period and no comparison, so the owner can't tell what they mean or whether they're good. Fix the KPI row before anything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three KPI numbers carry no unit, so they can be read more than one way (defect).** The figures are in the cards across the top.
   - "3.8" under Checkout conversion rate is most likely 3.8%, but nothing says so.
   - "64.20" under Average order value has no currency.
   - "12.4" under Active subscribers is the worst. A count of people can't be 12.4, so it has to be 12.4 thousand, a percent, or something else, and the reader has to guess. If they read 12,400 and the real figure is 12.4% of customers, the size of the business is wrong by an order of magnitude.

   These are the numbers the owner acts on and repeats to others, so an ambiguous one does real harm. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Tufte (*Visual Display*, ch. 2): the reader should never have to guess what a number measures. Fix: put the unit right on the number ("%", the currency symbol, or a "k" or full count), in the same weight or a lighter one.

2. **No period and no comparison, anywhere on the screen (defect).** The cards don't say whether 3.8 covers today, this month or all time. Only the chart's source line, in small gray type at the bottom, mentions September 2026, and the owner can't safely assume it applies to the cards too. With no previous period, target or trend beside each number, the owner can't tell a good month from a bad one, and that comparison is the reason to open a dashboard in the first place. Fix (Few): add the time period and a comparison, such as the change from last period, next to each headline number. State the period once, prominently, near "Shop overview" (a date-range control there is the usual way), so every figure on the page is tied to it.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Orders by delivery day" names the topic and leaves the reader to find the point: weekend peak, Sunday dip. Saying the finding in the title or in a short note on the bars would make the chart useful for planning at a glance. This is my own judgment, not a published rule.
- The y-axis has no unit. "Orders" is implied by the title, but labeling the axis (or adding "orders" to the 1,200 tick) removes the guess (Tufte, ch. 2).

**What's working.**
- **The page reads as one system.** One sans family, three clear type levels (page title, card label, big figure), and one accent orange shared by the wordmark and the bars. Keep this restraint when you add units and deltas: make them smaller and lighter, not new colors.
- **The chart is honest and quiet.** Bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, the days are in natural order, there's one hue and no legend, and the source line is dated. Nothing competes with the data.
- **The grid holds.** The three cards and the chart panel share edges and gutters, and the margins are generous, so the screen scans in one pass.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards change with a date filter or link through to detail. Whether "Active subscribers" means a count or a rate in the underlying data: check that before choosing its unit.
