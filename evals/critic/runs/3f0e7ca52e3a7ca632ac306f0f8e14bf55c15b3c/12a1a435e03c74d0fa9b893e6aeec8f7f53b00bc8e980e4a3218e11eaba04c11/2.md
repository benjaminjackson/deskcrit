**First read.** The eye goes to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and stops there, because none of them says what it measures. Then it drops to the orange bars, where Saturday peaks and Sunday falls off. It reads as a calm, tidy owner's dashboard for a small online bakery, close to a stock template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the shop owner's at-a-glance home screen, used to see how the business is doing and where to look next.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are clean and honest, but the dashboard doesn't do its main job. The three headline numbers have no units, no time period and no comparison, so the owner can't tell what they mean or whether they're good.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units, and two of them can be read more than one way (defect).** Top row, all three cards.
   - "3.8" under "Checkout conversion rate" is almost certainly a percent, but nothing on the screen says so.
   - "64.20" under "Average order value" has no currency.
   - "12.4" under "Active subscribers" can't be a literal count of subscribers. It means either 12.4 thousand or 12.4 percent of customers. Read the wrong way, the subscriber base is off by a factor of about a thousand. This is the number an owner repeats to an investor or a partner.
   - Fix: put the unit next to each value, using the % sign, the currency symbol, or "k". This is an editorial fix that takes seconds and removes the biggest misreading on the page.
2. **The numbers have no period and nothing to compare against, so they can't say good or bad (defect; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").**
   - A bare "3.8" doesn't tell the owner whether conversion went up or down, or whether it's on target. The KPIs are the most prominent thing on the page and they answer neither question.
   - The only date anywhere on the screen is the 12 px gray footnote under the chart ("September 2026"). The owner has to guess whether it also covers the KPI cards (Nielsen, recognition rather than recall).
   - There is no date-range control, so the owner can't change the period either (Nielsen 7).
   - Fix: put the period, the change from the previous period, and/or a target next to each number. Put the period once, visibly, near the page title, ideally as a control the owner can change.

**Smaller things**
- Chart title "Orders by delivery day": it names the topic, not the finding. The finding is the Friday–Saturday peak and the Sunday drop to roughly a third of Saturday. A title or note that says this saves the reader the work (taste).
- Chart y axis: it has no label. "Orders" from the title gets the reader most of the way there, but saying "orders delivered" on or near the axis removes any doubt (Tufte, ch. 2, on clear labeling).

**What's working**
- **Restraint.** There is one warm accent, taken from the wordmark, used on the bars and nowhere else. Everything else is neutral, so the data is the only loud thing on the page. Keep this when you add change indicators, and resist adding red and green everywhere.
- **The chart is honest and quiet.** The bars start at zero, the ticks are even, the gridlines are faint, the days are in natural weekday order, and the source is stated. Nothing in it needs fixing except the words.
- **Clean structure.** The cards and the chart panel share one set of edges and one gutter, and the label-over-value pattern repeats exactly in each card. You can add the units, periods and comparisons to it without redrawing anything.
