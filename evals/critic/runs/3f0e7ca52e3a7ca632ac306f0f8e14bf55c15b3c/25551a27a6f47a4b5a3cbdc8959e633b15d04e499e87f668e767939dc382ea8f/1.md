**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board", which is the right place to start. Then it hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals and the reading slows down. It reads as a ferry service's passenger notice for riders planning a trip. The heading feels calm and helpful, but the body feels like a sign that's shouting at you.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells ferry passengers, cyclists included, how the service runs so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but the piece fails at its main job, which is being read. All the body text is in capitals, so every sentence is slow work. The copy also leaves out the facts a passenger actually plans around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body is set in all capitals.** All three paragraphs, 2 to 3 lines each, are in caps at text size. Words in capitals all have the same rectangular outline, so the reader loses word shapes and has to decode letter by letter. On a page whose only content is these paragraphs, that slows the whole read. It also gives a polite notice an alarmed tone that the heading doesn't have. Set sentences and paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions (Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps"). The "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow can stay in caps. *Convention, and here a defect: it slows every line on the page.*
2. **The times a passenger plans around aren't given.** "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone whether they can catch the first boat to work or the last one home. "In winter" doesn't say which months. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say from when. The one place with real times is "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", so someone reading this at home learns the schedule only after they've walked to the dock. These are the lines a reader acts on and holds the service to, and as written each one can be read more than one way. The fix needs facts only the maker has: the first and last departure times, the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, and where the full timetable can be seen before arriving. Then the last paragraph points to a place the reader can reach now. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- "Lock them to the rail" doesn't say whether riders bring their own lock. If they do, say so, because a cyclist who arrives without one finds out at the boat.

**What's working.**
- Every element shares one clean left edge at about x=120. The eyebrow, heading, and paragraphs read as one column and give the eye a single path down.
- The type scale is tight: three sizes with clear jumps (small tracked label, body, large bold heading). The tracked gray eyebrow is exactly the right use of capitals.
- The spacing groups things correctly. The eyebrow sits close to the heading, there's a larger gap to the body, and the paragraph gaps are even. The lines are a comfortable length and the line spacing is generous.
