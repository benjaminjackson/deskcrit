**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board" right away, which is correct. Then it hits three blocks of solid capitals and slows down: the body reads as one gray slab, and you work through it word by word instead of scanning it. It reads as a plain, calm notes page for ferry passengers, and the capitals make it feel like a notice shouting at you.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells first-time ferry passengers what to know before a crossing (times, bicycles, winter service), read on a laptop or phone.

**Verdict.** The structure is clean and the hierarchy is right, but the page fails where it matters most. All the body text is set in capitals, so the information people came for is the slowest part to read. And the times that people will plan around are too vague to act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body is set in all capitals** (defect, convention). All three paragraphs, about 140 words, are uppercase at body size. Capitals take away the word shapes that readers recognize, so every line reads as an even row of rectangles. The reader slows down, loses their place more easily, and the tone shifts from helpful to stern. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says to keep capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions, and not to capitalize whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read." Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. The "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow is the right place for capitals, and the contrast with it will help once the body is mixed case.
2. **The times are too vague to plan around** (defect, copy). The details a passenger will act on are "between early morning and late evening," "every forty minutes," "in winter," and "one boat each hour." Read cold, none of them tells you whether you can catch a 6:30 boat, when the last one leaves, at what minute past the hour the boats go, or which months count as winter. Someone who reads "late evening" as 10 pm and arrives at 9:45 for a boat that stopped at 9 is stranded on the wrong bank. Fix: replace each vague phrase with the actual first and last sailing times, the departure minutes, and the winter dates. Only the operator has these facts. If the times change too often to print, say where the current ones live. The shelter timetable is only useful once you are already at the landing.
3. **The page does not do what its heading promises** (taste). "Before you board" suggests things to do or know before stepping on. Only the bicycle paragraph is that kind of advice (wait for foot passengers, lock to the rail). The rest is timetable. Either put the before-you-board items first, or keep the heading as is but lead with whatever a passenger most needs at the landing. This is a judgment about order, not a rule.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clear: a small spaced gray eyebrow, a bold headline at about 40 px, and body at about 18 px. That is three distinct levels with no near-duplicates, so the eye knows where to start.
- The single column sits on one left edge at x=120, with a comfortable measure (about 70 characters) and generous leading. Paragraphs are separated by space alone. Keep all of this when you switch to mixed case. Lines will get a little shorter in characters per inch, which is fine.
- The palette is restrained: near-black on white with a gray label. It is calm and highly readable, which suits a utility page.
