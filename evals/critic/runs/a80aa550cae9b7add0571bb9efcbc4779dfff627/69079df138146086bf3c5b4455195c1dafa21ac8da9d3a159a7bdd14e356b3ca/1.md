**First read.** The eye goes straight to the dark bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers," and then drops into pale grey text that is hard to read. It reads as a small help page for a local river ferry. It feels calm and tidy, but faded, as if the content were disabled or still loading. The last line, about the winter timetable, nearly disappears.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web info page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes and how bikes work, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. All the useful information is set in grey that looks too faint to read comfortably. The copy also leaves out the facts a passenger needs to plan around: real times, and when the winter change starts.

**What would move it most**

1. **The body text is too faint to read: defect.** Both paragraphs and the footnote look like a light cool grey (around #9CA3AF) on white. That's roughly 2.5:1 contrast, against the 4.5:1 that WCAG 1.4.3 asks for normal-size text. Check the actual hex to confirm. This text is the whole message, not secondary detail. Readers on a phone in sunlight at the landing, or anyone with weak eyesight, will strain or give up. Only the headline is dark, so the page reads backwards: the label is loud and the content is quiet. Set the body in a near-black or dark grey that clears 4.5:1. If you want the headline to stand apart, use weight and size for that (it already has both), not paleness in the body.

2. **The one warning is the least visible line on the page: defect.** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." is smaller than the body and the same pale grey, so it reads as fine print. Yet it's the only line that tells the passenger to do something, and missing it could mean a wasted trip. Give it at least body size and full contrast. Set it apart with weight, a short label like "Winter timetable," or a tinted panel, rather than shrinking it.

3. **The copy leaves out what a passenger plans around: defect.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing and no departure minute, so nobody can time their arrival at the landing. "Changes in winter" doesn't say from when or how. A reader could take "winter" as December to February or as the clocks changing. The wrong guess leaves them waiting for a boat that isn't coming. State the first and last departures, the minutes past the hour (or a link to the timetable), and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends. If those aren't fixed, say where to find them besides the physical noticeboard.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count three sizes. The footnote (~16 px) sits only a few pixels below the body (~19 px), so it reads like a slip, not a separate level. Keep two text sizes plus the headline, and mark the note with weight or position instead.
- Line length in the first paragraph looks like about 75 characters, at the top of Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range. Narrowing the column a little would make it more comfortable.
- "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" works, but the bike rule would scan faster as two short instructions ("Wait until foot passengers have boarded. Lock your bike to the rail on the lower deck.").

**What's working.**
- The headline's weight and size clearly beat the body, with no other effects. Keep that contrast and let the body become readable underneath it.
- The structure is right: short paragraphs, each on one topic (schedule, bikes, winter), with even, generous space between them. It's easy to skim once the text is dark enough.
- The single flush-left column with a wide left margin and lots of open space suits a short notice. The empty right side isn't a problem here.
