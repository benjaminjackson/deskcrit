**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a web page that lets ferry passengers plan a spring crossing: when the boats leave, from where, and until when.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The label says "Spring timetable", but there isn't a single time, date or first or last sailing on the page, so a passenger can't plan anything from it. The headline uses all the visual weight on a description, and none of it goes to the information people came for.

**What would move it most**

1. **There is no timetable on the timetable page.** *Defect.* The whole visible area holds a label, a headline and two sentences. The only numbers are "every forty minutes" and "about twelve minutes". A passenger still can't answer the three things they came to find out:
   - What time is the first boat?
   - What time is the last boat?
   - Which dates count as "spring through early summer"?

   "By daylight" makes the last one worse, because the daylight window changes week by week across the season. Above the fold, give the hard facts the viewer will hold you to: the season's start and end dates, the first and last departure from each landing, and the departure times themselves (or "every 40 min from 07:00 to 19:40"). If the timetable sits below this screenshot, it has still been pushed below the fold by a headline, on a page whose only job is the timetable. (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: the page should be self-evident.)

2. **The key line can be read two ways, and it's the line people will act on.** *Defect.* "Runs from the north landing to the market quay every forty minutes" can mean either of these:
   - A boat leaves each landing every 40 minutes.
   - A boat leaves the north landing every 40 minutes, so a return from the market quay comes less often, or at times you don't know.

   A passenger who picks the wrong reading waits at the wrong landing or misses the last boat back. "In calm weather" also raises a question the page never answers: what happens when it isn't calm? Are boats delayed, or cancelled, and where would you find out? Say both directions out loud ("Boats leave both landings every 40 minutes"), and add one line pointing to where weather changes are posted.

3. **The headline is too big for how long it is, and it's a label, not a message.** *Taste, with a cost.* Four lines of display bold for "Quayside ferry crossings by daylight, spring through early summer" is a noun phrase with no verb. It makes the viewer read a sentence at poster size before they learn anything they can use. The words also repeat the "Spring timetable" label above it and set up information the page never delivers. Cut it to a short name ("Quayside ferry"), or make the headline itself the most useful fact ("Every 40 minutes, 7am to 8pm, 1 March – 30 June"). Then give the space you free up to the times.

**Smaller things**
- The paragraph ends with "weather." alone on its last line. Rebreak it or adjust the measure (Bringhurst on runts).
- The headline's last line, "summer", hangs alone as a one-word line. Rebreak the headline at phrase boundaries, which will happen anyway once it's shortened.
- The "Spring timetable" label is small and mid-grey. It looks like it passes contrast, but measure it against 4.5:1, since it's the only thing that names the page.

**What's working**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes with big steps between them (small bold label, display headline, body text). Each size clearly has its own job. Keep that discipline when you add the times.
- Everything shares one firm left edge, and the generous margin keeps the page calm and readable.
- The body line length looks close to 70 characters, inside the comfortable range (Bringhurst), with easy line spacing.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- The empty lower third and right half could be a page that hasn't finished loading, or a timetable that sits further down. Check in a live browser whether the departure times exist and how far a visitor has to scroll to reach them.
