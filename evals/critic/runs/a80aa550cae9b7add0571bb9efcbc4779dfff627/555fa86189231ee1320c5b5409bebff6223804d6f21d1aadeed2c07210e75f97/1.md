**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the big serif headline, "Small-batch ice cream, made on Mill Street", then drops to the three red script subheads. In version 1 the eye then settles into the body copy and reads it easily. In version 2 the body copy is also in the script face, so the three columns become a dense, bouncy texture that you have to decode word by word. Both feel warm and local, but version 2 is harder work.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of a small local ice-cream shop. Its job is to tell nearby people what makes it special and get them to visit, check the flavours, or book a party.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The only real change is that version 2 sets the three body paragraphs in the brush-script display face instead of the plain sans. That makes the page's main content much slower to read and muddies the hierarchy, and nothing is gained in return. Version 1's biggest remaining problem is that the page asks the visitor to do nothing and shows no ice cream.

**What changed, and why it matters**
- **Body text in script (version 2, the three columns under "Our milk / Our fruit / Our tubs").** Script letters are joined, slanted, and vary in width, so word shapes blur together. Five-line paragraphs in it read as texture, not text. This is a **defect**: visitors skim or skip the copy that carries the shop's story (eleven miles up the valley, fruit from the Saturday market, fifty pence off for returning a tub). It also breaks the hierarchy. The script was what made the subheads and logo stand out, and now it is everywhere, so the red subheads separate from their paragraphs only by colour. A display script is for a few words at a large size, not for running text (Bringhurst; Butterick, *Practical Typography*). Version 1 does it right: script for the logo and subheads, a plain sans for reading.
- Version 2 also packs more words per line (about 50 to 55 characters) because the script is narrower. That's within range and changes nothing.

**What would move version 1 most**
1. **No next step (defect).** The page ends at the hours strip and leaves the bottom third empty. There's no link to today's flavours and no prompt to visit or book a party. The nav up top offers these, but nothing in the page leads to them. A visitor who likes what they've read has nowhere to go. Fix: after the three columns, add one specific ask, such as "See what's in the case today" pointing to Flavours, plus a secondary link for parties.
2. **No ice cream on an ice-cream page (taste, strongly held).** Everything here is words. For a product people choose by appetite, a photo of a scoop or the flavour board would do more than any paragraph, and it would give the eye a focal point beyond the headline. Fix: one strong image near the headline.
3. **The visit info is incomplete (defect).** The hours strip says when the shop is open but not where it is. "Mill Street" appears only in the headline, with no number and no town. Someone ready to go still has to search. Fix: put the address in the hours strip (with a map link) and make the strip a bit more prominent, since it's the information visitors act on.

**Smaller things.**
- The nav links (top right) are small and light next to the logo. They could be a little larger or darker.
- "Our tubs" is about packaging, but the fifty-pence return offer is the more useful hook and sits mid-paragraph. Lead with it.

**What's working.**
- In version 1 the type does clear jobs: a heavy serif headline, script only for the brand voice in the logo and subheads, and a plain sans for reading. Keep that split.
- The headline is specific and local ("made on Mill Street"), and the body copy is concrete: Monday and Thursday collections, the dairy eleven miles away, flavours leaving the board when out of season. It sounds like a real shop, not a template.
- The palette is warm and cohesive, and the dark plum text on cream has strong contrast.

Better: version 1
