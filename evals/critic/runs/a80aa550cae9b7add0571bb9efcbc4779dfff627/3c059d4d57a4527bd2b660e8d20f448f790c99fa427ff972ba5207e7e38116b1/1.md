**First read.** My eye goes first to the headline, "Plan the week with your whole team", then across to the blue "Create account" button, and then up into the form. It reads as the sign-up page for a team-planning SaaS called Tallyboard. It feels calm, tidy and competent, but also anonymous: nothing on the page shows what the product looks like or what makes it different.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a desktop sign-up page that turns a first-time visitor, probably a team lead, into a new free workspace.

**Verdict.** The layout works: one clear headline, one clear action, and a short form. What lets it down is the form itself. Every field is labelled only by its placeholder, so the labels disappear as people type. The copy is also generic and leaves the free-plan limit unclear, which is the one thing the viewer will hold you to later.

**What would move it most**

1. **The fields have no labels, only placeholders (defect).** All six inputs on the right card are labelled only by grey placeholder text. Once someone types, "At least 10 characters" disappears, so a person on character six of their password can't check the rule. The field also never says it is a password. It only shows a length rule. "name@company.com" is an example, not a label, so a person with a Gmail address may wonder whether they're allowed to use it. Once the form is filled in, nobody can check their entries before submitting, because every field now shows only their own text. Nielsen Norman Group flags placeholder-only labels as harmful for exactly these reasons. Fix: put a short, persistent label above each field ("Work email", "Password", "Company name"). Move "At least 10 characters" into helper text under the password field, where it stays visible while typing.

2. **"Free for up to five people. You can invite the rest of the team later." can be read two ways (defect, on a commitment line).** Reading A: the free plan stops at five, and adding the rest means paying. Reading B: you don't have to invite everyone now, and adding them later is free. The headline says "your whole team", which pushes people toward reading B. A team of eight who sign up believing B will feel misled at person six. Fix: say what happens at six in plain words ("Free for up to five people. Larger teams pay $X per person"), or drop the second sentence if the limit is soft.

3. **The pitch could be anyone's (defect).** "Plan the week with your whole team" fits every planning tool on the market. Nothing next to it shows what planning in Tallyboard looks like. The whole left column is one headline, two lines of text and empty space below. The viewer can't see anything that sets this apart from what they already use, so the page gives them no reason to sign up rather than leave. Fix: use that empty space below the copy to show the product. One screenshot of a team's week, or a single concrete line set against what they do now ("Replace the Monday spreadsheet…"), would let them judge the value against something they already know. That does more than a claim they have to take on trust.

**Smaller things**
- **Field borders:** the input borders look pale blue-grey on white, probably under the 3:1 needed to see where a field is (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure them and darken them if they fall short.
- **"Sign in" link:** under the button, "Sign in" has the same colour and weight as "Already have an account?", so it doesn't look clickable. Underline it or give it the link colour. That line also looks about 13 px; bring it up to body size.
- **Type scale:** I count about seven sizes. The logo (~20), the form heading (~22), the body text (~18), the inputs (~15) and the button (~16) sit only a few pixels apart, so they read as near-misses rather than levels. Keep the headline, one body size and one small size, and use weight for the rest.
- **Required fields:** nothing says which fields are required. Company name in particular could be optional. Mark the optional ones, or cut them.
- **Button text:** "Create account" just repeats the card heading. "Create free workspace" would tie the click to what the left column promises.

**What's working**
- **One primary action:** the only saturated element besides the logo is the blue button, so the eye never has to look for what to do. Keep the accent colour reserved for it.
- **Split layout:** the pitch on the left and the task on the right share a top line (the logo lines up with the form heading), which makes the page read as one composed piece.
- **Name pair:** first and last name sharing a row is the right exception to a one-column form. They are a short, closely related pair.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the fields show errors next to the field, in words, and keep what the user typed.
- Whether focus states are visible against those pale borders.
