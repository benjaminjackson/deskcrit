**First read.** The eye goes to the big blue "Planning a vegetable bed" and then drops down the left edge from one blue subhead to the next. It reads at once as a short beginner's how-to on a website or in a document. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, and a bit unfinished. The last section is a single line, and below it about a third of the page is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming a short guide for a first-time gardener, meant to be read through once and then glanced back at while working outside.

**Verdict.** The structure works: one title, clear section heads, and short paragraphs you can scan. What holds it back is that it doesn't commit to being a sequence of steps. The first step has no heading, nothing is numbered, and the steps leave out the facts a beginner needs to act, so it reads like notes and not like a plan.

**What would move it most**
1. **The first step has no heading, so it reads as an intro (defect).** The paragraph under the title ("Choose a spot… Mark the corners…") is a real step: where to put the bed and how to mark it out. It's styled the same way as the paragraphs under the subheads, but it has no subhead of its own. Someone scanning the blue heads sees soil → plants → watering and skips choosing the spot. Every other step is labelled, so the one unlabelled step breaks the page's own pattern. **Fix:** give it a heading of its own, such as "Choosing the spot", or turn it into a one-line intro that says what the page covers.
2. **It's a sequence that doesn't look like one (defect).** The order matters here. You choose the spot, then dig, then sow, then water. But the headings are unnumbered nouns, so nothing tells a beginner that the order is the method. If you're glancing back mid-task with dirty hands, numbers tell you where you are. **Fix:** number the headings ("1. Choose the spot", "2. Prepare the soil"…) and use the same verb form in every one. Right now the page mixes "Preparing" and "Choosing" with "Watering".
3. **The copy leaves out the numbers people act on (defect).** A beginner follows instructions literally, so the missing numbers are the ones they'll get stuck on:
   - "Mark the corners" doesn't say how big the bed should be.
   - "the depth of a spade" is fine, but "mix in compost" doesn't say how much.
   - "Sow in short rows" doesn't say when to sow or how far apart.
   - "Watering" gets one line, which makes it look like an afterthought next to the other sections.

   "Six hours of sun" is the one line that gets this right. It's specific, and you can check it against your own garden. **Fix:** give every step at least one figure like that: a size, an amount, a date or a spacing.

**Smaller things.**
- **Line length:** body lines run about 85–90 characters (for example, "Dig to the depth of a spade… Rake the surface level"), which is longer than Bringhurst's 45–75 (*Elements*, §2.1.2). Narrow the text column to about 600–650 px. The empty right side is already there to take up the slack.
- **Blue headings:** in a web context, blue can suggest a link. If the page will have real links, make them look clearly different from the headings, for example with an underline.
- **Line spacing:** it looks a little tight for lines this long. Narrowing the column fixes most of this.

**What's working.**
- **Type scale:** there are three clearly separate levels (title, subhead, body), all in one family. Hierarchy comes from size and weight together, and no two sizes are close enough to blur. Keep it.
- **Spacing:** every subhead sits clearly closer to its own paragraph than to the one before it, so each section holds together as a unit.
- **Plain, friendly copy:** lines like "things that forgive mistakes" speak to a nervous beginner in their own terms. Keep that voice when you add the missing numbers.
