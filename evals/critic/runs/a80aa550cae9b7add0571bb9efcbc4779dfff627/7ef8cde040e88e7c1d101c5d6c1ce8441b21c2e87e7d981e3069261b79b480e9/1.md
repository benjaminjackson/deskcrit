**First read.** The eye goes first to the bold serif headline, which is the only heavy thing on the page. It then drops to the left column, which is larger and darker, and reads it as the main text. The right column looks like a sidebar or a caption. The page feels calm and handsome but unfinished: everything sits in the top 45% of the frame and the bottom half is empty white.

**What it's for.** Nothing was given, so I'm assuming this is a small web page or section for a community boat shed, meant to make a local reader want to come and volunteer.

**Verdict.** The type is well made and the writing is warm, but the page undercuts its own purpose. The paragraph that asks people to volunteer is set smaller and quieter than the scene-setting paragraph, and it gives a reader nothing to act on: no time, no place, no way to say yes.

**What would move it most**

1. **The ask is the quietest thing on the page** (defect). The right column ("Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings…") is the only text that asks the reader to do anything. It is set at about 17 px against about 20 px on the left, with tighter line spacing. So it reads as a footnote to the description, and a skimming reader takes in the mood and leaves without seeing the invitation. Promote it. Either make the invitation the larger column, or keep the description first and give the invitation its own clearly different level: a short bold line such as "Come and help on Thursdays", then the details.

2. **The invitation can't be acted on** (defect, in the copy). "Thursday evenings" has no time and no address, and there is no contact, sign-up, or "just turn up". The reader has to work out how to join and whether they're allowed to simply arrive. "Nobody needs experience" and "a spare apron on the hook by the door" are the best lines on the page, because they remove the newcomer's two worries: skill, and feeling like an outsider. Finish the job. Give the exact time and place and say plainly what to do, e.g. "Thursdays, 6–9pm, at the shed at the end of [lane name]. Just turn up." This is a date and time someone will act on, so it has to be exact.

3. **Two body sizes that don't read as two levels** (convention: Bringhurst, *Elements*, §3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). Counting the headline, I see three sizes: about 56 px bold, about 20 px, and about 17 px. The two body sizes are close enough that they look like a mistake rather than a choice, and the line spacing differs as well. As a result the lines in the two columns sit on different baselines and drift apart down the gap (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Either set both columns at one size and leading, and use weight or position for any difference, or make the step big enough to read as deliberate. Combined with item 1, the better move is one body size plus a bold lead line for the invitation.

4. **The bottom half of the page is leftover space, not designed space** (taste). The content sits in a band across the top and roughly 400 px below it is empty, so the page looks like it's waiting for content that never arrived rather than calm and roomy. If this is the whole piece, give the space a job. A photo of the shed or the dinghies would do the most here: the copy is all about smell, tide, and timber, and the page shows none of it. Otherwise, move the text block lower so the white space frames it.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Notes from the boat shed" sets a mood but doesn't say what the place is or that it wants people. A short line under it (e.g. "A volunteer-run boat restoration shed in [town]") would help a stranger.
- The left column ends on a long last line and the right on a short one ("anyone who wants to help."). Neither is a runt, so this is fine as is. Just keep checking it when the copy is edited.

**What's working.**
- The headline is a properly heavy serif with tight spacing, and it clearly outranks the text. There is one obvious place to start.
- Both columns have a comfortable line length (roughly 50–60 characters) and are set flush left in a readable text serif, with good contrast between dark grey and white.
- The writing is specific and sensory ("clinker dinghies wait on trestles", "tar and cut cedar"), and the invitation's reassurances are exactly right. Keep the voice as it is.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty lower half is the design or a screenshot cut before more content (an image or footer) loaded or scrolled in. Check this in a live browser before acting on item 4.
- How the two columns stack on a phone. If they stack, the invitation lands second either way, which makes item 1 matter more.
