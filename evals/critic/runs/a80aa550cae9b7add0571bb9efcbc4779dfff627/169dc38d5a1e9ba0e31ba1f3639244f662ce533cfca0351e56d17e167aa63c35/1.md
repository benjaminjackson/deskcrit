**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three grey-black paragraphs of equal weight. It reads as a calm, plain information page for a small local ferry. It feels tidy and trustworthy but flat: nothing in the body stands out, so there's no quick way to answer "when is the next boat?"

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or notice that tells riders when the ferry runs, how long it takes and the rules for bikes, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is competent, but the page doesn't do its job. The facts a passenger acts on are vague and buried in running prose, and the page sends readers to a printed timetable somewhere else for the real answer.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times can't be acted on (defect).** The first and third paragraphs give "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "one boat each hour" in winter, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". The reader has no first or last departure time, no definition of winter, and no way of knowing which quay "the quay" is. "Before dusk" moves by hours across the season. Someone planning a return trip can't tell whether they'll be stranded, and the line can be read two ways: the last boat from the market quay, or the last boat in either direction. If the reader guesses wrong, they miss the last crossing. Fix: state clock times for the first and last boats in each direction, the date range for the winter timetable, and the landing each last departure leaves from. If the times truly vary, say how a passenger finds the time for today.

2. **Key facts are buried in prose (convention).** Times, crossing length, bike rules and winter changes all sit in three paragraphs of equal weight with no subheads, so a skimming reader has to read everything to find one number. Text that people skim should be broken up by headings, short paragraphs and lists (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; Nielsen Norman Group on scannability). Fix: add short subheads such as Timetable, Bicycles and Winter and changes, and pull the times out into a small two-column list or table so they're the first thing the eye finds after the title. That also gives the page the clear point of focus it lacks now.

3. **The page points elsewhere for its main content (defect).** "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing, and the noticeboard there lists any changes" tells someone reading this at home that the answer is on a board at the river. Fix: put the timetable, or a link to it, and the current changes on the page itself. Keep the shelter note as a backup.

**Smaller things.**
- The first paragraph says crossings take "a little longer" against the tide. Give a figure ("up to 20 minutes") so the reader can plan around it.
- "around the sandbank." and "high tide." sit as short last lines. Rewrap or adjust the measure so the paragraphs don't end on runts.
- The column runs at the top of the usual line-length range (about 70–75 characters). It's fine, but don't let it get any wider.
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category rather than what's inside. A title like "Ferry times and bike rules" would tell readers what they'll find.

**What's working.**
- The type is restrained and the two-level scale is clear: one bold heading and one body size, set flush left in a sans made for reading. The contrast looks strong. Keep this simplicity when you add subheads, using weight for them rather than a third size close to the others.
- Leading looks close to 1.5 and the space between paragraphs is clear and consistent, so the text is easy to read once someone commits to it.
- The voice is plain and courteous, as in "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle…". Keep it while you make the facts more exact.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see how the column reflows on a phone, which is where most passengers will read it, or whether the empty right half of the screen holds anything at other widths.
