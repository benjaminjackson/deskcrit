**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue 38% wedge, then the serif headline, then crosses to the list on the right. Around the upper-left edge of the pie, nine thin slices with tiny grey "3%" and "2%" labels form a fan of colour that reads as noise. It reads as a calm, well-made annual-report page about cost breakdown. The top half of the story comes across in two seconds. The rest doesn't come across at all.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in a company annual report that tells shareholders and staff where the operating money went, read on screen or in print.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and the list on the right does the job well. The pie doesn't. It has 14 slices, nine of them slivers, and it repeats the list while doing it worse. It also gives only percentages: there's no total, no amounts in money and no comparison with last year, so a report reader can't tell whether anything here is good or bad.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pie has too many slices** (convention; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1). Nine slices between 1% and 4% sit in the upper-left quarter of the pie, each a few degrees wide. You can't compare them by angle, and their outside labels give only a number, not a name. To learn which sliver is "Utilities" you have to match a small swatch on the right against a sliver on the left. That's a lot of looking back and forth for 22% of the money. Fix: keep the four or five largest slices (Salaries, Rent, Cloud hosting, Marketing, maybe Travel) and group the rest into one "Other, 22%" slice. The full breakdown can stay in the list. Or drop the pie for a sorted bar chart with names next to the bars. Either way, every part of the chart becomes readable without a colour key.
2. **14 different hues can't work as a key** (defect; WCAG 1.4.1; Wilke on keeping category colours to a handful). Several pairs are hard to tell apart even with full colour vision: Software licences blue vs Utilities indigo, Rent teal vs Equipment repair pale teal, Training brown vs Cloud hosting orange. Travel red also sits right next to Insurance green, the pair that colour-blind readers most often can't separate. So the swatches only work as a key for the biggest slices. Fix: once the slivers are grouped, at most about five colours remain, and the names can go directly on or next to the slices. If you keep the full list, it can drop the swatches for the small items, because its rows already carry the names.
3. **There's no money and no comparison** (defect for this audience). "Share of total operating costs" never says what the total was. Without a figure for last year, a reader can't tell whether 12% on cloud hosting is going up or is unusual. In an annual report, people will remember these figures and hold the company to them. Fix: put the total in the subtitle (e.g. "of £X.Xm"), add an amount column to the list, and ideally add a column showing the change from fiscal 2025.
4. **The headline names the topic, not the finding** (taste; no approved source, so this is my judgement). "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" makes the reader work out the point from the chart. The finding is plain: people and premises take more than half (54%). Say that, e.g. "Over half of operating costs went on people and premises." Keep the current line as the subtitle so the reader still knows exactly what's being measured.

**Smaller things.**
- The list is about 440 px wide, and each name sits far from its percentage. The thin row rules help, but narrowing the list to about 300 px would shorten the jump between name and number.
- The outside labels on the slivers look about 11 px and mid-grey on cream. They're probably below 4.5:1 contrast, so check it, or remove them once the small slices are grouped.
- About 100 px of empty cream sits under the source line, and the pie-and-list block sits high on the page. Centre the block vertically or make the pie larger.

**What's working.**
- The type works as a set: a small capitalised section label, a serif headline, a serif subtitle and sans-serif data. That's about four clear sizes, each with one job. Keep it.
- The list is sorted by value, has bold right-aligned figures, and the figures add up to 100. It's the most useful thing on the page. Build the fix around it.
- The source line, with its note that shares are rounded, is exactly the trust signal an annual report needs.
