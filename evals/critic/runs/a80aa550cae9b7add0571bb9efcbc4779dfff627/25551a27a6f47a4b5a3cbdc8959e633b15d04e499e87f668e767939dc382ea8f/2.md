**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", which is the right place to start. Then it hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, and the page goes from a calm notice to a dense grey slab that feels like it's shouting. It reads as a short notice for ferry passengers. It's tidy, but the body is hard work to read.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short web or info page telling ferry passengers (some with bicycles) when the boat runs and how boarding works, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but setting the body text in all capitals makes the one part people come to read slow and tiring. The copy also leaves out the facts a passenger would act on: actual first and last sailing times, and when "winter" starts.

**What would move it most**
1. **Running text is set in all capitals (defect).** All three paragraphs, about 8 lines in total, are in caps. Capitals have no ascenders or descenders, so every word becomes the same rectangle. The reader loses the word shapes they skim by and has to read letter by letter. The caps also make the body look louder than the heading above it, which flattens the hierarchy the heading sets up. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says caps are for headings shorter than one line and paragraphs should never be capitalised. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read." Fix: set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case. Keep capitals for the short "PASSENGER NOTES" label only, where they already work.

2. **The copy gives no times a passenger can plan with (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't answer when the first boat leaves or when the last one does. A reader could take "late evening" as 9 pm or 11 pm, and the cost of guessing wrong is missing the last boat home. "In winter" has no dates, and "one boat each hour" doesn't say on the hour or at what minute. The last paragraph then sends the reader to a printed timetable in a shelter, so the page admits the real information lives somewhere else. Fix: state the first and last departures from each landing, the months the winter timetable applies, and link to or include the timetable here. These are the numbers people remember and will hold the operator to.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 2, "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded": this reads as a description, not an instruction. Speak to the reader with "you" ("Wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded and lock it to the deck rail") so cyclists know it's a rule for them.
- "Twelve minutes in calm weather" raises the question of what happens in rough weather (longer crossings? cancellations?). Either answer it or drop the qualifier.
- Once the caps go, check line length. At this column width it looks close to 75 characters in mixed case, so it should be fine, but confirm it hasn't grown past about 75 (Bringhurst).

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: a small tracked label, one bold heading, one body size. Three levels with clear jumps, so each has an obvious job. Keep it.
- Short paragraphs, each on one topic (schedule, bicycles, winter), with generous space between them. The structure is already easy to scan; only the setting fights it.
- "Before you board" is a good heading. It's plain, speaks to the reader, and says when this information matters.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the caps come from CSS `text-transform` or the source text. If it's CSS, the fix is one line. Also how the column reflows on a phone, where most ferry passengers would read this.
