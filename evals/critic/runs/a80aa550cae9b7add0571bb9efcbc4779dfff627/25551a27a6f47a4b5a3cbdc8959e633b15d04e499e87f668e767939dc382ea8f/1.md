**First read.** My eye goes to "Before you board" and then drops into a grey-black block of capitals. The heading looks calm and well set, so the shouting body text below it comes as a surprise. It reads as a short information page for ferry passengers, but it feels like a notice someone typed with caps lock on.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or panel that tells passengers how the river ferry runs before they travel: frequency, bicycles, and winter service.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The text is short and the layout is clean, but all three paragraphs are set in capitals, which makes the only content on the page slow and tiring to read. The copy is also vague on the one thing passengers act on: when the boats run.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set entirely in capitals.** All three paragraphs (about 8 lines) are in capitals at text size. Capitals all have the same height, so words lose the shapes of ascenders and descenders that readers recognise them by. The reader has to spell their way through every line, and the text reads as shouting, which clashes with the polite tone of "Before you board." This is a **convention** with a real cost here: Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps" says not to capitalise whole paragraphs, and Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read." Set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. Keep capitals for the "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already uses them correctly.
2. **The schedule doesn't commit to times.** In the first paragraph, "between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell a passenger whether they can catch a 6:30 boat or a 22:00 boat home. That is the one fact they will plan around and hold the operator to. In the third paragraph, "in winter" has no dates, and "one boat each hour" doesn't say on the hour or at what minute. This is a **defect**: one person reads "early morning" as 6:00 and another as 8:00, and someone misses the last boat. Give the first and last departures from each landing, the winter start and end dates, and say "every 40 minutes" in figures so the eye can find the number. The fact that printed timetables are in the shelters only helps someone already standing at the landing. If there is an online timetable, link to it here.

**Smaller things.**
- Body line length: the capitals lines run to about 75–80 characters and look wide for capitals. In mixed case at the same measure this is less of a problem, but keep lines near 66 characters (Bringhurst).
- "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" describes what happens rather than telling the reader what to do. "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on and lock it to the side rail" says it to the cyclist.
- "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes in calm weather" leaves the reader to guess what happens in rough weather. Either say so (for example "longer in wind") or drop the qualifier.

**What's working.**
- The heading block. A small, letterspaced grey label over a bold heading in a different case is a clear two-level hierarchy that uses weight and colour, not just size. Keep it.
- The structure: one topic per short paragraph (schedule, bikes, winter) with generous space between them, which makes it easy to scan once the capitals are gone.
- The heading "Before you board" speaks directly to the reader's moment. It's better than a label like "Service information."

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't tell how the page reflows on a phone, which is where passengers at a landing will most likely read it. Check that the line length and the generous left margin hold up at narrow widths.
