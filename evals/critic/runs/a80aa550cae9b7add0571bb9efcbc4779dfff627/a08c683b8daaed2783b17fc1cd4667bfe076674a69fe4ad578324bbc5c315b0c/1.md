**First read.** The eye goes to the dark serif headline, then drops to the row of four illustrated cards, and stops there. It reads at once as a local home-services company, and it feels calm, friendly and tidy. After the cards there's nowhere to go. The only button is the small "Book a visit" in the top-right corner, and the eye never travels back up to it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage hero of a local home-services business. Its job is to get a homeowner to pick a service and book, and the subhead describes exactly that flow.

**Verdict.** It looks competent and pleasant, but it doesn't close. The page sets up a booking flow ("Pick a service, choose a day…") and then gives the visitor nothing to press in the place where they're deciding. The prices, which are what they'll hold you to, don't say what they're a price for.

**What would move it most**

1. **The ask is missing where the decision happens.** *Defect.* The subhead tells visitors to "pick a service", but the four cards have no button, link, arrow or label to show they can be picked. They look like static brochure tiles. The one action on the page is the nav button "Book a visit", which is small, far from the cards and outside the reading path. A visitor who has just chosen "Deep cleaning, from $189" has to look for how to act on it (Krug: don't make them think; Norman: signifiers). The label is also vague: book a visit for which service? Make the cards the start of the flow. Give each one an explicit action that names the service, such as "Book window cleaning". Alternatively, put one primary button under the subhead, and keep the nav button as the secondary route.

2. **The prices can be read two ways.** *Defect: this is the line people act on.* "From $89", "From $189", "From $24" and "From $65" have no unit. $24 for laundry could be per bag, per load, per pound or per week. $89 for windows could be per visit, per window or per house size. Visitors will assume the cheapest reading and feel misled at checkout, or assume the worst and leave. Add the unit and the basis: "From $24 per bag", "From $89 for a home up to 10 windows". There's a related contradiction: the headline promises "on your schedule", but the laundry card says "We collect on Monday and bring it back on Wednesday", which reads as a fixed day. If Monday is only an example, say so ("Collected one day, back folded two days later"). If it really is fixed, the headline over-promises.

3. **The headline could belong to any cleaning company.** *Taste, argued.* "Help around the house, on your schedule" names a category, not a reason to pick Fernway. The things that set a local service apart are where it operates, who turns up, and what happens if the job isn't right, and none of those appear above the fold. Even the service area is hidden in the nav. The subhead is good and concrete, so the headline should earn its size by saying something only Fernway can say: the town or area served, or the vetting and guarantee behind "vetted crew".

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "on your / schedule", leaving one word alone on the second line. The text box stops around 700 px while the right half of the hero sits empty. Widen it so the headline fits on one line, or break it at a phrase boundary.
- The type scale has two sizes that look nearly the same: the subhead (about 18 px) and the card titles (about 19 px) don't read as different levels. Make the card titles clearly bigger or bolder, or set the subhead visibly smaller and lighter.
- The window illustration uses a heavy dark outline; the basket, shirt and plant are flat filled shapes. Choose one drawing style for the whole set.
- The prices are small teal text pinned to the bottom of each card. They're the second most important fact on the card and currently the quietest. Give them more weight, or put them next to the card action.

**What's working.**
- The cards follow one consistent system: the same structure, tinted illustration panel, title, two-line description and price, with even gaps between cards. A visitor can compare the four services at a glance. Keep it.
- The subhead is the best line on the page. "Arrives with their own supplies" is a concrete detail that answers a real worry. Write more lines like it.
- The palette (warm off-white, dark slate, teal with coral and mustard accents) and the serif/sans pairing give a calm, trustworthy domestic feel that suits the business.
