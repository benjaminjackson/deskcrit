**First read.** My eye goes first to the bold headline "Tell us about your project", then across to the white card and down through three empty fields. At the bottom of the card it stops on "Send message", which looks like a plain line of text and not like a button. The page reads as a contact form for a small design studio. It feels calm and competent but generic, and the last step of the form disappears.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the contact page of a small design studio's website. Its job is to get a prospective client to send a short project brief.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the copy is honest, but the page fails at its one job: the submit control doesn't look like something you can press. Everything above it is fine, so the whole page hinges on fixing that one line.

**What would move it most**

1. **"Send message" has no button styling** (bottom left of the card). It's set in the same regular weight, size and near-black colour as the intro paragraph. It has no fill, border or underline. A visitor who has just typed a brief reaches the end and finds only a line of text, so they hesitate, hunt, or wonder whether the form is finished. Even the nav and footer links are blue and underlined, so this is the only interactive element on the page with no cue at all. **Defect** (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, affordances and signifiers: buttons look pressable through fill, border or shadow). The fix is a filled button that is clearly the strongest object in the card. Use one accent colour (the link blue would do), make it at least as tall as the inputs, and keep it in line with the fields' left edge. It should be the one high-emphasis action on the page.

2. **The intro asks for a budget and timeline, but the form gives them no place.** The intro says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The only place for them is a single short Message box, which shows about four lines. The visitor has to remember the instruction, which is on the other side of the page, and then format it themselves. Many won't, so you get briefs without the two facts you need to qualify a lead. **Defect** (recognition rather than recall, Nielsen 6). Either add two short optional fields, such as a budget dropdown and a timeline text box, or put a prompt inside the Message box and make it taller. Mark which fields are optional, because right now nothing says what is required.

3. **The card stops short of the page grid.** The nav and logo span from 56 px to about 1224 px. The text column and card end near 1068 px, which leaves a dead strip of about 155 px on the right. Under the card there's a large empty band before the footer. The space looks left over rather than planned, so the composition feels unfinished and slightly off to the left. **Convention** (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*: one grid governs the page). Either run the card to the nav's right edge, or set the two columns to fit the full content width. That also makes the inputs long enough for real email addresses and gives the textarea room.

**Smaller things**
- In the top nav, "Contact" looks the same as the other links even though you're on that page. Give the current page a state, such as heavier weight or no underline.
- The input borders are a light blue-grey on white. Check them against WCAG 1.4.11's 3:1 minimum for the boundaries of form controls; by eye they look borderline.
- The headline "Tell us about your project" could sit on any agency's site. Something tied to the studio, or to what happens next, would help. The subline already carries the useful promise, so the headline can afford to be specific.

**What's working**
- The subline: "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a concrete promise with a timeframe. It answers the visitor's real worry, which is whether anyone will read the message. Keep it, and keep it near the form.
- The labels sit above the fields in bold and stay visible. Each label is clearly closer to its own field than to the one above it, so the form reads cleanly from top to bottom.
- The type scale is restrained. There's one large bold headline, one body size, and bold small labels, all in a single family, with three or four clear levels and no near-miss sizes.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- What the button does on hover and focus, and whether it has a visible focus ring.
- How errors and the success message look after sending.
- How the two columns collapse on a phone.
