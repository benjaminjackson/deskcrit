**First read.** The eye goes first to the three big numbers across the top ($4.82M, 112%, 1,246), then drops into the large blue bar chart, then moves right to the small plan table. It reads straight away as the revenue home screen of a SaaS billing or finance tool, for a founder or finance lead. It feels calm, sober and trustworthy. The darkest lines on the page are the chart's black frame and gridlines, which give the middle a boxed-in, slightly heavy look.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a revenue overview for a SaaS company's leadership or finance team, who need to see within seconds how the business is doing and trust the figures enough to repeat them.

**Verdict.** The structure works: the order of importance is clear, every number has a label, a period and a comparison, and the chart is honest. The biggest problem is that two figures on the page appear to disagree about what "2025 revenue" is. A reader who notices will stop trusting the screen, and on a finance dashboard trust is the whole job.

**What would move it most**

1. **The plan table and the chart give two different 2025 revenue totals.** "Revenue by plan, 2025" totals **$4.82M**, which is exactly the "Annual recurring revenue, Dec 2025" figure in the first card. The monthly chart is labelled "recognised revenue, 2025". Read by eye, its twelve bars add up to about **$4.49M** (roughly 312 + 328 + … + 447 thousand). So the page offers two readings:
   - The table is really ARR by plan at December, mislabelled as 2025 revenue.
   - Or 2025 revenue is $4.82M and the chart is wrong.

   With the first reading, someone quotes "$4.82M revenue in 2025" to a board or investor and overstates it by about $330K. Either way, a careful reader finds the mismatch and starts doubting every other figure. There is a smaller oddity too: December's bar (about $447K) times 12 comes to about $5.4M, more than the stated ARR. That can happen with one-off or usage revenue, but nothing on the page explains it. **Defect.**
   - **Fix:** make the table title say exactly what it measures, e.g. "ARR by plan, Dec 2025", or show recognised revenue by plan so it adds up to the chart. If ARR and recognised revenue are meant to differ, add a line saying how.

2. **The chart's frame and gridlines are heavier than they need to be.** The full black box around the plot and the dark gridlines at every 100 are the strongest lines on the screen. They turn the bars into a cage and make the chart read as a spreadsheet object rather than a trend. The gridlines carry almost no information the axis labels don't already give. Tufte's data-ink principle (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*) is about exactly this. **Convention**, and it costs something here because the chart takes up most of the page.
   - **Fix:** drop the frame. Make the gridlines light grey hairlines, or keep only the baseline and a few faint ticks, so the bars carry the weight.

3. **The chart names a topic but doesn't say what happened.** "Monthly revenue, 2025" makes the reader work out the story, which is steady growth of about 43% from January to December with small dips in April and July. Leadership screens get glanced at, so the key point should be said, not left to work out. **Taste**, though most finance readers would benefit.
   - **Fix:** a takeaway subtitle such as "Up 43% Jan–Dec; dips in Apr and Jul", or mark the high and low points on the chart.

**Smaller things.**
- **Grid alignment:** the chart's right edge (about x 960) and the table's left edge (about x 1012) don't line up with the edges of the KPI cards above (about 907 and 925). Snap the chart and table to the same three-column grid.
- **Empty space:** the right column stops at about y 427, leaving an empty block below it next to the chart. Either balance the two columns or give the table more to show, such as each plan's share or growth.
- **Total row:** "Total" looks the same as the plan rows, because every value is bold. Set the plan values in regular weight and keep bold for the total, or put a heavier rule above it.
- **Date range pill:** "Jan to Dec 2025" looks halfway between a label and a control. If it is a picker, give it a chevron or a border; if not, make it plain text.
- **Heading alignment:** "Revenue by plan, 2025" sits a few pixels lower than "Monthly revenue, 2025". Align the two headings on the same baseline.
- **Table row order:** the plans run smallest to largest, so the biggest one, Business, sits just above the total. Consider listing it first.

**What's working.**
- **KPI cards:** every big number has a precise label, a period and a comparison ("+3 pts vs 2024", "+9% vs 2024"). That is exactly what makes a dashboard figure readable at a glance. Keep it.
- **Honest chart:** it starts at zero, states its units ("Thousands of US dollars, recognised revenue") and names its source ("closed invoices only"). That's rarer than it should be.
- **Restraint:** one accent blue, green only for deltas that also carry a + sign, and a type scale of about four clear levels (KPI figures, page title, section heads, body and labels) with no near-duplicate sizes.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date pill and the sidebar items react to hover or focus, and whether the chart has tooltips with exact monthly values.
