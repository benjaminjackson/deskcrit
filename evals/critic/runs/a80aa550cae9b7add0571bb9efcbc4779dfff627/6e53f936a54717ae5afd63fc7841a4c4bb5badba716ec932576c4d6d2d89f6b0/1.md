**First read.** The eye goes to the photo in the first card before it reaches the headline. That photo is the darkest, busiest and most detailed thing on the page, and it shows a grand wood-panelled hall with leather sofas. Next the eye reads the serif headline, then moves across the row of four cards. Overall the page looks calm, warm and trustworthy, a neat local home-services site, except for that one card, which looks like it came from another website.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a homeowner landing on a home-services site needs to see what the company offers and what it costs, then book a visit.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and mostly does its job, but the first card breaks it. A dark photo of a stately interior sits in a row of flat illustrations and pulls the eye away from the headline, and it doesn't look like the small homes this service is for. The second problem is the copy: the prices have no unit, and nothing tells the viewer how to go from a card to a booking.

**What would move it most**
1. **The window-cleaning photo breaks the set and takes over the page** (defect). The other three cards use simple flat illustrations on a pale mint tile, while card 1 uses a full-colour photo of a dark Tudor-style hall. Because it's the darkest and most detailed thing on the page, it becomes the focal point by accident, ahead of the headline. It also says the wrong thing: this is a service for "small yards" and ordinary homes, and the photo shows a manor or club room. You also can't see that the windows are clean, so it doesn't show the service either. The general point is common sense: pictures shown side by side read as one family, so a single odd one looks pasted in. Fix: use one style for all four. The easiest route is a flat illustration of a window and squeegee in the same palette on the mint tile. If you want photos, use them in all four cards with one shared light and colour treatment.
2. **The prices are what people will hold you to, and they don't say per what** (defect). The first three prices have the same problem:
   - "From $89" for window cleaning could be per window, per visit or per house.
   - "From $24" for laundry could be per load, per bag or per week. Since the description says the service runs on a fixed Monday and Wednesday schedule, someone could easily read it as a weekly price.
   - "From $189" for deep cleaning gives no sense of what home size gets that price.

   Someone who books expecting $24 a week and gets charged $24 a bag will feel misled. Fix: add the unit right next to the number, for example "From $24 per bag" or "From $89 per visit (up to 10 windows)".
3. **Nothing connects the cards to booking** (defect / convention: Norman, signifiers; Krug). The subhead promises "Pick a service, choose a day", but the cards have no button, arrow or link label, so the viewer can't tell whether a card can be clicked. The only action is "Book a visit" in the top-right corner, far from where the viewer is deciding. "Book a visit" is also vague: a visit for which service? Fix: give each card a clear action at its bottom edge, such as "Book window cleaning →" beside the price, or make it obvious that the whole card is clickable. The header button can stay as the general fallback.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "…on your / schedule", which leaves one word alone on the second line. Break after the comma instead ("Help around the house, / on your schedule").
- The headline copy is pleasant but could belong to any home-services company. The subhead carries the real promise: vetted crews who bring their own supplies. Consider moving "vetted" or "brings own supplies" into the headline so the viewer sees what's different here.
- The card descriptions vary in length, so there's an uneven gap above each price. Pinning the prices to the bottom works, but the space above "From $65" and "From $89" looks like leftover space rather than a choice. It's minor, and tightening the card height would fix it.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing works: a sturdy serif for the logo and headline, a plain sans for everything else. It feels warm and trustworthy, and the sizes are few and clearly different from each other. Keep it.
- The three illustrated cards (basket, T-shirt, plant) share one palette, one level of detail and one tile colour. They read as one family and match the brand's teal and coral. This is the system the window card should join.
- The laundry description, "We collect on Monday and bring it back folded on Wednesday", is the best line on the page. It's concrete, has a subject and a verb, and tells the viewer exactly what will happen. Write the other three descriptions the same way.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards can be clicked, and what happens on hover.
- What sits below the empty band under the cards. If the page really ends there, it has no reassurance (reviews, service area, how vetting works) before the viewer has to decide. Check in a live browser.
