**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, then drops to the italic standfirst and into the body. It reads as a long-form magazine feature from a small independent title, about a community garden. It feels calm and clean, but neutral: the setting could just as well be a SaaS blog. The second line of the headline holds only the word "line", which is the first thing that looks unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the opening screen of a long-read article on desktop. Its job is to draw a general reader in and keep them reading comfortably for several thousand words.

**Verdict.** It does the basic job: the order of reading is clear, the type is readable and the opening copy is good. But the lines of text are too long for long-form reading, and the headline breaks badly. A story that credits "Photographs by the residents" also shows no picture of the place, so it opens as a plain document instead of a feature.

**What would move it most**
1. **The text column is too wide for a long read.** The body and standfirst run about 85–90 characters per line. For example, "It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to" is one line. Over thousands of words, the reader's eye has to work harder to find the start of each next line, and it tires sooner. Bringhurst gives 45–75 characters as the comfortable range, with about 66 as the ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). The generous line spacing helps, but it doesn't make up for the width. *Convention, and it costs something here because reading comfort is the whole job.* Fix: narrow the text column to roughly 600–640 px at this type size, or raise the body size a step, so lines come in at about 65 characters. Measure a few lines in the browser to confirm.
2. **The headline breaks to leave "line" on its own.** "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" splits the phrase "bus line" and leaves a single word holding up the second line. That makes the biggest element on the page look accidental, and the line break reads as a pause that isn't meant to be there. *This one is taste and common sense: the reference entry for headline breaks has no confirmed source.* Fix: break at a natural phrase, such as "The allotment at the end / of the bus line". You can get that with balanced line wrapping, a narrower heading width, or a manual break.
3. **The feature opens with no image, even though its byline credits the photographers.** "Photographs by the residents" promises pictures, but the first screen shows none. That leaves a headline and three paragraphs of grey text to carry the opening on their own. The resident photographs are the most distinctive thing this piece has. Without them, the page looks templated: an Inter sans, a green label and centred text, which could be anyone's blog, and nothing tells the reader that this is a place with people in it. *Taste, argued on the job: a feature needs an entry point that isn't just text.* Fix: put a lead photograph above the headline or straight after the standfirst, full column width or wider. If the image exists and simply failed to load in this capture, check that in a live browser first.

**Smaller things.**
- Standfirst: it is set at body size and only differs by being italic, and four lines of italic sans is tiring to read. Make it a size larger in roman (upright) type, or a darker or lighter tone, and keep the italic for emphasis.
- Nav, top right: "Subscribe" looks the same as the section links, yet it's the one action that matters to the publisher. Give it a separate treatment, such as weight or a quiet button.
- Nav: the links are run together with "·" separators and set small, so each one is a small target. Space them as separate items with padding.
- Header: the "Groundwork" wordmark and the nav sit on slightly different baselines. Align them.
- Headline: the letter spacing looks like the text default at display size. Tighten it slightly (Bringhurst: display type needs less spacing than text).

**What's working.**
- The copy. The standfirst places the reader in a specific scene ("the queue for the shared water tap is the best place in the district to hear the news"). The first paragraph opens on a person, a date and a condition, and "Most of them are still in use" lands as its own short paragraph. Keep that voice and that rhythm of short paragraphs.
- The hierarchy is clean, with four clear levels: label, headline, byline, then standfirst and body. Each one is plainly different from the next, so the eye never hesitates.
- The quiet colour: neutrals plus one green for the "Feature" label, which fits the subject. Keep the green that restrained.
