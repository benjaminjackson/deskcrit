**First read.** The two versions are identical apart from alignment. Version 1 sets everything flush left on the same left edge as the "Kiln Yard Studio" logo. Version 2 centres the eyebrow, headline, both paragraphs and the price box, while the logo stays at the left. In both, the eye lands on the serif headline, reads down through the description, and stops on the price box with its rust-coloured button. It reads as a calm, honest course page for a local pottery studio.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a landing page that gets adult beginners to book a place on an eight-week evening pottery course.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. Centring in version 2 makes the two body paragraphs, which explain what you learn and what's included, harder to read. It also breaks the page's only alignment line, and it gains nothing in return.

**What changed and why it matters**
1. **The body copy is centred in version 2** (defect). Each paragraph runs three or four lines, and centred, every line starts at a different place. After each line the eye has to hunt for where the next one begins. This copy is what persuades: what you'll make, that clay and firings are included, that you keep your pieces. Making it harder to read weakens the case for booking. Centring suits a line or two of display type. It works against running text (Bringhurst; Butterick). Version 1's flush-left text with a ragged right is the right call.
2. **Version 2's centred column no longer lines up with the logo** (convention). In version 1 the logo, eyebrow, headline, paragraphs and price box all share one left edge at about x=88, so the page holds together as one block. In version 2 the logo sits alone at the far left and the content floats in the middle, so the header looks like it belongs to a different page (Müller-Brockmann, grids).
3. **The price box reads worse centred.** In version 1 the price, the details and the button stack on one left edge, so you can scan them as a list. In version 2 the three short centred lines, "Wednesdays, 6:30 to 9 pm", "Starts 8 January" and "Eight places", each start at a different point. They read as loose lines, not a set of facts.

**Remaining findings for version 1**
1. **"Eight places" can be read two ways** (defect, and it sits right next to the booking button). It could mean the class has eight places in total, or that eight are still free. One reading says "small class". The other says "no rush" or "act now". Say which one, e.g. "Eight students per class" or "5 of 8 places left".
2. **Nothing shows the result** (taste). This is a hands-on craft course, and the copy promises mugs and bowls you take home. The page has no picture of a student's mug, the wheels or the studio. The right half of the screen sits empty at this width, and a photo there would sell the course better than more text could.

**Smaller things**
- "$320" doesn't say whether that's per person for all eight weeks. Add "for 8 weeks" or "per person".
- "Starts 8 January" has no year or weekday. A weekday is implied by "Wednesdays", but the year is worth adding if the page could stay up across New Year.

**What's working**
- The headline says exactly what the course is and who it's for, in plain words. It works as the single dominant element.
- The description speaks to the reader ("you will learn…", "You take home everything you make") and names real outcomes, not vague benefits.
- The price box gathers price, schedule, start date and the ask in one tinted block. The button names the exact action, "Book a place", and it's the only saturated colour on the page apart from the eyebrow.
