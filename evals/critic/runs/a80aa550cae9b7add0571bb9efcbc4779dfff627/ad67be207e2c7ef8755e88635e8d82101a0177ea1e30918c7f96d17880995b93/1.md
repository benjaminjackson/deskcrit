**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board", the only heavy, large element, then down into three short paragraphs of body text. It reads as a practical notice for ferry passengers: calm, plain and trustworthy, like a well-set help page. Nothing competes with the heading, and the reading order is never in doubt.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page section telling first-time or occasional river ferry passengers how the service works, so they can plan a crossing and know what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** The typography is solid and does its job. What holds the piece back is the copy: it leaves out the facts a passenger would act on. They can read the whole note and still not know when the first or last boat leaves, or when winter hours begin.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule is described, not stated** (defect). Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", and paragraph 3 says "in winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour". Departure times are what a passenger plans around and later holds the operator to, and these lines can be read several ways:
   - Is "early morning" 6:00 or 8:00?
   - Is "late evening" 21:00 or 23:00?
   - Which months count as "winter"?
   - On the hour, or at some other minute?

   A reader who guesses late and arrives at 22:30 finds no boat. The text then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which helps only once they're standing at the landing, after the planning has already happened. The fix: give the first and last sailing as real times, give the winter dates, and on a web page link to the timetable instead of, or as well as, pointing to the shelter.

2. **The frequency information is split by the bicycle paragraph** (taste, but it slows scanning). Summer frequency is in paragraph 1 and winter frequency is in paragraph 3, with bikes in between, so someone looking for "when does it run" has to read all three. People skim a page like this for a few numbers: route, frequency, crossing time, hours. Put all the timing in one place, either as one paragraph or as a short run of label-and-value lines. Then give bikes their own block. "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" already reads well on its own.

**Smaller things.**
- **Line length:** the body lines run to about 82 characters, a bit past the 45 to 75 range (Bringhurst). Narrow the column by roughly 10% to bring it near 66. The leading is generous enough that this is minor.
- **Fares:** "At no extra charge" implies a fare that the note never mentions. Either state the fare or say where to find it.
- **"Twelve minutes in calm weather":** this leaves the reader to wonder what happens in bad weather. If crossings get longer or are cancelled, say so. That consequence is what the reader needs.

**What's working.**
- **Type scale:** three clear levels with big steps between them: a small letterspaced grey eyebrow, a large bold heading and comfortable body text. Each size has one obvious job, so the hierarchy comes through at a glance. Keep it.
- **Headline:** "Before you board" speaks to the reader and names the moment the note is for. That's much stronger than a category label like "Service information".
- **Spacing:** the eyebrow sits tight to the heading, the heading is clearly separated from the body, and paragraph gaps are consistent. Space alone shows the groups, with no rules or boxes needed.
