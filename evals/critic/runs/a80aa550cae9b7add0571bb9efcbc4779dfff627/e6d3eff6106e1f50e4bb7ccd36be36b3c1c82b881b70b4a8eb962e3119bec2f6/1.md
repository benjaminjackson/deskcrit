**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, then into the first paragraph, which reads easily. In a couple of seconds it's clear this is a long-form feature on a small community or local-journalism site. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. It also feels a little anonymous: the frame is a stock sans-serif article template, and the only thing with any character is the writing.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the opening screen of a feature on a desktop editorial site, and its job is to pull a casual visitor into reading a long story.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The hierarchy is clear and the prose is strong enough to carry it. Two things hold it back: the opening screen is all text and doesn't show the place or the people the story is about, and the body lines run a little long.

**What would move it most**

1. **The first screen promises photographs and doesn't show one** (defect for this job). The byline says "Photographs by the residents", which is the most distinctive thing about this piece, but the whole first screen is text on white. Below the third paragraph it's empty down to the fold. A reader deciding whether to commit to a long feature about beans, bathtubs and a bus-stop car park gets no picture of it. The page could belong to any story on any site. Put a residents' photograph high in the first screen: a full-width lead image under the headline, or one sitting where the empty space is now. That way the "made by the people who built it" angle shows before anyone reads a word. If the photos are further down, at least one belongs above the fold.

2. **The body lines are too long for comfortable reading** (convention: Bringhurst's 45–75 characters, about 66 ideal). The first line of the opening paragraph ("Ten years ago … Today forty") runs about 83 characters. Across a long feature, readers will lose their place more often on the return sweep, which is exactly the fatigue a long read can't afford. Narrow the text column to roughly 600–640 px, or raise the body size a step so each line holds about 65–70 characters. The headline can stay at the current width.

3. **The headline breaks with "line" alone on the second line** (convention: break headings at phrase boundaries; Bringhurst on runts). "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" splits "bus line" and leaves one short word on a line of its own. The headline's last beat lands weakly and the shape looks like an accident. Rebalance the break: "The allotment at the end / of the bus line", set by hand or with `text-wrap: balance`, or by narrowing the headline slightly.

**Smaller things**
- The headline (bold, roughly 44 px) looks set at default tracking. Tightening the letter-spacing a little would make it a firmer block.
- The opening paragraph is set exactly like the body text. A slightly larger or differently weighted lede would mark where the story begins. This is a matter of taste.
- The nav runs "Features · Food · Places · Subscribe" in one style, so Subscribe, the one action that commits the reader to something, reads as just another section. Give it its own treatment, such as a quiet button or a separate position.
- The nav links are small, close-set text. The click targets look small, so check they reach 24×24 CSS px (WCAG 2.5.8).
- The logo and nav sit on a wider frame (left edge around x=120) than the article column (x=280). That's fine for an article page, but nothing else on the page refers to the outer edge, so the two edges read as unrelated rather than chosen.

**What's working**
- The hierarchy is clean. The small green "Feature" kicker, a dominant headline, a quiet grey byline and dark body text are four clearly different levels with no collisions. That restraint is right for long-form work, so keep it.
- The body setting is comfortable apart from the measure: good leading for the size, flush left with a soft rag, dark text on white with clear contrast, and paragraphs marked by space only.
- The copy does the selling. "Car park nobody used" next to "forty households", the water tap as the place to hear the news, and the closing line about pallets and bathtubs are all concrete, specific and written for a stranger. The layout should step back for this text, and it does.
