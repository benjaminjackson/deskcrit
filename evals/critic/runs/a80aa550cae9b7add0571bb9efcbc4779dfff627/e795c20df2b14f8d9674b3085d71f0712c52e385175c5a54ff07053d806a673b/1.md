**First read.** The eye goes to the dark blue band across the middle of the heatmap. From there it goes up to the headline, then right to the three big numbers. Within two seconds it reads as a support-team report: tickets come in on weekday office hours and are thin at weekends. It feels calm, competent and easy to trust, which makes the one thing that doesn't add up (finding 1) matter more.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is a Reports screen in a helpdesk tool, used by a support lead to plan staffing against when tickets arrive.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The chart is well chosen and cleanly made, and the pattern reads at a glance. The biggest problem is that the chart and the "Busiest hour" figure disagree, and on a staffing report that undermines the number people will act on.

**What would move it most**

1. **The peak in the chart doesn't match the "Busiest hour" number (defect).** The hour labels sit centred over their columns: 09:00 over the 10th column, 12:00 over the 13th. That puts the darkest cells (30+, on Monday and Friday) in the 11:00 column. The 10:00 column is one step lighter on Monday and Friday, and the same shade from Tuesday to Thursday. Yet the right-hand column says "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00". Someone planning shifts sees the darkest cells say one hour and the number say another. They can't tell whether the chart, the figure or the labels are wrong, so they stop trusting both. Check the data and the label alignment first. If the labels are offset by a column, fix them. If 10:00 really wins on the raw averages but the binning hides it, the bins are too coarse at the top: split the 25–29 and 30+ range, or mark the busiest cell directly.

2. **The headline names the topic, not the finding (taste, common sense).** "When new tickets arrive" tells me what the chart is about. It doesn't tell me what it shows. The finding is something like "Tickets peak late morning on weekdays; weekends are a fraction of that". The reader has to work that out from the colours, and a skimming manager may not bother. A title that states the takeaway makes the page useful at a glance. The chart then supports the claim instead of carrying it alone. Put the finding in the headline or in a one-line summary under it, and keep the current subtitle as the description of what's plotted.

3. **"September" in the figures and "1 to 28 September" in the chart may cover different periods (defect, ambiguous wording).** The chart averages four whole weeks. "New tickets, September: 3,412" reads as the whole month, which has 30 days. If the two cover different days, the reader can't connect the total to the chart. If they cover the same days, the label says something it doesn't mean. "Up 4% on August" has the same problem, because a 31-day month compared with a 28- or 30-day one moves the percentage on its own. Name the exact range on the figure, or compare per-day rates.

**Smaller things**
- Type sizes: I count about seven or eight. The headline (~28 px) and the figures (~26 px) are almost the same size and bold, so they read as rivals, not as two levels. Make one clearly larger; the headline, if it gets the finding.
- The legend's colour bar starts around x = 278. That lines up with neither the heatmap's left edge (x = 88) nor its label. Align it to the chart.
- The 0–4 and 5–9 steps are both very pale and hard to tell apart from the white background on a weaker monitor. That's fine for "quiet hours", but nobody can read 0–4 from 5–9 reliably. Accept it, or lighten the ramp less at the bottom.
- "Median first reply" is about response, not arrival. It's useful context, but it's the one figure the headline doesn't cover. Label the column as overall health, or move that figure out.
- The page has no text version of the pattern near the chart; a one-line summary (finding 2) would also give it one.

**What's working**
- A day × hour heatmap with one blue ramp in seven ordered steps is the right encoding for "when". The weekday office-hours block and the quiet weekend are visible before you read a label.
- The source note is exemplary. It states the time zone and that hours mean when a ticket was opened, not answered, which heads off the most likely misreading.
- Every figure on the right comes with a comparison to the previous month, so the reader can tell good from bad without looking anything up. Keep that pattern.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the heatmap cells show exact values on hover. If they do, a reader could check the 10:00 vs 11:00 question in finding 1 themselves.
