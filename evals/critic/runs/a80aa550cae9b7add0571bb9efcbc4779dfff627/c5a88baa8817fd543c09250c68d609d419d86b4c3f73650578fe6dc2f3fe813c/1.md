**First read.** The eye lands on "Landing stage rebuild", drops into the intro paragraph, then goes to the flat blue-grey picture. The boxed "Get timetable changes" form on the right is the second place it stops. It reads as a short news item from a small ferry operator, with a timetable sign-up next to it. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, a public-service page that doesn't try to sell you anything.

**What it's for.** My assumption: it tells passengers about the rebuilt landings and gets them to sign up for timetable change alerts.

**Verdict.** The article reads well, but the sign-up form can't do its job. It promises "one short note" and never asks where to send it: there's no email or phone field. The picture also doesn't show the one thing the article is about, which is the ramp.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form never asks how to reach the person (defect).** The card at top right asks for "Your name" and "Home landing", then says "We send one short note when the timetable changes." A reader looks for where to put their email address, finds nothing, and either gives up or presses "Sign up" without knowing how the note will reach them. Either way, no alert can be delivered. Name is the field you need least, yet it's the one the form asks for first. Make the contact field the first and only required field, labelled with what it is ("Email address" or "Mobile number for texts"), and make name optional or drop it. Then make the button say what happens: "Email me timetable changes" names the exact result, where "Sign up" could mean anything (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*).

2. **"Home landing" is a blank text box for what is really a choice from a short list (defect, Nielsen heuristic 5, error prevention).** The operator has a fixed set of landings, but the second field asks people to type one from memory. Spellings will vary, some people will type a town instead of a landing, and some won't know what "home landing" means. Any of these can send them the wrong alerts or none. Make it a dropdown or radio list of the actual landings. Replace the label with words the passenger uses, such as "Which landing do you usually sail from?" (Nielsen heuristic 2, match with the real world).

3. **The picture doesn't show the claim the text makes (taste).** The caption says "The new north landing, seen from the water at low tide". The key line underneath says the ramps "now meet the deck at every tide, so prams and wheelchairs roll straight on." The drawing has a grey slab, a lighter box and a blue rounded bar, with no visible ramp, no tide level and no boat deck. A reader can't tell which shape is the ramp, so the picture adds nothing the text hasn't already said. That one line about step-free boarding is the news that matters most here. Either use a real photo of a pram or wheelchair on the ramp, or label the drawing ("ramp", "deck", "low-tide line") so it shows the step-free join. If it's a placeholder, it's taking up the most prominent spot on the page.

**Smaller things.**
- The intro paragraph lists the features (wider ramps, covered waiting area, kiosk) but saves the line that matters to the reader, step-free boarding at every tide, for after the picture. Move it up into the intro.
- The form box doesn't say why it sits next to this article. A line like "Sailing times can shift while the north landing settles in" would link the two.
- There's a large empty space under the last paragraph down to the footer note. That's fine for a short article, but the left column ends abruptly; a "Back to news" link or the next item would give the page an ending.

**What's working.**
- The intro paragraph is concrete and sounds like a person talking: "Benches face the water, and a small kiosk sells tea and bread rolls while the boat is loading." Keep that voice.
- The form labels sit above their fields, close to their own field and clearly apart from the one before. The single filled blue button is the only strong action on the page, so it's obvious what to press.
- The type scale is tight: headline, form heading, body, and one small size for captions and labels. Each level is clearly different, and the column width keeps the intro paragraph a comfortable length to read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- What "Sign up" actually does: the confirmation, and the error states if a field is left empty. With no contact field, it's worth checking what the live form really collects.
