**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline, then drops into three columns of serif text that look the same. Nothing in the columns stands out, so the eye just starts reading the first column from the top. It reads as a calm, plain information page about a local ferry, maybe from a council or transport site. It feels tidy and quiet, but also unfinished: the text block sits in the top-left, with an empty band down the right and an empty bottom third.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a page for passengers, whether locals or visitors, telling them how the ferry runs, what they can bring and how the service changes, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** It reads cleanly, but it doesn't work as a passenger page. The facts someone would plan around are buried in three identical blocks of prose, and the key times are vague. The headline also promises a page about the seasons that the text doesn't deliver.

**What would move it most**

1. **A passenger can't find or trust the facts they need to act on.** *(Defect.)* Each column covers a different topic: the route and how often boats run, bicycles, and the winter timetable. But all three look the same and have no labels. Someone with one question, like "can I bring my bike?" or "when's the last boat?", has to read all three columns to find the answer (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: people scan, they don't read). Once they find it, the answer is vague. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" is a deadline, and it can be read two ways: a fixed time that happens to fall before dusk, or a time that moves with sunset. If someone guesses wrong, they miss the last boat home. "Between early morning and late evening" has the same problem for the first and last sailings. "Every forty minutes" and "one boat each hour" are useful, but they're buried mid-sentence.
   **Fix:** Give each column a short label that says what's in it ("Route and times", "Bicycles", "Winter service"). Pull the first sailing, the last sailing, how often boats run and how long a crossing takes out of the prose into a few short lines a person can glance at. Replace "before dusk" with a clock time, or say exactly where the current time is posted ("the last boat leaves at the time shown on the landing noticeboard").

2. **The headline promises something the page doesn't give.** *(Defect.)* "Through the seasons" sets the reader up to compare summer, winter and the times between. Only the third column mentions a season, and only winter. The first two columns don't depend on the time of year at all. So the reader keeps looking for the summer service and never finds it.
   **Fix:** Either give the headline the job the page actually does ("Taking the river ferry"), or add the summer and shoulder-season service so the promise holds. If the seasons are the point, put them side by side so the difference is the first thing the reader sees.

3. **The columns are too narrow, and the layout leaves the page half-used.** *(Convention, and here it costs something.)* Each column looks like about 28 to 32 characters per line. That's well under the 45 to 75 range Bringhurst gives for reading text. The result is a line break every four or five words, a choppy rag, and paragraphs that look longer than they are. Meanwhile about a fifth of the width on the right is empty, and the block stops at around 60% of the height. Ruder argues that empty space works when it is set in clear contrast to the printed area. Here it doesn't look chosen; it looks like the content ran out.
   **Fix:** Let the text use the full width. With labelled sections from point 1, two wider columns, or a single column around 60 to 70 characters wide, would read better. If you keep three columns, stretch them across the full type area so each one gets closer to 40 to 45 characters.

**Smaller things.**
- Column 1 runs two lines longer than the others, so the bottom edge is ragged. Once the columns are wider this will matter less; otherwise accept it, but don't pad the copy to make the columns even.
- "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes in calm weather, and a little longer when…": "a little longer" gives no number. If tides regularly add time, say roughly how much ("up to 20 minutes").

**What's working.**
- The type pairing is clear: a bold sans headline over a serif text face. The two differ in form and weight as well as size, so the two levels are obvious and nothing collides. Keep that pairing and the two-size scale.
- The text is dark, solid and set at a comfortable size, with even line spacing that keeps lines on the same baselines across the columns. Contrast looks well above 4.5:1. Keep it.
- The copy is plain and concrete: who does what, to what, and in what order ("Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). That's exactly the voice a passenger page needs; it just needs the times made exact.
