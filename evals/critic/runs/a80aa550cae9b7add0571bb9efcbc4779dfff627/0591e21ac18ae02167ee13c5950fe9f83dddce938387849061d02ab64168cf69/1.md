**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline and stops there, because its four lines are crushed into each other. The descenders of "Quayside ferry" run into the tops of "crossings", "daylight," runs into "through early", and the "g" and "y" of line three sit on top of "summer". It takes real effort to read the headline as words. After that you get a small grey label, a short paragraph, and a lot of empty white. It reads as the top of a timetable page for a river ferry, and right now it feels broken rather than bold.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the opening screen of a public web page that tells people when and where the spring ferry runs, so they can catch it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The headline's lines overlap so badly that the most important text on the page is hard to read and looks like a rendering bug. Behind that sits a second failure: a page labelled "timetable" never gives a single time.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline's lines collide (defect).** All four lines of the headline overlap: descenders of each line cut into the letters of the next, and the comma after "daylight" lands on "early". The reader has to pull the words apart letter by letter, and anyone glancing at the page will take it for a broken page, which costs trust in the timetable too. A multi-line heading can be set tighter than body text, but only while ascenders and descenders stay clear of each other (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, p. 37; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, p. 28). Open the line spacing until no letter touches the line above or below. At this size that means line spacing at or a little above the type size, not well below it as it looks now.
2. **A timetable with no times (defect).** The label says "Spring timetable" and the headline says "by daylight, spring through early summer", but the page never gives a first sailing, a last sailing, a start date, or an end date. "Every forty minutes" doesn't help unless you know when the forty-minute cycle starts. A reader who came to catch a ferry leaves without the one thing they needed. They have to guess what "by daylight" means in March versus June, and a wrong guess means a missed boat. Put the actual times and dates directly under the headline, at a size near the body text or larger: first and last departure from each landing, and the dates the timetable runs.
3. **The headline is too long for the size it's set at (taste).** Fifteen words at display size fill four lines and push everything useful below. Most of the length is detail ("spring through early summer") that belongs in the dates line, set plainly. A shorter headline, such as the route or "Quayside ferry, spring timetable", would fit on one or two lines. That would ease the spacing problem and bring the times up to where the eye lands.

**Smaller things.**
- Grey "Spring timetable" label: it's small and light. Check its contrast against white reaches 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3).
- Body paragraph, last line: "weather." sits alone on the third line. Adjust the measure or wording so the paragraph doesn't end on a single short word.
- Right side and lower half: the empty space is fine on its own, but once the times are added it's the obvious place for them.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (label, headline, body) with big steps between them, so each one's job is obvious. Keep it that way when you add the times.
- The body copy is concrete and written the way a person speaks: it names both landings, the frequency, and the crossing time, with a unit on every number. "In calm weather" is an honest qualifier. Keep this voice for the schedule.
- The left edge is strong: label, headline, and body all hang from the same line, which makes the page calm and easy to scan.
