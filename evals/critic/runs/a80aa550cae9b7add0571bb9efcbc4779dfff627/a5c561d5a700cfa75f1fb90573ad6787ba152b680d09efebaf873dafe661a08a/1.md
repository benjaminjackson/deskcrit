**First read.** My eye goes to the headline first, then straight to the June bar, which towers over the others. Next it picks up "$4.82M" on the right. It reads as a credit union's half-year report to its members, and it says "deposits are growing nicely." It feels calm, trustworthy and well ordered. The striped bars are the one thing that feels off. They shimmer.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a member-facing report page that tells ordinary members how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026, and that they should be able to trust and repeat its numbers.

**Verdict.** The layout and labelling are better than most report pages, but the chart's main event is misleading. June's jump, the biggest thing on the page, is mostly a one-off bonus payroll, and the page only says so in grey small print under the chart. One of the three headline figures is also labelled in a way that misdescribes what it measures.

**What would move it most**

1. **June's spike tells a story the data doesn't support (defect).** The June bar ($1,150k) jumps $282k after five months of rises of $40–80k. A viewer reads that as the trend speeding up, and the headline "rose every month" backs that reading. The cause is in the footnote, "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll," in the smallest, palest text on the page, below the chart frame where few will read it. That bonus probably also inflates "$4.82M, up 6.1%" and all three June figures in the cards, unless June 2025 had the same bonus. A member who repeats "deposits are accelerating" has been misled by the page's own focal point. Fix: put the explanation on the June bar itself, for example a short note beside it ("includes mid-year bonus payroll"), or split the bar into regular deposits and bonus. In the cards, say whether the comparison with 2025 is like for like. Common sense: the reason for the biggest change belongs where the eye already is.

2. **"Average deposit, Jun 2026: $214" measures something else (defect).** $214 × 5,374 accounts ≈ $1.15M, which is exactly June's total. So the figure is the average deposited *per account*, not the average size of *a deposit*. A member reads "the typical deposit is $214" and compares their own deposit to it, which is the wrong comparison. On a figure members will remember and repeat, a label that can be read two ways is a real error. Fix: label it "Average deposited per account, Jun 2026," or show the true per-deposit average if that's what you mean.

3. **The striped bar fill is chartjunk (convention: Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").** The diagonal blue-and-white hatching on all six bars shimmers at this size and makes the bar tops less crisp. The tops carry the data. The stripes encode nothing, since every bar is the same series. Fix: plain flat fills. If you want to mark June as different (see 1), use one changed fill for June only, so the pattern means something.

**Smaller things.**
- "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025" and "Up $11 on Jun 2025": "on" for "compared with" is a British turn of phrase and reads awkwardly to US members. Use "Up 6.1% from Jan–Jun 2025."
- Unit mismatch: the axis and labels use "$1200k" and "$1150k", but the card uses "$4.82M". Use one style throughout ("$1.2M", "$1.15M"), or at least add thousands separators.
- The value labels on every bar make the gridlines and most of the y-axis redundant. Lighten or drop the gridlines and keep just the baseline.
- The right column of cards ends around y≈618 while the chart panel ends around y≈693. The bottoms don't line up, which leaves a loose gap at the lower right. Either align them or let the cards fill the chart's height.
- The card figures ($4.82M, roughly 32 px bold) are about the same size as the page headline. Headline and figures compete for second place after the chart, so make one of them clearly bigger.
- The green "Up…" lines and the grey footnote look borderline for contrast at this small size. Check them against 4.5:1.
- Rounding: "$612k"-style labels add up to the $4.82M total, which is good. Keep that consistency when you rescale the units.

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding, not just the topic, and the subtitle and chart title give the measure and unit exactly ("US dollars per month"). Keep this. Only qualify the claim so it survives the June point.
- The bars start at zero, run in time order and have direct value labels, so no legend is needed and reading the chart costs almost nothing.
- Each card gives a figure together with what it's compared against (vs the same period last year). That's exactly what lets a member tell good from bad.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bars or cards have hover detail, and how the two-column layout reflows on a phone, which matters for a member report.
