**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline first. Then it drops to the big navy slice and its "38%". After that it jumps to the grey "25%" slice in the top left, which is the second-biggest thing on the chart and has no name. It reads as a calm, tidy annual-report page about cost breakdown. It is competent, but it tells you nothing new.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one page of a printed or PDF annual report. It shows shareholders and staff where the company's operating money went in fiscal 2026, so they can see what drives costs.

**Verdict.** The chart is clean and honest, but it doesn't do the job of an annual-report page. There is no total and no comparison, so a reader can't tell whether 38% on salaries is good, bad or new. A quarter of the spend sits in an unexplained "Other" slice.

**What would move it most**
1. **The shares have no amount and nothing to compare against.** This is a defect, and the convention is Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design". The headline and subtitle say "share of total operating costs", but the total never appears anywhere on the page. No figure carries a prior-year value either. A shareholder reading "Cloud hosting 12%" can't tell whether that is £400k or £40m, or whether it grew. Those are the two questions a cost page in an annual report exists to answer. The fix: put the total operating cost for the period next to the headline. Then give each category its amount and its change from fiscal 2025. The legend's right-hand column already has room for "£4.1m · +6%".
2. **"Other" is 25%, and that's too big to leave unexplained.** This is a defect, judged on common sense. It is the second-largest slice, bigger than rent and twice the size of hosting. It sits top-left where the eye lands early, and it is the only label that tells the reader nothing. In a financial document, an unexplained quarter of spend looks evasive even when it isn't. Few's fix runs the other way here: "Other" is meant to gather slivers, not hold the second-biggest block. Split it into its two or three biggest parts. If it can't be split, add a footnote saying what it contains.
3. **The headline names the topic but not the finding.** This is taste. The rule's sources aren't on my approved list, so I'm arguing it on its own terms. "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" is accurate, but it leaves the reader to work out the point. If the story is "People are 38% of our costs; cloud hosting is now 12%", say that, and say it to the reader. The chart then backs up a claim instead of just sitting there.
4. **The legend repeats what the slices already say, and it sits far from them.** This is a convention (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, direct labelling). Every percentage appears twice. Each category name, though, appears only in a legend about 100–300 px to the right, so to name a slice you have to match a small colour square across the page. Pick one approach. Either name the slices directly ("Salaries and benefits 38%") and drop the legend, or keep the right-hand list as a proper table with amounts and year-on-year change (see 1) and take the numbers off the slices. The table option also fills the empty lower-right quadrant, which right now makes the page look unfinished.

**Smaller things**
- White "12%" on orange and "16%" on teal look below 4.5:1. They pass only if they count as large bold text (WCAG 1.4.3). Measure them, and use dark text on those two slices if they fail.
- The "Other" grey slice against the off-white background looks close to the 3:1 contrast minimum for meaningful graphics (WCAG 1.4.11). Check it.
- The slices have five unrelated hues for categories that aren't ordered. That's acceptable, but there's no emphasis. Once the headline says what the story is, keep one accent hue for that category and push the rest toward neutral.
- The kicker "4.2 COSTS" in small tan capitals looks faint. Check its contrast.
- The source line is good. Add "Percentages may not sum to 100" only if they don't. These do sum to 100.

**What's working**
- Restraint: no 3D, shadows or gridlines. The pie has five slices running clockwise from 12 o'clock in descending order, with "Other" last. That is the case where a pie is acceptable.
- The type scale is clear: serif headline, serif subtitle, sans for data and source, and the page needs no more sizes than that. The subtitle gives the exact period and the basis ("twelve months to 30 June 2026").
- The source and rounding note are where a careful reader would look for them.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether this page sits next to a prior-year version elsewhere in the report. If it does, item 1 is less urgent, but the page still needs a total.
