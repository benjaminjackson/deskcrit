**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading at top left, then down three plain grey-black paragraphs. Everything to the right of them, about half the frame, is empty white. It reads as a plain information page for people using a small river ferry: quiet and tidy, but it feels like a draft. Nothing past the heading stands out, so a passenger looking for "when is the next boat?" has to read every line to find out.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an information page or posted notice that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bicycle. They will glance at it, often in a hurry.

**Verdict.** The type is clean and readable, but the page doesn't do its job. The facts a passenger acts on are vague and buried in running prose, and the right edge of the text has been broken by hand into a ragged shape that looks unfinished.

**What would move it most**

1. **Nobody can plan a trip from this page (defect, the copy).** Every time is vague: "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "one boat each hour" in "winter", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger can't tell when the first or last boat leaves, which clock times "every forty minutes" lands on, or which months count as winter. "Before dusk" is the worst one, because it's the line that decides whether someone gets stranded. It can be read as "at dusk" or as "some time before dark, check first", and a passenger who picks the first reading misses the boat. The page then sends people to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", so the one thing they came for is somewhere else. The fix is to state the first and last departures from each landing as clock times, give the dates for the winter timetable, and give the last winter sailing as a time.

2. **Facts you scan for are written as an essay (defect).** The things a passenger scans for (frequency, crossing time, bikes, winter changes) are spread across three paragraphs. The second sentence of paragraph 1 runs about 45 words, and it uses the sandbank and the harbour wall to explain a delay that, from the reader's side, is just "add a few minutes at low tide". Nielsen ("How Users Read on the Web") is clear that text people skim needs short chunks, headings, or lists. The fix is to put the operating facts in a short labelled block (Route, Frequency, Crossing time, Last boat, Winter) and keep a little prose only for the bicycle procedure. The low-tide delay should become a single sentence: "At low tide, allow up to N extra minutes."

3. **Hand-set line breaks give the column a broken edge (convention).** The lines don't break where the column runs out of room. In paragraph 1, "market" ends a line with room left for "quay", the next four lines stop around the same point, and the last line ("sandbank… at low tide.") runs about 70 px past all of them. In paragraph 2, "boarded," breaks early with room for "and", which leaves a stepped two-line tail. The result is a right edge with notches and one bulge, so the column has no consistent width. Bringhurst (2.1.3) and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, "The line") both warn against staircases and holes in ragged setting. The fix is to remove the manual breaks and set a fixed column width (around 60–65 characters), so the browser breaks lines and the rag evens out.

**Smaller things.**
- Heading: "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what's on the page. Something like "River ferry: times, crossing and bikes" tells the reader what they'll find.
- Bicycle paragraph: "at no extra charge" assumes you know the base fare, but no fare appears anywhere on the page. Add it, or say where to pay.
- Paragraph 1: "north landing" and "market quay" are fine labels, but say which end the times refer to once you add times.
- The empty right half is leftover space rather than shaped space. That's fine on a single-column reading page, but if this is a fixed-size notice, the block could sit on a deliberate margin instead of hugging the top left.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: two sizes, with the bold heading clearly above the regular body text. The contrast comes from both weight and size, so the hierarchy is unambiguous. Keep it, and give any new labels their difference through weight, not a third size.
- The body text has comfortable leading and looks like dark grey on white with strong contrast. The measure (about 55–65 characters) is in the readable range. Once the hard breaks are gone, the paragraphs will read well.
- The tone is calm and plain, which suits a local public service. Keep that voice when you add the hard facts.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the hand line breaks come from the source text or from a narrow container. Check in a live browser at a few widths: if they're hard breaks in the source, they'll look worse on a phone.
