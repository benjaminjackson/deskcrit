**First read.** My eye goes straight to the big headline, "Plan the whole trip in one place", then the paragraph below it, then the dark green "Get started" button. Then it stops at the button next to it, "Start now", and has to work out how the two differ. They don't differ. After that the eye drifts into the empty right half of the page and finds nothing. It feels calm, tidy and well set, but it's thin. It reads as a travel-planning tool, and it only becomes clear that it's for groups once you read the paragraph.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the top section of a marketing homepage. Its job is to get people who organise group trips to try the product.

**Verdict.** The structure is clean and the order of the page is right. But the decisive part, the action you're asked to take, is broken: you get two buttons that mean the same thing. The page also never shows the product or what makes it different, so a visitor has no reason to click either button.

**What would move it most**

1. **Two buttons offer the same action** (defect). "Get started" and "Start now" sit side by side under the paragraph. The words are plain synonyms, so the visitor stops to guess what the outlined one does differently. Some will hesitate, and the main button gets less attention because the pair splits it. The styling isn't the problem: the outlined second button is correctly weaker. What's wrong is that it has no job of its own. Fix: keep one button for this action. Either drop the second one or give it a different job, such as "See how it works" or "See pricing", since both already exist in the nav (Nielsen heuristic 8; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*).

2. **The button doesn't name what you'll do, and the headline could be anyone's** (defect). "Plan the whole trip in one place" is the same promise every itinerary app makes, so the biggest words on the page don't tell a visitor why this tool and not another. The part that actually sets it apart is in the paragraph: a group agreeing on a trip "without a dozen chat threads". That's the sharpest line on the page, but it's in the smallest text in the hero. "Get started" doesn't say what happens when you click it either: do you sign up, start a trip, or invite people? Fix:
   - Move the group angle into the headline, so it speaks to the visitor about what they get, for example ending the chat-thread mess.
   - Make the button one concrete step from the visitor's own world, like starting a trip and sending the link to the group chat they already use.
   - That chat is also the obvious thing to compare against: it's the tool they have today and know is painful.

3. **Half the screen is empty, and the product is never shown** (taste, argued). The right half of the hero, and everything below the buttons, is blank cream. That empty space isn't framing anything; it looks like a missing image. For a product whose whole pitch is "everything in one view", the strongest proof would be that view: a trip with flights, stays and day plans, and group members agreeing on them. Without it, the visitor has to take the claim on trust. Fix: put one clear product image in the right-hand column, top-aligned with the headline. Keep it to one image; the plain feel of the page is worth keeping.

**Smaller things.**
- The paragraph's grey text on the cream background looks a little light. Check that it reaches 4.5:1 contrast (WCAG 1.4.3).
- There's a large gap between the nav and the headline and an even larger empty area below the buttons. Once the image is in, move the block up so the space sits around the content rather than under it.
- The nav links are small and close in colour to the paragraph. That's fine as it is, but check that the gaps between them give enough to click (at least 24 px targets, WCAG 2.5.8).

**What's working.**
- **The type scale.** There are four clear sizes (headline, paragraph, button, nav) with big steps between them. The headline is set tight and heavy, so its two lines read as one unit. Keep this scale.
- **One left edge.** The logo, headline, paragraph and buttons all start on the same line, which makes the page read in a calm, quick order.
- **The colours.** One green is used only for the logo and the actions, with dark text on cream. Because green isn't spent on decoration, the button stands out without any help.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half is meant to hold an image that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check this in a live browser before acting on finding 3.
- How the hero looks on a phone.
