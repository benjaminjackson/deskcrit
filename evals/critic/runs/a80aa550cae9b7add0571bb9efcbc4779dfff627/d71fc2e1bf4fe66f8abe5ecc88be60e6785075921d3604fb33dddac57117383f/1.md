**First read.** The eye goes to the headline "Plan the week with your whole team", then right to the blue "Create account" button, then up into the form. Within two seconds it reads as a sign-up page for a team-planning SaaS. It feels calm, tidy and competent. It also feels generic: nothing in the frame shows what Tallyboard looks like or why it beats what the team uses now.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you gave no brief: this is the sign-up screen for a free team-planning product, and its job is to get a team lead to create a workspace.

**Verdict.** The form is clean and the one action is clear, so as a screen it works. As a pitch it is weak. The left half makes one generic claim, the offer line can be read two ways, and the form asks for more than a free trial needs.

**What would move it most**

1. **The offer line is ambiguous on the point people will hold you to.** The line under the headline says "Start a free workspace for up to five people. You can invite the rest of the team later." It can be read two ways:
   - Reading A: people six and up are free too, you just add them later.
   - Reading B: people six and up cost money.

   A team lead with eight people can't tell whether they are signing up for something free or a trial that will bill them. If they assume A and it turns out to be B, they feel misled the moment they invite the sixth person. **Defect.** Pricing is the line the viewer acts on. Fix: say plainly what happens at person six, e.g. "Free for up to five people. Larger teams are $X per person a month." If it really is free, say "Add more people any time, still free."

2. **The left half doesn't make the case, and it leaves a hole in the page.** "Plan the week with your whole team" could be the headline of any calendar, task or standup tool, so the viewer learns nothing about what's different here. The left column stops at about y=360, while the card runs to about y=665. Everything under the subhead, roughly the bottom-left third of the screen, is empty grey, so the page sits lopsided and unfinished rather than deliberately open.
   - Copy: **defect**. A viewer who can't see what's different has no reason to fill in five fields.
   - Composition: **taste**. The fix is a new layout, not a rule.

   Fix: use that space to show the product and its value on the viewer's terms. A cropped view of a real week board works, or one line that sets it against what they use now ("Replaces the Monday spreadsheet and the Slack thread"). Then the headline can claim something only Tallyboard can.

3. **The form asks too much and hides a rule where it disappears.**
   - The password rule, "At least 10 characters", lives only in the placeholder. It vanishes on the first keystroke, so people who forget it fail on submit. **Convention:** Nielsen's recognition over recall; field hints must stay visible. Fix: move it to helper text under the field.
   - "Company" and the split first/last name add effort to a free sign-up. Nothing marks any field optional. For a workspace of five, the company name can be asked after sign-up or inferred from the work-email domain. **Taste**, but each field you cut lifts completion. Fix: drop Company or mark it optional, and consider one "Full name" field.

**Smaller things.**
- "Already have an account? Sign In": the link looks the same grey as the sentence around it, with no underline, so returning users may not see it's clickable. It also uses "Sign In" while the rest of the page uses sentence case. Make it the accent blue or underline it, and write "Sign in".
- Input borders look pale grey on white and may fall below the 3:1 needed for component edges (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure the border colour and darken it if it fails.
- The placeholders "Enter first name", "Enter last name" and "Company name" repeat their labels and add noise. Leave them empty, or use them for real examples like the email field does ("name@company.com").
- Button label: "Create account" repeats the card heading. "Create free workspace" would tie the click to the offer.
- Type sizes: the logo (~18 px) and subhead (~17 px), and the labels (~14 px), placeholders (~15 px) and footer (~13 px), sit only a pixel or two apart. Merge them onto fewer sizes and separate them with weight and colour instead.

**What's working.**
- There is one clear primary action. The solid blue button is the only saturated block on the page, and the blue is used only for the brand and the action. Keep that restraint.
- The labels sit above the fields, close to their own field and well clear of the one before, so the form reads top to bottom without effort.
- The headline is set large and bold, with tight leading, so its two lines read as one unit and it clearly beats the card heading.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the inputs and button, how errors show up (these matter for the password rule in particular), and whether the two-column layout collapses cleanly to one column on mobile.
