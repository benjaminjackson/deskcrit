**First read.** The eye lands on "Crossing the river" because the brush script is the biggest and darkest thing on the page. It then jumps across to "Good to know", which is set in the same script, before it comes back to the serif headings on the left. It reads as a small, friendly information page for a local ferry. The feel is warm and casual, close to a café menu, and it doesn't quite read as a transport service you'd plan your day around.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page passengers check to find out when the ferry runs and what they can bring, usually just before a trip, often on a phone.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to scan, but it doesn't give the one fact a passenger came for. "When the boats run" gives no times. Three unrelated typefaces also make a simple page feel stitched together, and that undercuts the trust a timetable page needs.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule section never gives a time.** *Defect.* Under "When the boats run", the copy says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves before dusk". A passenger can't plan from that. They don't know whether early morning means 6:00 or 8:00, or when dusk is in November. These are the lines people act on and later hold the operator to. "Before dusk" can be read as 30 minutes before sunset or two hours before, and someone who reads it the wrong way misses the last boat. The fix is to give real first and last departure times for summer and winter, with the month the winter timetable starts. If the times change too often to print, say "First boat 6:20, last boat 21:40 (see timetable for today)" and link to it from that line, not only from the sidebar.

2. **Three unrelated typefaces, with the same job split between two of them.** *Convention (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 7–8: faces should agree or contrast clearly, never conflict).* I count a casual brush script (the H1 and "Good to know"), a sturdy serif (the subtitle, the H2s and the sidebar body), and a sans (the nav, the main body text and the button). Contrast on its own isn't the problem. The problem is that body text appears in two faces: the main column is in the sans at about 18 px, and the sidebar paragraph is in the serif at about 20 px. So the aside looks louder and more literary than the main content, and the reader can't tell which voice is the page's. The fix is to pick one reading face for all paragraphs and one face for headings, and make the script either a single deliberate accent or remove it.

3. **The script works against the job.** *Taste.* A casual brush script says "weekend café" when the page needs to say "dependable public service". At the sidebar size, "Good to know" is also the hardest line on the page to read. If you want the warmth, keep it to the one H1 and set "Good to know" in the same serif as the other H2s. That also stops the sidebar heading competing with the page title for first look, which is what sends the eye across the page in the first read.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: the subtitle (about 26 px, grey serif) and the H2s (about 28 px, bold serif) are only a couple of pixels apart. They're told apart by weight and colour, which works, but set the subtitle clearly smaller or clearly lighter so the near-match doesn't look like a mistake.
- Sidebar text is bigger than the main body text (about 20 px vs 18 px), so the secondary content outweighs the primary. Match the main body size or go smaller.
- The card's right edge (about x=1150) doesn't line up with the nav's right edge (about x=1184), so the right margin wobbles. Align it to the header's content edge.
- "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter" sends a reader who is online to a physical shelter. Lead with the online timetable and mention the printed one as the backup.
- The nav has no current-page indicator. That's fine if this page isn't one of the three nav items, but if it is, mark it.

**What's working.**
- The two-column layout with one clear sidebar card and a single filled button gives the page one obvious action. Keep that: one primary button, in the box, next to the thing it refers to.
- The H2s state the passenger's question ("When the boats run", "Bicycles and luggage") instead of using category labels. Keep that voice for any sections you add.
- The bicycle copy is concrete and in sequence (who boards first, where to lock up). It's a model for how the schedule copy should read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the sidebar stacks on a phone. If it drops below the main text, the only button falls off the first screen, so check the order at narrow widths.
