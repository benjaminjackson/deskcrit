**First read.** The eye goes to the big serif headline, then to the tallest bar (705, Q3 2026), then moves left to right along a row of identical blue 3D columns. It reads as a board-deck slide about quarterly shipments, and it looks tidy and corporate. Nothing on the chart points to the "third quarter" in the headline, so you have to find Q3 2025 and Q3 2026 yourself.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is slide 3 of 12 in a board sales review, there to show the shipment pattern and set expectations for Q4.

**Verdict.** It's clean and nearly honest. But its most important number is a forecast drawn exactly like the actual results, and the chart doesn't show the point its headline makes. A board will remember 560 as a result, and it will miss both the Q3 pattern and the bigger story, which is growth.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast looks like a fact.** This is a defect. The Q4 2026 bar (560) has the same fill, depth and bold label as the seven actual quarters. The only hint that it's a forecast is a bracket in the subtitle and the source line. Board members read bars, not footnotes. They will treat 560 as booked, and they will hold you to an implied 17% rise over Q4 2025 (480) as if it had already happened. Projections should look different from observed data (Wilke; Few). Draw Q4 2026 as an outline or hatched bar, label it "560 (forecast)" on the bar, and if you have a range, show it.
2. **The headline's claim isn't drawn.** This is partly convention and partly taste. "Shipments peak each third quarter" is a good takeaway title, but all eight bars are the same blue, so the viewer has to find the two Q3s and check the claim alone. "Each" also rests on only two cycles. The easiest fix is to colour the Q3 bars in the one accent and turn the rest grey (Tufte; Wilke: neutral for context, one accent for the point). I'd also argue, as taste, that seasonality isn't the board's news. Every quarter of 2026 is above the same quarter of 2025 (455 vs 410, 590 vs 520, 705 vs 610). That growth is what a board acts on, and the slide leaves them to work it out. Consider a headline that says both, like "Shipments up about 15% a year, still peaking in Q3", and pair or annotate the year-on-year comparison.
3. **The 3D bars.** This is convention and a mild defect. The extruded tops and side faces add ink but no data (Tufte, chapter 5 on chartjunk; Wilke: no 3D). They also blur where each bar ends: the top face rises above the true value, so the 705 bar looks like it reaches about 725 on the gridlines. The printed values save it from misleading anyone, but the effect makes the chart look like a template and costs a little accuracy. Use flat 2D bars.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count about seven sizes. The subtitle, chart title, unit line, axis labels and source line all sit between roughly 12 and 16 px, so they don't read as separate levels. Keep the headline, one text size and one small size, and separate the rest with weight and colour (Refactoring UI).
- The y-axis numbers and gridlines repeat the value labels already printed on every bar. Drop the axis, or mute it further (data-ink, Tufte).
- Subtitle: "eight quarters to Q4 2026 (Q4 2026 is the forecast)" names Q4 2026 twice and hides the key fact in brackets. Try "Q1 2025 to Q3 2026 actual; Q4 2026 forecast."
- Below the source line there's a band of about 130 px of empty space while the chart's top is cramped under its unit label. Give that space to the chart or centre the block vertically.
- "Units shipped by quarter / Thousands of units" sits about as close to the subtitle as to the chart. Move it nearer the plot so it reads as the chart's label.

**What's working.**
- The headline states a finding, not a topic. Keep that habit, and make the chart prove it.
- Units ("Thousands of units"), a zero baseline, direct value labels and a dated source line are all present. That's the basic honesty a board chart needs.
- The calm left-aligned layout and the restrained single-hue palette leave room for one accent colour to do real work once you add it.
