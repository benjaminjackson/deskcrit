**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading at top left, then drops straight into three even paragraphs of comfortable grey-black text. It reads as a plain, calm notice page, like a council or harbour info page. It feels trustworthy and quiet. Nothing in the body stands out, so the eye just starts reading from the top.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for people about to take the river ferry, who mainly want to know when it runs, how long the crossing takes, and whether they can bring a bike.

**Verdict.** The type is set well, but the page doesn't yet do its job. The facts a passenger acts on are vague, and they're buried in running prose, so someone checking quickly can't plan a trip from it.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times can't be acted on** (defect). The page says boats run "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "in winter" there's "one boat each hour", and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger can't plan on any of that:
   - It gives no first or last departure time.
   - It doesn't say which months count as winter.
   - "Before dusk" changes from week to week.
   - "Leaves the quay" can be read two ways: the market quay only, or both landings. If someone guesses wrong, they're stranded on the wrong bank.
   - "Every forty minutes" has no starting time, so you can't work out the next boat.

   These are the lines people remember and hold the operator to. The fix: give clock times for the first and last boats, give dates or months for the winter timetable, and name the landing for the last crossing (e.g. "last boat from market quay 16:10, from north landing 16:30").

2. **Key facts are buried in prose** (convention: text people skim should be broken up with headings, short paragraphs and lists; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*, on pages people scan). The three facts nearly every reader came for are frequency, crossing time and last boat. They sit mid-sentence in paragraphs one and three, with nothing to make them stand out from the advice on bags and noticeboards. Readers have to read the whole page to find them. The fix:
   - Put the timetable first, as a short block a reader can take in at a glance (route, frequency, first and last boat, crossing time, winter changes).
   - Add short subheads for the rest, such as "Timetable", "Bicycles" and "Timetable changes".
   - Write the numbers as digits ("40 minutes", "12 minutes"). This is common sense rather than a cited rule: digits are easier to spot when skimming.

**Smaller things**
- Heading: "Notes for ferry passengers" could be about any ferry. Name the route (north landing to market quay) so readers know they're on the right page.
- Paragraph 3 mixes two topics: the winter timetable, and where to find printed timetables and notices. Split it so "where to check for changes" can be found on its own.
- Layout: the text column sits at the left, leaving the right ~40% and the lower third empty. That's fine for a reading page, but it's where the timetable block from finding 2 could go (taste).

**What's working**
- **Measure and leading.** Lines look like roughly 65–70 characters, and the line spacing is generous, so the text is easy to read (Bringhurst's 45–75 range). Keep this when you restructure.
- **A clean two-level scale.** The heading differs from the body in both size and weight, with no near-duplicate sizes. It's restrained, and it gives you room to add one subhead level without muddling the scale.
- **Spacing.** The heading sits closer to its text than to the page top. Paragraphs are separated by space only, with no indents as well.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where passengers will read this on the landing.
