**First read.** My eye goes to the bold heading, then into three plain paragraphs. It reads as a public notice from a small ferry service: calm, tidy and unhurried. Within a few seconds I'm looking for "when's the next boat?" and there's nothing to look at. I have to read every sentence to find the timetable, and when I do, it has no times in it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or info-screen notice for people about to take the ferry. They want to know when it runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** It looks clean, but it doesn't do the job yet. The facts a passenger acts on are vague, and they're buried in running prose with nothing to scan.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule has no actual times (defect).** In paragraph 1, "between early morning and late evening" doesn't give a first or last sailing. In paragraph 3, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" changes with the date, and doesn't say which side's "quay". "Winter" doesn't say when that timetable starts. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say from when. These are the lines a passenger will plan around and hold the operator to. "Before dusk" could mean 15:30 or 17:00, and someone who reads it the generous way misses the last boat. "The quay" could mean the market quay or both landings, and someone who reads it wrongly waits on the wrong bank. Fix: give clock times for the first and last boat in each season, the date the winter timetable starts, and which landing each time applies to. Also stop sending people to "printed timetables in the shelter". Someone reading this away from the landing can't see those.

2. **Everything is in prose when it should be scannable (defect; Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** Times, how often the boat runs, how long the crossing takes, bike rules and the winter change are all mixed into the same three paragraphs at the same weight. A passenger checking quickly reads it all just to pull out one number. Fix: put the schedule first as a small table or a short list of lines (route, how often, first and last boat, summer and winter). Then give the bike rules their own short section with a subheading. The prose can stay for the context around it.

3. **The type has two levels, and the page gives the reader no entry points (taste).** I count two sizes: a heading of roughly 40 px and body text of about 19 px. That's a clean scale, and I wouldn't add sizes for the sake of it. But the content clearly has sections (schedule, bikes, winter), and nothing marks them. A bold subheading at body size or a bit larger for each section would let the eye jump straight to "Bicycles". As Refactoring UI says, use weight before adding size.

**Smaller things.**
- **The words "Notes for ferry passengers" could head any notice (copy).** Name the route, e.g. "North landing ⇄ Market quay ferry", so the viewer knows straight away it's the right page.
- **The bike rules are written as description.** "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" never says what the rider should do. Make the rules instructions: "Wait until foot passengers are aboard, then wheel your bike on and lock it to the side rail. Leave your bags on the bike."
- **"A little longer when the tide turns against the boat" has no number.** If you can, give one (for example, "up to 20 minutes") so people with a connection to make can plan.
- **The right half and bottom third of the page are empty.** The left-hung column is fine for a document. On a wide screen, though, it reads as unfinished. The schedule summary could go in that space, or you could accept the empty space on purpose and keep the column. Either way, decide it.

**What's working.**
- **The text is easy to read.** The lines run about 65–70 characters, in Bringhurst's comfortable range. The line spacing is generous, the left edge is clean, and the dark text on white has strong contrast. Keep all of this when you restructure.
- **The tone is plain and friendly.** The sentences are short and concrete ("lock them to the rail along the side of the deck"). Keep that voice in the new subheadings and lists.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't tell how the column reflows on a phone. Most passengers will check this on one at the landing, so look at a narrow viewport.
