**First read.** The eye goes to "Tell us about your project" at top left, then across to the white card and down three fields to the blue "Send message" button. It reads as a small agency's contact page within a second or two. It feels calm, competent and plain, but also anonymous: the logo is the only thing that says whose page this is.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a design studio's contact page, meant to get prospective clients to send a useful first enquiry.

**Verdict.** It works. A visitor knows what this is and what to do, and there is one clear action. The biggest gap is that the form doesn't help people write the message the copy asks for, so the studio will get thinner enquiries than it could.

**What would move it most**

1. **The copy asks for a budget and timeline, but the form gives no place or prompt for them.** The paragraph on the left says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." Then the visitor faces an empty "Message" box about 120 px tall, roughly 400 px to the right of that sentence. By the time they start typing, the request is out of view and out of mind. The box is also small for "tell us about your project", which tells them to keep it short. The result is enquiries like "Hi, can we chat about a website?" that need a second round of emails before anyone can quote. This is a **defect** against the page's own goal: put the prompt where people act on it (Nielsen 6, recognition rather than recall; Nielsen 10, help placed next to the field). Fix: add helper text under the "Message" label ("What you need, your budget range, and when you need it by") and make the box taller. Or add two optional short fields for budget (a range picker) and timeline. Mark them optional so the form doesn't feel heavier.

2. **The headline could sit on any agency's site, and nothing on the page shows why this studio.** "Tell us about your project" is the stock line for contact pages. The only specific promise is "reply within two working days", which is good but is hygiene, not a reason to choose them. A visitor who came straight here, or who is comparing three studios in three tabs, gets nothing to remember Fernwick by. This is **taste** on copy, but it has a cost: the page asks for effort and offers no reason. Fix: say who they are for or what happens next in the visitor's terms, e.g. what the first call covers, or one line on the kind of work they take on.

3. **The form card stops short of the right margin, so the layout leans left.** The nav ends at about x=1224, but the card's right edge is at about 1068. That leaves a ~156 px strip on the right with nothing in it, and the column edges don't line up with anything above. The left column's text also stops around x=440, well before the card. Together they read as two blocks placed by hand rather than on one grid. This is a **convention** (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*: blocks take their edges from one grid). Here it costs some polish, not usability. Fix: align the card's right edge to the nav's right margin, or centre the pair as a group within the page margins.

**Smaller things**
- "Contact" in the nav looks exactly like the other links. Show it as the current page (bold, or no underline plus an indicator) so people know where they are (Nielsen 1).
- No field is marked required or optional. If all three are required, say so once above the form. If any are optional, mark them.
- The input borders look light grey on white, possibly under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Check the border colour against #fff.
- There's no plain email address for people who would rather write from their own mail client or attach a brief. Add one line under the button.
- The space between the card and the footer is a large empty band. It's harmless on desktop, but it shows the page has very little content.
- The headline breaks "your / project" with "project" alone on the second line. A slightly wider heading box would keep it to one line, or break after "about".

**What's working**
- One action, clearly dominant. "Send message" is the only filled, saturated element on the page, so there's no doubt where the visitor ends up. Keep it the only filled button.
- Labels sit above the fields in bold, close to their own field and clearly apart from the one before. The form reads cleanly from top to bottom in one column.
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a concrete promise with a number in it, and it cuts the uncertainty of a contact form. Keep that kind of specific promise.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Focus states on the fields and button, and what happens after sending (confirmation, error messages, whether typed text is kept on error). These matter here because the whole page is one form.
