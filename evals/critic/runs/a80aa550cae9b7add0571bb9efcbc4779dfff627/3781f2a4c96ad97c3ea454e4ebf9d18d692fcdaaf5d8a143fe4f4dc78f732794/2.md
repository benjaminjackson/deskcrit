**First read.** The eye goes to "Tell us about your project", then across to the white form card, and down to the blue "Send message" button. It reads as a design studio's contact page within a second or two. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also stock: with another name in the top left, this could be any studio's page.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the contact page of a small design studio, where a possible client who already looked at the work sends a first project enquiry.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The path from heading to button is clean, and nothing gets in the way of sending a message. The biggest gap is that the page asks for a budget and a timeline, but the form gives no place to put them, so many enquiries will arrive without them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The request for budget and timeline sits apart from where people type.** The paragraph on the left says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form on the right has only one plain "Message" box, and it looks about 120 px tall, which invites a few lines rather than a brief. To follow the request, the visitor has to remember a sentence from the other column while writing. Most won't. You get "Hi, we need a new website" and have to write back for the basics, which uses up the "reply within two working days" promise on a follow-up question instead of a real answer. This is a **defect**: it relies on recall instead of recognition (Nielsen heuristic 6; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). The fix is to put the prompt where the typing happens. Best is two short optional fields under Message, "Budget range" (a few set ranges) and "Timeline". Cheaper is hint text right under the Message label, plus a taller box so a proper brief looks expected.

**Smaller things.**
- **Headline copy:** "Tell us about your project" could be on any studio's page. That costs little here, since the visitor has already chosen to get in touch, but a line in the studio's own voice, or one that names the kind of project you take on, would make the page yours (taste).
- **Nav:** "Contact" looks exactly like the other links, so nothing shows which page you're on. Give the current page its own state, for example bold with no underline.
- **Required fields:** none of the fields are marked required or optional. If all three are required, say so once above the form. If budget and timeline fields get added, mark them optional.
- **Input borders:** the grey borders look light against white. Measure them; they need at least 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11).
- **Right edge:** the card stops at about x = 1067, while the nav runs to about x = 1224. That leaves a strip about 160 px wide on the right that lines up with nothing. Either take the card out to the nav's right edge or make the gap clearly deliberate (taste; Müller-Brockmann, one grid).
- **Headline break:** "project" sits alone on the second line. A slightly wider heading column would set it on one line, or break it as "Tell us about / your project".

**What's working.**
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is specific and easy to check. It's the most persuasive line on the page, so keep it, and keep it this close to the heading.
- The form is built correctly: one column, labels above their fields and visible at all times, and one primary button that clearly outranks everything else. Keep this structure if you add fields.
- The hierarchy is clean: one dominant heading, a short supporting paragraph with a comfortable line length, and a plain light-grey background that makes the white card the obvious place to act.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the fields and the button, validation and error messages, what the visitor sees after sending (it should confirm the two-day reply), and how the two columns stack on a phone.
