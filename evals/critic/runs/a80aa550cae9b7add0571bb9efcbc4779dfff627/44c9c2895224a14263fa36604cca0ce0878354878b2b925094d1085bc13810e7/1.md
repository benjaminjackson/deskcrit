**First read.** The bold sans heading catches the eye first, then the eye drops into three calm serif paragraphs and reads straight down. It reads as a plain, tidy information page from a small local ferry service: quiet, trustworthy, a little old-fashioned. Nothing competes for attention, and nothing jumps out either. A passenger looking for a time has to read every sentence to find one, and then finds there isn't one.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers what they need to plan a crossing (when boats run, how long they take, bikes, winter changes), read on a laptop or phone before or at the landing.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound, but the page doesn't do its job. The facts a passenger will act on are written as loose prose ("early morning", "before dusk", "the quay"), so nobody can plan a trip from it. Fix the words before touching the layout.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule facts can't be acted on** (defect). The first paragraph says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", and the third says "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk." These are the lines passengers remember and later hold the operator to, and each can be read more than one way:
   - "Early morning" and "late evening" give no first or last sailing, so a commuter can't tell whether a 6:30 boat exists.
   - "Before dusk" moves by an hour or more across the winter, so a reader who takes it to mean 5 pm may get to the landing after the last boat has gone.
   - "Leaves the quay" could mean the market quay or "whichever quay you're standing at". If it means the market quay, someone waiting at the north landing doesn't know when the last boat reaches them.
   - "In winter" has no dates, so a reader in early November can't tell which timetable applies.
   
   Fix: give real first and last departure times from each side, the dates of the winter timetable, and the winter last sailing as a clock time. If the times really do follow the light, say so and point to where the exact time is posted. Write numbers people act on as digits ("every 40 minutes", "about 12 minutes"). Spelled-out numbers read gracefully but are slower to spot when scanning, and digits are where a scanning eye looks. That last point is common sense, not a cited rule.

2. **The most dangerous fact comes last and is buried mid-paragraph** (defect, and the convention "text that people skim is broken into chunks"). The fact most likely to strand someone, the winter reduction and the early last boat, sits in the final paragraph after the bicycle note, and the sentence about printed timetables trails after it. With no subheads, nobody can jump to "when does it run" or "can I bring my bike." Fix: put the timetable first, with winter set apart in its own visible block rather than as an afterthought. Add short subheads (Times, Winter, Bicycles, Timetables) so each question has a place to land. Times would read better as a small table or list than as a sentence.

**Smaller things.**
- "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing": a reader on this page will ask whether there's one online. Link it or say there isn't one.
- Bicycles paragraph: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" describes a procedure but doesn't tell the reader what to do. "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on and lock it to the rail" speaks to them directly.
- The third paragraph ends on a two-word line ("each landing."). It's a minor runt; rewrapping or a small edit would fix it.
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what the page answers. "Ferry times and what to know before you cross" tells a searcher they're in the right place.

**What's working.**
- **The reading type is well judged.** Body lines look about 65–70 characters long, with generous leading (roughly 1.6). That's inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range and comfortable to read. Keep both the measure and the leading when you add structure.
- **The sans heading over serif text is a clear contrast, not a muddle.** Bold sans against regular serif differs in form, weight and size at once. With just two text sizes, the hierarchy is clean. Keep that scale: subheads can be the sans at body size in bold, with no third size needed.
- **The flush-left column with near-black text on white** is quiet and legible, which suits a public-service page.
