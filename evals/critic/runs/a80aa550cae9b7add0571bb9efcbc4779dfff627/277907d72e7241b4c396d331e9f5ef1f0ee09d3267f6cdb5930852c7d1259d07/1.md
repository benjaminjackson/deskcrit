**First read.** My eye lands on the bold serif headline, then drops straight into the underlined italic phrases, which stand out more than anything else in the text. In the first second it looks like a web page with five links. Then it reads as a short set of instructions for people helping at a village fete. It feels calm, friendly and tidy, and a little bare.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short page or email telling stallholders and helpers the rules for the day, so the person reading it can pick out the times and rules and remember them.

**Verdict.** The page mostly works. The type is clean and the key facts are marked. The biggest problem is how they're marked: italic plus underline on a screen reads as a link, and it is two kinds of emphasis on one phrase. The facts are also buried in prose, and one deadline can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The marked phrases look like links.** All five phrases, from "arrive before ten" to "on the field only", are underlined and italic. On a screen, underlined text means "click here", so readers will try to tap them or wonder where they go. Each one also changes two things at once. Bringhurst (§3.5.1) and Lupton (*Thinking with Type*, p. 94) both say emphasis inside text needs only one signal. Stacking two makes the paragraphs look busy and makes the emphasis louder without making it clearer. *Convention, with a real cost.* Fix: use one signal. Bold works best here, because these are facts people will scan back for. Keep underlines for actual links.
2. **The facts are buried in sentences.** There are five rules: an arrival time, cake labels, the raffle time, a ticket deadline and parking. A helper on the morning of the fete wants to glance at the page and find "when do I need to be there?". Right now they have to read two paragraphs to find it. The marked phrases help, but only partly. *Defect for this job.* Fix: make it a short list, one rule per line, with the time or place first ("10:00: arrive and set up your stall"). That also lets the formatting do the work the underlines are doing now.
3. **The ticket deadline can be read two ways.** "All tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box." Collected by whom? It could mean winners must claim their prizes by four, or that sellers must hand in ticket stubs by four. If a winner reads it the wrong way, they lose the prize. That is the kind of line people hold you to, so it's a defect, not a style note. Fix: name who does what: "If your ticket wins, collect your prize by 4pm. Unclaimed prizes go back in the box." "Arrive before ten" also leaves out who should arrive (stallholders? everyone?) and whether that's am. Say "Stallholders: arrive before 10am."
4. **The basics are missing.** The page never gives the date, the venue, or which door "the door" is. Stallholders may already know, but a page called "Notes for the summer fete" may get forwarded. Without these facts, someone new can't act on any of the times. *Defect if this goes beyond people already briefed.* Fix: add one line under the headline with the date and place.

**Smaller things.**
- There are two type sizes, headline and body, with a strong step between them. That's fine for a page this short. If you add a date line, set it smaller or lighter instead of adding a third size in between.
- "only." sits alone on the last line of the second paragraph. That's a runt. Rewording or a list (finding 2) fixes it.
- "o'clock" looks like it uses a straight apostrophe. Check it and use a curly one (’).
- The text block sits high and to the left, with a large empty area below and to the right. If this is a full page, that space looks left over rather than planned. Taste, and fine for an email.

**What's working.**
- The type is easy to read. The serif body is a comfortable size with generous line spacing, and lines run about 60–70 characters, inside Bringhurst's range. Keep all of that.
- The headline does its job without fuss. It's bold, it's the same family as the body, and it's clearly the first thing to read.
- The voice is warm and plain ("go back in the box", "three o'clock sharp"). It sounds like a person, which suits a village fete. Keep that tone when you rewrite as a list.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the underlined phrases really are links. If they are, they need link text that says where they go, and the emphasis question changes.
