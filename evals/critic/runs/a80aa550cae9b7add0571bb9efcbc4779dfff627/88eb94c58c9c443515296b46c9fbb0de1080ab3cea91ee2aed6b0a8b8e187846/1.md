**First read.** My eye goes to the bold sans heading, then runs into four paragraphs that look exactly alike. It reads as a plain information page for people using a small river ferry, calm and tidy. Nothing in the text stands out, so to find "when is the next boat" I have to read every line.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or printed notice for passengers who need to plan a crossing: when boats run, how long they take, and the rules for bikes and bags.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is mostly sound, but the page doesn't do its main job well. The facts a passenger acts on (frequency, first and last boats, the winter change) are buried in prose and stated loosely, so the reader can't plan from it. A smaller issue: the paragraphs are marked twice, with a large indent and a gap.

**What would move it most**

1. **The timetable facts are vague and buried.** *Defect.* "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening," "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk," and "in winter" all leave the reader guessing:
   - What time is "early morning"?
   - Which quay does that last crossing leave from? The first paragraph names two: the north landing and the market quay.
   - When does winter start and end?

   These are the lines people plan around and later hold the operator to. "Before dusk" read as 4pm and read as 5pm sends a passenger to different boats, and the wrong one strands them on the far bank. The page also sends readers to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which is no help to someone planning from home. Fix: give actual times, name the landing for the last boat, and give dates for the winter schedule. Then pull those facts out of the running text into a short summary at the top, like "Every 40 min, 6:30–21:00 · Winter (Nov–Feb): hourly, last boat from market quay 16:00". Keep the prose for the softer notes.

2. **Paragraphs are marked twice, and the indent is far too big.** *Convention, partly common sense.* All four paragraphs open with an indent of roughly four ems (about 47 px) and also have a blank gap between them. Either one alone separates paragraphs. With both, each paragraph looks like a separate island and the left edge looks chewed up. Bringhurst (*Elements*, 2.3.1) also says the first paragraph after a heading starts flush left. Here it is indented too, so the heading and the text below it don't line up on the left. The "never both" rule itself isn't confirmed in the sources; it's common sense, because the second mark adds nothing. Fix: keep the space and drop the indents, which suits short notice paragraphs on screen. Or keep indents of about 1 em, remove the gaps, and set the first paragraph flush.

**Smaller things.**
- The paragraph about rebuilt landing stages is news, not instructions. It's the least useful paragraph, so it belongs last, as it is now, or cut. The useful part, wider ramps, matters to people with bikes and prams, so move that into the bicycle paragraph.
- Several lines describe instead of telling the reader what to do: "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" and "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded". Say it to the reader: "Board after foot passengers, lock your bike to the side rail, and leave your bags on it."
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" is generic. Naming the route ("North landing ⇄ Market quay: passenger notes") tells a reader at once that they're on the right page.

**What's working.**
- Line length looks like 65–75 characters at a comfortable leading. That's right for reading (Bringhurst's 45–75 range). Keep the column width.
- The pairing works: a bold sans heading over a sturdy text serif gives two clearly different levels without extra sizes. The type scale is a clean two steps. Don't add more.
- The colour is quiet, near-black on white, with strong contrast and no decoration. That fits a public notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the page reflows on a phone, where most passengers would check it. Check that the column and any added summary hold up at narrow widths.
