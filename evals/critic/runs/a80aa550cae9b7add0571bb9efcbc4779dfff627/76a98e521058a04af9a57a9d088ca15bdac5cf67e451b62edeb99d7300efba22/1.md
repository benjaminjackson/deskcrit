**First read.** The eye lands on "Landing stage rebuild," then jumps right to the bold blue "Sign up" button, which is the strongest colour on the page. Then it drops to the illustration. It reads as a ferry operator's news or info page with a timetable-alerts sign-up on the side. It feels calm, clean and civic. A second look shows that everything below body-text size is almost invisible.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public page telling passengers about the rebuilt landing stages, with a sign-up for timetable change alerts that it wants them to complete.

**Verdict.** The layout and the body copy are sound, but the page doesn't work as it stands. Every small text element is shrunk to about 3–4 px, so nobody can read it. That includes the form labels, which means the sign-up form can't be filled in with any confidence.

**What would move it most**

1. **The small text size is unreadable, and that breaks the form.** *Defect.* Four things are affected: the two field labels above the inputs at about y≈135 and y≈200, the note under "Sign up," the figure caption under the illustration, and the footer line at the bottom left. All of them render at roughly 3–4 px tall. Even at full resolution I couldn't read them; they show as grey specks. The worst of it is the form. The viewer sees two empty boxes and no idea what goes in which, so they guess (is the second one email? phone? a stop name?) or they leave. The note under the button is probably saying how often they'll hear from you and how to stop. That is exactly the reassurance that gets someone to hand over contact details, and nobody can see it. Text this small also fails any reasonable legibility floor, and WCAG's contrast rules assume text people can actually read. This looks like one broken token for the "small" size rather than five separate choices. Fix it at the root: bring the caption/label size up to about 13–14 px, put the labels in the main text colour, and put placeholder or helper text inside the inputs only if it adds something.

2. **The type scale has a hole in the middle.** *Convention* (Bringhurst; Refactoring UI). I count four sizes: the H1 at about 34 px, the form heading at about 22 px, body at about 18 px, and the microscopic size. A gap of about 18 down to about 4 means the small level is not a smaller step, it's a different universe. That gap is why item 1 happened without anyone noticing. Keep three steps plus one small detail size that is still readable (≈13–14 px), and make the finer distinctions with weight and colour.

3. **The sign-up's ask is vague about what you get.** *Defect, copy.* "Get timetable changes" and "Sign up" don't say which route or stop, how you'll be told, or how often. The traveller's real question is "will I hear if *my* boat is cancelled?" A sharper heading would answer it, something like "Get a message when your crossing's times change," and the button could say the action, e.g. "Send me changes." If the second field picks your route or landing, say so in its label. That ties the sign-up to the article's subject: this landing, your trip.

**Smaller things.**
- The illustration (the grey block, darker deck and blue bar) is too abstract to show anything. The blue rounded bar uses the button's exact colour and shape, so it reads as a second, inert button. Use a photo or a labelled drawing, or drop it.
- The figure caption sits about 10 px under the image, closer than anything else. Once it's readable it will be fine, but check that the spacing groups it with the figure and not with the next paragraph.
- The lower half of the left column, from y≈520 to the footer, is empty. The article ends after one sentence. Fine if that's all there is, but the closing line about prams and wheelchairs is the best news on the page and is buried below the picture. Consider moving it up into the lead.
- Input borders are a darker slate than the card border. That's good for affordance, but check they reach 3:1 against white (WCAG 1.4.11). They look close.

**What's working.**
- The body copy is concrete and human: "benches face the water," "a small kiosk sells tea and bread rolls," "prams and wheelchairs roll straight on." It tells the viewer what changes for them. Keep that voice and carry it into the form copy.
- The two-column split gives the article and the sign-up clearly separate jobs. The bordered card makes the form a distinct, findable unit without shouting.
- Body line length is around 60–65 characters, in the comfortable range (Bringhurst).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the microscopic text is a rendering or capture fault (for example a missing CSS variable or a broken `rem` base) rather than a design choice. Check it in a live browser. Focus states and error messages for the form are also not visible.
