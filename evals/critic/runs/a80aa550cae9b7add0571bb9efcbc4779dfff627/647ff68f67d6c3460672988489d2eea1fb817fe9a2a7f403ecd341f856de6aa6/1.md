**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading, then down three short, evenly spaced paragraphs in a quiet sans on white. It reads as a plain information page for people using a local river ferry: calm, tidy, easy to read. It takes about ten seconds. Then you notice you still don't know when the next boat leaves.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a reference page that tells passengers when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and how to bring a bike, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and has no real faults. As passenger information it fails, because every time a passenger would act on is vague. "Early morning", "late evening", "every forty minutes" with no starting point, "before dusk", "in winter": nobody can plan a trip from this.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times a passenger acts on can't be pinned down (defect).**
   - **First paragraph:** "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first sailing, no last sailing and no anchor for the forty-minute cycle. A reader can't work out whether a boat leaves at 9:00 or 9:20.
   - **Last paragraph:** "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" is the line people will hold the operator to, and it can be read two ways:
     - Reading one: it leaves some time before dusk, so arrive well ahead.
     - Reading two: it leaves around dusk.
     - Dusk moves by over an hour across a winter. Someone who reads it the second way gets stranded on the wrong bank.
   - **"In winter":** it doesn't say which months, so a passenger in March or November can't tell which timetable applies.
   - **"One boat each hour":** it doesn't say on the hour or at what minute.
   - **Fix:** give clock times, for example "First boat 6:40, then every 40 minutes; last boat 22:00", and for winter give the dates and a real last departure. If the times really do change with daylight, say so and point to where the day's times are posted.

2. **The facts are buried in prose when people come to look them up (convention: text people skim should be chunked; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*).**
   - Passengers come back to a page like this to check one thing: the next boat, the last boat, the bike rule. Here that means rereading full sentences.
   - Pull the schedule into a small block of labelled lines or a two-row table: summer and winter, first boat, frequency, last boat, crossing time. Keep the bike paragraph as prose, since it describes a procedure.
   - This also forces item 1 to be fixed: you can't put "late evening" in a table cell.

3. **Direction and fare are missing (defect).**
   - "Runs from the north landing to the market quay" could mean the ferry only goes one way. Someone at the market quay needs to know when the boat comes back.
   - "At no extra charge" tells the reader there is a fare, but the page never says what it is or how to pay.
   - Add one line for the return direction and one for the fare and how to pay.

**Smaller things.**
- **Heading:** "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any ferry's page. Name the crossing, for example "North landing ⇄ Market quay ferry", so people know they've found the right one.
- **"About twelve minutes in calm weather":** this raises the question of what happens in bad weather (longer crossings, cancellations?) and doesn't answer it. One clause would.

**What's working.**
- **Measure and leading:** lines run about 65–70 characters with comfortable line spacing. That is right in Bringhurst's range, so the prose reads without effort. Keep it.
- **Type scale:** there are two sizes, a bold heading and regular body, with a clear step between them. The heading sits closer to its text than to anything else. Nothing competes.
- **Bike paragraph:** this is the one piece of copy that does its job. It says who does what, in what order, and where: wheel on after foot passengers, lock to the side rail. Write the timetable to that standard.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the single column reflows on a phone. At this width it should be fine, but check it, because many passengers will read this on the landing.
