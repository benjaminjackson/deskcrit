**First read.** The eye goes to "Harbour Lights festival" at top left, then drops into three columns of grey-looking prose that are all the same weight. It reads as a local newsletter or a small events web page, and it feels pleasant, calm and old-fashioned. After a few seconds I still couldn't tell you the date, the times, or how to volunteer without going back to read every line.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or flyer section telling local families when and where the festival's events are and asking for volunteers.

**Verdict.** The type is decent, but the page fails at its main job because every date, time and place is buried in three blocks of running prose. A reader who skims, which is most of them, leaves without knowing when to show up. The volunteer request is a dead end.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts people come for are buried in prose. (Defect.)** In the three columns, "first weekend of September", "ten each morning", "noon", "Saturday night… at dusk", "Sunday afternoon", "by one o'clock" and "at four" all sit mid-sentence at body weight. A parent checking when the pie entries are due has to read the whole third column to find "by one o'clock". The columns also have no headings, so you can't tell that column 2 is the lantern walk and column 3 is the pie contest until you've read into each one. The children's boat race is mentioned once in column 1 and never gets a section, so a reader may wonder whether they missed it. Nielsen ("How Users Read on the Web") is clear that text people skim needs headings, short chunks and lists.
   - **Fix:** give each column a short heading that names the event ("Lantern walk", "Pie contest", and "Boat race" if it deserves one).
   - Under each heading, put a two- or three-line block of when and where (day, time, place) ahead of the colour prose. Keep the prose; it has charm.
   - The page has only two type sizes, so it needs a middle level. Make that level from weight (a bold subhead at body size or a little larger), not another size.

2. **The headline and the dates leave out what the reader needs to decide. (Defect.)**
   - "Harbour Lights festival" is a name and nothing more. It doesn't say when or where.
   - "The first weekend of September" has no year and no actual dates. A reader has to work out the dates themselves, and if this page is still online next year it will mislead them.
   - "Leaves the town hall at dusk" gives no clock time, so families with small children can't plan around it.

   These are exactly the details people remember and hold you to.
   - **Fix:** put the actual dates and the place directly under the headline, at a clear second level (for example "Sat 6 – Sun 7 September 2026 · Old market square"), and give the lantern walk a start time.

3. **The volunteer request leads nowhere. (Defect.)** "Volunteers are still needed to hand out programmes, carry chairs, and guard the cake tent…" is the one ask on the page, and it's buried in the middle of column 1 with no name, phone number, email, link or button. Anyone it persuades has no next step, so the request is wasted.
   - **Fix:** take it out of the paragraph and make it its own short block, with the one exact thing to do: who to contact and how ("Help for an hour on Saturday morning: email … or sign up at the school stall").
   - Keep the seagull line. It's the best sentence on the page and it does the persuading.

**Smaller things.**
- The middle column starts about half a line lower than columns 1 and 3 (its first line sits around y≈160 against ≈145). Its lines fall between the lines of its neighbours, so the three columns don't form rows across the gaps (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Set all three column tops to the same line.
- The space around each vertical rule isn't even: column 1's text stops about 20 px short of its rule, while columns 2 and 3 start about 10 px after theirs. Use one equal gap on both sides, or drop the rules and let a wider gutter separate the columns.
- The lower 40% of the frame is empty. That's fine if this is a scrolling page, but on a flyer it's wasted space that the time-and-place blocks above could use.
- "festival" in lower case in the headline looks like an oversight next to the capitalised "Harbour Lights" (taste). Choose a case style on purpose and keep to it.

**What's working.**
- The serif text face, the size and the leading make comfortable reading. Line length of roughly 45–50 characters per column is within Bringhurst's range. Keep all of that when you add structure.
- The headline is clearly dominant through weight and size, so the page does have one place to start.
- The copy has a real voice ("the pie that makes the judges laugh", "guard the cake tent against hungry seagulls"). It doesn't read like a template, and that is what makes people want to come. Keep it, and add the facts around it rather than cutting it.
