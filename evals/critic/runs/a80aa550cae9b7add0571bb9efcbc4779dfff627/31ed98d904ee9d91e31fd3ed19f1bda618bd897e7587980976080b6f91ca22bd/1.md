**First read.** The eye lands on the title "River ferry timetable", then on the heavy black grid, which is the loudest thing on the page. The cells all carry equal weight, so the eye reads row by row from the top and nothing points it anywhere. It feels clean but bureaucratic, like a spreadsheet printed as it was.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a passenger at the north landing or on a phone wants to find, quickly, the next ferry they can catch today to market quay.

**Verdict.** It's legible and correct, but it's built as a data dump, not around the passenger's question. The question is "which boat can I catch today?", and to answer it the viewer has to cross-check the time against the "Runs" column on every row, while thick borders and very wide columns slow each look-up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The day condition sits in the last column, so every row has to be read twice.** A weekend rider has to scan all seven rows and check the far right to rule out 12:40 and 17:10. A weekday rider has to notice that 19:30 isn't theirs. The thing that decides whether a row is valid at all is the last thing read. It's a defect: it slows the viewer and invites a missed or wrong boat. The fix is to split by day type: two short lists, "Weekdays" and "Weekends", each showing only the boats that run. Or keep one list and put the day condition right next to the departure time, marking the exceptions rather than repeating "Daily" five times.
2. **The heavy full grid outweighs the data.** Every cell has a dark border about 2 px thick, so the lines are darker and longer than the numbers they hold. The table reads as a cage, and rows don't stand out from columns. That breaks a convention (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*: ink that carries no data should be removed or muted) and it costs something here. The fix is to drop the vertical rules, make the row rules thin and light grey or replace them with spacing, and keep one firmer rule under the header.
3. **The columns are far too wide for what's in them.** Times of 5 characters sit in columns about 300 px wide, so the gap from "Morning" to "06:40" to "06:52" spans most of the page. The eye has to travel across empty cells and can drift to the wrong row (a defect for a look-up table). The fix is to size the columns to their content and let the table be narrower than the page, which also brings departure and arrival close enough to read as a pair.
4. **The "Period" column repeats itself and adds nothing.** "Morning" and "Morning", "Midday" and "Midday", "Afternoon" and "Afternoon" take up the first and most prominent column, which is where the eye starts each row. The times already say when the boat runs. This is taste, but a strong preference: either drop the column, or turn the periods into small group labels with a little extra space between groups, so the first thing read on each row is the departure time.
5. **"Spring season" doesn't say when it applies.** The subtitle gives no dates, so a passenger can't tell whether this timetable is the one in force today. On a timetable, dates are what people hold the operator to (a defect). The fix is to give the date range, e.g. "Valid 1 March – 31 May".

**Smaller things.**
- Every crossing takes 12 minutes. Saying "Crossing takes 12 min" once would let the arrival column shrink or move into a quieter second position.
- Times look left-aligned. Set them in tabular figures, flush right or centred in a narrow column, so the hours line up vertically.
- The header labels "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are clipped. "Departs North landing" and "Arrives Market quay" match the subtitle's place names, so a stranger doesn't have to connect "north" to "North landing".
- There is no return direction and no line saying where to find it. Riders will look for the trip back. Add a pointer or a second table.
- The space between the subtitle and the table is about the same as the space between the title and the subtitle. Tighten the title group so it reads as one unit above the table.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clear: one large bold title, a grey subtitle, bold headers and regular body text. That's four levels with clear steps, all in one family, so nothing competes in style.
- The rows run in time order and the 24-hour times are easy to read, so the basic structure is sound. The problems are in the framing, not the data.
- The generous left margin and the single left edge shared by the title, subtitle and table give the page a calm, ordered frame. Keep that alignment when you narrow the table.
