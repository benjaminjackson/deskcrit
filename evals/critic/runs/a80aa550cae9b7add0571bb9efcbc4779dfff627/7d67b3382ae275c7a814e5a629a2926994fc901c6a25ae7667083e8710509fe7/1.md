**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the bold serif headline, drops to the byline, and moves into three short paragraphs. It reads as a cycling-route write-up on a small editorial site: calm, plain and practical. The two versions are identical except for one thing, the colour of the body text. In version 1 it's a pale cool grey. In version 2 it's a darker grey that is still softer than the headline.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an article people read from start to finish, by riders planning this route, who need to take in practical details such as surface, tyre width, café hours and lighting.

**Verdict.** Version 2 does the job better. The body text is the whole point of the page, and in version 1 it looks pale enough to slow reading and push contrast close to the minimum. Version 2 fixes that without losing the soft feel. Nothing else changed, and what remains is small.

**What would move it most**
1. **Body text contrast (the change between versions). This is a defect in version 1.** The pale grey in version 1 looks like about #80–#88 on white. That is around 3.5–4:1, below WCAG 1.4.3's 4.5:1 for text this size. You can see the cost: the paragraphs look washed out next to the near-black headline and the darker byline, so the main content reads as secondary. Thin serif strokes at this weight make it worse on lower-quality screens, in sunlight, or for older readers. Those are the conditions you'd expect for someone checking a ride route on a phone. Version 2's darker grey looks comfortably above 4.5:1, and the paragraphs now read as the main content. To confirm, sample both hex values and check the ratio. In version 2, any further hierarchy should come from size and weight, not from making the text lighter again (Refactoring UI).

**Smaller things** (these apply to version 2)
- Line length in the body column runs about 80–85 characters ("The towpath runs flat for thirty-one miles, and for most of that distance it is"). That's above the 45–75 range Bringhurst gives. Narrowing the column by roughly 10–15% would make it easier to find the start of the next line.
- The practical facts are buried in running prose: 31 miles, 32 mm tyres, cafés close at four, lights from October. That's taste, but a rider planning a trip will scan for exactly these. A short facts box under the byline would serve them better.

**What's working**
- The type scale is clean. There are four levels (site name, headline, body, byline), each with an obvious job, and no sizes that look accidentally close.
- The serif headline paired with sans-serif metadata separates the article from the site chrome without any extra decoration.
- The copy is specific and lands on the reader: "puddles that cover the whole path", "no street lamps at all". It tells riders what will actually happen to them. Keep it.

Better: version 2
