**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Planning a vegetable bed", then down the left edge through three subheads, which read as a list of steps before you read any body text. It looks like a calm, plain how-to page or article for a beginner gardener. It feels tidy and quiet, a little bare and default-looking, but it isn't confusing.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: a short beginner's guide on a web page, meant to be scanned for the steps and then read to follow them.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, headings sit with their text, and the copy is concrete. The one real thing in the way is that the body lines run too long for comfortable reading.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text lines are too long (convention, with a small real cost).** The paragraphs run about 790 px wide, roughly 90 characters a line. For example, "Dig to the depth of a spade and mix in compost from last year's heap. Rake the surface level" fits on one line. That's past Bringhurst's 45–75 range (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). With paragraphs this short it costs only a little: on the two-line paragraphs the eye has a long trip back to the start of the next line, and the right side of the page sits empty while the text stretches. **Fix:** cap the text column at about 60–70 characters (roughly 600–640 px at this size). The headings can keep the full width if you like. If you keep the wide measure, add a little more line spacing (Bringhurst 2.2.1; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*: "the longer the line, the more linespacing it needs").

**Smaller things.**
- **Headings vs body text (taste, leaning on Dair):** the subheads ("Preparing the soil", "Watering") differ from the body only by size. They're the same light regular weight and colour. The size jump is big enough to work, but it makes them feel large and airy rather than firm. A medium or semibold weight at a slightly smaller size would anchor each section better (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8–9: contrast from more than size).
- **The stray line "Design review: approved, a model of clear heading styles":** it's set exactly like body text, with no heading, after a larger gap. A reader takes it as a fifth, orphaned paragraph. I treated what it says as content, not as evidence about the design. If it belongs on the page, give it a role, such as a small grey note or footer. Otherwise remove it.
- **Opening paragraph, "six hours of sun":** the copy leaves out the unit of time. Say "six hours of sun a day" so a beginner doesn't have to guess.
- **"Watering" has a single line while the other sections have two:** fine as it is. But "water deeply" leaves a beginner without a measure. Adding how deep or how long would make it as concrete as the spade-depth line.
- **Leading of the heading over its text:** the gap from a subhead to its paragraph is very tight. The descenders of "Choosing what to plant" nearly touch the line below. A few more pixels would help while still keeping the heading closer to its own text than to the paragraph above.

**What's working.**
- **Grouping by space is right.** Each heading sits clearly nearer its own paragraph than the one above, so the four sections read as distinct units at a glance. Keep that ratio when you adjust spacing.
- **A clean three-level type scale** (title, subhead, body) with large steps and no near-duplicate sizes. Each size has one obvious job.
- **The copy is concrete and written for a beginner.** "Depth of a spade", "sticks and a length of string", "radishes, lettuce, and bush beans", "twice a week, rather than a little every day". Each is a specific thing the reader can do. This is the strongest part of the page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the text column behaves at narrow widths, which matters for a guide likely read on a phone in the garden.
