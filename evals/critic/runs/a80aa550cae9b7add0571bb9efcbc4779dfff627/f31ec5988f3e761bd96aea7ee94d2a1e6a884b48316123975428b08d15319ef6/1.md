**First read.** My eye goes to the bold heading, then has to work across a very wide block of grey text. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry. It feels calm and tidy, but the actual facts (how often, how long, the last boat) are hidden inside running sentences, so I have to read every word to find them.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a passenger, possibly at the landing on a phone, needs to quickly find when the ferry runs, how long the crossing takes, what to do with a bike, and when the last boat leaves.

**Verdict.** It's clean but it doesn't do its job well. The text runs far too wide, and the details a passenger acts on are written vaguely ("early morning", "before dusk") and buried mid-paragraph, so the page informs without answering the question someone actually arrives with.

**What would move it most**
1. **The measure is roughly twice as long as it should be** (defect, convention). Each paragraph runs the full content width, about 1,120 px. The first line is around 128 characters, against Bringhurst's comfortable range of 45 to 75, with 66 as the ideal (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). At this length the eye loses its place on the return sweep, and you can feel it in lines like "hold a wider course around the sandbank" and "walking to the stairs." Fix: cap the text column at about 60–70 characters (roughly 600–680 px at this size). Leave the rest of the width empty, or use it for a short facts panel (see 3).
2. **The times a passenger acts on can't be acted on** (defect, copy). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are exactly the lines people plan around and later hold the operator to, and neither gives a time. "Before dusk" can be read two ways: a fixed timetabled departure that happens to fall before sunset, or a time that moves with the season. A passenger who guesses wrong gets stranded on the wrong bank. "The timetable thins to one boat each hour" doesn't say which months count as winter. Fix: give actual first and last departure times for each season and the months each season covers. If the last winter boat really does follow sunset, say so and point to where that day's time is posted.
3. **Nothing can be found by skimming** (defect). Three paragraphs of equal weight, with no subheads and no figures pulled out, mean frequency, crossing time, bike rules and winter changes all carry the same visual weight as "the crew must hold a wider course around the sandbank." Someone checking the page on a quay won't read 140-character lines to find "forty minutes." Fix: give each paragraph a short subhead ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter and changes"). Or pull the four key facts (every 40 min · ~12 min crossing · bikes free · winter: hourly) into a short list or panel above the prose, and keep the prose for the explanation.

**Smaller things.**
- Line spacing looks about 1.5×. That's fine at a normal measure, but slightly tight for lines this long. It fixes itself once the measure is shortened.
- The heading-to-text and paragraph gaps are a bit tight for this width. Opening up the space between paragraphs a little would help them read as separate topics, especially if you don't add subheads.
- Copy: "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes… a little longer when the tide turns" leaves "a little" unquantified. If the delay can be 5–10 minutes, say so, because people will be catching connections.
- "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends someone already on this page somewhere else. Link or show the timetable here instead.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: two sizes with a clear step between them, and a bold heading that's distinct without shouting. Keep that restraint when you add subheads. Use the body size in semibold rather than a third size.
- The text colour is a near-black on white, with strong contrast. Nothing is faint.
- The bicycle paragraph is well written. It says who does what and when ("riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded"), so a first-timer knows exactly what to do. Bring that same concreteness to the times.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the text column behaves on a phone. If most readers are on mobile, the measure problem may only affect desktop, but the vague times and lack of skimmability matter on every screen.
