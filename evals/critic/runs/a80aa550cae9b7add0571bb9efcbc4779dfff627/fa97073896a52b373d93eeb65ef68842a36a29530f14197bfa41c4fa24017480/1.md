**First read.** The eye goes to the bold headline at top left, then drops into three centred blocks of grey-black text, then off into the empty right half of the frame. It reads as a plain information page for ferry riders. It feels calm and tidy at a glance, but uneasy: the headline sits on a left edge and the text below it doesn't share that edge.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or info page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and the rules for bicycles, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't yet do its job well. The facts a passenger needs (how often, how long, last boat, bike rules) are buried in centred prose and given as vague times, so you have to read every line to find one answer, and some answers still aren't there.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is centred.** All three paragraphs are four lines each and centred under a flush-left headline. Every line starts at a different spot, so the eye has to hunt for the next line. The ragged left edge also breaks away from the headline's left edge at x≈120, which makes the block look as if it slid out of place. This is a **convention** (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.3), and here it costs reading speed with nothing gained. Fix: set the paragraphs flush left on the headline's edge, and keep centring for a few short lines only.
2. **The times a passenger acts on are vague.** "Between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "a little longer when the tide turns" are what people use to plan, and none of them gives a time. Someone reading this can't tell whether a 7:10 or a 21:30 boat exists, so they'll either miss the last boat or not trust the page. This is a **defect**. Fix: give the first and last departure times for summer and winter. If the times change, say in one line that the shelter timetable is the authority.
3. **Reference information is written as an essay.** Frequency, crossing time, winter changes and bike rules are mixed into running prose, with no labels, so nothing can be scanned. This is a **defect**: a passenger checking "can I bring my bike?" has to read the whole page. Fix: give each topic a short subhead ("Timetable", "Bicycles", "Winter") or put the key numbers in a short list or table, so each answer can be found by its label.

**Smaller things.**
- The empty right half of the canvas makes the column look stranded rather than placed. Either put the column on a deliberate grid or centre the measure in the page. This is taste.
- There are two type sizes: the headline and the body. That's a clean scale, but adding subheads (item 3) needs a clear third step, set in bold at about body size, not a new in-between size.
- In paragraph two, "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" leaves the reader to work out why it matters to them. Saying it as an instruction ("Keep your bags on your bike during the crossing") is clearer.

**What's working.**
- The line length looks to be about 60–70 characters, right in the comfortable range (Bringhurst). Keep the column width when you switch the text to flush left.
- The headline is strong and plain, and clearly in charge, so the page has one obvious entry point.
- Line spacing and paragraph spacing are generous and even, which keeps the text calm and readable.
