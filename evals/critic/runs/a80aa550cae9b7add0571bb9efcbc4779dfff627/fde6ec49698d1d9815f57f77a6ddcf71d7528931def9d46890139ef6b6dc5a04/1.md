**First read.** The eye goes straight to the white headline on the saturated blue, then to the white "Try it free" button. The order is clear: headline, subhead, buttons, done. It reads in about three seconds as a budgeting app for individuals or households. It feels clean and capable but anonymous. The blue band, white buttons and three-column feature row could belong to any fintech or SaaS product. The right 40% of the hero is empty blue, and the eye drifts there looking for something and finds nothing.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the first screen of a consumer budgeting app's marketing site and its job is to get a stranger to start the free trial.

**Verdict.** It's competent, and the copy is better than average, but it tells instead of shows, and it doesn't answer the one fear that stops people signing up. The page asks people to "connect your accounts" and doesn't say until the third feature, below the hero, that the app can't move their money.

**What would move it most**

1. **The trust answer is in the wrong place.** The hero subhead asks the viewer to "Connect your accounts". For a money app, that's the moment of most doubt: *can this thing touch my bank?* The answer, "The app can see balances and history but can never move money," is the third of three equal-weight columns, outside the blue band, in the smallest body text on the page. The viewer meets the worry right beside the button, and the reassurance is somewhere they may never look. **Defect**: it costs sign-ups at the exact moment someone decides whether to click.
   - Fix: put a short trust line directly under the buttons, in plain words, such as "Read-only: Penny Path can never move your money."
   - Also rewrite the column heading so it says the outcome, not the category. "Read-only access" makes the viewer work out what it means; "We can't move your money" doesn't.

2. **"Try it free" can be read two ways, and it's the button that commits the viewer.** "Free" might mean a free tier for good, or a trial that starts charging after a while. Nothing nearby says which, or whether a card is needed. Someone who reads "free for good" and later gets billed will hold it against you. Someone who suspects a trial hesitates. **Defect**: an ambiguous line on the one action that commits the viewer.
   - Fix: say exactly what's free in the button or in a line right under it, such as "Free for 30 days, no card needed" or "Free for one account."
   - Better still, make the ask let people judge the value on their own terms. "See last month's spending in 2 minutes" points at their own recent money, a result they can check against what they already believe, instead of asking them to trust the claim.

3. **Nothing on the screen shows the product.** The headline promises *seeing* where every dollar goes, but there's nothing to look at. There's no category breakdown, no "you've spent $412 of $500 on groceries" alert, no screen at all. The viewer is asked to imagine the payoff. **Taste, argued:** for a product whose whole promise is visual clarity, one real-looking screen of a month's categories with one alert firing would do more than any line of copy. It would also give the page a focal image and fix the generic look. (I can't tell from the still whether the empty right half is a missing image; see the last section.)

**Smaller things.**
- **Feature row spacing.** The three features sit about 45 px below the blue band, with about 200 px of empty white under them. They look pinned to the hero, not placed. Give them more room above and either close the gap below or let the next section start in view.
- **Type scale.** I count about seven sizes: headline (~56), subhead (~20), logo (~19), feature headings (~18), button labels (~17), nav and body (~15). The 17 to 20 cluster doesn't read as separate levels (Bringhurst; Refactoring UI). Keep four sizes: display, subhead, heading/label, body. Let weight do the rest.
- **"Automatic categories" copy.** The heading could sit on any budgeting app. The body text, "fix any of them in one tap", is the part that sets it apart. Lead with that.
- **Secondary button weight.** "Watch the tour" is the same size and bold weight as the primary, with a heavy 2 px white border on the blue. The fill still separates them, but you could drop the border or lighten the label to make it clearly second (Material, primary versus secondary action).

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** "See where every dollar goes this month" is concrete, about the viewer, and has a time frame. Keep it.
- **The subhead.** "Connect your accounts, set a limit for each category, and get a heads-up before you go over" gives the whole product in three verbs and ends on what the viewer gets. That's the right model for the rest of the copy.
- **The hierarchy.** It's simple and unambiguous: one display line, one supporting line, one filled button. Nothing competes with the headline. Hold on to that restraint when you add a product visual.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **The empty right half of the hero.** The headline is held to about 60% of the width, as if a column was saved for an image. Check in a live browser whether an image or illustration should be loading there. If the emptiness is deliberate, finding 3 becomes the top issue.
- **Contrast of the outline button and nav links.** Both look fine by eye, but measure the white outline against the blue for 3:1 non-text contrast if you change the blue.
