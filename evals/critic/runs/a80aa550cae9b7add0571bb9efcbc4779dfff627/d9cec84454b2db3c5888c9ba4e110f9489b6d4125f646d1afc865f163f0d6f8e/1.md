**First read.** The bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers" catches the eye first. Below it are three even grey-black paragraphs, and nothing in them stands out, so the eye just starts reading at the top. It reads as a calm, tidy notice page from a small transport operator. It feels quiet and trustworthy, but it reads like an essay, not a timetable.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an information page for people about to use a river ferry. They need to find out quickly when boats run, how long the trip takes, the bicycle rules and the winter changes, and then plan around that.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound, but the page doesn't do its job well. A passenger who wants one fact, like the last boat tonight, has to read all the prose to find it, and when they find it the copy gives an impression rather than a time. The biggest problem is the copy and its structure, not the visual styling.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times a passenger acts on are vague, so they can't plan with them** (defect). "Between early morning and late evening" in paragraph 1 and "leaves the quay before dusk" in paragraph 3 are exactly the facts people hold an operator to. Both can be read more than one way. Is early morning 6:00 or 8:00? Does "before dusk" mean ten minutes before or an hour before, and dusk moves by an hour across the winter. If a reader guesses late, they're stranded on the wrong bank. "In winter" has the same problem, because it gives no dates for when the hourly timetable starts and ends. **Fix:** state the first and last departures from each landing as clock times, and give the dates the winter timetable applies. If the times really do follow daylight, say how to find tonight's last boat, for example "the last crossing is posted on the noticeboard each Monday." Keep the reference to the printed timetables in the shelter, but don't make it the only way to get the answer.

2. **The facts are buried in running prose, so the page can't be skimmed** (convention: Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; Nielsen Norman Group on how people scan). The page holds four different kinds of information: frequency, crossing time, bicycles, and winter service. All of them are set as identical paragraphs with no labels, and the numbers are spelled out as words ("forty minutes", "twelve minutes"), so they don't stand out from the text around them. Someone looking for "can I bring my bike?" has to read from the top to learn that it's paragraph 2. **Fix:** give each paragraph a short subhead, such as "Timetable", "Bicycles" and "Winter", set in the body size and bold (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger), so you don't add a new size. Set the key numbers as figures ("every 40 minutes", "about 12 minutes") so they catch the eye on a scan. The timetable facts could become a two- or three-row list.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not the service. Add the route ("North landing ⇄ Market quay ferry") so a visitor arriving from a search knows they're on the right page.
- Paragraph 1, "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat": put a figure on it ("up to 20 minutes") if people are timing a connection.
- Measure: the longest lines, such as "on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail along", run to about 70 characters. That is at the top of the comfortable range (Bringhurst), so don't let the column get any wider.

**What's working.**
- The type is set well for reading. It's a plain sans at a generous body size, with leading of about 1.5 and a ragged right edge with no awkward breaks. Keep these settings when you add structure.
- The scale is just two sizes, a bold headline and the body text, with a clear step between them. Make the new subheads bold at body size, not a third size squeezed in between.
- The tone of the copy is calm and specific about how things happen ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). That reads like a person explaining, so keep it. Just put clock times and dates next to it.
