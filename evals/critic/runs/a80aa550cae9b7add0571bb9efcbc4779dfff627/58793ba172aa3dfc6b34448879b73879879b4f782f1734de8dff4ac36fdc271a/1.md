**First read.** The eye goes first to the row of four buttons in teal, purple, orange and blue, because they're the only strong colour on the page. Next it goes to the heading "Low-light houseplants", then across the four green plant drawings, which all look alike. It reads as a clean, calm plant-shop category page. It feels tidy and generic, and the rainbow of buttons feels unplanned.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a product listing page for a small online plant shop. Its job is to help someone with a dim room choose one of these four plants and add it to the cart.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the copy is better than most, but the page doesn't help anyone choose. The coloured buttons pull the eye away from the plants and suggest differences that don't exist. The four near-identical drawings and thin card details give the viewer almost nothing to compare.

**What would move it most**

1. **Four colours on one action (defect).** The "Add to cart" buttons do exactly the same thing, but each has its own hue. Viewers expect a colour to mean something, so they look for a difference: is the purple one a sale, or is orange a warning? They find none. The buttons are also the most saturated things on the page, so they outrank the plants and the prices. Your eye lands on the buttons before you know what you'd be buying. Material Design's colour roles and Apple's HIG on colour both say one role should get one colour. The fix: give all four buttons one shared colour, and keep any other colour for a real difference in role or state. A muted green or the terracotta of the pots would tie the button to the plants instead of fighting them.

2. **The pictures don't show the plants (defect).** All four cards use the same pot and nearly the same two or three green leaves. A snake plant has stiff upright blades, a ZZ plant has glossy leaves in a ladder up each stem, and a pothos trails. None of that shows here, and the "Pothos" drawing doesn't trail at all. On a shop page the picture is how people choose, and here it can't tell one plant from another. All that's left is four names and four prices, so the page is no better than a text list. The fix: use real photos, or drawings that capture each plant's shape, and show them all at the same scale so size and habit can be compared.

3. **The cards leave out the comparison this page promises (defect, copy).** The subhead makes a precise promise: "do well more than six feet from a window". Then the cards drop it. Each card gives only pot size and final height. Someone picking for a dim room wants to know how much light each plant can take, how often to water it, and whether it's pet-safe. Without that, the choice comes down to price, which is probably not what the shop wants. The fix: add one line per card that tells the viewer what living with the plant means. For example, "Handles a north-facing room; water every 2–3 weeks." Use the same structure on every card so the four can be read across.

**Smaller things**
- Weak type contrast: the plant name (bold, about 18 px) and the price (bold, about 20 px) are nearly the same size and weight, so they look like the same level. Make the price clearly bigger, or make the name lighter.
- The white label on the orange button looks borderline for WCAG's 4.5:1 minimum, because the label is bold but not large. Measure it. This goes away if you use one shared button colour.
- The cards have a border and the images have a grey panel inside them. Two layers of boxing is more than the page needs. Drop one and let spacing do the grouping.
- Below the cards, the bottom quarter of the page is blank. If this is all the content, add a way onward, such as a care-guide link or "More plants for shade".

**What's working**
- The heading and subhead are specific and in the viewer's own terms. "More than six feet from a window" is something a buyer can check against their own room. Keep that voice.
- The grid is clean: one margin, equal gaps between cards, and the image, name, details, price and button stacked the same way on every card. That makes the cards easy to read across, and it's the right base for the comparison line in finding 3.
- The type scale is mostly disciplined: the heading, a name/price level, and a small detail level, all in one family.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- What the buttons do on hover, focus and after "Add to cart": whether the cart count updates or the user gets any confirmation.
- How the four-column row reflows on a phone.
