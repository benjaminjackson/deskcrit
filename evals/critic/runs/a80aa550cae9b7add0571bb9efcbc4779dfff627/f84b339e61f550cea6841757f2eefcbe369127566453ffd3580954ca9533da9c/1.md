**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline. It's four lines of heavy sans that fill the top half of the screen. Then it drops to a short grey-black paragraph and a lone sentence under it. It reads as the header of a ferry timetable page. It feels clean and confident, but there's nothing below it. It looks like a page that stops before it gets to the point.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling ferry passengers when they can cross between the north landing and the market quay this season.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. It's labelled "Spring timetable", but it has no times, no dates and no first or last sailing, so a passenger can't plan a crossing from it. The biggest problem is that the headline takes the most space and says the least, while the facts a rider needs are missing or vague.

**What would move it most**

1. **There's no timetable on the timetable page (defect).** The eyebrow says "Spring timetable", but the only schedule information is "every forty minutes" in the body text. Forty minutes from when? A rider standing at the north landing can't tell whether the next boat leaves at 9:20 or 9:40, when the first boat goes, or when the last one comes back. "By daylight" makes it worse, because the last sailing moves as the days get longer, and the page never says how. Give the first and last sailings, at least by month, plus a departure time each boat leaves from. Ideally add a simple table of departures from each landing. This is the content of the page, and right now it isn't there.

2. **The dates a rider will rely on can be read two ways (defect).** "Spring through early summer" has no start or end date. Some readers will take it to mean March to June, others April to mid-July. If someone plans a crossing for 10 July and the service stopped on 30 June, they're stuck on the wrong bank. Give exact dates ("23 March – 30 June"). The same goes for "in calm weather": say what happens when it isn't calm. Are crossings slower, or cancelled, and where would a rider find out?

3. **The headline uses the most space to say the least (taste, with a practical cost).** At four lines of very large bold type, "Quayside ferry crossings by daylight, spring through early summer" pushes the useful content down, and it carries the same vague phrases as finding 2. Make it a short title ("Quayside ferry — spring timetable"), and use the space it frees for the first-and-last-sailing line. That way the eye lands on something a rider can act on.

4. **The last line reads as a leftover note (defect).** "The cramped, overlapping heading makes this page hard to read." is a comment about the design, not information for passengers. A rider would be puzzled by it, and it makes the page look unfinished. It isn't even accurate: in a close crop of the heading, the line spacing is tight, but the descenders in "crossings" and "through" clear the next line. Delete it.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline's line spacing is about as tight as it can go before letters collide. Opening it a touch (around 1.1) would stop "crossings" and "spring" from crowding each other. A shorter headline makes this moot.
- The gap between the paragraph and the last line is larger than the gap between the headline and the paragraph, for no reason. Once the stray line goes, set one spacing rule for the content that replaces it.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (a small bold grey eyebrow, a large headline and comfortable body text) with big steps between them and nothing in between. Keep it when you add the timetable, and use weight for any new level rather than another size.
- The body text is set at a good measure, roughly 70 characters per line, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Its first sentence is plain and concrete: it names both landings and the crossing time. More of the page should sound like that.
- The single left edge, shared by the eyebrow, headline and body, gives the page a clear spine. Hang the timetable table off the same edge.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the four-line headline reflows on a phone, where it will probably fill the whole first screen and make the missing content even more noticeable.
