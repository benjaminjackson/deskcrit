**First read.** My eye goes straight to the tilted blue "SALE" sticker on the right. It's the only saturated thing on the page, and the red lettering on it shimmers. Next I go back to the left for the headline, "Linens and cookware for the weekend", and then down to four plain product cards. The page reads as a quiet, tasteful homewares shop with a loud sticker pasted on top. Up to the sticker it feels calm and a bit generic.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the home page of a small homewares store, built to get visitors to shop a weekend sale.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do the job yet. The sale is announced but you can't act on it. The loudest element is hard to read and leads nowhere. The products below show no sale prices, so the "40% off" promise never shows up where people shop.

**What would move it most**

1. **The SALE badge is red text on saturated blue, and it vibrates** (top right of the hero). The two colours have almost the same lightness. By eye the contrast is about 1.5:1, which is far below the 3:1 WCAG 1.4.3 asks for even at large sizes. Squint or turn it grey and the word almost disappears. The edge between red and blue also seems to shimmer, which Albers (*Interaction of Color*) and Itten (*The Art of Color*) both warn about. This is the most-seen element on the page, and it's also the hardest to read. **Defect.** Fix: make one colour clearly lighter or darker than the other, for example white lettering on the blue, or mute one of the two.

2. **The hero has no way to act on the sale.** The headline and "Up to 40% off through Sunday" make an offer, but there's no button. The badge looks like a sticker, not a control. The only way into the sale is the small "Sale" link at the far right of the nav. Visitors who want the deal have to hunt for it. **Defect.** Fix: add one primary button under the subhead that names exactly where it goes, such as "Shop the sale: linens and cookware". Either drop the badge or turn it into that button, so you don't have two loud things.

3. **The cards don't show the sale.** "Popular this week" lists $34, $89, $52 and $68 with no original price and no discount. A visitor can't tell whether these items are in the sale or what they save, so the "40% off" claim has nothing to back it up. Prices are exactly what people hold you to. **Defect.** Fix: if these items are reduced, show the old price struck through next to the new one. If they aren't, put sale items in this row instead.

4. **The copy could belong to any store, and the deadline is vague.** "Linens and cookware for the weekend" can be read two ways: products to use at the weekend, or a sale that lasts the weekend. Only the subhead settles it. "Through Sunday" doesn't say which Sunday or what time, and that's the line people will check if they come back Sunday night. **Defect** (the deadline) **and taste** (the headline). Fix: let the headline state the offer ("40% off linens and cookware, this weekend only") and give the deadline a date and time ("ends Sunday 30 Nov, midnight").

**Smaller things.**
- The header has no cart, search or account link. Shoppers expect a cart at top right (Jakob's law / Nielsen 4), so add one.
- The hero text starts about 8 px further in than the logo and the cards, which share the left edge at about 48 px. Line it up with that edge.
- The rotated sticker is out of character with the calm, square layout. Tilted, saturated and in a different type style, it looks pasted on rather than part of the brand (taste).
- The subhead (~18 px) and the "Popular this week" heading (~20 px) are only a step apart in size. The heading's bold weight does the real work, so this is fine, but don't add another size near them.

**What's working.**
- The neutral palette and the warm, muted card image colours feel coherent and fit the subject. Keep that calm base and give it one accent with a job to do.
- The card system is clean: an even gap between cards, a bold name above a plain price, and one consistent inset. It's easy to scan, so keep it and add the sale price to it.
- The headline is set large and bold, and its two lines sit tight together, so it reads as one unit.
