**First read.** The eye goes to the "Overview" heading, then across the three big numbers, then drops to the three coloured lines, which take up most of the screen. The top half reads straight away as a tidy, calm analytics overview. The chart reads as "things are going up," and then you get stuck: you can't tell which line is which channel, what the values are, or which weeks the points stand for.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an analytics overview for a marketer or founder who wants to see in a few seconds how traffic and sign-ups are doing over the last 12 weeks, and which channels are driving them.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The chart takes about 60% of the screen and tells the viewer almost nothing, and the conversion card gives a figure that the other two cards contradict. The layout is sound. The information isn't there.

**What would move it most**

1. **The conversion-rate change is wrong, and it's marked green as good news.** *Defect.* Visits rose 6.1% and sign-ups rose only 2.4%, so conversion must have fallen. Working back from the two cards, last period was about 3,820 sign-ups from about 173,600 visits, which is 2.20%. That means conversion dropped about 0.08 points to 2.12%. The card says "+0.1 pts" in green. A viewer who trusts the card will think conversion is improving when it's getting worse, and that's the one number on this screen most likely to drive a decision. Fix the calculation or the copy, and show a fall in the colour and sign you use for a fall. Check this one against the source data before anything else.

2. **The chart has no legend, no labels, no y-axis values and no dates.** *Defect.* This is the "Visits by channel" panel. The three lines (blue, orange, green) have nothing on them that names a channel, so the title promises a breakdown the viewer can't read. The y-axis has gridlines with no numbers, so you can't tell whether blue is 2× orange or 1.1×, or whether the axis starts at zero. The x-axis has no week labels, so you can't tell when the dip in the blue line at about two-thirds of the way across happened. Every chart needs labelled axes with units, or direct labels, so the reader never has to guess what a mark measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few). The fix is to name each line at its right-hand end in its own colour, which works better than a legend for three series (Tufte; Wilke). Then add a few y-axis values and week or date labels along the bottom.

3. **The chart title names a topic instead of saying what happened.** *Taste, but it matters here.* "Visits by channel" makes the viewer work out the story, which they currently can't do (see 2). Once the lines are labelled, a title such as "[Channel] drove most of the 6.1% rise" would connect the chart to the Visits card above it and answer the question a viewer actually brings to this screen. The other two cards also have nothing below them that explains them: conversion moved, and nothing on the page says why.

**Smaller things.**
- Change text: every change is green and carries its sign, which is the right pairing. Once a negative figure appears it needs red (or a neutral colour) and a "−", or the colour stops meaning anything.
- The chart panel has a dead band of about 70 px below its baseline, where the x-axis labels should go. Use it for them.
- Plot area: four horizontal gridlines with no values are ink that carries no information. Either label them or reduce them to the baseline.
- "+0.1 pts" works as shorthand for analysts. For a general audience, "+0.1 percentage points" removes any chance it's read as +0.1%.
- "Last 12 weeks" in the top right looks like a label, not a control. If it's meant to be a date-range picker, it needs something that shows it can be clicked.
- Blue, orange and green are a reasonably colour-blind-safe set, but direct labels (finding 2) are what make that safe for every viewer.

**What's working.**
- The KPI cards: a clear label, a big number and a comparison underneath. That's three distinct levels in a tight type scale, with no near-duplicate sizes, and every number comes with its comparison. Keep this structure exactly as it is.
- Restraint: one neutral grey background, white cards, and colour kept for the data. Once the chart is labelled, nothing else will compete with it.
- One grid: the cards and the chart panel share the same left and right edges, with an even gap between them, so the page reads as one system.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the chart shows values on hover. Even if it does, a dashboard should be readable at a glance without hovering. Check it live.
