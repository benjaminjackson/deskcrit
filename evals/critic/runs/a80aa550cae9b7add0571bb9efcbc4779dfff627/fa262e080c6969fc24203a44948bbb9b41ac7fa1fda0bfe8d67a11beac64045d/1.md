**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the headline "Change the card you pay with" and then drops into the intro paragraph. In version 1 it then moves down three bold subheads, so you can see the page's structure at a glance: add, make default, declined. In version 2 the eye has nothing to step down through after the intro. The rest of the page reads as one grey column of text, and you have to read it to find your place.

**What it's for.** My assumption is that this is a help-centre article for a customer who wants to swap their payment card, or whose card was just declined. They arrive with one task and want to jump to the step that applies to them.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The only change is the three subheads. In version 1 they are bold and a step larger than the body text. In version 2 they are regular weight and about body size, so they stop working as headings. That matters most for someone who lands here because their card was declined.

**What changed, and why it decides it**
1. **The subheads in version 2 have lost their weight and size (defect).** "Add a new card", "Make it the default" and "If the card is declined" are set in regular weight, about the same size as the paragraphs under them. They look like loose first lines of each paragraph. A help article gets scanned, not read (Nielsen Norman Group on how people read online), and these subheads are what a reader scans. In version 2, someone whose card was declined has to read the whole page to find the one paragraph meant for them. Version 1 sets them bold and one clear step up, which gives a clean scale: title, subhead, body, breadcrumb. Each size has an obvious job (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger). Version 2 makes two levels nearly the same, so one of them disappears. Keep version 1's treatment.

**Remaining findings for version 1**
1. **The interface labels look the same as the prose around them (defect).** "Use for payments", "Add card", "Save", "Settings" and "Billing" are set like ordinary words. "Open the menu and choose Use for payments" stumbles on a first read, because "Use" looks like a verb, not the name of a button. Set the labels people click in bold, so the reader can match the words on the page to the words on the screen.

**Smaller things.**
- The first two sections are steps in order, but nothing on the page says so. Numbering them ("1. Add a new card", "2. Make it the default") and keeping "If the card is declined" unnumbered would show which part is the main task and which part is troubleshooting.
- The intro says "nothing is charged when you make the change". That line covers money, so check it is literally true for every plan, for example that there is no card-verification hold. Customers will hold you to it.

**What's working.**
- The copy is unusually good. The headline uses the reader's own words, every step names the exact place to go and what to do, and the declined section says what to check first and then what to do next.
- The column is comfortable to read. Lines look to be within about 65–75 characters, the line spacing is generous, and there is clear space between sections.

Better: version 1
