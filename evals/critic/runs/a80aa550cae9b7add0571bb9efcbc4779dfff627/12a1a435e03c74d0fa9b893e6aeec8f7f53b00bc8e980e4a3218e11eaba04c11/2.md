**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers across the top (3.8, 64.20, 12.4), then drops to the orange bars and lands on the tall Saturday bar and the short Sunday one. It reads as a small bakery's internal dashboard: calm, tidy and competent. Then the numbers stop me. I can't tell what any of the three means.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an at-a-glance overview for the bakery's owner or staff, used to check how the shop is doing and plan the delivery week.

**Verdict.** The layout and chart are solid, but the headline numbers don't work: they're the most prominent thing on the page and the least readable, because none has a unit, a period or a comparison. Fix those three cards and this is a good dashboard.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three KPI numbers can be read more than one way (defect).** In the top row of cards:
   - "3.8" for conversion is probably a percent, but it could be a ratio.
   - "64.20" for average order value has no currency.
   - "12.4" for active subscribers is the worst. You can't have 12.4 subscribers, so it's 12.4 thousand, or a rate, or a growth figure. The viewer has to guess.

   These are the figures an owner acts on and remembers. If "12.4" is read as 12,400 when it means a 12.4% change, they plan production or spending on the wrong number. Set the unit with the number ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400" or "12.4k"). Your rendering choice for the number itself is fine.
2. **The numbers have no period and no comparison (defect).** Because of this, no card can say whether things are good or bad. Is 3.8% up or down? On target? Over what period? The chart says September 2026, but the cards don't say whether they cover the same month, so the viewer can't connect the top of the page to the bottom. Put the period and a comparison next to each number, such as the change from last period or the target (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). A small line like "▲ 0.4 pts vs August" under each figure would do it, with the up/down shown by arrow or sign, not by colour alone.
3. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding (convention).** "Orders by delivery day" makes the viewer work out the finding. The data has a clear story: orders climb through the week, peak Saturday at about 1,130, and drop to about 380 on Sunday. That matters for staffing and baking. A title or note like "Saturday is the peak; Sunday orders are a third of Saturday's" makes the chart useful at a glance. Optionally, put the value on each bar so nobody has to read it off the gridlines.

**Smaller things.**
- The y-axis has no unit label. The title implies "orders", but "Orders" at the top of the axis would remove any doubt.
- The axis ticks, day labels and source line are small and mid-grey. They look close to the 4.5:1 limit, so check them (WCAG 1.4.3).
- The brand orange is used for both the logo and every bar. That's fine now, but if you ever highlight one bar (say, the peak), you'll need a muted base colour for the rest. Taste.

**What's working.**
- **Clean layout.** The three cards and the chart panel share one grid, the same left and right edges, and one gap between cards. The page reads as one ordered thing.
- **An honest chart.** The bars start at zero, run in natural weekday order, use one colour with no legend, have faint gridlines, and there's a source and date line. Keep all of that.
- **Clear hierarchy.** The type scale is tight: label, big figure, section heading, small detail, with no near-duplicate sizes. The bold, dark numbers in the top row are the right thing to dominate, once they mean something.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states for the nav items, whether the numbers update or have a date picker, and how the three cards reflow on narrow screens.
