**First read.** My eye goes straight to the three big numbers: 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4. Then it drops to the orange bars and stops on the Saturday peak and the Sunday drop. It reads as the overview screen for a small bakery's online shop. It looks calm, tidy and trustworthy until I try to read the numbers. Then I stall, because none of the three tells me what it measures.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the home screen for the bakery's owner or staff, who check it to see how the shop is doing and whether anything needs attention.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the chart is honest. But the three numbers the page leads with can't be read on their own, and they can't be judged either. The first thing anyone sees is the part of the page that tells them the least.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline numbers have no units, and one of them can be read two ways** (top row of cards). *Defect.*
   - "3.8" is probably a percent, but nothing says so.
   - "64.20" has no currency sign.
   - "Active subscribers: 12.4" is the worst. You can't have 12.4 subscribers, so the viewer has to guess: 12,400? 12.4 thousand? A 12.4% rate? The number of subscribers is exactly what an owner remembers and repeats to a supplier or a bank. If they guess wrong, they'll plan around the wrong number.

   Tufte (*Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2) says the reader should never have to guess what a number measures. The fix is to put the unit on the number itself: "3.8%", "£64.20" (or whatever the shop's currency is), and "12,400" or "12.4k".

2. **The numbers have no time period and no comparison, so the viewer can't tell good from bad** (same three cards). *Convention.* Is a 3.8% conversion rate up or down? Is it for today, this month or all time? The chart's source line says September 2026, but the cards don't say whether they cover the same month. A bare number here only invites a question, and this card is the owner's main reason to open the page.

   Few (Perceptual Edge, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") gives the fix: put the unit, the time period and a comparison next to each headline number, such as the change from last month or the target. A small "▲ 0.4 pts vs August" line under each figure would do it, with "September 2026" stated once at the top of the page.

**Smaller things.**
- **Chart title** ("Orders by delivery day"): it names the topic but not the finding. Something like "Saturday is the busiest delivery day; Sunday has the fewest orders" would tell the viewer what to plan for. This is my own judgment, not a published rule: on an ops screen the point is what to do about the pattern.
- **Small grey text** (axis ticks, day names, source line): it's small and mid-grey. It probably passes, but check it against 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3).
- **Exact values**: the chart has no labels on the bars, so the exact counts have to be estimated against the gridlines. Put the value at the top of each bar if staff will plan baking volumes from it.

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honest and quiet.** The bars start at zero, the days are in their natural order, there's one colour, the gridlines are faint and the source is dated. Keep all of that.
- **The type scale is clear.** I count about five sizes, each with an obvious job: the big numbers, the page title, the card and chart titles, the labels, and the small tick and source text. Weight does the rest. When you add units and comparison lines, keep them in the small sizes so the big number stays dominant.
- **The colour is restrained.** The brand orange is used only for the wordmark and the data, so the bars carry the eye.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I can't tell whether the cards show units or periods on hover or in a tooltip. Even if they do, the figure the owner glances at should carry them without any interaction.
