**First read.** The eye goes first to the three big black numbers across the top (3.8, 64.20, 12.4), then drops to the orange bars, where Saturday stands out as the tallest and Sunday as the odd one out. It reads as a tidy, calm overview dashboard for a small bakery's online shop. It looks clean and trustworthy right up until you ask what the numbers mean. Then it stalls.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the owner or staff of Crumbwell Bakes open this daily or weekly to see, at a glance, how the shop is doing and when orders arrive.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are competent, but the page fails at its main job. None of the three headline numbers can be read as stated: they have no units, no time period and no comparison. A viewer can't tell what they mean, let alone whether they're good.

**What would move it most**

1. **The three KPI numbers have no unit, period or comparison** (defect). They're the largest things on the page, so this matters most. Each number leaves the viewer to guess:
   - **"3.8"** is probably a percent, but nothing says so.
   - **"64.20"** is money in an unknown currency.
   - **"12.4" active subscribers** is the worst case. You can't have 0.4 of a subscriber, so it must be 12.4 thousand, or a percentage, or a rate. Different viewers will settle on different answers, and someone reporting "12" subscribers to a partner would be wrong by a factor of a thousand.
   - None of them says what period it covers. The only date on the page is in the chart's source line ("September 2026"), and nothing tells you it applies to the cards.
   - None shows a change, a target or last period's figure, so even read correctly, the owner can't tell whether 3.8% is a good month or a bad one.

   Fix: put the unit in the number ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400"). Add the period ("September 2026", or "last 30 days") once, above the cards or in each label. Add a comparison next to each number, such as the change from last month or progress against a target (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). This is cheap to do and changes the page from decoration into information.

2. **The chart title names the topic but not the finding** (convention, worth following here). "Orders by delivery day" makes the viewer work out the story: Friday and Saturday carry the week, and Sunday drops to about a third of Saturday. If the low Sunday is because of reduced delivery or a closure, a viewer may read it as a problem when it isn't. Fix: state the takeaway ("Friday and Saturday bring 40% of orders", or whatever the true figure is). Add a short note on Sunday if it's a capacity limit rather than lower demand. The source line states the period; this note is a separate addition.

**Smaller things.**
- **Source line and axis labels:** the source line under the chart and the axis tick labels are small and mid-grey. They look close to the 4.5:1 limit, so check their contrast (WCAG 1.4.3).
- **Y-axis:** it has no unit label. The title implies orders, so this is minor, but "Orders" next to 1,200 would remove any doubt.
- **Orange used twice:** the same orange is the brand wordmark and the bars. This does no harm on this screen, but if the chart ever needs a highlight colour (say, for the busiest day), there's no second accent available. This is taste.
- **Empty band at the bottom:** there's roughly 80 px of empty grey below the chart. That's fine for a one-screen dashboard. Note it only because a comparison row or small table would fit there.

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honest:** bars start at zero, days are in their natural order, the gridlines are faint, and there's no legend or decoration. Keep it this restrained.
- **The type scale is clear:** large figures, a medium page title and card titles, and small labels. Three or four sizes with obvious jobs, with weight and grey doing the finer work.
- **The structure is simple:** three equal cards on one grid with even gutters, and a single wide panel below that shares their outer edges. The page is easy to scan once the numbers mean something.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards or bars show exact values and units on hover, and whether the nav items are links to working views. Hover doesn't excuse the cards, though: the headline numbers have to make sense without it.
