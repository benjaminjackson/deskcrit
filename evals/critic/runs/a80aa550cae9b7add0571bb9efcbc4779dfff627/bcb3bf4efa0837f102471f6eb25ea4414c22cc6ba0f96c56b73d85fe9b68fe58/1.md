**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes," then down the centred stack to the blue "See the timetable" button. That path is clean and quick. Then it hits a line under the button, "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge," which talks about the page's own layout and is wrong, because everything on the page is centred. Overall it reads as a quiet, plain, templated landing page for a small local ferry.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about it, so I'm assuming it's the top of a ferry operator's website. Its job is to tell a local or visitor that the crossing is quick and easy and send them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The top two-thirds mostly works: the headline is clear, there's one action and it's easy to find. Two things hold it back. A stray line at the bottom undermines the whole page, and the copy leaves out the facts a rider actually needs to plan a trip.

**What would move it most**
1. **Remove the line under the button.** Defect. "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge" sits at the bottom centre, in the same size and colour as the body text, so it reads as part of the page. A rider can't make sense of it. It's a design note that leaked into the build, and it describes the opposite of what's on screen. It sits right after the call to action, so it's the last thing people read, and it makes the page look unfinished or auto-generated just as they decide whether to click. Delete it. If that spot needs content, use something a rider would want there, such as the fare or where the boat lands.
2. **Give the rider the facts they will plan around.** Defect: this is copy people act on. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives a frequency but no first or last sailing. Someone deciding whether they can get home tonight can't tell if "late evening" means 9 pm or 11 pm, and guessing wrong strands them. The page also never names the place the boat goes to: "north landing" is only the departure point, and "the bay" is the only hint of where you end up. Price is missing too, even though "Bicycles travel free" suggests there's a fare for people. Fix: put actual times in the paragraph ("first boat 6:40, last 22:20"), name both ends of the route, and give the adult fare. The timetable link then becomes a way to check details, not the only way to learn the basics.
3. **The page shows nothing of the crossing.** Taste. The selling point is the experience: "calm," "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck." But the page is black text on white with nothing to see. Anyone's page could look like this, so nothing makes a first-time visitor picture the trip. One wide photo of the deck or the bay behind or beside the headline would do more for "calm" than any amount of copy. If photos aren't possible, use a simple route map with the twelve minutes marked on it.

**Smaller things.**
- Centred paragraph (lines 2–3 of the body): a centred paragraph of several lines has ragged edges on both sides, so each new line starts in a different place. Set the body and list flush left in a narrower column (Bringhurst; Tschichold on not centring everything by default). At three lines the cost is small.
- The feature list repeats the paragraph. "Seats on the open deck" restates the paragraph's own "open deck," and "Bicycles travel free" repeats the bicycle mention. Either cut those two from the paragraph or swap them for list items that add something new: fare, accessibility, whether you can bring a dog.
- The list has no bullets or other marker, so the three centred lines look like loose captions rather than a list. Left-align them with ticks or bullets.
- Type sizes collide: the body looks about 20 px and the list about 18 px. That's too close to read as two levels. Use one size, or separate them by weight or colour rather than size (Refactoring UI).
- Headline line break: "twelve / calm minutes" splits the key phrase. Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes."
- The brand name, "Coastal Ferry Co.", is a small blue line in the same blue as the button, so it looks like a link and is easy to miss as a brand. Give it a logo or a distinct treatment, and keep the blue for things you can click.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a specific promise you can check, with a number and a feeling ("twelve calm minutes"). That's far better than generic copy. Keep it.
- There's one primary action, with a label that says exactly what happens when you click ("See the timetable"). It has a solid fill and strong contrast, and nothing competes with it.
- The plain type and generous space suit the "calm" message. Add facts and a picture without crowding the page.
