**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three grey-black paragraphs of equal weight. It reads as a plain, calm information page, more like a notice pinned in a shelter than a timetable. Nothing past the heading pulls the eye, so you end up reading every word from the top to find what you came for.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page that tells people about to take the ferry when it runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and readable, but the page doesn't do its job. The facts a passenger needs to act on are vague or missing, and the ones that are there are buried in running prose. Someone checking "when is the next boat?" leaves without an answer.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times a passenger acts on can't be pinned down.** *Defect.* "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "in winter", "one boat each hour" and "before dusk" are all things a passenger plans around, and none gives a time they can use. When is early morning: 6:00 or 8:00? Does "every forty minutes" run from the hour? Which months count as winter? "Before dusk" moves by about two hours across the season, so a reader who guesses wrong misses the last boat home. The page even says the real times are on "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing". That sends the reader to a physical place to get what this page should have told them. Fix: state the first and last departure from each landing, the summer and winter dates, and the actual winter last crossing, or link to the timetable right here.

2. **Facts people scan for are buried in paragraphs.** *Convention* (basics: text that people skim is broken into chunks; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Nobody reads a ferry page from start to finish. They come with one question: how often, how long, can I bring my bike, is it different in winter. Here every answer sits mid-sentence in the same weight as everything around it, so the reader has to read all of it to find one fact. The first paragraph makes this worst: it's one 60-word sentence that starts with the timetable and drifts into the crew steering round a sandbank. Fix: break the page into short labelled pieces (Times, Crossing time, Bicycles, Winter), each leading with the fact. Put the numbers in figures ("every 40 min", "about 12 min") so they jump out. Cut or shorten the sandbank detail to what it means for the passenger: "allow up to X minutes at low tide."

3. **The delay note never says how long.** *Defect* (copy). "A little longer when the tide turns against the boat" leaves passengers with a connection to make unable to plan. Give a figure ("up to 20 minutes on low-tide mornings") and say who it affects.

**Smaller things.**
- The last line of paragraph one is "tide." on its own. This is a runt (Bringhurst), and it will mostly go away once you split that sentence.
- The column sits on the left of a 1280 px frame and leaves the right half as empty leftover space. On a reading page that's acceptable, but it looks unplanned. Either centre a reading column or use the right side for a compact times table. *Taste.*
- The bicycle paragraph is clear, but "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" is an instruction and could open the section, ahead of the fare note.

**What's working.**
- The type is easy to read: a well-made sans at a comfortable body size, dark text on white, leading that suits the line, and a measure of roughly 65–75 characters. Keep all of that.
- The type scale is simple: one bold heading and one body size, with a large, clear step between them. No sizes fight each other. Keep it to two or three levels when you add section labels, and make the labels distinct by weight, not by a third size.
- The tone is plain and human ("Bicycles ride on the lower deck at no extra charge"). Keep that voice when you add the hard numbers.
