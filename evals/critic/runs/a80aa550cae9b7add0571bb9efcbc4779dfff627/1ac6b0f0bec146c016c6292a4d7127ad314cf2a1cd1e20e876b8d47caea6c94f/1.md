**First read.** The eye goes straight to the headline. It's huge, heavy, near-black, and nothing competes with it. Then it drops to the grey subhead and stops on the blue button. Within about two seconds I know this is a weekly planning tool for teams. It feels calm, confident and clean, but also generic: Inter on white with a blue button could be any SaaS launched in the last five years. Below the button, the bottom third of the screen is empty white.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a marketing site for team leads or managers, and its job is to get them to start a trial.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy and type are solid. The page fails at persuasion, not layout. It promises "one honest picture of the next five days" but never shows that picture. It then asks the viewer to start a trial on trust, through a look and a button that could belong to any product.

**What would move it most**

1. **The promise is never shown** *(defect)*. The subhead's whole pitch is a picture: tickets, meetings and leave combined into one view of the week. Nothing on the screen shows it. The bottom third (roughly y 560–800) and the right side beside the subhead are empty. A team lead has to imagine what "honest" means and whether it fits how their team works, and most won't bother. The product is a visual artifact, so the artifact is the best argument for it. Put a real week view on this first screen, ideally one where a meeting or someone's leave visibly takes capacity away from planned tickets. That makes "the week your team *actually* works" something the viewer sees for themselves. The stock look matters less once the product is visible, because the product is the one thing on this page that can't be anyone else's.

2. **The ask is generic and asks for trust** *(defect, copy)*. "Start a free trial" names a commitment, not an outcome. Hearing this exact button on every SaaS page has taught viewers it means a sign-up form and an empty workspace. Nothing here lets them judge the value against something they already trust. The strongest ask would point at their own world: something like "See this week's real capacity" or "Connect your Jira and see this week". It names a specific first step and puts the result next to the plan they already believe. Keep the trial framing in a small line under the button ("Free for 14 days, no card"), not as the button label.

**Smaller things.**
- Subhead: "leave" is the British/HR term. US readers may stumble on it or briefly read it as the verb. "Time off" is unambiguous if the audience includes the US.
- Subhead colour is a mid grey. It looks fine for contrast, probably around 5:1, but check it meets 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3), because it's carrying the actual explanation.
- The headline's last word, "works", is a little vague. "Plan the week your team actually has" would make the capacity point sharper. That's my taste, not a rule.

**What's working.**
- **Type scale.** There are four clear levels: the roughly 80 px headline, the roughly 20 px subhead, then nav and button text at about 16 px, with the logo set apart by weight. The headline differs from the subhead in weight and colour as well as size, which is the contrast Dair asks for. Keep this scale.
- **Headline setting.** The tracking is tight and the leading is tight, so the two lines read as one unit. The line break after "team" falls at a natural phrase boundary.
- **One primary action.** Only the button is filled. "Sign in" and the other nav links stay as quiet text, so there's no competition for the click.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The empty bottom band might be a hero image or product shot that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check in a live browser. If an image does live there, make sure some of it shows above the fold at 1280×800. If it doesn't, finding 1 stands as written.
- I couldn't see the button's hover and focus states.
