**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Harbour Lights Festival". The cream italic is huge on deep navy, so nothing competes with it. Then it goes up to the amber date and down to the two-line description. It reads as the hero of a small-town event page: calm, warm and a little nostalgic. Within three seconds I know what it is and when it is. I don't know where it is or what I'm meant to do next.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a web page for a free local harbour festival, and its job is to get people nearby to put the day in their diary and turn up.

**Verdict.** The look is strong and the hierarchy is right. As an event announcement, though, it leaves out the two facts people act on: where the festival is, and which year. With no next step either, the reader can admire it but can't plan around it.

**What would move it most**

1. **It never says where.** This is a defect. The copy mentions "the quay" and "the old net shed", but never names the town or harbour. Those phrases only mean something to people who already know the place. A stranger, or someone who sees the page shared, can't tell whether this is twenty minutes away or two hundred miles. Location is the second thing anyone needs after the date. Put the place name in the amber line with the date (for example "Saturday 14 June, from noon · [Town] Harbour"), or in a line right under the headline. Don't leave it for further down the page.

2. **The date has no year, and the weekday may not match the year people will assume.** This is a defect. In the dates people act on, any line that can be read two ways counts as a defect. 14 June was a Saturday in 2025. In 2026 it falls on a Sunday, and in 2027 on a Monday. If this is the 2027 festival, "Saturday 14 June" is wrong, and people will turn up on the wrong day or decide it's last year's page. If it's an archived 2025 page, a reader today can't tell it's out of date. Check the date and add the year.

3. **Nothing to do after reading.** This is a convention (Krug: make the next step obvious). The page ends on "Free entry for everyone." Below that is about 200 px of empty navy, so the eye reaches the bottom and stops. For a free event, the useful next steps are specific ones: "Add to calendar", "See the day's programme", "How to get there and where to park". Pick one and make it the single clear action below the paragraph. The empty space at the bottom is where it should go.

**Smaller things.**
- The amber line says "from noon" but gives no end time. Lantern boats at dusk suggest the evening, so add an end time (for example "noon till 10pm") so people know whether to come in the afternoon or after dark.
- The bottom fifth of the frame is empty, and the content sits in the top three-quarters. That makes the empty space look like leftover space rather than space chosen to give the headline room. The CTA in finding 3 would fix this. Otherwise, move the block down a little. (Taste.)
- The heading's line spacing is tight, which is correct. The descender of "g" in "Lights" comes close to the "l" of "Festival" below it, but they don't touch, so leave it.

**What's working.**
- The scale contrast is excellent. There are three clearly separate levels: a small tracked amber eyebrow, a very large italic display line, and a sans paragraph at reading size. The eyebrow and paragraph are close in size, but caps, colour and tracking keep them apart, which is the right way to do it (Refactoring UI: distinguish by weight and colour, not by another size). Keep this scale.
- The palette fits the subject. The navy reads as water at night, and the single amber accent reads as lantern light. Keep amber for that one job and don't spread it around.
- The paragraph copy is concrete and evocative: lantern boats, music on the quay, a fish supper. Each one is a specific thing you can picture, and "Free entry for everyone" answers the cost question before anyone asks it. The measure is comfortable, at around 60 characters.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the very large headline wraps on a phone, and whether "Harbour Lights" breaks badly at narrow widths. Check this at 320–390 px.
