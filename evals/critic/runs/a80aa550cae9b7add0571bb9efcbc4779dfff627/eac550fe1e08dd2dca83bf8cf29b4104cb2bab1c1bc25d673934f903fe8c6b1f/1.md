**First read.** The bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers" gets the eye first. It then drops into two easy paragraphs and stops at a smaller last line. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy. Nothing fights for attention.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: a web page that tells someone planning a crossing when the ferry runs, whether they can bring a bike, and what might change their plans.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read, but the page doesn't give a passenger enough to plan a trip. It has no actual times. The one warning that could leave someone stranded is set as the smallest, quietest line on the page.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule has no times anyone can plan around (defect).** The first paragraph says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening." A passenger can't tell from that when the first boat leaves, when the last one leaves, or which minutes past the hour it sails. Times are exactly what a reader acts on and later holds the operator to. "Late evening" could mean 8 pm to one reader and 11 pm to another, and the one who guesses late misses the last boat. "Every forty minutes" doesn't line up with the clock, so even knowing the interval, the reader can't work out a departure time. The fix: give the first and last sailing from each landing, and give the departure times, or a few sample times, so the pattern is plain. "First boat 6:20 from the north landing, last 22:40 from the market quay" does the job the current sentence only points at.

2. **The warning is the smallest thing on the page (defect).** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." is the only line that tells the reader to do something, and the only one that protects them from a wasted trip. It's set a clear step smaller than the body text and sits last, where it reads like a footnote and is easy to skip. The page ranks its information backwards: the bicycle rule gets more weight than the warning that the whole schedule above might be wrong. The fix: bring it up to body size at least. Better, put it right next to the schedule it qualifies, and use weight or a short label to set it apart, not a smaller size (Refactoring UI: emphasise with weight and colour, not just size).

3. **The warning is too vague to act on, and it sends people to the place they're trying to plan a trip to (defect).** "In winter" doesn't say which months or from what date. "The noticeboard at the landing" is only useful once you're standing at the landing, which is too late to plan from home, and the page doesn't say which of the two landings it means. Read as a stranger would, the line leaves the reader wondering whether the timetable above applies today. The fix: give the dates the winter timetable runs, and either show the winter times here or say where to find them before leaving home.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what the page answers. Something like "Ferry times and bikes: north landing to market quay" tells a searcher at a glance that they're on the right page. This one is taste.
- Bicycle paragraph: "lock them to the rail along the side of the deck" is clear and specific. Just check that it doesn't need a word on whether riders bring their own lock.
- Measure: the body lines look about 72 to 75 characters long, at the top of the comfortable range (Bringhurst: 45 to 75). That's fine as it is; don't let the column get any wider.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined. I count three sizes, each with an obvious job. The headline is set apart by weight as well as size, the body text is generous with comfortable line spacing, and the contrast is strong. Keep this restraint when you add the times; a small table or a short list will fit it without adding a fourth size.
- The bicycle paragraph is the model for the rest. It says who does what, when, and where ("after foot passengers have boarded", "the rail along the side of the deck"), and it settles the cost ("at no extra charge"). Write the schedule and the winter note to that standard.
- Space separates the blocks cleanly, with no rules or boxes, so the page stays quiet and quick to scan.
