**First read.** The eye goes to the bold headline, "Notes for the summer fete", then gets pulled down the paragraphs by the bold phrases: "labelled with their ingredients", "collected by four", "on the field only". The two italic phrases barely register on a first pass. It reads as a calm, tidy, slightly old-fashioned notice from an organiser to helpers. It's pleasant to look at, but you have to read it rather than scan it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a short practical notice for stallholders, bakers and visitors to a village or school fete, telling them what to do and by when.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is competent, but the piece doesn't do its job well. The instructions that matter are buried in prose, and the emphasis has no consistent logic. Someone skimming for "what do I need to do" picks up the bold items and misses the most time-critical one: arrive before ten.

**What would move it most**

1. **The emphasis has no system, so the most urgent instruction is the least visible.** *Defect.* Five phrases in six lines are emphasised, using two different signals for the same kind of information. Two times are italic ("arrive before ten", "three o'clock sharp"), and the third time is bold ("collected by four"). Bold is also used for non-time rules ("labelled with their ingredients", "on the field only"). Bold jumps out when you skim and italic doesn't, so a skimmer gets the cake labels, the four o'clock deadline and the parking, but not the arrival time. The arrival time is the one instruction that breaks the day if people miss it. Readers also look for meaning in a difference: "is italic a time and bold a rule?" Then "collected by four" breaks that pattern. Lupton (*Thinking with Type*) says emphasis inside text usually needs only one signal, and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) warns that mixed emphasis is restless and gets in the way of reading. Fix: pick one signal and use it only for what the reader must act on. Bold is the right choice here because it survives skimming. Then check that the arrival time gets it.

2. **Separate rules for different people are packed into two paragraphs.** *Defect (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").* This covers four instructions for at least three audiences: stallholders (arrive before ten), bakers (label cakes, leave them on the trestle), raffle ticket holders (the draw at three, the four o'clock deadline) and drivers (park on the field). Each person has to read all of it to find their own line. Once each rule has its own line, the emphasis problem in point 1 mostly goes away too. Fix: set it as a short list, one instruction per line, each starting with the time or the action. Group or label the lines by who they're for if that helps.

3. **The copy leaves out or blurs the facts people will act on.** *Defect.* There's no date, no venue and no contact, so this only works if it's attached to something else. "Collected by four" can be read two ways. It could mean winners must claim their prizes by four, or that organisers will collect the tickets. A reader who takes it the second way loses their prize. "Or the prizes go back in the box" doesn't say whether that means a redraw or that the prize is simply lost. "The trestle by the door" assumes you already know which door. Fix: say who does what, by when: "Winners: claim your prize by 4 pm, or it goes into a redraw." Add the date and place under the heading.

**Smaller things.**
- The apostrophe in "o'clock" looks straight rather than curly. Zoom in to check, and use a real apostrophe (’).
- The block sits in the upper left with a large empty area at the lower right. That's fine for a note, but if this is a poster or a page, either size the type up to fill the space or let the space look deliberate.
- "Fete" without the circumflex is accepted British usage. Just keep the spelling consistent wherever else the event is named.

**What's working.**
- One serif family throughout, with a heavy headline against the regular-weight text. The heading is obviously the heading without needing to be huge, and that contrast in weight is worth keeping.
- The line length (about 70 to 75 characters) and the generous line spacing make the text comfortable to read. The space between paragraphs is clearly bigger than the line spacing, so the two paragraphs read as separate.
- The tone is warm and plain, which suits a community event. Keep that voice when you turn it into a list.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Where this is published (printed sheet, email or web page) and therefore how big it will be in use. That affects whether it needs to work as a poster readable from a few metres away.
