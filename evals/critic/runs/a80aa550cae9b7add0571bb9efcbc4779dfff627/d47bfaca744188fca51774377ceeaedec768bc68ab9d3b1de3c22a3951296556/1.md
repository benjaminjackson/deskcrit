**First read.** The eye goes to "About the trust" and then drops into a single grey block of eleven lines. Nothing inside the block stands out, so I either read every word or leave. It reads as a tidy, quiet "About" section for a small local charity. It feels calm and honest, and a little like a Word document.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is the About section of a small conservation trust's website. Its jobs are to tell locals what the trust is and to get some of them to turn up to a work day.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean, but the section doesn't do its job. The one thing a reader could act on, the open invitation to the Saturday work days, is buried in the middle of one unbroken paragraph. It has no clear date, no time, no meeting place and no next step.

**What would move it most**

1. **The invitation is hidden inside the history.** The paragraph covers four things in a row: what the trust looks after, how it began in 1987, its size (staff, volunteers, members), and then an open invitation to work days with what's provided and what the work involves. They all get the same size, weight and colour. A reader who skims, which on a web page is most readers, takes in the first two lines and never reaches "anyone is welcome." That half is the only part that asks anything of them, and the most generous part: tools, gloves, a hot drink, no experience needed. **Defect.** A long unbroken block on a screen is a known problem (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Fix: split it in two. Put the description and history first. Then give the invitation its own short block with its own heading, something like "Join a work day." The four reassurances (tools, gloves, hot drink, no experience) can be a short list.

2. **"Every second Saturday" can be read two ways, and there's no next step.** It could mean every other Saturday (fortnightly), or the second Saturday of each month. Someone who reads it the wrong way turns up on the wrong day, alone, at a quarry. "For the morning" gives no start time, and the text never says where to meet or whether to tell anyone first. **Defect:** this is the line people will act on and later hold you to. Fix: state the rule plainly ("the second Saturday of every month, 9am to 12:30") and give the next date. Name the meeting point. End on one exact action the reader can take, such as "Meet us at the quarry car park on Saturday 13 December. No need to book." Better still, add a link to the dates.

3. **"About the trust" is a label, not a reason to read on.** It names the section but doesn't say who the trust is or why a passer-by should care. It's the only large element on the page, so it's wasted on a line that could head any charity's page. **Taste, argued:** the heading is the one line every visitor reads. Use it to say what the trust does ("We keep the moor's paths open") or to name the place.

**Smaller things.**
- The figures "eleven miles", "four staff", "around ninety volunteers" and "just over two thousand" are spelled out in the middle of sentences. Set as figures, or pulled out as a short row of facts, they would show the trust's scale at a glance.
- The text column sits on the left with about 480 px of empty white to its right. That works for a single column of text, but at this width the section looks unfinished. A photo of a work day, or the work-day block from point 1, could fill that space and give the page a second thing to look at.

**What's working.**
- The measure looks to be about 60 to 65 characters, with generous line spacing (around 1.6) and a plain, readable sans-serif. That is comfortable reading (Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range). Keep it when you split the text.
- The heading sits tight to its paragraph, is clearly bolder and larger, and uses only two sizes. The hierarchy is simple and doesn't fight itself.
- The tone is good: specific, modest and local ("clearing drains, mending stiles, and cutting back gorse"). That concrete detail does more to recruit volunteers than any slogan would. Keep it and give it room.
