**First read.** The eye goes to the tall left bar, then to the headline, then down the staircase of bars to "Events". It reads as a clean internal growth report: calm and trustworthy. Only after the chart does the eye find the right-hand note, and that note holds the point of the page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a Q3 growth report page that tells the team or leadership where sign-ups came from and what will change because of it.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The chart is honest, sorted, directly labelled and sourced, and the numbers add up (5,400 total; organic is 34%; podcast plus events is 670). The biggest problem is that the chart and the conclusion are separate. The headline only names the topic, every bar looks the same, and the two findings plus the budget decision sit in small grey-black text off to the side. So a viewer who only glances at the chart leaves knowing the ranking but not what it means.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart doesn't show the story the note tells** (convention, and taste in how to fix it). The note picks out two things: organic search (the first bar) and podcast ads plus events (the last two bars). In the chart all six bars are the same blue, so the eye can't link "a third of all new accounts" to a bar, and the low pair that drives the budget decision looks like just the end of the row. Charts read best when one accent marks the point and the rest goes neutral (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*; Few). The fix: keep the blue for organic search, grey the middle bars, and give the podcast/events pair its own treatment. Then put a short note on the chart itself, such as "34% of sign-ups" above the first bar and a bracket over the last two reading "670 together". The bars would then make the argument without the sidebar.

2. **The headline names the topic, not the finding** (convention). "New sign-ups by channel" tells the viewer what they're looking at. The most important line on the page is the decision, "The events budget moves to referrals in Q4", and it sits as the last sentence of a small side paragraph. A chart title or caption should state the takeaway. Here the page also has a decision, which is what readers will act on and later hold the team to. The fix: make the headline the finding, for example "Organic search brought a third of Q3 sign-ups; events and podcasts barely moved", and keep the current line as the subtitle. Also give the Q4 budget move a line of its own instead of leaving it as the end of a paragraph.

3. **"What changed" has no "before" to compare with** (defect, small but real). The note says organic went "up from a quarter in Q2", but the chart shows Q3 only, so the viewer has to take the change on trust. The panel heading promises change, and nothing on the page shows any. The fix: add a thin Q2 marker or a faint tick on each bar. At minimum, add the Q2 organic count or share to the note so the claim can be checked.

**Smaller things.**
- Every bar has its value printed on it, so the y-axis and its gridlines say the same thing twice. Drop the axis, or drop the gridlines and keep only the baseline (Tufte, data-ink).
- The note says budget moves to referrals but not why. Add the reason in the viewer's terms, e.g. "referrals brought 1,210 accounts, nearly five times events".
- The sidebar heading (y≈175) lines up with neither the subtitle nor the top of the chart (≈195). Align it to one of them so the two columns share a top line.
- The empty lower quarter of the frame is fine if this page sits in a longer report. If it's the whole page, the content looks like it is floating near the top.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest: bars start at zero, are sorted by value, are labelled directly, and there's no legend. Keep all of that when adding emphasis.
- The subtitle and source line are well written. They say what is counted ("by the channel of the first visit"), the exact date range, and the total, so no one has to guess what a number means.
- There is little to look at: one hue, one typeface, a quiet nav with the current section shown in bold. Adding emphasis will work because there is so little competing for attention.
