**First read.** The eye goes to the masthead, "The Wexley Gazette", then drops onto three bold headlines of the same size side by side and doesn't know which to read first. Next it notices the holes in the body text, worst in the first column ("months of repairs to its arches.") and the middle column ("the old corn exchange from next"). Below the stories, the bottom half of the screen is empty. It reads as a quiet, tidy small-town paper, but it looks unfinished rather than calm.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the front page or home screen of a local news site or newsletter, for residents who want to see quickly what has changed in town.

**Verdict.** It doesn't work yet. The type is good and the copy is good, but two things get in the way: justified text in narrow columns has broken the reading texture, and the page has no lead story, so the reader gets no help deciding what matters.

**What would move it most**

1. **Justified text in columns this narrow is full of holes.** This is a convention and a defect here. The columns look about 45–50 characters wide. At that width justification can't keep word spaces even, so some lines open into gaps several spaces wide: "months of repairs to its arches.", "the old corn exchange from next", "a regional prize for the vegetable", "overdue after a wet summer kept". The eye trips on each gap, and the grey of the column turns blotchy. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 34) says that below about 50 characters "even wordspacing becomes difficult, if not impossible". Bringhurst (§2.1.1–2.1.2) calls the result "white acne". **Fix:** set the body ragged-right, which suits this measure and this informal tone. If it must stay justified, turn on hyphenation and widen the columns.

2. **Three equal stories, so there's no front page.** This is a defect. The three headlines are the same size and weight in three equal columns, so nothing tells the reader which story matters most. A local paper's front page exists to make that call for them. The reader has to scan all three to decide, and the page reads as a list, not an edition. **Fix:** make one story the lead. The bridge reopening is the obvious pick because it affects the most people. Give it a bigger headline and a wider column or a full-width slot, and set the other two below it as secondary stories.

3. **The bottom half is leftover space, not designed space.** This is taste, argued. The content ends about halfway down, and the space below isn't shaped by anything. Ruder's tension between printed and unprinted areas needs the empty space to be planned, and this looks like the page ran out. Readers take it as a half-loaded page or a thin edition. **Fix:** this follows from fix 2. A lead story with more weight, plus an image or a dated "In brief" strip, would fill the space. Otherwise, size the page to its content.

**Smaller things**
- The masthead has no date or issue number. That's what tells a news reader the page is current, so add a dateline under the double rule.
- The masthead is not much bigger than the story headlines (it looks about 36 px against about 24 px). It reads as one more heading, not the name of the paper. Make it clearly bigger, or set it apart another way, such as letter-spaced or set in its own style.
- The bridge story leaves the reader to put the dates together: cars return in April, but walkers and cyclists in March. The facts are right, but a reader skimming the first line takes "April" as the reopening date. Move the March line up, or put it in the headline ("Bridge reopens to walkers in March, cars in April").
- The market story's "from next month" has no fixed point on a page with no date. Give the month.

**What's working**
- The type: a sturdy serif in a dark, warm grey, with generous line spacing. It reads well and suits a local paper. Keep the typeface and the leading when you change the alignment.
- The double rule under the masthead is a small, apt newspaper cue that does its job without fuss.
- The copy is plain and concrete. Every story says who did what, where and when ("sixty stalls, a small cafe, and a stage for local bands on the first Saturday of each month"). It's the strongest part of the page.
