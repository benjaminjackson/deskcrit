**First read.** The eye goes to "Pricing", then straight across the three prices, $9, $29 and $79, which are the heaviest marks on the page. Then it stops. Nothing in the cards tells you what to do, so the eye drifts up to the blue "Start free" button in the far top-right corner, or down into the empty lower third. It reads as a clean, competent, generic SaaS pricing page for a scheduling or booking tool. It feels calm and finished, but there is no way forward from it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the pricing page of a booking and calendar product for individuals up to mid-size companies. Its job is to help a visitor choose a plan and start on it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The prices are easy to read, but you can't act on a plan from where you read it, and the page doesn't help you choose between plans or say exactly what you'd pay. As a layout it's tidy; as a pricing page it stops halfway.

**What would move it most**

1. **No plan has an action (defect).** None of the three cards has a button. A visitor who settles on "Team" has to work out on their own that the generic "Start free" button in the header, about 900 px away, is how to get Team. They also can't tell whether that button starts Team, Solo, or a trial of something else. The page fails at the moment it exists for. Give each card its own action at the bottom, worded with the plan: "Start Team free". Make one of the three the primary filled button and the others outline buttons, so there is still one high-emphasis action on the view (Material Design and Refactoring UI on primary versus secondary actions).

2. **Nothing helps the visitor choose (defect).** The three cards are identical in weight, border and layout. Nothing is marked as the plan most teams pick, so a visitor gets no signal and has to compare three lists of three lines each. Pick the plan you want most people on, probably Team, and make it dominant: a tinted or heavier border, a "Most teams choose this" label, and the filled button from point 1. Breaking the repetition of the cards on purpose is what makes that one card the place to look.

3. **The price is ambiguous (defect, and these are words people hold you to).** "$29 / month" can mean two things: $29 for the whole team, or $29 for each person. The Team plan is called "Team" but is limited by "Up to 10 calendars", which makes this worse. If the price is per user and a reader takes it as a flat fee, they feel misled at checkout. If it's flat and they take it as per user, they think it's too expensive and leave. Say it outright: "$29 a month for your whole team" or "$29 per user, per month". Also say whether "calendars" means people, rooms or booking pages.

4. **The plans don't say whether they build on each other (defect).** Solo lists "Booking page" and "Email reminders". Team doesn't. Read literally, Team has no booking page, and Business has neither booking pages nor reminders. A buyer can't tell whether moving up a plan means losing features. Start the Team and Business lists with "Everything in Solo, plus:" (or "Everything in Team, plus:"), then list only what's new. That also lets the lists say more: three lines per plan is thin for a $79 decision.

5. **The copy could sit on anyone's page (convention: copy is part of the design).** "Pricing" is a label, not a headline. "Simple plans for teams of every size" is a line any SaaS company could use, so the viewer learns nothing about why this product is worth $29. Use the headline to tie price to the viewer's own world: what a booking costs them today, or what one missed meeting costs them. "Change or cancel whenever you like" is the strongest reassurance on the page, and right now it sits in grey subtitle text. Keep it, and repeat it near the buttons, where the hesitation actually happens.

**Smaller things.**
- Subtitle: the centred two-line text leaves "you like." alone on the second line. Widen the measure slightly or rewrite it so it fits on one line (Bringhurst on runts).
- Lower third: about 230 px of empty white below the cards looks like the page ran out, not like space left on purpose. Use it for the reassurance line, a short FAQ ("What counts as a calendar?") or a logo strip, or tighten the page.
- Navigation: "Pricing" isn't marked as the current page, so nothing tells the visitor where they are (Nielsen 1, visibility of system status).
- "Start free" doesn't say how long the free period lasts or whether a card is needed. State both near the plan buttons.
- Type scale: I count about six sizes (heading, prices, subtitle, plan names, nav and "/ month", features). The subtitle and plan names are close in size and get by only because the weights differ. That's acceptable, but one of them could drop to body size.

**What's working.**
- The prices are the clear visual anchor in each card: big and heavy, with "/ month" set small and light next to them. That's the right weight relationship (bolder, not bigger, per Refactoring UI). Keep it when you add the buttons.
- One shared grid with even gutters between the cards, and plenty of padding inside them, makes the page calm and easy to scan. Highlighting one plan will stand out precisely because everything else is this consistent.
- The colour is restrained: one blue, used only for the brand name and the action. Keep it that way, and let the blue mark the primary button on the recommended plan.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the page continues below the fold, for example with a comparison table or an FAQ. If it does, part of point 4 and the empty-space note may already be handled, but the cards still need to make sense on their own.
