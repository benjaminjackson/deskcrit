**First read.** The eye goes to "How can we help?" on the navy band, then down to the white search bar, then to the grid of eight cards. Within a second it reads as a standard help center for a SaaS product called Harbor. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and it also feels like a template: nothing on it is specific to Harbor.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing page of a product's help center. Its users arrive with a problem and want to search for the answer, or find the right topic, as fast as possible.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the layout is disciplined and nothing gets in the way. The two things holding it back are both small: the search bar doesn't look like a search control, and the card descriptions describe categories when they could name the problems people arrive with.

**What would move it most**

1. **The search bar has no signals that it is a search box.** The white field in the hero has no magnifier icon, no submit button, and no label apart from the placeholder "Search for articles". On the navy it reads first as a white bar. People recognise it as search only by its place under the headline and by reading the grey placeholder. Search is the main action on this page, and its usual cues are missing (Nielsen, Jakob's law: search goes with a magnifier icon; Norman, signifiers). **Convention.** Fix: put a magnifier icon inside the left edge of the field. Either add a "Search" button or make the field itself plainly the action. Make the placeholder concrete, so people know they can type their problem: "Search for answers, e.g. reset my password". The page also calls the content "guides" in the nav and the subtitle but "articles" in the field. Pick one word.

2. **Half the card descriptions are lists of nouns where they could name tasks.** In the grid, four cards start with a verb that matches what someone came to do: "Set up…", "Track a delivery…", "Connect…", "Fix sync problems…". The others are noun lists ("Passwords, two-step sign-in, and recovery." "Plans, payment methods, and receipts.") or speak from the company's side ("How we protect and store your data."). Someone who is locked out thinks "I can't sign in", not "recovery". The verb-led lines let them match their problem to a card at a glance. The noun lists make them translate first. **Taste, argued:** this is copy, but on a page whose whole job is routing people, the copy does the routing. Fix: write all eight as tasks in the user's words, e.g. "Reset your password, set up two-step sign-in, or get back into your account." Write "Your data" lines in terms of what the reader can do or find out.

**Smaller things**
- **Short last lines.** In six of the eight cards, the description ends with one word alone on the second line ("your team.", "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). Balance the line breaks or trim the copy so each card breaks cleanly.
- **"Browse by topic" is too close to the card titles.** It looks about 18 px bold, next to card titles of about 16 px bold, so it doesn't read as a separate level. Make it clearly larger, or set it as a smaller muted label.
- **"Orders and shipping" sits oddly next to the rest.** Next to workspaces, teams and integrations, it makes a stranger unsure what Harbor sells. If Harbor really does ship hardware, a one-line product descriptor near the logo would settle it. If not, this card needs checking.
- **There's a lot of empty space above each card title.** The icon sits about 40–50 px above the title, which makes the cards taller than they need to be. If the space were tightened, both rows would fit with more room to spare.
- **Nothing makes it specific to Harbor.** Apart from the name, it could be anyone's help center. That's not a problem for a help page, but a direct "Contact us" or "Status" link near the search would serve people who don't find an answer better than the nav does.

**What's working**
- **The navy band does its job.** It isolates the question and the search, so the page has one obvious focal point and the eye moves clearly from heading to search to topics. Keep the band and keep it that simple.
- **The serif headline against the sans text** gives the page a touch of warmth without any decoration. The two faces contrast clearly instead of clashing.
- **The grid is disciplined.** It has four equal columns, even gutters, one icon style with a consistent stroke weight, and the left edges line up from the logo down to the cards. That consistency is a big part of why the page feels trustworthy.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether each whole card is clickable, and what hover and focus states the cards and the search field have.
- What sits below the fold, such as popular articles or a fallback to contact support. That matters a lot for this kind of page.
- How the four-column grid reflows on a phone.
