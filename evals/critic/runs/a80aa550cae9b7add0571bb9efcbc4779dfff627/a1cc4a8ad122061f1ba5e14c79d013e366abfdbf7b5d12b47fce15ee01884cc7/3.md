**First read.** The eye goes straight to "How can we help?" in the white serif on the navy band, drops to the white search field, then moves down into the grid of eight cards, reading it left to right. Within two seconds it reads as a software company's help centre for customers of a team or workspace product. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also familiar: this is the standard help-centre layout, done cleanly.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the help-centre landing page for a SaaS product. Its job is to get a customer with a problem to the right article fast, either by searching or by picking a topic.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The order of importance is clear, the layout is disciplined, and nothing on the page gets in the way. What holds it back is that the search, the main route for someone with a specific problem, is a plain white bar with no search icon and no button. And the topics only let people browse by category, so anyone with a specific error has to guess which card holds the answer.

**What would move it most**

1. **The search field doesn't look like a search field.** The white bar under the headline (about 360–920 px across, 216–264 px down) has no magnifier icon, no button, and only the placeholder "Search for articles". The headline and its white-on-navy contrast make it findable. But nothing tells the viewer how to run a search (Enter? Is there a button?), and once they start typing, the placeholder disappears and the field has no visible label left. People recognise search by its icon (Jakob's law, NN/g). Field labels shouldn't live only in the placeholder (WCAG 3.3.2, "Labels or instructions"). **Convention, with a small real cost.** Fix: add a magnifier at the left of the field and either a "Search" button joined to its right end or an obvious Enter hint. Making the field taller (to around 56 px) would also make it the clear second thing the eye lands on, rather than just a strip.

2. **The cards only let you browse by category; there's no route for someone who arrives with a specific problem.** Most people come to a help centre because something just went wrong, but all eight cards are equal-weight categories. Some overlap, so the viewer has to guess. "Fix sync problems" sits under Troubleshooting, but someone whose calendar sync broke could just as easily open Integrations. Sign-in errors could be under "Account and sign-in" or "Troubleshooting". Every wrong guess costs a click and a back. **Defect (mild): it slows the viewer down.** Fix: add a short "Popular articles" or "Common problems" list of 4–6 specific titles in your users' words (e.g. "Google Calendar stopped syncing", "I didn't get my two-step code"), either under the search or above the grid. Then settle where sync and sign-in problems live, so each topic has a clear boundary.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Browse by topic" heading (~18 px bold) is barely bigger than the card titles (~16 px bold), so it doesn't read as a separate level. Make it clearly larger, or set it as a quieter label (smaller, muted, letterspaced) so it reads as a caption over the grid rather than another title.
- Most card descriptions break with one word alone on the last line ("your team.", "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). Rebalance the line breaks or tighten the copy so each description fits on one line or breaks evenly.
- Card borders look very pale against the #F5F7FA-ish background. The cards still separate thanks to the white fill, but nothing signals that a card is clickable. A subtle chevron or arrow on each card would help, or a slightly stronger edge.
- Cards with a one-line description ("Orders and shipping") leave an empty strip at the bottom, compared with their neighbours. That's fine, but tightening every description to one line would make the row look deliberate.
- "Browse a topic or search the guides." is filler that repeats what the page already shows. Use that line to say what's actually there (e.g. how many guides, or "Answers for admins and team members"), or cut it.
- "Privacy and security: How we protect and store your data" describes the company rather than what the user can do there. Something like "Control who sees your data, export or delete it" would tell the viewer what they can do on that page.
- The mix of "Orders and shipping" (returns, deliveries) with workspace and integrations topics is surprising for a SaaS product. If physical orders are real, fine. If it's leftover template copy, remove it, because it makes the viewer wonder which product this is.

**What's working.**
- The headline and search sit alone on the navy band, so the page has one clear focal point and an obvious first action. Keep that separation.
- One grid controls the whole page: the logo, "Browse by topic" and the left column of cards share a left edge at about 64 px, the right column of cards lines up with "Contact us", and the card gutters are equal. That's why the page feels calm and trustworthy.
- The serif headline against the sans-serif everywhere else is a clear, deliberate contrast that gives the page a bit of character without noise. The icon set shares one stroke weight and level of detail.
