**First read.** My eye goes to the bold headline "Landing stage rebuild" first. Then it drops to the illustration, where the bright blue rounded bar on the water pulls almost as hard as the blue "Sign up" button on the right. This reads as a news or info page for a small local ferry service. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, a little like a public-sector page. It also feels unfinished, because the picture looks like a wireframe placeholder.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a page for ferry passengers about the rebuilt landing, with a side form to sign up for timetable-change alerts. That sign-up is probably the action the page wants.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and easy to read, but the page's only action is broken. The form can't deliver what it promises because it never asks where to send the note. The article also hides its one piece of real news for passengers in the last line.

**What would move it most**

1. **The sign-up form has no email or phone field (defect).** The card on the right asks for "Your name" and "Home landing", then promises "We send one short note when the timetable changes." There is nowhere to send that note. A passenger who signs up gets nothing, or the form fails in a way they can't see. A careful reader who spots the gap won't trust it. This is a gap in the system's model, not in the styling (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, conceptual model). The fix:
   - Add the contact field, labelled for what it does, such as "Email for timetable changes".
   - Say what channel the note comes by: email or text.
   - Consider whether "Your name" is needed at all. Every field you cut makes signing up more likely.

2. **"Home landing" is a free-text box for a value that has a fixed set of answers (defect).** People will type "North", "north landing", "N. stage" or misspell it. Then the alert that depends on this value either goes to the wrong people or matches nobody. Error prevention means limiting the input to valid choices (Nielsen, heuristic 5; Norman, constraints). The fix is a select menu or radio buttons listing the actual landings.

3. **The headline names the project, and the news for passengers is buried at the bottom (defect in the copy).** "Landing stage rebuild" is a label with no verb, so it tells passengers nothing about what changed for them. The line that matters most is the last one on the page, below the figure: "The ramps now meet the deck at every tide, so prams and wheelchairs roll straight on." That is a big change for parents and wheelchair users, and many readers will stop before reaching it. The fix:
   - Lead with that change in the headline or the first sentence, said to the reader. For example: "Step-free boarding at every tide."
   - Move the benches and the kiosk after it, as the nice extras.

4. **The boat in the illustration uses the button blue and the button shape (convention).** The rounded blue bar on the water at the right of the figure is the same blue as "Sign up", with similar corners. It is the most saturated thing on the left side of the page. So it competes with the real action and looks clickable when it isn't (Norman: things that aren't interactive shouldn't look interactive). The fix: keep the accent blue for the button and links only, and draw the boat in a colour from the illustration's own muted palette.

**Smaller things.**
- **Illustration:** it is flat grey and blue blocks with no ramp, deck or tide in it. That undercuts the caption, "seen from the water at low tide", and the ramp claim the article makes. Use a real photo, or a drawing that shows the ramp meeting the deck.
- **"Sign up" button:** the label is generic. Name the outcome, for example "Send me timetable changes".
- **Card heading vs. headline:** the card's heading sits on a different line from the page headline (about 101 px vs. about 77 px). The card's top edge also nearly lines up with the headline but misses. Align the tops so the two columns clearly share a grid.
- **Empty space and footer:** the large empty band below the article leaves the footer note floating on its own. Tighten the page height, or anchor the footer to a clear bottom margin.
- **Small text:** the caption, the form labels and the footnote look about 12–13 px. Check that they are at least 13–14 px and still pass 4.5:1 contrast.

**What's working.**
- **Type scale:** it is disciplined. There are four clear sizes (headline, card heading, body, small), and each has an obvious job, with nothing a step or two apart.
- **Form field details:** the labels sit above the fields and stay visible, and the fields have borders dark enough to see. Keep that when you add the contact field.
- **Body text:** the line length looks to be about 65–75 characters, with comfortable line spacing. It reads easily.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see focus and error states on the form, what happens after someone presses "Sign up" (is there a confirmation?), or how the two columns stack on a phone. Many passengers will be reading this at the landing.
