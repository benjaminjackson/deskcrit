**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif "Harbour Lights festival", then drops into three equal grey blocks of running text and has nowhere else to go. Nothing in the blocks stands out, so I have to read all ~180 words to find out when it is, where it is, and what's on. It feels calm, literary and parish-newsletter. That fits a village festival, but it reads like an article, not an announcement. The bottom 40% of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a web page or flyer section telling local families what's on at the festival, when and where, and asking for volunteers.

**Verdict.** This is well-set text that does a poor job as an event notice. Every fact someone would act on, like the dates, times, places and how to volunteer, is buried mid-sentence in equal-weight prose. You have to read every line just to answer "when do I turn up?"

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts people act on are hidden in paragraphs (defect).** "First weekend of September", "old market square", "ten each morning", "Saturday night… at dusk", "Sunday afternoon", "by one o'clock" and "at four" all sit inside running sentences in all three columns, at the same size and weight as "hungry seagulls". People skim a notice like this. They don't read it (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). So the people who most need a time, such as pie entrants with a one o'clock deadline, have to dig for it. Pull the when and where out as a line just under the headline. Give each event its own short fact line (day · time · place) above its paragraph.

2. **The three columns have no headings, so the structure is invisible (defect).** Each column is really its own topic: the overview, the lantern walk and the pie contest. But nothing says so, and they look like one article flowing across three columns. To find the pie contest you have to start reading column 3. Give each column a short heading ("Lantern walk", "Pie contest"), in bold or a step above body size. That adds a third level to what is now a two-level page, and the reader can jump straight to what they care about.

3. **The headline names the event but gives no reason or date (defect).** "Harbour Lights festival" is the only thing seen first. A passer-by learns the name and nothing else: no date, no year, no place. The date and place belong right next to the headline, because that's what people remember and plan around. "First weekend of September" with no year or dates also makes people work it out. Write the actual dates.

4. **The volunteer ask has no next step (defect).** "Volunteers are still needed…" is the only call to action. It's in the middle of column 1 and gives no contact, link or place to sign up. As written, nobody can act on it. Give it its own line with one concrete step (who to email, or where to sign up). The children's boat race has the same problem: it's mentioned once and never given a day or time.

**Smaller things.**
- Gutters are lopsided. The text sits about 11 px right of each rule but about 20–28 px left of it, so the column rhythm looks off (Müller-Brockmann, equal intervals). Centre the rules in the gutters, or drop them and use space alone.
- The rules stop at different heights, following each column's own length. Either run them the same depth or remove them. They add little that a wider gutter wouldn't.
- The empty bottom 40% reads as leftover, not as chosen space. Bring the composition down or crop the frame once fact lines and headings are added.
- Straight apostrophes in "children's" and "o'clock". Use curly ones (Butterick).

**What's working.**
- The type is sound. The serif is made for reading, the measure is about 45–50 characters with comfortable leading, the baselines line up across the columns, the rag is gentle and contrast is strong. Keep all of that when you add structure.
- The scale is clean: two clearly different sizes and a bold headline. Add headings by weight or one clear step, not by bringing in near-duplicate sizes.
- The copy has real warmth and specific detail ("the pie that makes the judges laugh", "guard the cake tent against hungry seagulls"). It's local and particular, not templated. Keep that voice in the paragraphs, and move the hard facts out of them.
