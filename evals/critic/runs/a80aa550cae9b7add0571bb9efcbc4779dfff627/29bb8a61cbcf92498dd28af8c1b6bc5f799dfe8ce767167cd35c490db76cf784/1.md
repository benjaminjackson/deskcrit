**First read.** The eye lands on "Travel lighter with the Wayfarer pack" right away, drops to the grey line under it and then to the black "Shop the pack" button. That path is clean and takes about two seconds. Then it runs out: the right 40% of the frame and the bottom third are blank white, so the eye goes looking for the bag and finds nothing. It reads as a product launch hero for a travel backpack. The tone is calm and capable, but empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top section of a product or launch page. Its job is to get someone who travels with carry-on luggage to want the Wayfarer pack and click through to buy it.

**Verdict.** The words and the type do their job well. As a page that sells a physical bag, though, it fails, because you never see the bag. That gap is what's holding it back. If a product image is supposed to sit on the right and just didn't load, most of this verdict goes away (see the last section).

**What would move it most**

1. **There's no product to look at.** The copy makes three physical promises: it fits under an airline seat, opens flat like a suitcase and weighs less than a kilo. The viewer is asked to believe all three without seeing the bag. "Shop the pack" then asks them to commit to something they've only read about. The empty area on the right (roughly x 860–1280) is exactly where the eye goes after the headline, and it finds nothing. That costs desire and trust, and it makes the page look unfinished. *Defect.* Fix: put the bag in that right column, ideally opened flat. That one picture proves the claim that sets this bag apart, and it's stronger than a plain studio shot.

2. **The ask gives no price, and the comparison is in the copy but not on screen.** "Shop the pack" is clear about the next step, but the viewer has to click before they can judge whether the bag is worth it. The best comparison is already in the subhead: "opens flat like a suitcase", "fits under an airline seat". That puts the bag next to things the viewer already knows and trusts, which is the right instinct. Neither the button nor any image picks up that comparison, though. *Defect* (missing price at the point of commitment) plus *taste* (using the comparison). Fix: show the price near the button, or in it ("Shop the pack — £X"), and let the image show the bag under a seat or opened like a suitcase.

3. **"Fits under an airline seat" can be read two ways.** One reading is every airline. The other is some airlines' under-seat sizes. Underseat limits differ by airline, and this is a guarantee the buyer will hold you to at the gate. If they read it as "all airlines" and get stopped, you lose the customer and probably get a return. *Defect* (it's a guarantee the viewer will act on). Fix: name the standard, for example "fits the under-seat limit on most European budget airlines (40×30×20 cm)", or link "Compare sizes" to that.

**Smaller things.**
- Composition: all the weight sits in the upper-left quarter and nothing balances it, so the frame tips left. Item 1 fixes this too.
- The whole page carries no brand mark or navigation. If this is the full design and not a crop, the viewer can't tell who's selling the bag. Check that.
- "Compare sizes": the only size mentioned is 22 litres, so it's unclear whether other sizes exist. "Compare 22 L and 30 L", or whatever the sizes are, would tell the viewer what they'll find.
- "Travel lighter" is a line any bag brand could use. The specific promise is in the subhead. Consider moving some of it into the headline, for example "The carry-on that opens like a suitcase".

**What's working.**
- **Type scale.** I count four sizes, each with its own job: eyebrow (~14 px bold blue), headline (~75 px heavy), subhead (~19 px regular grey) and button/link (~15 px bold). There are big steps between them, and the lower levels differ by weight and colour rather than near-identical sizes, which is what Refactoring UI recommends. The headline is tightly leaded so its two lines read as one unit, and the break keeps "Wayfarer pack" together. Keep all of this.
- **Primary and secondary actions.** The filled dark button beside an underlined text link gives one clear main action and one visible but quieter alternative. Both look clickable, and the link doesn't rely on colour alone.
- **The subhead.** It's concrete, measurable and written from the traveller's side (seat, suitcase, kilo), and its measure of about 65 characters is comfortable. This is the strongest copy on the page. Build the image and the ask around it.
