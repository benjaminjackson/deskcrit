**First read.** The eye goes to the tall blue Organic search bar first, then slides down the staircase of bars, each a different colour. The page reads as a clean internal growth report, a breakdown of Q3 sign-ups by channel. Next you notice the bright orange and red bars, and they compete for attention without saying anything. The note on the right, which carries the actual story, only gets read after the chart.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in an internal growth dashboard that shows the team where Q3 sign-ups came from and backs a budget decision (moving the events budget to referrals).

**Verdict.** This is a solid, honest chart, and the numbers check out: the bars add up to 5,400, 1,840 is a third of that, and podcast ads plus events come to 670. Its biggest problem is that the chart shows a distribution while the page is making an argument. The two claims on the right (organic search grew since Q2, and podcast ads and events are weak) are never shown in the chart. Six unrelated colours spread the attention evenly instead of pointing at either claim.

**What would move it most**

1. **Six colours that mean nothing (convention, and it costs something here).** Each bar has its own hue, but the axis labels already name the channels, so the colour adds no information. It does add emphasis in the wrong places. The orange Referral and red Paid social bars are the loudest marks on the page. Red usually signals a problem, so Paid social looks like trouble when the text never mentions it. Organic search, the actual story, gets a cooler blue. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 19.1) both flag a single series drawn in many colours. Fix: draw all bars in one neutral colour and keep one accent for what the reader must notice. Here that is Organic search, plus perhaps a second treatment for Podcast ads and Events, since the budget decision is about them.

2. **The chart doesn't show the change the page talks about (defect).** The side note's main claim is "up from a quarter in Q2", but the chart has only Q3. The reader has to take the growth on trust, and can't see whether Referral, the channel getting more money, grew or shrank. A page headed "What changed this quarter" needs the earlier quarter on it (Few, pitfall 2: give every figure a comparison). Fix: add Q2 next to each Q3 bar as a pale paired bar or a tick mark, or at least put the Q2 figure next to Organic search and Referral.

3. **The title names the topic, not the finding (taste, my judgment; no approved source).** "New sign-ups by channel" could sit on any dashboard. The finding is buried in a small 15px-ish paragraph in the right column, which the eye reaches last. Fix: put the takeaway in the title, e.g. "Organic search brought a third of Q3 sign-ups, up from a quarter in Q2", and keep the current title as the subtitle. Then the chart proves the headline instead of sitting beside it.

**Smaller things.**
- Gridlines and the y-axis (0–2,000) repeat information the value labels on each bar already give. Drop the axis and gridlines, or the labels, but not both (Tufte, data-ink).
- The note heading "What changed this quarter" is only bold, at about body size, and sits about 20px below the chart's top edge rather than on a shared line. Align its top with the chart's 2,000 gridline or with the subtitle.
- "The events budget moves to referrals in Q4" is the only decision on the page and reads as an aside. Give it its own line, with the amount if it's known, because readers will hold the team to it.
- The second paragraph stops at the fact ("fewer than 700 accounts") without a reference point. Add the share ("12% of sign-ups") or a cost per account so the reader can see why that justifies moving budget.

**What's working.**
- Sorted bars, a zero baseline, direct value labels and a clear source line with dates and total. Keep all of these, because they make the chart easy to trust.
- The subtitle defines the metric exactly ("by the channel of the first visit"), which settles how sign-ups are attributed before anyone asks.
- The restrained page (one typeface, a quiet nav, plenty of space) leaves the data in charge once the colour is calmed down.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bars have hover tooltips or a data-table alternative for screen readers. The side note covers the key points in text, which helps either way.
