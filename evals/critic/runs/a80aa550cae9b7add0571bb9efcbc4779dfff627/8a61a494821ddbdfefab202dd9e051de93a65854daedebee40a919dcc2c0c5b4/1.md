**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big serif quote, which takes up the top two-thirds of the frame. Then it drops to the bold sans line, and only last reaches the small grey paragraph. It reads as the top of a local library newsletter story, and it feels warm, calm and a little literary. Nothing competes with the quote, which is good. But the straight typewriter quote marks and the hyphen are the first thing a designer's eye snags on.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the lead story of a library newsletter (email or web). It should hook regular patrons with a human voice and tell them the reading room reopens on Monday and what has changed.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy and tone are sound, and the piece mostly works. Two things hold it back. The headline is set with typewriter punctuation at a size where every mark shows. And the copy never tells a stranger why the quote matters, so the actual news ends up in the faintest text on the page.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline's punctuation is wrong, and at this size it's the most visible thing in the piece.** The opening `"`, the nested `'old'`, the apostrophe in `It's`, and the closing `,"` are all straight marks. `room - nobody` uses a hyphen with spaces where it needs a dash. At roughly 90 px these look like a typo in the one line everyone reads. The dash is a convention (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 5.2.1): use a spaced en dash or a closed em dash. Curly quotes have no checked source behind them, so take that part as common sense: at display size, straight marks look like unedited copy pasted in. While you're there, hang the opening quote mark in the left margin. Right now it pushes "It's" in from the edge that "room" and "anything" hold, so the quote's left edge looks ragged. (The source for hanging is only partly confirmed, but the effect gets bigger with type size, and here it's large.) Defect for the dash, taste for the rest.

2. **The quote has no point for someone who doesn't already know the story.** "It's the 'old' reading room – nobody calls it anything else" implies the room has a new name, or that "old" is a joke. But nothing on the page says which. The paragraph below talks about lamps, shelves and puzzles and never mentions a name. Readers have to work out why "old" is in quotes, and most won't bother. Meanwhile the facts they'll act on and remember ("reopens on Monday") are in the smallest, palest text. The direction: make the first line of the paragraph say what the volunteer is reacting to, such as a new official name or the refit itself, and end on what it means for the reader ("Come and see it from Monday"). Also give Monday a date, because patrons will hold the library to it. Defect.

3. **The attribution is styled louder than the news.** "said a volunteer who has stamped returned books for thirty years" is bold, near-black and slightly larger than the grey paragraph. So it reads as a second headline, and the paragraph reads as fine print. An attribution is supporting text. Make it lighter (regular weight, or the grey) and let the news paragraph take that second slot (Refactoring UI: emphasise with weight and colour, not another size). Naming the volunteer would also earn more warmth than "a volunteer", and in a local newsletter people will want to know who it is (taste). Taste, with a small hierarchy cost.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line break: "reading / room" splits the name of the thing the story is about. Re-break so "reading room" stays on one line.
- The sans text uses two sizes that look about 2 px apart (attribution ≈20 px, paragraph ≈18 px). They read as an accident rather than two levels. Pick one size and separate the two lines by weight or colour.
- The ending `,"` leans on the attribution to finish the sentence, but the size change breaks that link visually. Either end the quote on a full stop, or bring the attribution closer and make it plainly subordinate.

**What's working.**
- There is one clear entry point. The large serif quote with plenty of space around it gives the eye an obvious place to land, and a human voice is the right hook for a library audience.
- The small, letterspaced rust-coloured "LIBRARY NEWSLETTER" kicker identifies the piece without competing, and it ties in with the warm cream background.
- The body paragraph is set at a comfortable width (roughly 55 characters a line), and it's left-aligned on the same edge as everything else, so the column feels orderly.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the grey paragraph meets 4.5:1 against the cream. It looks close but probably passes; measure it.
