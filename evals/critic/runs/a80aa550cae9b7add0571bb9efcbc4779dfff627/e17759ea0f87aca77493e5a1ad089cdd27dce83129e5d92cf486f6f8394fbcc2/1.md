**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline, "Shipments peak each third quarter", then drops to the tallest bar, Q3 2026 at 705. Next it notices that the right half of the chart is higher than the left. It reads as a calm, competent board slide from a company that has its house style in order. Nothing jars.

**What it's for.** My assumption: page 3 of a 12-page board pack. It shows directors the quarterly shipment trend and the Q4 outlook, to inform discussion and decisions about the year ahead.

**Verdict.** The craft is clean, but the slide undersells its own data and blurs fact with forecast. The headline points to the seasonal pattern and misses the bigger story: every quarter of 2026 beat the same quarter of 2025. The Q4 2026 forecast is also drawn exactly like the actual results, so a director scanning the chart takes 560 as fact.

**What would move it most**

1. **The forecast bar looks like a real result.** The Q4 2026 bar, far right, has the same solid blue and the same bold label as the seven actual quarters. The only clue is a parenthetical in the subtitle and a clause in the source line. A board member glancing at the chart sees 560 as booked shipments and may quote it, plan around it, or hold management to it. This is a number people act on, so I count it as a **defect**. Projections should look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Fix: give the forecast bar a distinct treatment, such as an outline, a lighter tint or hatching, and put the word "Forecast" on the bar itself or under its axis label. If you have a range from the September forecast, show it as an error bar. Then you can drop the parenthetical from the subtitle.

2. **The headline states the weaker finding.** "Peak each third quarter" rests on two cycles. The stronger and more useful finding sits right next to it: Q1 rose from 410 to 455, Q2 from 520 to 590, Q3 from 610 to 705, and the Q4 forecast is 560 against 480. That is growth of about 11–17% every quarter, and about 14% for the year. A director wants to know whether the business is growing, and right now they have to work that out bar by bar. This is **taste, argued on common sense**: the title should state the finding the board most needs. Fix: lead with the growth, for example "Every quarter of 2026 shipped more than the same quarter of 2025, and Q3 is still the peak". Or keep the seasonality headline and add a line that states the year-on-year rise in numbers.

3. **The chart doesn't show the comparison the data supports.** The eight bars are evenly spaced, with no break between Q4 2025 and Q1 2026. The two years don't read as two cycles, so neither the seasonality claim nor the growth story is visible at a glance. You have to read the axis labels to pair Q3 with Q3. This is **taste**. Fix: add a clear gap or a "2025 | 2026" grouping label between the years. If growth becomes the headline, consider pairing the same quarters side by side, with 2025 in a muted tone and 2026 in the brand blue. Position on a common scale then does the comparison for the viewer.

**Smaller things.**
- The y-axis and gridlines repeat what the direct labels already show. Drop the axis numbers, or the gridlines, to cut ink (Tufte).
- The subtitle says "Q4 2026" twice in one sentence. Once the forecast is marked on the chart, cut the parenthetical.
- About 120 px of empty space sits under the footer, so the content block floats high. Check this against the other pages in the pack. If it's the deck's template, ignore it.

**What's working.**
- The structure is right for a board pack. The brand and the meeting are named at the top, and the units are stated directly under the chart title ("Thousands of units"). The source and page number are in the footer. Nobody has to guess what a number measures. Keep all of it.
- The chart is restrained: one colour, direct value labels, faint gridlines, an axis that starts at zero, and no legend. Keep this discipline when you add the forecast treatment, and make it the only visual exception.
- The type scale is tight. I count about four sizes: serif headline, subtitle, bold chart label, and small grey detail. Each has an obvious job, and the serif/sans pairing contrasts clearly rather than clashing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the grey small text (subtitle, axis labels, source line) clears 4.5:1 on white. It looks borderline, especially on a projector, so check the actual grey values.
