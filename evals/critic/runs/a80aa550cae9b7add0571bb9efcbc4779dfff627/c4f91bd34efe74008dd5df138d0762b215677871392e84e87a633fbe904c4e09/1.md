**First read.** Your eye goes straight to the big serif quote, and it is a good line: warm, a bit wry, in a voice you can hear. Next it drops to the bold sans attribution, which reads like a second headline, and only then to the small grey paragraph. That paragraph holds the actual news: the room reopens Monday. The whole thing feels calm, literate and local, like a well-made community bulletin.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the lead item of a library's email or web newsletter. Its job is to tell patrons the reading room is reopening and make them want to come.

**Verdict.** It's well made and has real character, but the hierarchy is the wrong way round for a newsletter. The headline is a quote that only makes sense once you know about the renovation, and the renovation itself is in the quietest text on the page with no actual date. A skimmer gets the charm and misses the news.

**What would move it most**

1. **The news is in the smallest, palest text.** "Reopens on Monday with new lamps, new shelves, and a table set aside for jigsaw puzzles" sits in grey type that looks about 18 px, below a bold attribution that outweighs it. A reader who skims the headline and moves on never finds out anything is happening. Worse, the quote leans on that missing context: the quote marks around 'old' only make sense once you know the room has been redone. Read cold, "It's the 'old' reading room" leaves you guessing old compared with what. **Defect.** It hides the main message and makes the headline harder to read. Keep the quote as the hook, but put the fact in front of the reader before or right beside it. One way is a short kicker that carries the news, replacing the generic "LIBRARY NEWSLETTER", such as "The reading room reopens Monday". Another is to lift the reopening sentence into the attribution's weight and colour so it becomes the second thing the eye reads.

2. **"Monday" with no date.** This is the one detail a reader acts on, and in a newsletter that sits in an inbox or gets read days late, "Monday" can mean two different weeks. Read it on a Tuesday and you might show up a week late, or think you've missed it. There's also no time or opening hours. **Defect** (it's a date the reader will hold you to). Fix: "Monday, [date]", plus hours if they've changed.

**Smaller things**
- **The opening quote mark doesn't hang.** The “ sits on the left text edge, so "It's" starts indented compared with "room" and "anything" below it. At about 96 px this is easy to see. Hang the mark in the margin so the letters line up. (This is common sense at display size; the checklist has no confirmed source for it.)
- **The attribution is heavier than its job.** "said a volunteer who has stamped returned books for thirty years" is bold, near-black and slightly larger than the body text. It competes with the news line and nearly collides with it in size, with only about 2 px between them. Pick one approach: drop it to regular weight at body size, or, if you follow finding 1, give that weight to the news line instead.
- **"LIBRARY NEWSLETTER" could sit on anyone's page.** It's the only spot of colour and the first thing in the reading order, and it tells the reader nothing they didn't already know. Use it for the news or the issue date.
- **The empty bottom fifth of the frame looks like leftover space, not a choice.** If this is the top of a longer email, that's fine. If it stands alone, move the block down or give it something to anchor to, such as a date or a "Visit" line.

**What's working**
- **Type scale and pairing.** You have a clear display serif against a small sans: three working sizes plus the kicker, with big jumps between them. The quote's line spacing is tight enough that it holds together as one unit.
- **Small details are right.** True curly quotes, single quotes correctly nested inside double, a real em dash, and a good break at "reading / room—nobody calls it / anything else,". The body text's line length (about 60 characters) and its grey on cream both look comfortable to read.
- **Leading with a human voice is the right instinct** for a library newsletter. It's specific, local, and the volunteer's "thirty years" earns trust. Keep it; just don't make it carry the news on its own.
