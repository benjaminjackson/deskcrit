**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading, then drops into a column of text that looks more like a list of separate lines than three paragraphs. The line spacing is so wide that each line floats alone, and the gaps between paragraphs are barely bigger than the gaps between lines. It reads as a plain, calm information page. It feels sparse rather than considered.

**What it's for.** You gave me no context, so I'm assuming this is a web or print notice telling ferry passengers how the service works, read once and then checked again for times.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. Very loose line spacing breaks up the paragraphs. Under that sits a bigger problem: the facts a passenger acts on, like times and the last boat, are buried in prose and left vague.

**What would move it most**
1. **The line spacing pulls the text apart (defect).** In all three paragraphs, the space between lines looks about 2.4 times the type size. The ~45 px line pitch is close to the ~64 px pitch across a paragraph break, so the extra space that should mark a new paragraph almost disappears. The page reads as about eight loose lines, not three grouped thoughts, and the eye has to work to jump each gap. Bringhurst and Hochuli ask for leading that suits the typeface and the measure. These lines run about 60–70 characters, which needs only ordinary leading. Saying this much is too much is my judgment, not a sourced figure. Fix: bring line spacing down to about 1.4–1.6 times the type size. Keep a clear paragraph gap of about one blank line, so the space between paragraphs is plainly bigger than the space between lines. Measure the CSS line-height to confirm.
2. **The words a passenger acts on are vague (defect).** "Between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes", "in winter" and "before dusk" each make the reader rebuild a time on their own. Dusk is around 4 pm in December and much later in March. A passenger who reads "before dusk" as 5 pm can miss the last boat. Fix: give first and last departures as clock times from each landing, give the months when the winter timetable runs, and give the last winter departure as a time.
3. **The facts are hidden in paragraphs (defect).** Someone comes back to this page to check one thing: when the next boat leaves, or whether they can bring a bike. Right now they have to reread the prose to find it (NN/g on how people scan; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Fix: pull the schedule out into a short table or list (frequency, crossing time, first and last boat, winter changes) and keep a sentence or two of prose for the bicycle rule.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what's on the page. Something like "River ferry: times, crossing and bicycles" tells the reader what they'll find.
- The bicycle paragraph doesn't say which rail or how bikes get off at the other side. Add one clause on getting off if it matters.
- The heading-to-body gap is about the same as a paragraph gap, so the heading doesn't sit clearly above the text. Give it a bit more space above than below.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is tight: one bold heading size and one body size, with a clear step between them. Keep it that way, and don't add sizes when you split out the timetable.
- The measure is right. Lines run about 60–70 characters, in a readable sans, set flush left on a narrow column. Don't widen it to fill the empty right side.
- The plain tone and dark text on white are easy to read and fit a public notice.
