**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline at top left, then drops into four even serif paragraphs that all look the same. It reads like a calm, well-set note or an essay page. Nothing in the body stands out, so a passenger looking for "when is the next boat?" has to read every line to find out.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page of practical information for people about to take the river ferry, and that most of them will skim it for times and rules.

**Verdict.** The type is careful and pleasant to read, but the page fails at its job. The facts a passenger acts on (when boats run, when the last one leaves, what to do with a bike) are vague and buried in prose with no structure at all. Readers who skim, which is most passengers, won't find what they need.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times are not times.** "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" (paragraph 1) and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" (paragraph 3) are the lines a passenger will plan around and hold the operator to. Neither gives a clock time. "Before dusk" moves by hours across the winter, so someone who reads it as 5 pm can miss a 3:40 boat. "The quay" can also be read two ways: the market quay, or whichever landing the reader is standing at. If they guess wrong, they're stranded on the far bank. *Defect.* Fix: give first and last departures from each landing as clock times, in both summer and winter. If there's a printed timetable, say where to find it online too, not only "in the shelter", which helps nobody who is still planning at home.

2. **Nothing can be skimmed.** Four paragraphs with the same weight, size and spacing hold four different kinds of information: timetable, bicycles, winter timetable, and the rebuilt landings. The page has two levels, headline and body, and the body gives no way in. *Convention* (text people skim should be broken up by headings, short paragraphs and lists: Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; Nielsen Norman Group), and here it costs real time. Fix: add short subheads such as "Timetable", "Bicycles" and "At the landings", and put the times in a small table or list so the numbers can be scanned.

3. **Related facts are split, and the order is wrong.** The summer timetable is in paragraph 1, but the winter version that changes it sits in paragraph 3, after the bicycle rules. A reader who stops after paragraph 1 believes boats run every 40 minutes all year. *Defect.* Fix: put both timetables in one section. Lead with what everyone needs (times and the last boat), then bicycles, then the landing facilities. The landing paragraph is pleasant background but carries nothing to act on, so it belongs last and lightest, or can go.

4. **The headline names a category, not a use.** "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any ferry's page, and it doesn't tell the reader the route. *Taste,* but it matters on a page people reach from a search: they want to confirm quickly that this is their ferry. Fix: name the crossing, for example "North landing ⇄ market quay ferry: times and bicycles".

**Smaller things**
- Paragraph 2 ends with "crossing." alone on its last line, a runt. Rewrap it or edit a word so the last line holds more than one word.
- "lock them to the rail": does the rider bring their own lock? Say so, since a cyclist who arrives without one has a problem.
- "one boat each hour": say whether boats leave on the hour or at another fixed minute, so passengers can time their arrival.
- The empty right 40% and bottom third look like leftover space rather than shaped space. That's fine for a reading column, but a short "At a glance" box of first and last boats could use it.

**What's working**
- The type is well paired and well set. The bold sans headline and the serif body differ clearly in more than size (Dair's contrast of structure and weight). The body measure looks about 65–75 characters (Bringhurst's range), leading looks about 1.5, paragraphs are marked by space only, and the near-black on white has ample contrast. Keep all of this when you add structure.
- The tone is plain and courteous ("The crew asks that…"). Keep the voice and add the hard facts to it.
