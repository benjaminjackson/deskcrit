**First read.** The eye goes to the bold serif title, "Winter timetable at the harbour", then reads down the left column, which has no heading. Next it lands on "Fares and cards", the only subheading. It reads as a quiet, nicely set information page for a small ferry service. Then the trust drops: I came for a timetable, there are no times, and the "Fares" section never mentions a fare.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or notice that tells winter ferry passengers when boats run and what it costs. That means people looking up one fact, not reading from start to finish.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The typesetting is careful, but the page promises a timetable and fares and gives neither. Its two labels tell the viewer to expect content that isn't there.

**What would move it most**
1. **The title promises a timetable and the page has no times.** *(Defect.)* The title says "timetable", but the only time-like facts are buried in the left column's prose: the office opens "at seven", and boats leave "every ninety minutes". Ninety minutes from when? The first sailing time and the last sailing time are both missing, so a passenger can't work out a single departure. These facts are looked up, not read, so they belong in a table or list of departure times (Few and Tufte on tables for looking up exact values; Krug on making a page scannable). Put the actual sailings near the top of the left column in a small table, and move the prose about the office and the weather notices below it.
2. **"Fares and cards" labels a section about something else.** *(Defect; WCAG 2.4.6 Headings and labels.)* Under it come crossing times, the wind, bicycles, prams, wheelchairs and dogs. There's no price, and the season card is only mentioned in the *left* column. Someone scanning for the fare finds the heading, reads all four paragraphs, and leaves without a number. Either add the fares and card prices under this heading, or rename it to what's actually there (e.g. "On board" or "Crossing and luggage") and give fares a section of their own.
3. **The two columns aren't built the same way.** *(Convention: one program for repeated elements, Gerstner; text lining up across columns, Müller-Brockmann.)* The right column has a heading and the left has none. That pushes the right column's text down about 40 px, so its lines sit between the left column's lines instead of level with them. The page looks like two pieces pasted side by side, and the left column reads as an introduction when it's really a section. Give the left column a heading at the same size (e.g. "Opening hours and sailings") so both columns start and run on the same baselines.

**Smaller things.**
- The two grey rules around y≈585 separate nothing: below them is about 200 px of empty page. Remove them, or put the content they seem to be waiting for (the timetable, the fares) under them.
- There's a large, uneven gap under the text (the right column stops about 50 px higher than the left). Let the page end with the content, or gather that space on purpose.
- Single short words sit alone on the last line in two places: "bags." and "thirty-five." Rewrap them or edit the lines slightly.
- "Crossings are shorter when the wind is from the west" stops at a fact. Finish it with what it means for the passenger, e.g. "so expect about twenty minutes on a westerly day and up to thirty-five in rough weather."
- "The captain may skip a sailing" is a commitment-level line. It reads as either cancelled or delayed, and that changes what a passenger does. Say which, and where to check before leaving home, not only "the board beside the gate".

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: three sizes (title, subhead, body) with clear steps between them, set in one serif family. Keep it.
- The body text is comfortable. Each line looks to be about 55–65 characters, the line spacing is generous, paragraphs are separated by space rather than indents, and the dark text on white is easy to read. It suits a calm, local-notice tone.
- The two-column grid is simple, with even margins and a clear gap between the columns. Once both columns have headings, it will carry the content well.
