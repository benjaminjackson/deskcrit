**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big white headline, then drops to the yellow "Get tickets" button. It reads as a landing page for a small, thoughtful tech conference, and it feels calm and confident. In version 1 the headline looks cramped on that first glance; in version 2 it reads in one easy pass.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a conference website, and its job is to tell people who build software what the event is, when and where it is, and to get them to buy a ticket.

**Verdict.** Both versions do the job, and version 2 does it better. Only one thing changed: the headline's line spacing (and so everything below it moved down about 24 px). In version 1 the two lines are so tight they almost touch. In version 2 they read as two clean lines.

**What changed, and why it matters**
1. **Headline line spacing (defect in v1, fixed in v2).** In version 1 the space between "Three days on building" and "quieter, slower software" is almost gone. The descenders of "days" and "building" nearly touch the tops of "quieter" and "slower", so the two lines merge into one dark block. The reader has to pick the lines apart, and the one line that sells the event looks like a mistake. Tight leading suits big display type, but this has gone past tight to colliding (Bringhurst: leading should fit the face and the size; Butterick: big headlines want less line spacing, but never so little that the lines touch). Version 2 opens it to about 1.1, which keeps the headline compact and leaves clear air between the lines. The extra gap down to the subhead also separates the headline from the subhead more clearly.

**Remaining findings for version 2**
1. **The button doesn't say what you get (taste, but it's what the viewer acts on).** "Get tickets" is clear, but the page never says what a ticket costs or whether you can come for one day. People at the point of deciding will look for that. If the price (or "from £X") or a line about availability sits beside or under the button, they can decide without leaving the hero.
2. **The bottom third of the screen is empty (taste).** Below the button, about 280 px of plain navy says nothing. A short look at the speakers, or a small strip of programme highlights, would give people a reason to scroll and some proof that it's worth the ticket. As it stands, the hero promises a lot and shows nothing to back it up.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav links (top right): the light grey on navy looks fine for contrast, but the "·" separators make the links read as one line of text, not four separate links. Spacing alone would separate them better.
- Subhead: "tools that others rely on every day" is good, but it describes the audience in general terms. Naming one concrete kind of tool would let readers see themselves in it faster.

**What's working.**
- The headline copy is specific and has a point of view ("quieter, slower software"). It could not sit on just any conference's page. Keep it.
- The date and venue line sits above the headline in the same yellow as the button. That ties the facts to the action and puts when and where up front, before you've even read the pitch.
- The type scale is tight: headline, subhead, a small label size and the nav, with big jumps between them. Every size has an obvious job.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the headline wraps on narrow screens. Check that the version 2 line spacing still holds when it breaks onto three or four lines.

Better: version 2
