**First read.** The eye goes to the bold sans heading, then hits three blocks of heavy brush script and slows to a crawl. You read it word by word, working out each letterform. It looks like a practical notice for ferry passengers that someone has dressed up as a hand-lettered café chalkboard. The tone and the content don't match.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short information page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how bikes work and what changes in winter, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The practical information is set in a decorative script, so a page meant to be scanned in seconds has to be deciphered. Second, the times people will plan around are vague.

**What would move it most**

1. **The three paragraphs are set in a brush script** (all the running text, lines ~150–520). Script letterforms join, slope and vary, and this one is set heavy. Word shapes blur together: "timetable thins", "twelve minutes" and "lower deck" take real effort, and the numbers buried in the sentences are what readers need and are the hardest to pick out. Anyone with low vision, dyslexia or a weak grasp of English will struggle more. Rule: paragraphs belong in a face made for reading, and script is for a few words of display (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1). Nothing here needs it. **Defect.** Fix: set the paragraphs in a plain text face. The heading's sans at regular weight would do. If you want a touch of the river-town character, use the script for a few words at most, such as a small tagline. Don't use it for anything a passenger has to act on.

2. **The times are too vague to plan by.** "Early morning and late evening", "before dusk" and "every forty minutes" with no starting time are exactly what passengers will hold the operator to. "Before dusk" moves by about two hours across the winter, so someone reading it in December can't tell whether a 4 pm boat exists, and might miss the last one. "One boat each hour" doesn't say at what minute past the hour. **Defect** (copy). Fix: give the first and last departures as clock times for summer and winter, and the minute pattern ("on the hour", or ":00 and :40"). If the times really do change, say where to check them online. At the moment the only pointer is "printed timetables in the shelter", which you only see once you're at the landing.

3. **The page makes the reader dig the facts out of prose.** Three facts drive decisions: when the boat runs, bikes ride free, and the winter timetable is reduced. Each sits mid-paragraph with nothing to make it stand out. Someone glancing at this on a phone at the quay has to read every sentence. **Taste**, but strongly held for a notice like this. Fix: give each paragraph a short lead-in or subhead ("Timetable", "Bicycles", "Winter"), or start each paragraph with its key fact in bold, so the page can be scanned.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: two sizes (heading at roughly 36 px bold, body at roughly 26 px heavy script). Because the body is so heavy, the heading barely outranks it. Once the body is a regular-weight text face this sorts itself out.
- "Notes for ferry passengers" is a generic heading. Name the route ("North Landing ⇄ Market Quay ferry") so readers know at once they're on the right page.
- "A little longer when the tide turns against the boat": give a figure ("up to 20 minutes") so people can plan connections.
- The column sits left with the right half empty. That's fine for reading, but at this width, with a text face, the lines will hold more characters. Keep the measure at about 65 characters.

**What's working.**
- The paragraph spacing is clear and even, with one topic per block, and that separation is a good base for adding subheads.
- The copy is plain and humane, and "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" says exactly who does what and when. Keep that voice when you add the numbers.
- The dark text on white has strong contrast, so the faint-text problem doesn't apply here.
