**First read.** The eye goes to "Help for ferry passengers", then to the two subheads, then down the left column. I read it as a plain notice or help page for people using a small local ferry. It feels calm and official, with nothing to stop on. That's fine for help text, though it looks like the default styles of a document rather than something designed.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a help page or posted notice that gets passengers on board smoothly and tells them how to report a problem. They may be skimming it on a phone or standing at the landing.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The structure is clear, it's easy to read, and the two columns match the two moments a passenger is in. The biggest problem is the copy: several items send you somewhere else for the one fact you need, or tuck that fact into the end of a long sentence.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts the reader will act on are missing or vague.** This is a defect. There are three cases:
   - "The last boat leaves before dusk" (left column, item 1) is the line that strands someone if they misread it. "Before dusk" could mean 4 pm or 6 pm, and the reader can't plan around it. It also only says what happens in winter, so a summer passenger can't tell whether the rule applies to them.
   - "Send it to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter" (right column, step 3) tells someone reading this at home to go back to the quay to find out where to write.
   - Nothing says what happens after you complain: whether you get a reply, how soon, or whether you get a refund. That's what decides whether anyone bothers.

   The fix: put the facts on the page. Give the real last-sailing time or the rule ("about 30 minutes before sunset; check the board"), print the address or email, and say what the reader gets back and when. Where a fact really does change, say where to find it in one short clause, not as the main point of the item.

2. **The key point of each bullet comes last.** This is a defect for skimmers. Each item is a 25–30 word sentence, and the part that decides what you do sits at the end:
   - item 1: "last boat leaves before dusk"
   - item 2: "machine is slow to give change"
   - item 4: "keep the gangway clear"

   Someone glancing at this at the landing reads the first few words and moves on. The fix: lead with the action and the fact, and cut the explanation down, e.g. "Last boat leaves before dusk in winter. Check the timetable board first."

3. **Wrapped lines run back under the bullets and numbers.** This is a convention and costs a little here. In both lists the second and third lines of each item go back to the left margin, under the "•" or "1.". The markers then don't stand out from the text, so you have to read for the start of each item instead of seeing it. The gap between each bullet and its first word is also much wider than a word space, which makes it look accidental. Bringhurst (4.4.2) says lists should be clarified by spatial positioning and that numbers can be hung in the margin. Lining wrapped lines up with the first word is common sense rather than a rule from a source. The fix: use a hanging indent so wrapped lines align with the first word, and hang the numbers the same way.

**Smaller things.**
- The numbers in the right column are typed into the text, not set as a list, so they don't line up the way the bullets do. Set both lists the same way.
- A lot of empty space below the right column makes the page feel unfinished. The contact details from finding 1 would fill it usefully.

**What's working.**
- **Splitting by moment:** "Before you board" and "If something goes wrong" match how a passenger thinks, so they find their half right away. Keep that split.
- **Bullets versus numbers:** the boarding tips are unordered and the complaint steps are ordered and numbered. That choice is right.
- **Type scale:** there are three clearly different sizes (title, subheads, body) and nothing in between. Body lines look to be around 55–60 characters long with generous line spacing, so the text is easy to read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this is a web page or a printed notice. If it's posted at the landing, check the body size from reading distance, because it looks small for a wall.
