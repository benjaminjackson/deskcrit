**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the hero of a ferry company's website, meant to get a casual visitor to see the crossing as easy and pleasant and then check sailing times.

**Verdict.** It does the basic job: the order is clear, the headline is specific and nicely worded, and there is one obvious action. What holds it back is that the copy leaves out the facts people plan a trip around: where the boat goes, when the first and last boats leave, and what it costs. The empty right half adds to the feeling that something is missing.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule line can't be planned around** *(defect)*. The paragraph says "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". Times are what a viewer acts on and holds the operator to. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:30, and "late evening" could mean 20:00 or 23:00. Someone planning a return trip reads one of those, and if it's the wrong one they're stuck on the far side. The page also never says where the boat goes: "the north landing" of what, crossing to where? "Cross the bay" only works for people who already know the route.
   - **Fix:** name both ends and the first and last sailings, e.g. "Boats leave North Landing for [Town] every 40 minutes, 6:40 am to 10:20 pm."
   - **Optional:** add a price, or "from £x". "Bicycles travel free" is the only price signal on the page, and it makes people wonder what everyone else pays.

2. **The ask sends people away for the one thing they came for** *(taste, argued)*. "See the timetable" is a specific ask, which is good. But it makes the timetable a separate trip, when the most useful single fact is the next departure.
   - **Fix:** pull that fact onto the page and let the button go deeper, e.g. "Next boat 10:40 · See today's timetable". The viewer then checks the page against their own watch and plans, rather than taking "every forty minutes" on trust.

3. **The empty space reads as leftover, not as a choice** *(taste)*.
   - All the content sits in a column on the left, about 160–790 px across and 90–570 px down.
   - The right ~40% and the bottom ~30% are blank white, so the weight piles up in the top left with nothing to balance it.
   - On a ferry page, that right half is where a viewer expects to see the boat or the bay, and its absence makes the page feel like a picture failed to load.
   - Ruder's point is that empty space works when it's set against something. Here it isn't set against anything.
   - **Fix:** either put one strong image there (the deck, the water), which would also back up "calm", or make the emptiness clearly deliberate: a narrower page, centred vertically, with the text block given generous margins on purpose.

**Smaller things.**
- **The feature list** ("Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board") has no bullets or other markers, and its text (about 18 px) is only a step below the paragraph (about 20 px). It reads like loose leftover lines rather than a list. Either make it clearly a list (markers or icons, maybe in a row), or set it at the same size as the paragraph.
- **The list repeats the paragraph.** Bicycles and the open deck are already in the paragraph. Cut them from one place or the other, or use the list for new facts (step-free access, dogs, price).
- **Type scale:** I count five sizes: label ~14, headline ~56, paragraph ~20, list ~18, button ~18. The 20 and 18 sizes collide. Four levels would be enough.
- **Headline line break:** "twelve / calm minutes" splits the key phrase. "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" keeps it together.
- **No header or navigation:** there's no logo, menu, or way to buy tickets. If this is the whole top of the page, a returning visitor has nowhere else to go.

**What's working.**
- **The headline** is concrete ("twelve minutes") and has a mood ("calm"). That beats almost any generic ferry slogan, so keep its wording.
- **One clear action:** a single high-contrast filled button, with nothing competing with it.
- **The paragraph type:** comfortable line length (about 65–70 characters), generous line spacing, and a clean left edge with the headline and label all on one line.
