**What it's for.** My assumption: the top of a ferry service's home page that gets a visitor to decide this crossing works for them and go to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound: one clear headline, one clear action, and nothing competing. But the words leave out what a ferry passenger needs to decide, and the page shows nothing of the bay it's selling, so it reads as competent and anonymous.

**What would move it most**

1. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on (defect).** The headline and paragraph never say where the boat goes. "Cross the bay" from "the north landing" doesn't name the other side. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no times, so a visitor can't tell whether a 7:00 or a 22:00 crossing exists. Nothing on the page gives a price. These are the things people plan around and later hold you to, and the page sends them through the button to find all three. The direction: name both landings in the headline or the line under it. Replace "early morning until late evening" with the actual first and last sailings (e.g. "06:40 to 22:20"). Add the fare. The strongest version also gives the next departure ("Next boat 10:40 from North Landing"). That lets the visitor check the service against their own plans instead of taking "every forty minutes" on trust. The button label is good because it names exactly what you get. Keep it, but don't make it the only route to basic facts.

2. **Half the frame is leftover space, and nothing shows the experience being sold (taste, argued).** The paragraph promises an open deck with the harbour sliding past, but the page shows only Inter, a blue button and white space. The empty area runs from about x=800 to the right edge and below the button. It isn't shaped or set against anything, so it reads as unfinished rather than calm. Ruder's point is that empty space works when it stands in tension with what's printed, and here nothing creates that tension. The cost is that this layout could belong to any software company. Nothing tells a visitor this crossing is pleasant, which is the actual reason to choose a ferry. The direction: fill that space with one strong photograph of the boat, the deck or the bay, aligned to the text column's top edge and running off the right edge. Or, if there's no good image, make the type itself fill the frame. If an image was meant to be there, see the last section.

**Smaller things.**
- **Feature list (y≈397–463).** The three lines have no markers and are spaced like body text, so they read as a stray fourth paragraph. They also look one size smaller than the paragraph (roughly 18 vs 20 px), which reads as an accident rather than a separate level. Either set them at the body size with a marker or icon, or make them clearly smaller and grouped.
- **"Bicycles travel free."** This is a price promise and the most concrete benefit on the page, but it's buried at body weight in an unmarked list. Give it some emphasis, or move it up next to the fare once the fare is added.
- **Headline line break.** The break after "twelve" separates the number from "minutes". Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" so the claim reads as one unit.
- **"Coastal Ferry Co." in the button blue.** Using the accent blue on a small bold label next to the only button makes it look like a link. Set it in the neutral text colour, or make it the logo.

**What's working.**
- **"Twelve calm minutes."** It gives a specific number and the feeling of the trip in one line. Keep both when rewriting.
- **Clean hierarchy.** The headline clearly dominates, it holds together with tight line spacing, and the paragraph's line length looks comfortable (about 65–70 characters).
- **One action.** There's a single solid button whose label names exactly what you get. Nothing competes with it.
