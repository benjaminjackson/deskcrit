**First read.** The eye lands on "Changing your pickup time" because it's the top-left heading. It then moves down through four bold headings that all look the same, without stopping on any of them. It reads as a help or FAQ page for a bakery's pickup orders. It feels calm and orderly, but plain: it's grey Inter on white, and if you swapped "loaf" for "parcel" nothing would tell you it was a bakery. The page has no title, no bakery name, and no way into your order, so the first question is "whose page is this, and where am I?"

**What it's for.** My assumption: an FAQ for customers who ordered bread online and are about to change, hand off, or cancel a pickup, or who need to check allergens.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and the grouping is right. The page falls short on the words. Two of the four answers involve a deadline or a handover, and both can be read two ways. The allergy answer sends an online customer to a card they can only see in the shop. Fix the copy first; the layout is close.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cancellation deadline and the handover rule can each be read two ways (defect).**
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time before the end of the day before. Someone collecting at 9 am who cancels at 6 pm the evening before is inside the deadline on one reading and too late on the other. They will hold the bakery to the reading they chose.
   - "show it at the counter along with a name": whose name? The person who ordered, or the person collecting? If the friend gives their own name and staff expect the buyer's, the bread isn't handed over.
   - "Open your order" doesn't say where (email link, account page, app). Nothing here links to it, so the first answer ends on a step the reader can't take from this page.
   - Direction: state the cutoff as a time ("until 24 hours before your pickup time"), name the name ("your name, as on the order"), and make "your order" a link.
2. **The allergy answer doesn't answer someone who is ordering online (defect).** "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps only once you're standing in the shop, after you've ordered and paid. An allergic customer needs the ingredients before ordering. As written, they have to buy blind or not buy. For a page about allergens, that is the costliest gap here. Direction: say where the ingredient list is online (on each product, or a link), and keep "ask the baker" as the fallback.
3. **The page has no title or context, so nothing tells you where you are (defect).** The four headings are rightly equal; they are parallel questions. But nothing sits above them, so the eye has no starting point. The page also never says whose bakery this is or that these are pickup questions; the reader has to work that out from the answers. Krug's test, that a page says what it is within seconds, fails here. The fix (Tschichold, *The New Typography*) is to make one element clearly the first thing seen and set the rest back by differences of size: here, a page title such as "Picking up your order" in the bakery's name, above the questions and clearly larger than them.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run about 95–110 characters ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…"), well past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the text column to about 600–650 px. The answers are short, so this costs little today, but it will once an answer grows.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies paragraph (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrowing the column will probably move it. If not, rebalance the break.
- "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" doesn't say what happens after that: is the order resold, refunded, or lost? Add the consequence in the reader's terms ("after that, we can't keep it for you").

**What's working.**
- The spacing does the grouping. Each heading sits about 10 px above its answer and about 45 px below the previous answer, so every question-and-answer pair reads as one unit without rules or boxes. Keep that ratio.
- There are two type levels, bold headings at roughly 32 px and regular body at roughly 18 px. They differ in size and weight, the scale is tight, and the body is large and dark enough to read comfortably.
- The headings name the reader's task ("Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else") rather than a system term, so a scanning reader finds their question by its first words.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Taste. Let the bakery's own voice carry the page, since "After that the bread has already been baked for you" is the one line here that sounds like a person. Write the other answers in that voice, so the page reads as this bakery and not as any shop's help template. That warmth is what makes a small business worth ordering from again.
2. Builds on finding 3. Taste. Once there's a title, give the page one bakery cue in colour or type, kept to the title. At the moment the page could belong to anyone (Refactoring UI argues for personality through a few deliberate choices rather than decoration).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether "your order" links anywhere in the live page, and how the column reflows on a phone, where most pickup customers will read it.
