**First read.** The eye lands on the title "New sign-ups by channel", then drops into the chart and bounces across six bars, each a different strong colour. Red, orange and green are the loudest, so it never settles on the tall blue Organic search bar. The two sentences that hold the actual news sit in the small right-hand column and get read last, if at all. It feels tidy and calm, a competent internal analytics page.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a Growth dashboard page that tells the team where Q3 sign-ups came from and what changed, so they can back the decision to move the events budget into referrals.

**Verdict.** It's clear, honest and correctly built, but it shows the data without making the point. The colours, the topic-only title and the hidden side note leave the viewer to find the story alone, and the main claim ("up from a quarter in Q2") has nothing on screen to back it.

**What would move it most**
1. **Six colours that mean nothing compete with the bars that matter.** The six bars are blue, orange, red, teal, green and purple, but the axis labels already name every bar, so the colour carries no information. Worse, the warm red and orange on Paid social and Referral stand out more than the blue leader. Red and green also read as bad and good, so Paid social looks like a problem and Podcast ads looks like a win, which nobody intended. The page's own story is about three bars: Organic search (up), and Podcast ads plus Events (cut). The fix: draw all six bars in one neutral and give one accent colour to the bars the text talks about. That means Organic search, or Podcast ads and Events if the budget move is the point. *Convention* (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 19.1).
2. **The finding sits in a side note instead of on the chart.** "New sign-ups by channel" names the topic. The finding ("Organic search brought a third of all new accounts, up from a quarter") is in 15 px text off to the right, top-aligned with nothing. A viewer who skims sees six bars and learns nothing they couldn't guess. Put the finding in the headline, or annotate it right on the Organic search bar, and move the budget note next to the Podcast ads and Events bars. The side column could then go, or shrink to supporting notes. *Taste, common sense*: a dashboard that answers "so what?" saves every reader the job of working it out.
3. **The key comparison isn't shown.** The claim "up from a quarter in Q2" is the reason this page exists, but the chart only shows Q3, so the viewer has to take the growth on trust. Show Q2 for each channel, as a pale paired bar or a tick mark, or at least give the change next to the Organic search value (e.g. "1,840, +X vs Q2"). Then the reader can see the rewrite paid off instead of just being told. *Convention* (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2).

**Smaller things.**
- Lower third and under the side column: about a third of the screen is leftover blank space, and the right column stops halfway down. If Q2 data goes in, give the chart that room, or make the chart taller so the side column has something to line up with.

**What's working.**
- The bars are sorted by size, start at zero, carry their exact values, and are labelled on the axis with no legend. Nothing here has to be decoded. Keep all of it.
- The source line is a model: where the data came from, the exact dates and the total (5,400, which matches the bars). It makes every number checkable.
- The side copy is concrete and true to the data: "a third" (1,840 of 5,400) and "fewer than 700" (410 + 260 = 670) are both right, and the second paragraph ends on a decision rather than a description.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the number 1,840 lead. Set it with its share ("34% of new accounts") as a big figure beside the headline, so the page answers its question before the eye even reaches the chart. *Taste*: on a Growth page, the one number that changed the plan should be the first thing seen.
2. Turn "The events budget moves to referrals in Q4" into something to check later: give the expected Q4 referral effect, or a link to the plan. Then next quarter's page can show whether it worked. *Taste*: a dashboard that records its own bets earns trust.
