**First read.** The eye lands first on the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month." It then drops to the tall June bar, which is the loudest mark on the page, and moves right to the $4.82M card. It reads as a half-year report from a credit union to its members. It feels calm, sober and trustworthy, and it is plainly set.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a public page telling credit union members, in a glance, how member savings did in the first half of 2026 compared with last year.

**Verdict.** It does its job, and better than most report pages. The biggest problem is that the most dramatic thing on the page, June's jump to $1150k, is mostly a one-off bonus payroll. That is explained only in a grey footnote below the chart, so a member's first takeaway is "growth is speeding up," and that takeaway is wrong.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June bar tells a story the data doesn't support.** *(Defect.)* Jan to May rises by $40–70k a month. June then jumps about $280k, and that bar is the one thing you see after the headline. The reason ("June includes the mid-year bonus payroll") sits in 12–13 px grey type under the chart panel, where few people read. Members leave thinking deposits took off in June. The June card figures, average deposit $214 and 5,374 accounts, are also bonus-month figures, and nothing next to them says so. The fix is to explain the jump at the June bar: a short label next to it ("includes mid-year bonus payroll"), or that bar split or shaded to separate the bonus from regular deposits. The explanation has to be in front of the viewer when they look at the bar. This isn't from an approved source; it's common sense: an outlier that drives the chart needs its explanation where the eye is.
2. **The comparison lines are compressed into shorthand.** *(Defect, copy.)* The green lines say "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025," "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025." Using "on" to mean "compared with" is a British way of saying it. A US member can read "Up $11 on Jun 2025" as something that happened in June 2025. These are the numbers a member will remember and repeat, so they need to read one way only. Put the verb and the point of reference back in: "Up 6.1% from the same six months of 2025," "$11 more than in June 2025." While you're there, end on what it means for the member, for example "your credit union took in more than last year." (Nielsen heuristic 2: use the audience's own words.)

**Smaller things.**
- Numbers are written three ways: "$4.82M" in the card, "$1150k" on the June bar, and "$1200k" on the y-axis, with no thousands separator. Use one format, such as $1.15M and $1.2M, or label the axis "thousands of dollars" and drop the k.

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding, not just the topic, and the subhead pins down exactly what's being counted: "new money paid into member savings accounts." That second line rules out the obvious misreading that these are balances. Keep both.
- The chart itself is honest and quiet: bars start at zero, there's one blue, faint gridlines, direct value labels, a plain title with units, and a source line. Nothing distracts from the data.
- Every number in the cards comes with a comparison to last year and a plain-English label ("Accounts that made a deposit"). That turns three numbers into three answers.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *(Taste.)* The headline picks month-on-month growth, which could just be the normal pattern of the year. The stronger claim for members is already on the page: "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025." A headline built on that year-on-year rise would make the page's case on terms a member already trusts, last year, and it can't be explained away by the season or by June's bonus.
2. *(Taste.)* Show the same months of 2025 in grey behind each blue bar. Every bar then carries its own comparison, so the reader doesn't have to take the 6.1% on trust; they can see it month by month (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, on showing comparisons).
