**First read.** The eye goes straight to the black headline, "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks," then drops to the orange "Start your free week" button. Then it stalls on the grey "Keep your streak going" card just below the button before moving on to the three steps. It reads as a clean, calm landing page for an app that teaches you to speak a language. It's tidy and friendly, but it looks a lot like every other SaaS template, and the illustration on the right adds almost nothing.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is the home page hero for a language-learning app, and its job is to get first-time visitors who want to speak a language to start the free week.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The promise is specific, there's one clear action and the steps are plain. Its biggest weakness is that the rest of the hero doesn't back up the headline. The illustration just repeats the word "conversation," and the streak card speaks to people who already use the app, right at the point where a newcomer is deciding whether to sign up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The streak card is aimed at the wrong person and sits in the wrong place.** "Keep your streak going" assumes you already have a streak, but someone seeing this hero for the first time doesn't. It sits only about 28 px under the button, about the same gap as headline to subhead. Because of that it reads as part of the call to action, so the last thing a visitor reads before clicking is a retention nudge. Its statistic also has no comparison point: "finish the course twice as often" as whom? This is a defect: it weakens the moment of decision and makes a claim the visitor can't check. The spacing part breaks a convention: the gap between groups should be clearly larger than the gap inside one (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II). Fix: take it out of the hero, or rewrite it as a reason for a newcomer to start ("Five minutes a day is enough: people who practise five days in a row finish twice as often as those who don't"). Then move it clearly away from the button.
2. **The illustration only repeats the headline.** Two empty speech bubbles in a teal box say "conversation," and the headline already says that. A strong visual and headline add up to one idea that neither has alone. This one adds nothing, and it's the main thing that makes the page look like a template, so it could be anyone's product. This is taste, argued on its own terms. Fix: show the thing only Parlo has, for example a real exchange in Spanish with the tutor's feedback on each word (the "Speak" step), or a day-1 vs week-10 contrast that makes "ten weeks" concrete.
3. **The illustration panel ignores the grid.** The box runs from about x 676 to 1076. None of the three step columns below it start or end there, and its right edge stops about 148 px short of the right margin that the nav and the third column share (x ≈ 1224). That leaves a dead strip on the right and makes the hero look placed by eye. This breaks a convention (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "Construction of the grid"). Fix: extend the panel to the right margin, or line its edges up with the step columns.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "in / ten weeks", so the promise is split mid-phrase. Break it before "in" so "in ten weeks" stays together (common sense; the source for this rule isn't confirmed).
- "Start your free week" is a commitment, but nothing near it says what happens after day 7 (whether a card is needed, what it costs). One line under the button would answer the question a cautious visitor has before clicking.
- "Practice with a tutor that listens" can be read two ways: a human tutor, or software. Say which, because it changes what the visitor thinks they're signing up for.

**What's working.**
- The headline promises a specific outcome with a time frame ("a real conversation in ten weeks"), not a generic boast. Keep that specificity.
- There's exactly one primary action, and the warm orange is the only saturated warm colour on the page, so it comes forward against the cool teal and the greys.
- The three steps (Listen, Speak, Use it) have short, concrete lines that say who does what, and they share the margins of the hero text above them.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the "Speak" step become the hero picture: one phrase, the learner's attempt, and per-word feedback. This is taste. For a product that sells speaking, showing the feedback lets the visitor judge the value for themselves instead of taking the headline on trust.
2. Turn "ten weeks" into something the visitor can see. Under the headline or in the steps band, show what someone can actually say at week 1 and at week 10. This is taste. It makes the headline's number concrete and checkable, and it gives the button a reason to be clicked today.
