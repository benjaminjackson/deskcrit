**First read.** The eye lands on "Low-light houseplants", then drops straight into the row of four buttons, because they are the loudest thing on the page and each is a different colour: teal, purple, orange, blue. The plants come after that. It reads as a small, tidy plant shop's category page. The cards and type feel calm and friendly, but the button colours make it look like a UI kit demo.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a product listing page for an online plant shop, where a shopper who lives in a dim flat picks a plant and adds it to their cart.

**Verdict.** The page is solid and close to done. Its structure, type and copy work. Two things hold it back: four buttons that do the same job in four colours, and plant drawings that don't show the plants being sold.

**What would move it most**
1. **Four colours for one action (defect).** The "Add to cart" buttons in the lower third of each card are teal, purple, orange and blue. Shoppers read colour as meaning, so they will assume the buttons differ: a different offer, a sale, a warning on the orange one. Nothing explains the difference, so it costs a moment of doubt on every card. The buttons are also the most saturated things on the page, so they take the eye away from the plants and prices. On their own, each looks like a fine button. Together they break the one-role, one-colour rule (Apple HIG, "Color"; Material Design colour roles), and they make four groups out of products that belong to one set. Purple and royal blue also have nothing to do with the soft greens and terracotta everywhere else. Fix: give all four buttons one shared colour, and keep any other colour for a real difference in role or state. The teal or a deep leaf green would suit this shop.
2. **The pictures don't show the product (defect).** All four drawings are the same terracotta pot with two or three generic leaves. A snake plant has stiff, upright, sword-shaped leaves, but here it gets rounded ones. The pothos caption says "trails to 6 ft", but the drawing shows two upright leaves. Someone choosing between four plants by eye can't tell them apart, and the drawing even contradicts its own caption. On a shopping page the picture is most of what makes someone buy. If these are placeholders, this is a capture problem. If they ship, it's the biggest gap after the buttons. Fix: show each real plant, as a photo or an accurate drawing, in the same frame and lighting so the set stays consistent.

**Smaller things.**
- Navigation, top right: "Houseplants" looks the same as its neighbours, so nothing marks where the shopper is. Mark the current item with one cue, such as weight or an underline (NN/g, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Orange "Add to cart" button, third card: white bold text on mid-orange looks below 4.5:1. Measure it. This goes away if you adopt one darker button colour (WCAG 1.4.3).

**What's working.**
- The subtitle "Four plants that do well more than six feet from a window" turns a vague category into a test the shopper can check against their own room. Keep that specificity.
- Each card has a clean, consistent stack: name, then a spec line (pot size, mature size), then a bold price, then the button. Space separates the levels with no extra rules, and the four cards line up exactly on one grid.
- The type scale is tight: one family, about four sizes with clear steps (title, product name and price, spec, nav), with weight doing the rest.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the spec line answer the low-light shopper's real question. "Grows to 2 ft" is useful, but this audience is also choosing by care. One short fact per plant, such as how often to water or whether it's pet-safe, would let them compare without opening four pages. The "Care guides" link in the nav shows the shop already has this content. (Taste)
2. The pale sage panel behind each plant is the page's one soft colour. Taking that sage into the page heading area or the single button colour would give the shop a recognisable look instead of a template look. (Taste)

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover, focus and pressed states of the buttons and nav links. What happens to the cart count after "Add to cart".
