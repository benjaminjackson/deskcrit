**First read.** The eye lands on "See where every dollar goes this month" straight away, then drops to the white "Try it free" button. Then it drifts right into an empty field of blue and finds nothing. It reads as a personal budgeting app for individuals and households. It feels clean, confident and trustworthy, but it also feels anonymous: Inter, an electric-blue band and three feature columns could belong to any fintech start-up.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the top of a marketing landing page meant to get people who manage their own or household money to start a free trial.

**Verdict.** The words and the order of importance are solid. The top screen fails at one thing: it promises you'll *see* where your money goes and then shows you nothing. The right half of the hero is empty, so the strongest part of the pitch rests on words alone.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero promises a view and shows none.** About 560 px on the right of the blue band, next to the headline, are blank. "See where every dollar goes" is a visual promise: the visitor should be looking at the month broken down by category, ideally with a "heads-up" alert from the subhead. Without that, they have to take the claim on trust, and every budgeting app makes the same claim. If a product image was meant to sit there and didn't load, this finding is about the capture, not the design; check it in a live browser. If it is intentional, put the product there: one real-looking screen showing a category near its limit. That makes the headline, the subhead and the picture one idea instead of three restatements. *Defect*, because the visitor can't judge the value. That a visual and its headline should make one idea together is my own judgment (*taste*); the catalog's source for it isn't one I can cite.
2. **The page tips left.** The headline, subhead, buttons and logo all sit in the left 60% of the hero. The right side carries only flat colour, with nothing to balance them, so the composition looks unfinished rather than calmly open. Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance") says a heavy side needs a counterweight. Fixing finding 1 fixes this too. If you don't want a product image, the headline needs to span the full width as a deliberate statement. *Convention*, and here breaking it costs you.
3. **"Try it free" and "Watch the tour" are generic asks.** They could sit on anyone's page, and "Try it" doesn't say what happens first. The headline already has a concrete hook, so build the ask from it, for example "See this month's spending" or "Connect an account free". That way the click promises the exact result the headline described, using the visitor's own money as the comparison. *Taste*, but it bears directly on conversion.

**Smaller things.**
- "Read-only access" card: "The app can see…" switches to a faceless "the app" right where trust matters most. "Penny Path can see your balances… but can never move your money" says it to the visitor.
- "Sign in" in the nav is styled the same as the marketing links. Returning users usually expect it to be set slightly apart (a convention from similar products). This is minor.

**What's working.**
- The headline is concrete, short, and set big and tight (two lines with close leading), so it reads as one unit and clearly leads the page. Keep that wording.
- The subhead does exactly what it should: three verbs (connect, set a limit, get a heads-up) tell you who does what and when, with no gap to fill.
- The buttons have a clear primary and secondary: a solid white fill against a white outline on blue, so there's never doubt which one is the main action. The three feature columns share the hero's 64 px left edge and have an even rhythm.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let one number lead the hero image: a single category line such as "Groceries: $412 of $450", with the heads-up showing. For an app selling "before you go over", one near-limit number does more than a full dashboard screenshot. *Taste.*
2. The three feature headings are category labels ("Shared budgets"). Write them as what changes for the visitor, for example "Split the household bills without a spreadsheet". The cards would then argue for the product instead of listing features, which also answers the generic look. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half of the hero and the roughly 200 px blank band under the features are the design or a partial capture. Check both in a live browser.
- Focus states on the two buttons and the nav links.
