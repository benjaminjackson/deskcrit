**First read.** The eye lands on three bold, equal-sized coloured cards and moves left to right across "Learn to swim", "Join the lanes", "Family hour". It stops a little longer on the orange card, only because that one is warm and comes first. It reads as a pool's programme page, and it feels friendly and confident. Then the eye drops into three large empty coloured areas and finds nothing more: no pool name, no times beyond the copy, and nothing to do next.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is a section of a public pool or leisure centre's website, meant to get adults and families to sign up for or turn up to one of three sessions.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The cards are well set but they only describe the sessions; nothing leads and nothing tells you what to do. The caption under the cards claims "one offer clearly leads the page", and the page shows the opposite. That line is content, not evidence.

**What would move it most**
1. **No offer leads; the three cards are equal on purpose or by default** (defect). The cards have the same size, the same headline size and weight, and fully saturated colours at nearly the same mid-dark value. In grayscale they would be three identical blocks. So the eye has no starting point and treats them as interchangeable. The only pull comes from the orange card, because warm colour comes forward (Itten), and that is an accident of colour choice, not a decision. Tschichold asks that importance be shown by differences of size. Pick the session the page exists to sell, probably "Learn to swim" if adult classes are the thing that needs filling, and make it clearly first: bigger, or the only card in a strong colour. Set the other two back by size or by a quieter tint.
2. **Each card stops at a description and never gives an ask** (defect). "Small adult classes on Tuesday evenings…" tells you what it is, but not what time, how much, or how to get a place. Nothing leads the eye from the headline to a next step, so every path ends inside the card. Each card needs one specific action written in the viewer's terms, such as "Book a Tuesday class", "See lane times", "Check Sunday sessions". The lead card also needs the fact a visitor decides on, like the start time or price. Classes need booking, lanes and family hour probably don't, so the asks should differ.
3. **More than half of each card is empty colour** (defect, partly taste). All the text sits in the top 45%. Below "with a coach in the water beside you." there are about 300 px of solid orange, green, and purple doing nothing. That is not breathing room. It is filler that pushes the page's actual job, the ask, off the screen. Either fill that space with the ask and the key facts from finding 2, or shorten the cards so the space gathers outside them.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Design review: approved, one offer clearly leads the page" line under the cards is review text left on the page. Remove it before shipping.
- The pool's name and the section heading are missing. A visitor landing here can't tell whose sessions these are. Add a short heading above the row.
- The three unrelated saturated hues cover roughly half the frame. After finding 1, keep one strong colour for the lead card and let the others go quieter (Refactoring UI, Material colour roles: one accent, used sparingly).
- The row sits left of centre: the left margin is about 56 px and the right is about 96 px, so the page leans slightly left. Either centre the row or run it to the same margin on both sides.

**What's working.**
- The headlines are verbs and invitations ("Learn to swim", "Join the lanes"), not category labels like "Lessons" or "Lap swim". They say what you'd do, so keep that voice.
- The body copy names real details: "a coach in the water beside you", "three speeds to choose from", "floats and toys for the children". That is the kind of specific reassurance a nervous adult swimmer or a parent needs.
- The type is clean. There are two sizes with a big step between them, the headlines are tight bold display type that holds together across two lines, and white text sits comfortably on all three colours.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let "a coach in the water beside you" become the lead card's main promise, not body copy (taste). For adults afraid of the water, that one line answers the real objection. Giving it real weight would do more for sign-ups than any colour change.
2. Tie the colours to water and the building rather than to a generic tile set (taste). The orange, green, and purple could belong to any app. A pool page that owned one colour from its own signage or tiles would feel like a place you could walk into.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Nothing that matters for this piece.
