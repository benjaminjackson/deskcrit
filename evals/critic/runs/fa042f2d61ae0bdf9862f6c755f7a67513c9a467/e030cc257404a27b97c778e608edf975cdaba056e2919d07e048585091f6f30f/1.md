**First read.** The eye lands on "See where every dollar goes this month", a big white bold headline on navy, then drops to the subhead and the white "Try it free" button. Then it drifts right, into a wide navy field with nothing in it, and stops. It reads as the landing page for a consumer budgeting app. It feels calm and trustworthy, but it also feels like a hero that is waiting for its picture.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the top of a marketing page for a personal-budgeting app, meant to get individuals and couples to start a free trial.

**Verdict.** The words and type are clean, and the offer is clear in five seconds. The biggest problem is that a headline that promises *seeing* sits next to half a hero of empty navy. The page never shows the one thing it sells, so it looks unfinished.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half of the hero is empty, and the headline asks for a picture.** Defect. From about x = 800 to the right edge, across the full height of the navy band, there is nothing. All the visual weight is on the left, so the band looks lopsided (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). The empty area isn't shaped or framed. It reads as leftover space, or as an image that failed to load, rather than space chosen on purpose (Tschichold; Müller-Brockmann). The bigger cost is to the argument. "See where every dollar goes" is a promise to the eye, and the viewer is shown no dollars, no categories and no "heads-up" alert, so they have to take the claim on trust. If a product image is meant to sit there and didn't load, this is a capture problem. If not, it needs fixing. Fix: put the product in that space, such as one month's category bars with one category close to its limit and the warning showing. That way the picture and the headline make one argument together.
2. **"Try it free" can be read two ways, and it asks for trust instead of showing value.** Defect, because this is the button that commits the viewer. "Free" could mean a free trial that turns into a charge, or a free plan. Someone who signs up thinking it's free forever and later gets billed will blame you. And the ask only says "try us". It doesn't tie the product to anything the viewer already has. Your subhead already holds a better ask: connect an account and see last month's spending sorted, a result they can check against what they remember spending. Fix: make the button name that result and state the terms next to it, e.g. a line under the buttons saying how long the trial is and whether a card is needed. "Watch the tour" is fine as the secondary action.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing. The type, alignment and spacing hold up: one left edge at 64 px runs from the logo through the hero to the first feature column, the three columns are equal, and the feature text lines run about 45 characters.

**What's working.**
- **The copy talks to the viewer.** The subhead says who does what in plain verbs: "Connect your accounts, set a limit for each category, and get a heads-up before you go over." Keep that voice when you rewrite the button.
- **"Read-only access … can never move money"** answers the biggest worry anyone has about linking a bank, in one sentence, without legal-speak. It earns its place in the feature row.
- **The hierarchy is clear.** One headline at roughly three times the size of the subhead, a solid white primary button and an outlined secondary one. The eye never has to choose where to start.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the hero image be a single "heads-up" moment, e.g. "Dining out: $38 left, 9 days to go", rather than a full dashboard screenshot. Taste. One alert lands the product's most distinctive promise faster than a busy screen would, and it gives the navy field one focal point to balance the headline.
2. The navy, Inter-style sans and three-column feature row are the default fintech kit. Right now this could be any budgeting app's page, so nothing makes it memorable. Taste: give the "heads-up" state one warm accent color, used only for that moment in the product image and nowhere else. Then the brand's single color *is* its main feature (Albers, *Interaction of Color*, on how one color changes with its context).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- About 220 px of blank white sits below the feature row (y ≈ 580–800). Check in a live browser whether the page continues below the fold or the capture cut off mid-load.
- Check in a live browser whether the hero's right half is meant to hold an image that didn't load.
- Hover and focus states on the two buttons and the nav links can't be seen in a still.
