**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", moves right to the white card, and drops straight to the blue "Send message" button. Within two seconds it reads as the contact page of a small agency or studio. It feels calm, tidy and finished. It also feels anonymous: apart from the words "Fernwick Studio", nothing tells you whose page this is or why you'd choose them.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: the contact page of a design or creative studio. Its job is to turn a prospective client into a project enquiry that is useful to act on.

**Verdict.** It works. Someone can send a message without friction, and the hierarchy is clean. The biggest problem is that the intro asks for a budget and a timeline, but the form gives no place for either. Second, a studio's own page shows none of the studio's craft, so the enquiries it collects will be thinner and less committed than they could be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy asks for information the form doesn't collect.** The intro under the headline says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form has only Name, Email and one Message box. The visitor has to remember that request while typing free text, and most won't. The studio gets messages without the two facts it most needs to qualify a lead, and then has to write back for them, which uses up part of the promised two-day reply. This is a defect, argued as common sense: a page should put in front of the visitor what the next step needs. Fix: add a Budget field with a few ranges to pick from and a Timeline field, both marked optional. Or, if you want to keep the form to three fields, move the prompt into the Message box as helper text. The message box itself is also short (about 120 px tall) for "tell us about your project". Make it taller so it invites more than a line or two.
2. **The headline could be on anyone's page, and so could the page.** "Tell us about your project" over a default-looking form, with blue underlined links and a grey background, is the stock contact page. For a studio, this page is often the last thing a prospect sees before deciding to write, and it gives no reason to pick this studio over the next tab. This is a defect for this job, not just a matter of looks: the visitor can't tell what's different here, so nothing builds the commitment that makes for a good brief. Direction: make the headline name the kind of work or client Fernwick takes on. Let one element of the page (the wordmark, the type, one colour) show the studio's hand. A short line of proof next to the form would also help, such as a recent client or a link to one relevant case study.

**Smaller things.**
- Navigation, top right: "Contact" looks exactly like Work, Services and About, so nothing shows which page you are on. Mark the current item with one cue, such as bold, a heavier underline or a bar (Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist", NN/g).
- Form labels: nothing says which fields are required. The message reads as optional while name and email presumably aren't. Add an asterisk to required labels and explain it once, or add "(optional)" after the optional ones (Budiu, NN/g).
- Field borders: the grey edges on white look light, possibly under WCAG 1.4.11's 3:1 for input boundaries. Measure them and darken if they fall short.
- Grid: the header runs from 56 px to about 1224 px, but the body content stops at the card's right edge, about 1068 px. That leaves an unexplained empty strip on the right, and no other block lines up with that edge. Either widen or shift the two columns to the header's right edge, or narrow the header to match (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- Headline break: "your / project" separates "your" from "project". Rebreaking to "Tell us about / your project" keeps the phrase together. This one is taste.

**What's working.**
- There is one clear path: headline, intro, form, button. The button is the only filled colour block on the page, so the action is never in doubt. Keep it as the single strong colour.
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a concrete, checkable promise. It is the most persuasive line on the page, so keep it and keep it specific.
- The form is set correctly. It is one column, each label sits above its field and closer to it than to the field above, there is no placeholder-only labelling, and the labels are a heavier weight than the body text. Keep this structure through any visual change.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the "two working days" promise carry more weight, for example as a line sitting right above the Send button. It answers the visitor's last hesitation ("will anyone read this?") at the moment they decide. Taste.
2. The empty lower half of the left column, under the intro, could hold one small, specific piece of evidence, such as a single project thumbnail or a one-line quote from a client. It would sit opposite the form so the visitor weighs it right as they write. This is taste, and it is not a call to fill the space: one item, not a sidebar.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the fields and the button; input types (for example, whether the email field checks the format); validation and error messages; the confirmation after sending.
