**First read.** The eye lands on the bold two-line headline, "Update the card that pays for your subscription", then drops into an even grey block of body text with blue words scattered through it. It reads as a help-centre article from a SaaS billing product, written for an account owner or admin. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, and a little bare: there is a lot of empty space in the bottom third and to the right.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a self-serve help article: a customer arrives wanting to change their payment card and should leave knowing exactly how, without contacting support.

**Verdict.** The page looks well made, but the article doesn't do the one thing its title promises. It never says how to update the card. It explains what happens around the change (open invoices, permissions, declines) but leaves out the steps and gives no link to the place where you make the change. Fix that and this is a strong help page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The how-to is missing.** The headline promises a task. The first sentence only says you can do it "from the billing page", with no link and no path to that page. The links that are there (billing history, roles and permissions, paying by invoice, restore your previous plan) all lead somewhere else. Someone who came to change a card reads three paragraphs and still has to go and find the billing page themselves, and some will give up and click "Contact support", which is the cost this page exists to avoid. This is a defect: the page doesn't show what the user needs next, and it makes them rely on memory instead of seeing it (Nielsen, heuristic 6: recognition rather than recall). The fix should achieve this: the action sits at the top of the article. Turn "the billing page" into a direct link, or add a short numbered list of the clicks (e.g. Settings → Billing → Payment method → Replace card) right under the headline, then move the open-invoice note and the permissions note below it as conditions.
2. **Nothing shows where you are.** In the left "In this section" list, "Update a payment card" (the current page, second item) looks exactly like the other four. In the top navigation, "Billing" also looks the same as its neighbours. A reader who lands here from search can't see where this page sits or what the related pages are. They have to work it out from the small grey breadcrumb. This is a convention: Nielsen Norman Group's menu-design guideline says to show the user's current location, and leaving it out is the most common menu mistake. Mark the current item with one clear cue, such as a bolder weight, a left bar or a tint, and do the same for "Billing" in the top navigation.

**Smaller things.**
- Inline links (body paragraphs): they stand out only by blue colour, with no underline. Against the near-black body text, that blue looks well under the 3:1 contrast WCAG asks for between link and text (G183). Measure it. Underline the links, or at least make sure they reach 3:1 and underline on hover and focus.
- Body measure (all three paragraphs): the first line runs about 83 characters, beyond the 45 to 75 range Bringhurst gives. Narrow the text column by roughly 10% or raise the body size slightly.
- Naming (sidebar, breadcrumb, headline): one page goes by three names: "Update a payment card", "Payment methods" and "Update the card that pays for your subscription". That's common sense, not a published rule: pick one phrase so people trust they've landed on the page they clicked.

**What's working.**
- The type hierarchy is clean and works. There are four levels: a bold H1, a bold H3-size subhead, comfortable body text at about 18 px with generous leading, and small grey sidebar and breadcrumb text. Each one is clearly a step apart, with no near-duplicate sizes. Keep it.
- The copy is plain and specific where it matters: "after three days and after seven days", "moves to the free plan", "keeps the old card until it is paid". Those are exactly the facts people hold support to, and they're stated without hedging. "When the new card is declined" is a heading that names what follows and answers the next worried question.
- The restraint is right for a help centre: one grey range, one link blue, no ornament, and the subhead sits clearly closer to its own paragraph than to the one above.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the "When the new card is declined" timeline become something the reader can scan in two seconds. Put the day-3 retry, the day-7 retry and the move to the free plan on their own lines, so a worried admin sees how long they have. That's taste: on a billing page, the deadline is what people act on.
2. Use the empty right-hand column under the header for a short "Still stuck?" block that links to "Contact support" with the account context filled in. That's taste: it catches the reader at the moment the article fails them, instead of sending them back to the top navigation.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indicators on the sidebar and inline links; whether the breadcrumb items are links; how the two-column layout reflows on a phone.
