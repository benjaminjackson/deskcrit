**First read.** The eye lands on "Pricing", drops to the three big prices, reads across $9 / $29 / $79, and stops there. Nothing in the cards leads anywhere, so the eye wanders back up to the blue "Start free" in the top right corner. It reads as a SaaS pricing page for some kind of scheduling or booking tool. It is calm and clean, and it looks like a template: no plan stands out and nothing asks you to choose.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a public pricing page that should help a small business or team pick a plan and sign up for it.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and legible, but it doesn't do the job of a pricing page. You can compare the three plans but you can't choose one: there is no action on any card and no plan is recommended, so the decision ends in a dead end. That is the one thing in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plans can't be chosen.** Each card ends on its feature list, with no button. The only action is "Start free", top right, about 300 px above and 600 px to the side of the cards, and it names no plan. A viewer who has just settled on Team has to keep that choice in their head, travel up to a generic button, and trust it will remember Team for them. That's the point where people stop. This breaks natural mapping: the control should sit next to the thing it changes (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*). It also breaks recognition over recall (Nielsen, heuristic 6). And it breaks the convention people know from every other pricing table, where each tier has its own button (Jakob's law, Nielsen Norman Group). **Defect.** Fix: put a button at the foot of every card that names the plan, such as "Start Team trial".
2. **Nothing helps the viewer decide.** The three cards are identical in weight, border and colour, so the page gives no hint which plan suits "teams of every size". Most viewers want to be told the usual pick, and the brand blue is sitting there unused below the nav. **Taste, argued:** a pricing page that won't recommend makes the viewer do the sizing work alone, and three equal options make that slower. Fix: make one plan the default. Team is the natural one, given the subhead talks about teams. Give it the only filled blue button and a short "Most teams pick this" label. The other two get outline buttons.
3. **"Start free" can be read two ways.** All three plans cost money, so "Start free" means either a free trial or a free plan that isn't shown here. If it's a trial, the viewer doesn't know its length or whether a card is needed. If they read it as a free tier, they'll feel misled when they hit a payment step. **Defect**, because this is a line the viewer commits on. Fix: say exactly what's free, such as "Free for 14 days, no card needed", either under the heading or on the buttons themselves.
4. **The page never says what the product does.** "1 calendar", "Booking page" and "Shared availability" let you guess it's a scheduling tool, but someone who arrives straight at /pricing from search or an ad can't say what they'd be paying for. The subhead, "Simple plans for teams of every size", could sit on any SaaS site, so it tells the viewer nothing that sets this product apart (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: a page should be self-evident). **Defect** for new visitors. Fix: name what you get in the subhead, in the viewer's terms, such as "Let clients book time with your team without the back-and-forth".

**Smaller things.**
- Nav: "Pricing" looks exactly like the other menu items, so nothing shows that this is the current page. Mark it with one cue: bolder weight, an underline or the brand colour (Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist", Nielsen Norman Group).
- Subhead: "you like." sits alone on the second line (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Narrow or widen the measure, or rewrite the line, so it breaks as a phrase.
- Feature lists: it's not clear whether Team includes Solo's booking page and email reminders. Add "Everything in Solo, plus:" at the top of the Team and Business lists. **Common sense:** without it the plans look like they swap features rather than add them.

**What's working.**
- The prices are the right size and weight. The figure is big and bold and "/ month" is small and grey, so the three costs read at a glance in one sweep. Keep that split.
- The type is restrained: about four sizes (page heading, price, plan name, body), each clearly different, all in one family. Bringhurst would sign off on it.
- The three cards share one grid, with the same inner margins and the plan name, price and features on the same baselines across all three, so comparing them is easy.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the brand blue mean "act here" and nothing else. It's already the colour of the logo and "Start free". Put it only on the recommended plan's button and its label, so the one saturated colour in the area of the cards points to the decision. **Taste:** on a page this quiet, one accent does all the directing (Refactoring UI, on keeping accent colour scarce).
2. Make the feature lines say what each plan lets you do. "Reports" and "Shared availability" are category names. "See which team member gets booked most" or "Clients pick any free slot across the team" let the viewer measure the plan against their own week. **Taste:** a feature named as an outcome sells the upgrade, and a label doesn't.
