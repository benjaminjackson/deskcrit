**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading at top left. It's clear and well weighted. Then it drops into three centred paragraphs, and the heading's crisp left edge disappears: every body line starts in a different place. The page reads as a short information page for people who use a river ferry. It feels calm and plain, but unfinished, because the column stops about 60% of the way across and the right side of the screen is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how to bring a bike, and where to check for changes, and that they read it before travelling.

**Verdict.** It doesn't quite do its job. The type is readable but the page is laid out on two systems that fight each other. The bigger problem is that the facts a passenger needs to plan a trip are vague and buried in prose: there's no first boat, no last boat and no winter hours.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable facts can't be acted on** (defect, copy). The first and third paragraphs are what a passenger will plan around and later hold the operator to. But "between early morning and late evening", "every forty minutes" with no starting time, and "before dusk" all leave the reader to guess. Someone checking whether they can catch a boat at 7:15 on a December evening can't tell from this page. Dusk moves by hours across the winter, so "before dusk" can be read two ways: one passenger reads it as about 4 pm, another as whenever it gets dark tonight. The wrong reading leaves someone stranded on the quay. Fix: give real times for the first boat, the last boat and the winter cut-off, and the minutes past the hour when boats leave. Put these as short scannable lines or a small table above the prose, so the page answers "when can I go?" before anything else. The bicycle rules can stay as prose.
2. **The body is centred under a flush-left heading** (convention: Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.3). All three paragraphs are four lines long and centred, so each line starts somewhere new, anywhere from about x≈124 to x≈157. The reader has to hunt for the start of every line. Some lines also land a few pixels off the heading's edge at x≈120, so they look like a missed alignment rather than a choice (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on ambiguous placement). Nothing on the page explains the centring. It looks like a default. Fix: set the paragraphs flush left on the same edge as the heading, so the page hangs from one line. This one change also fixes the ragged left edge and the near-misses.
3. **The page tips to the left** (taste, with Arnheim on balance). All the content sits in the left 60% of a 1280 px screen. The right 40% and the bottom third are empty, with no element there to explain the space. It doesn't look like planned open space. It looks like a layout that never got its second column. The line length is good (about 65–70 characters), so keep the column width. Fix it in one of two ways: centre the whole column as a block in the window, or put the timetable from finding 1 in the right-hand space as a second column, so the empty area does a job.

**Smaller things.**
- There's no shared grid: once the body is set flush left, the heading, paragraphs and any timetable should all take their left edge from one column line (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).

**What's working.**
- The heading stands clearly above the text through both size and weight, so there's no doubt where to start (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger). Two sizes, each with an obvious job, is the right type scale for a page this short.
- The body text is set well for reading. It looks about 19 px in a plain sans, on lines of roughly 65–70 characters with generous leading, and the paragraphs are separated by space alone. Keep all of this when you re-align.
- The bicycle paragraph is good copy. It says who does what and when ("Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded"). Use it as the model for rewriting the timetable lines.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: make the next departure the thing that leads. A passenger opens this page to answer one question, so a stronger version puts the departure times in the largest type after the heading and lets the prose explain around them (taste).
2. The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not a promise. Something closer to what the reader wants, such as "Crossing the river by ferry", tells a stranger straight away that this is the page for them (taste).
