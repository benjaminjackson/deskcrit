**First read.** The eye goes straight to the huge black headline and catches on it: its four lines are set so tight that they run into each other. The tails of letters like "y" and "g" cut into the line below, so the headline looks like a pile of words before it reads as a sentence. Then the eye drops to the short paragraph and stops. It reads as the top of a ferry timetable page, plain and confident, set in a sans-serif that looks like Inter. Apart from the headline it looks calm and well made.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the opening of a web page for a local river ferry's spring timetable. It needs to tell riders what the service is and when it runs, quickly.

**Verdict.** It doesn't work yet, and one thing is to blame: the headline's lines overlap, so the biggest, most important text on the page is also the hardest to read. Everything else is sound, and once the spacing is fixed this will be a clean, strong opening.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline lines collide (defect).** In the 4-line heading, the descenders of "Quayside", "daylight", "through" and "early" run into the letters of the line below, and "summer" sits on top of the line above it. The space between lines looks well below the type size, maybe 50 px for type around 80 px. A reader has to pull the lines apart in their head before they can read them. That costs a second on the one line that is supposed to read instantly, and it looks like a bug, not a style choice. Bringhurst (*Elements*, p. 37) allows negative leading in a display line "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide". Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 28) faults exactly this. Fix: open the headline's line spacing until no letter of one line touches a letter of the next. Something near 1.0–1.1 times the type size usually works for a face like this. It can stay tighter than the body text, but it can't overlap.
2. **The headline is too long for its size, and the page never gives a time (defect in the copy).** "Quayside ferry crossings by daylight, spring through early summer" is 9 words at display size, so the page needs 4 lines to say what a 2-line headline could. The paragraph gives the frequency ("every forty minutes") and the trip length ("about twelve minutes"), but never the times a rider needs. What are the first and last sailings, and on what dates does "spring through early summer" start and end? "By daylight" moves with the season, so it isn't a time anyone can plan around. The two readings are "it runs from sunrise to sunset" and "it runs on a fixed daytime schedule", and a rider who guesses wrong misses the last boat. Fix: put the hours and dates in words near the top ("Every 40 minutes, 7:00 to 19:00, 1 March – 30 June"), and shorten the headline so it fits in 2 lines at this size. "Spring timetable" already says when.

**Smaller things.**
- The body paragraph ends with "weather." alone on its last line. Rewrap it or widen the text box a little so the last line holds more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The headline breaks "early / summer", leaving "summer" alone and splitting the phrase. No approved source covers breaks in headings, so this is common sense: break at phrase boundaries. Rewriting the headline (finding 2) will likely fix this too.

**What's working.**
- There's one clear type scale with 3 well-separated levels: a small bold grey "Spring timetable" label, a very large heavy headline, and an easy-to-read paragraph at about 20 px. Each size has an obvious job; keep it.
- Everything hangs from one left edge at about 120 px, which gives the page a quiet structure.
- The paragraph is easy to read: about 68 characters per line, comfortable line spacing, dark text on white.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let one number lead. "Every forty minutes" is the most useful fact on the page for someone deciding whether to walk down to the quay. Taste: setting "40 min" as a large figure next to or under the headline would answer a rider's first question at a glance, without reading the paragraph.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the headline's spacing behaves the same at other screen widths. On a narrower screen the headline will wrap to even more lines, so check that the line-spacing fix holds there.
