I've finished the checklist and the deep reads. Here's the full critique.

**First read.** The eye goes straight to the six colour blocks. The mustard "Glasshouse" tile and the brick-red "Rose arch" tile pull hardest, and the small "Garden show gallery" title is easy to miss. It reads as a tidy, flat, slightly toy-like grid. The catch is that there's nothing to look at in a gallery: every "picture" is the same pale circle on a coloured field, so it feels like a wireframe, not a show.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web gallery that lets visitors browse the areas of a garden show and choose which to visit or click into.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but as a gallery it doesn't do its job because there are no pictures. Six flat colour fields with identical circles show nothing of roses, herbs or a pond. The tiny misalignment under "Rose arch" is the only real craft slip.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures show nothing (defect).** In all six tiles the image area is a flat colour with a centred pale circle. The viewer comes to a gallery to see the garden. Here they can only read the captions, so the visual half of the page carries no information and nothing tempts them to click. The circles are also the same in every tile, so they do no work at all. If these are placeholders or images that failed to load, the fix is simply the real photos. If they're meant to be the final art, replace them with photography or illustration of each area. The colour can stay as a frame or a tint, but not as the subject. Until then, this reads as unfinished.
2. **"Rose arch" caption sits off the line (defect, convention).** In the top-left tile the title and description sit roughly 2–4 px higher than "Herb bed" and "Pond edge", so the title almost touches the image. Across the row the baselines don't line up, and that tile looks like it came from a different build. This is a near-miss, and a near-miss reads as an error, not a choice (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*; Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, on lines aligning across columns; Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*, on repeated elements following one program). Give that caption the same top spacing as the other five. The bottom row is already correct.

**Smaller things.**
- Palette: six unrelated saturated hues (brick, green, blue, ochre, mustard, purple) cover about half the page with no system behind them. Once real photos arrive, drop the colour fields or reduce them to one neutral, and let the photos supply the colour (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*, "Define your shades").
- Pale circles on mid-tone fields have weak figure-ground, especially the mustard and the brown. This goes away with real images.

**What's working.**
- The grid is disciplined. Three equal columns share one gutter, the left edges are shared, and the outer margins are generous. Keep it exactly as it is when the images arrive.
- Grouping is clear. Each caption sits tight under its image, and the large gap between rows keeps the six tiles separate (proximity done right).
- The type has two levels that are easy to tell apart: a bold dark title and a smaller grey description. That's enough, with no extra sizes.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. The descriptions already name specific things, like "Climbing roses over the east gate" and "Tomatoes in late July". Make each photo show exactly that detail, the gate or the July tomatoes, so the picture and the line complete each other and don't just label each other. That turns a list of areas into reasons to visit. (Taste.)
2. "Garden show gallery" is a category label, not a heading. A line that says when or where the show is, or what the visitor can do here, would give the page a reason to exist and a clear place for the eye to start. (Taste.)

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the tiles are clickable, and their hover or focus states. Nothing in the image marks them as links, so check this live if they lead to detail pages.
- Whether the colour fields are images that failed to load. Check in a live browser.
