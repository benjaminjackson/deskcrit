**First read.** The eye goes first to the big serif headline, "Shipments peak each third quarter", then straight to the tallest bar, 705 in Q3 2026. From there it moves left to right along the bars. This reads as a slide from a company board pack: calm, tidy and corporate. The chunky 3D bars make it look like a default office chart, which clashes with the careful type around them.

**What it's for.** Assumption: one page of a board sales review, showing directors how quarterly shipments are trending into the October meeting, including the Q4 forecast.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read, but it hides the two things a board most needs from it. The forecast is drawn exactly like the actuals, and the headline repeats a seasonal pattern the board likely already knows instead of the growth the data shows. The 3D bars make every value a little less exact for no gain.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast looks like a result.** Q4 2026 (560), at the far right, has the same colour, fill and 3D treatment as the seven actual quarters. The only sign that it's a forecast is a bracket at the end of the subtitle and a clause in the source line. A director scanning the chart will read 560 as something that already happened, and the drop from 705 will look like a slump already in the books rather than a projection. Defect, and a published convention: projections should look different from observed data and should show their uncertainty (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Fix: make the bar look different from the actuals, for example as an outline or hatched bar, label it "560 (forecast)", and show the forecast range if there is one.

2. **The headline states the least useful fact on the page.** "Shipments peak each third quarter" is true, but only two Q3s back it up, and seasonality is something the board likely already knows. What the bars show is growth: every 2026 quarter beats the same quarter of 2025, by roughly 11% (Q1), 13% (Q2) and 16% (Q3), with the forecast at about 17% for Q4. As written, the headline lets a director come away thinking "same pattern as last year" when the news is "up on last year every quarter". Defect (copy). Fix: lead with the year-on-year growth in plain words to the board, for example that 2026 is running ahead of 2025 in every quarter, and keep the seasonal peak as the supporting point.

3. **The 3D bars add ink and blur the values.** Each bar has a lighter top cap and a dark side face, and the value labels float above the cap. Readers can't tell whether a bar ends at its front edge or at the back of the cap. That gap is small on this scale, but it is pure noise in a chart that already prints every value. It also makes this page look like a spreadsheet default next to the well-set type. Convention (Wilke, ch. 26.1; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfalls 7 and 11; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*). Fix: use flat bars, so each value is read from one length.

**What's working.**
- The headline is a sentence that makes a claim, not a label like "Shipments by quarter". The change in item 2 is about which claim to make, so keep the habit.
- The chart is fully labelled: units ("Thousands of units"), an axis starting at zero, direct value labels on each bar, light gridlines and a named source. A director never has to guess what a number means.
- The type is calm and clearly ranked: a serif headline, a grey sans-serif subtitle, a bold chart title, and small grey metadata in the header and footer. The left edge at 80 px runs cleanly from the company name down to the source line.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: have the chart carry the year-on-year comparison too. Pair each 2026 quarter with its 2025 quarter, or show 2025 in grey behind 2026 in the existing blue. The growth the headline claims would then be visible at a glance, which is what a board slide is for. Taste.
2. Use the blue to highlight only the bars the headline talks about and set the rest in grey. If the seasonal point stays, that means the two Q3 bars; if growth leads, the 2026 bars. The chart then proves the headline without the reader having to hunt for it (Wilke on highlighting one series and greying the rest). Taste.
