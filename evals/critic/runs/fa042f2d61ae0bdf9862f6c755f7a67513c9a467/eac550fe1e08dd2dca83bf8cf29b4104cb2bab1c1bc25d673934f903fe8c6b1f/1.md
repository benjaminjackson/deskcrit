**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then moves down two even paragraphs of prose and fades out at the small last line. It reads as a plain, calm, practical info page, maybe a web page or a notice. It feels tidy and quiet. Nothing on it says "this matters, act on it", including the one line that does.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a short info page that tells ferry passengers, including cyclists, when the ferry runs and how to board, so they turn up at the right time.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read, but its priorities are backwards. The only line that can save a wasted trip (timetables change in winter) is set in the smallest, quietest type. The facts a passenger plans around (service hours, how often it runs) are buried in prose, and the hours are too vague to plan with.

**What would move it most**
1. **The warning is set as the least important thing on the page.** *Defect.* "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard…" at the bottom looks about 16 px, against body text of about 19 px. That reads as a footnote or a near-miss size, not a clear level (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1 on sizes from a scale; Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: a weak contrast reads as an accident). So the line a winter traveller most needs is the one a skimmer skips, and it comes after the bicycle details. Fix: raise it at least to body size, give it a contrast that is obvious at a glance (weight, or a quiet tint or rule), and move it up next to the schedule it qualifies. Then there are two text sizes plus the heading, and each has a clear job.
2. **The hours can't be planned around.** *Defect (copy).* "Between early morning and late evening" and "every forty minutes" give no first or last sailing and no times when departures fall. A passenger can't tell whether a 6:30 or a 22:00 crossing exists, so they either show up and wait or go and find the noticeboard anyway. Fix: state the first and last departures and the minute past the hour from each landing. Even better, give a small departure table for each side, which you can also scan faster than a paragraph. *Common sense, no catalog rule.*
3. **The key facts are written as prose, so you can't scan them.** *Taste.* Frequency, crossing time, bike cost and the boarding order all sit mid-sentence, at the same weight as everything else. Someone checking the page on the dock wants "every 40 min · 12 min crossing · bikes free". Fix: pull the three or four numbers out into short labelled lines or a compact list, and keep the prose for the how-to (wheel on after foot passengers, lock to the rail).

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- The reading measure and leading are right. Body lines run about 74 characters with comfortable line spacing, so the paragraphs read without effort. Keep that column width.
- There is one typeface, dark text on white, and a heading that clearly leads through weight as well as size. The page is calm and readable with nothing that doesn't need to be there.
- The bicycle paragraph is good copy: it says who does what and in what order ("Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). Write the schedule with the same precision.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: let the "forty minutes" number lead. A large "Every 40 min" with "12 min crossing" beside it answers the passenger's first question before they read a sentence. *Taste.*
2. The empty right side of the column is room for a simple route line, North landing → Market quay. That shows which way and between which two points at a glance and gives the page a recognisable shape of its own. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where people check this on the dock.
