**First read.** The bold headline lands first, then the eye drops straight into three even grey paragraphs and reads down them. There's nothing else on the page. It reads as a short notice for a local river ferry: calm, plain, trustworthy, and as dry as a leaflet pinned in the shelter. Once I'd read it, I still couldn't have told you what time the first or last boat leaves.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or notice that tells passengers how the ferry runs, so they can catch the right boat, bring a bike, and plan around winter hours.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and competent. The words let it down. Every fact a passenger would plan a trip around is either vague or buried in the middle of a sentence. So the page reads nicely but doesn't answer "when can I catch it?"

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on are never given.** "Between early morning and late evening", "every forty minutes", "one boat each hour", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk", "about twelve minutes… a little longer". This is all in paragraphs 1 and 3. A passenger can't turn any of it into a departure. Is "early morning" 6:00 or 7:30? Is "before dusk" 15:45 or 16:30 in December? Which months count as "winter"? Do boats leave on the hour or at :20? Two passengers will read these differently, and the one who reads them generously misses the last boat home. Then the page sends them to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing", so the information they need is only available once they're standing at the landing. **Defect** (the copy carries a schedule the viewer will hold the operator to, and it can be read more than one way). Fix: give the first and last departure times, the minutes past the hour, and the winter dates. If times change by season, say so plainly and link to the current timetable rather than only pointing to the shelter.
2. **The facts are written as prose, so nobody can scan them.** Frequency, crossing time, bike rules, and winter changes are four separate things. Here they're woven into flowing sentences under one generic heading, "Notes for ferry passengers". Someone checking on a phone at the quay has to read every word to find the one number they came for. The paragraphs are short, so this isn't a wall of text. But each paragraph holds a different topic with no label, and the numbers (forty, twelve) are spelled out and set in the same weight as the words around them. **Taste**, argued on what a scanning reader needs (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web", says people skim). Fix: give each paragraph a short subhead that names what it covers ("Timetable", "Bicycles", "Winter service"). Put the key figures first in each, as numerals.

**What's working.**
- The type is properly set for reading. Body text is a comfortable size in a plain sans. Lines run about 65–72 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Leading is generous, and paragraphs are separated by space alone. Keep this setting when you add structure.
- The hierarchy is clear. There are two sizes, and the headline differs from the text in both size and weight (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger), so the eye never hesitates over where to start.
- The bicycle paragraph is good copy. It says who does what, in what order: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail". That's the standard the other two paragraphs should meet.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let one number lead: the time of the last boat. For a ferry passenger, the risk that matters is being stranded on the wrong bank. Setting "Last crossing: 18:40 (winter 16:00)" at headline weight under the title would make the page earn its place. **Taste.**
2. The right 40% of the frame is empty. It could hold a small timetable table in the same face with tabular figures: north landing and market quay as columns, departures as rows. The prose would explain and the table would answer, and the reading column would stay as it is. **Taste**: Tufte's case for a table when the reader needs to look up exact values.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where this page will most likely be read at the landing.
