I've reviewed the page, and it needs work. The typesetting is fine; the problem is that it never gives a passenger an actual departure time.

**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then down into three paragraphs of grey-black prose. It reads as a plain, calm information page, close to an unstyled article template. Nothing in the body pulls the eye. You have to read it top to bottom to find out anything, and the right half and lower third of the frame are empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and the rules for bicycles. The likely reader is someone deciding whether, and when, to catch it.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read, but the page doesn't do its job well. The facts a passenger acts on are buried in prose and left vague, and the page never gives a departure time.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule has no times in it, so a passenger can't plan from this page.** This is about the copy. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "In winter" each leave the reader to guess. Is the first boat at 6:00 or 7:30? Is "before dusk" 16:00 or 16:45 in December? Which months count as winter? Departure times and the last boat are exactly what people act on and later hold the operator to. Read one way, "before dusk" means "plan to be at the quay by mid-afternoon". Read another, it means "there's a boat until around sunset". The wrong reading leaves someone stranded on the far bank. The third paragraph then sends the reader to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which is the opposite of what a page like this is for. *Defect.* Fix: give the first and last departure for each direction and season, with real clock times and the months winter covers. If the timetable changes weekly, say where online the current one lives, not only the noticeboard.

2. **The key facts are buried in three paragraphs of the same weight.** Frequency (40 min, hourly in winter), crossing time (about 12 min), bikes free and the last boat are all set at body size in running sentences. A passenger who wants one fact, like "can I bring my bike?" or "when's the last boat?", has to read everything to find it. The heading is the only point of emphasis on the page, and it carries no information. *Defect* for this job: it slows down the reader the page exists for. Fix: put the schedule first, as a few labelled facts or a small table (Every 40 min · 12 min crossing · First / last boat · Winter: hourly, last boat [time]). Then give the bicycle rules their own short block with a label, so each topic can be found at a glance.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing. The type, spacing and alignment hold up.

**What's working.**
- The body setting is comfortable. The text looks about 18–19 px with generous line spacing, and the lines run roughly 65–70 characters (Bringhurst's range), so reading the whole thing is easy. Keep that size and measure when you restructure.
- The heading is clearly set apart from the body by size and weight, with only two levels, so the page never feels muddled. Keep the scale this simple when you add labels: use weight, not a third or fourth size.
- The plain, quiet tone suits a public-service notice. Nothing here oversells.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let one number lead: "every 40 minutes" is already in the text and is the reason you can just turn up without a timetable. Set it large under the heading as the page's anchor, with crossing time and last boat as two smaller facts beside it. A passenger gets the answer in one glance. *Taste.*
2. Use the empty right column for the timetable itself. On a 1280 px screen there's a whole column of unused space next to the prose, which is the natural place for a compact times table while the notes sit to its left. That turns the page from a description of the service into something you can plan with (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*: let the columns carry different kinds of content). *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where people standing at the landing will read it.
