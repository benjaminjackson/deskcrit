**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three even paragraphs of grey-black text in a column on the left. The right 40% of the frame and the bottom third are empty. It reads as a calm, plain information page for a small local ferry, maybe a town or parish website. It feels quiet and trustworthy, a little bare.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells someone planning a crossing when the boat runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bicycle. Correct me if it's for something else.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and competent, and the page has no layout defect worth ranking. What stops it doing its job is the copy. It never gives a single time a passenger could act on, so a reader leaves knowing roughly how the ferry works but not when to show up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule has no times in it** (defect, copy). The first paragraph says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". The third says "one boat each hour" in winter and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". The reader came for a departure time and the page has none. They can't tell whether the first boat is at 6:00 or 8:30, which minute past the hour the forty-minute cycle starts from, when "winter" begins, or whether "before dusk" means 15:45 or 16:30. The last-boat time matters most: get it wrong and someone is stranded on the wrong bank. The page also sends people to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing", which means walking to the landing to find out when to walk to the landing. Fix: put the real times in the text. That means first and last departures from each side, the minutes past the hour, and the winter dates and last sailing. A short timetable just under the title would do it, with this prose underneath as background. This is the one change that turns the page from a description into something a passenger can use.
2. **The facts aren't in the order a passenger needs them, and nothing labels them** (taste, close to defect for this job). Each paragraph covers one topic (regular service, bicycles, winter), which is sensible. But the winter rule, which overrides the first paragraph for months of the year, comes last, after the bicycle rules. A reader in December who stops after paragraph one plans around a forty-minute service that isn't running. The paragraphs are short enough that this isn't the "long unbroken block" Nielsen warns about. It's an ordering problem. Fix: put the seasonal exception next to the schedule it changes. A plain bold run-in or a small heading per topic ("Times", "Bicycles") would let a cyclist go straight to their paragraph.

**Smaller things.** None from the checklist.

**What's working.**
- The text is set properly for reading. It's a plain sans in near-black on white, the body looks about 18–19 px, lines run about 60–72 characters, leading is comfortable, and the right edge is ragged. Keep all of this when you add times and headings.
- The hierarchy is simple and right: one bold title clearly above one body size, with no extra sizes or decoration. Two levels are enough for a page this short, and adding a small heading per topic would make three, which still holds.
- The prose voice is calm and concrete in its details, like "lock them to the rail along the side of the deck" and "the crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle". It sounds like a person who runs the boat, and that's worth keeping alongside the hard numbers.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the last-crossing time lead. The cost of missing the last boat is what passengers remember and hold the operator to. If that time sits in the heaviest weight on the page, directly under the title, it does more for the passenger than anything else here. Taste.
2. Use the empty right column. The right 40% of the frame is blank, and it's a natural place for the timetable or a small map of the north landing and the market quay, next to the prose instead of below it. Then the column of prose explains and the side panel answers "when?" at a glance (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*: give different kinds of information their own field on one grid). Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where most people will check ferry times on the quay. Check that any timetable you add stays readable without sideways scrolling.
