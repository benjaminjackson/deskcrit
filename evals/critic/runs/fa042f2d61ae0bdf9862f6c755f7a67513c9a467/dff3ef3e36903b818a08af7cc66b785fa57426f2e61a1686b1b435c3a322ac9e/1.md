**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Changing your pickup time" and then moves down the four bold headings. Within a couple of seconds it reads as the help section of a bakery's pre-order site. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. Then the eye hits a fifth line at the bottom, "Design review: failed, the headings are impossible to pick out." It doesn't belong to the page, and it makes the page look unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a customer help/FAQ block for a bakery's order-ahead service, read on desktop by someone who has already ordered or is about to.

**Verdict.** The structure works: the headings are easy to pick out, so that review line's claim is wrong. What lets the piece down is the words. A leftover internal note is showing on the live page, and the two lines about commitments (the cancellation deadline and what the collector must show) can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **Remove the stray review note** (y≈565). Customers see an internal design comment on a public page. It is set in body style after a slightly larger gap, so it reads like a fifth answer whose heading is missing, and "failed" makes the whole service look broken. Delete it. *Defect* (common sense; no approved source covers leftover copy).
2. **Make the cancellation deadline and the collection rule exact.** Customers act on these lines and will later hold the bakery to them.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" has two readings: 24 hours before the pickup slot, or by the end of the day before. A customer who cancels at 9 pm the night before an 8 am pickup will believe they were on time and the bakery won't. Name the cutoff: "until 6 pm the day before your pickup" or "up to 24 hours before your pickup time."
   - "along with a name" doesn't say whose name. Is it the collector's or the person who ordered? Is it a spoken name or photo ID? Whoever turns up at the counter with the wrong one gets turned away. Say "your name (the name on the order)" or whatever the real rule is.
   - Lesser, same family: "Every loaf has a card by the till" only helps someone already in the shop. A customer ordering online with an allergy can't check before they pay. Either point to where ingredients appear online or say so plainly. Also, "a new slot from the list" doesn't say how late a change can be made.
   *Defect*: each gap leaves the viewer to fill in the meaning, and they may fill it in differently from the bakery.
3. **Narrow the text column.** The body lines run about 95–100 characters ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…"), well past Bringhurst's 45–75 (ideal ~66, *Elements of Typographic Style* §2.1.2). The answers are short, so the cost is small, but on long one-line answers the eye has to travel the full width and the page reads less like a quick scan. Cap the measure at around 60–70 characters, about 620–680 px at this size. *Convention*, with a mild cost here.

**Smaller things.**
- There is no page title above the four headings. That's fine inside a larger page, but on its own nothing says whose bakery this is or that this is "Ordering help." Add a title one clear step above the headings.
- "anything." sits alone as the last line of the allergies answer (y≈384), a runt (Bringhurst §2.4.2). Narrowing the measure will likely fix it; otherwise rewrite the line.

**What's working.**
- The heading/body contrast is right. Bold at roughly 30 px against regular at roughly 18 px differs in both size and weight, so each topic can be found at a glance. Keep this two-level scale; it doesn't need a third size.
- Spacing groups the content correctly. Each heading sits tight above its answer and well clear of the answer before it, so the four Q&A pairs read as four units.
- The voice is plain and human ("the bread has already been baked for you"). It explains the rule instead of just stating it.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let each answer lead with the one fact a customer holds you to, such as the cutoff time, the two-hour hold or the order number. Put it first in the sentence (or bold it) so a scanning reader gets it without reading the whole answer. *Taste.* On a help page people skim for the number, and Krug's *Don't Make Me Think* argues for pages built for that kind of scanning.
2. Order the four topics by how urgent they are. "Cancelling an order" and "Allergies and ingredients" carry the most risk (money and health), but they sit third and fourth. *Taste.* On a help block, the order of the headings is the hierarchy.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this block sits under a page title or navigation that names the bakery, and how the column behaves on a phone.
