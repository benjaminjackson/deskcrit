**First read.** The eye lands straight on "Learn to swim". It is huge, black and bold, with nothing near it competing. Next it drops to the grey line under it, then across the two small blocks ("Join the lanes", "Family hour"), and stops on a grey sentence addressed "to the reviewer" that plainly isn't meant for a swimmer. It reads as the top of a local pool or swim-school page: calm, clean, a little bare. Then it just ends, with nothing to do.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing view for a pool's adult swim lessons, meant to get a nervous adult to sign up, with the other two programmes as secondary offers.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy at the top works, but the page doesn't do its job. It makes an offer and gives the viewer no way to take it up. It also still has a leftover internal note in its own body text. Those two things, not the styling, are what stand in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **There is nothing to act on.** This is a defect, and there is no checklist line for it. A reader who decides "yes, Tuesdays, a coach beside me, that's what I need" has nowhere to go. There's no button, no "Book a first lesson", no price, no time, no place, no number to call. The eye goes headline → offer → two side offers → empty bottom third (y≈540–800) and stops. The whole page builds towards a decision and then drops it.
   - **Fix:** put one specific ask straight under the subhead, for example "Book your first Tuesday class", with the time and the price beside it. Then the ask is the second thing the eye meets, not something it has to look for.
   - A stronger ask would also let the viewer judge the value against something they already trust. For example: "Your first class is free: if you can't float by the end, you don't pay." That beats an ask that only asks them to believe the claim.
2. **There's an unfinished internal note in the page copy.** "Note to the reviewer: no element leads this page, so answer fail." (y≈516) is a defect, and it's common sense, not a cited rule. It's set at the same size and grey as the subhead (~22 px), so a visitor reads it as part of the offer and is confused by it. Treat it as text on the page, not as a judgement of the design. What it says is also false: "Learn to swim" leads clearly. Delete it before anything ships.
3. **The levels under the headline are too close in size.** This breaks a convention. I count about five sizes:
   - headline (~90 px)
   - subhead (~22 px)
   - card headings (~20 px, regular weight)
   - note (~22 px)
   - card body (~16 px)

   The subhead and the card headings are only a couple of pixels apart. "Join the lanes" and "Family hour" look like the subhead's little siblings rather than labels for separate offers, and the subhead doesn't feel clearly above them. Bringhurst (*Elements*, 3.1.1) says sizes come from "a modest set of distinct and related intervals", and Müller-Brockmann and Lupton both call for levels you can tell apart at a glance.
   - **Fix:** keep three sizes under the headline: subhead, card heading, body. Make the steps clearly different, or separate the card headings by weight (semibold) rather than another near-identical size.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** It truly dominates by scale and by darkness, sits tight-tracked and flush left on the 96 px edge, and leaves no doubt where to start. Keep that contrast in scale when you add the ask.
- **The subhead copy.** "Small adult classes on Tuesday evenings, with a coach in the water beside you" says who, when, and what's different, and ends on the reader ("beside you"). That's exactly what a nervous adult needs to hear. Don't cut it shorter.
- **The text left edges.** All the text shares one left edge at x≈96, and the two side offers sit on one baseline. That gives the page a quiet, orderly feel that suits a reassuring offer.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 1.** Let one fact lead under the headline, for example the class size ("Six swimmers, one coach"), set in the same heavy weight as "Learn to swim". The thing that makes these lessons different is that they're small. Putting that as a number the eye can grab turns a pleasant promise into a reason to choose this pool. This is taste.
2. Give the page one colour of its own. Right now it is all black and grey, and nothing says water. One pool-blue on the ask and the card headings would warm the page and mark what's clickable without adding clutter. Albers (*Interaction of Color*) argues that one colour's effect depends on the restraint around it, and this page already has that restraint. This is taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the page continues below the fold with a booking section. If it does, finding 1 shrinks, but the ask still belongs in the first screen.
