**First read.** My eye lands on the dense black brush script that fills the left half, and only then goes up to the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers". It reads like an information page for a local river ferry, with times, bicycles and winter hours. But the script makes it feel like a café chalkboard or a wedding card, so plain practical facts take effort to read.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page of practical information for ferry passengers. Readers need to pick out the crossing times, the bicycle rules and the winter changes quickly and correctly.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The page is clear and well spaced, but the running text is set in a heavy brush script, which makes simple facts slow and tiring to read. On a page whose whole purpose is to be read, that is the one thing that matters.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are set in a script typeface** (defect). All three paragraphs use a heavy, joined brush script. The words run together, the letter shapes are irregular, and the thick strokes fill in. Readers have to decode words like "minutes", "timetable" and "landing" instead of recognising them at a glance. That costs most for the people who most need this page: older passengers, people with dyslexia or low vision, and anyone reading on a phone at the landing. The facts they will act on ("every forty minutes", "one boat each hour", "before dusk") are the hardest parts to scan. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1) says text faces should not carry extra ornament. Fix: set the paragraphs in a face made for reading. The simplest choice is the sans already used for the heading, at regular weight. Keep script, if you want it at all, for a few words of display.
2. **The two typefaces clash, and the script sets the wrong mood** (convention and taste). The heading is a sturdy, neutral sans that says "information". The body is a casual brush script that says "invitation". Dair (*Design with Type*) calls this conflict: two faces that belong to different worlds, with no clear reason they are together. The reader gets mixed signals about whether this is official guidance or decoration. Fixing finding 1 with one family solves this too. If the page needs character, get it from somewhere that doesn't touch reading, such as a small illustration or a coloured heading.

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- The heading is one line, bold, clearly larger, and sits near its text. It is plainly the place to start, so keep it.
- The structure is right. There are three short paragraphs, one topic each (schedule, bicycles, winter), divided by space alone. Lines run about 74 characters, and the column has a generous left margin. Once the face changes, the page will read easily.
- The copy is good. It is concrete and specific ("lock them to the rail along the side of the deck", "printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing"), and it reads like a person explaining things.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Pull the numbers out of the "every forty minutes" and "one boat each hour" paragraphs. Make the schedule a two-row summary near the top (summer every 40 min, winter hourly, last boat before dusk), so passengers checking times on a phone find them without reading prose (taste). Tufte would point out that a small table beats prose for looking up exact values.
2. Give each paragraph a short bold label ("Timetable", "Bicycles", "Winter"). Passengers would then go straight to the one question they came with (taste).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see how the column reflows on a phone, which is where people standing at the landing will likely read it.
