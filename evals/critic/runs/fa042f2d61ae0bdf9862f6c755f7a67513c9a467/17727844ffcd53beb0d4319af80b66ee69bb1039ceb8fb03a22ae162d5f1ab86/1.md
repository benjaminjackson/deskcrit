**First read.** The eye lands on "How can we help?" in the navy band, drops to the search field, then goes straight to the yellow-and-blue credit card and the green shield in the card grid. Those two are the only full-colour objects on the page, so they pull the eye before the six other topics do. It reads as a calm, competent SaaS help-centre home page. It is tidy and trustworthy, and it is also generic.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing page of a product's help centre. Customers who are stuck either search or pick a topic and get to an answer quickly.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The structure is sound and the search is clearly the main way in. The one real fault is that two topic icons come from a different icon set. They give Billing and Privacy a priority they don't have, and they break an otherwise consistent system. It's competent, but nothing about it is specific to Harbor.

**What would move it most**
1. **Two icons break the set and pull the eye to the wrong topics.** Defect plus convention. Six cards use thin navy line icons (bolt, key, box, plug, bell, lifebuoy). "Billing and invoices" has a filled, multicolour credit card with an orange chip, and "Privacy and security" has a glossy green gradient shield with a check mark. In a grid where every card has equal rank, these two are the only saturated objects. The eye jumps between them diagonally and skips "Getting started" and "Troubleshooting", which are probably what most visitors come for. They also make the page look assembled from two icon packs instead of designed as one thing (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind follow one program). The green check-shield also reads a little like a status badge ("secure / OK") rather than a topic marker. Fix: redraw both in the same navy line style, with the same stroke weight and no fill or gradient. If a topic really should stand out (say, Getting started for new users), do it on purpose with position or one accent, not because of where the icon came from.

**Smaller things.**
- Card descriptions: five of the eight end on a short orphaned line ("your team.", "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). That makes the row look ragged (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Fix it by rewording to fit, or with a slightly wider measure or `text-wrap: pretty`.

**What's working.**
- **Clear order of importance.** The serif display headline on the navy band, then one wide white search field, then "Browse by topic" give an obvious path: search first, browse second. Keep the serif for the headline only. Its contrast with the sans body is what separates the levels.
- **The grid holds.** Four equal columns with equal gutters, card text aligned to the header margin, matching padding, and baselines that line up across each row. It scans in seconds.
- **The card copy is concrete.** "Track a delivery or start a return" and "Fix sync problems and error messages" describe tasks in the customer's own words, not category labels.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Taste. The blank space under the grid and the empty band beside the search field could hold the three or four most-read articles ("Reset your password", "Download an invoice"). Most help-centre visitors have one of a few common problems, and one click straight to the answer beats search or browse.
2. Taste. "How can we help?" and "Browse a topic or search the guides" could sit on any help centre. A line in Harbor's own voice, or a search placeholder with a real example ("Try 'change billing email'"), would show the viewer what to type and that the product knows them.
3. Taste. The navy is already the brand's only colour. Commit to it: icons, card titles on hover, and the logo all in that navy, with no second hue anywhere. That would make the restraint read as a deliberate identity rather than a default.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The focus ring on the search field and cards, and whether the search field gets an accessible label (it has only a placeholder; the visible headline works as its label on screen, but check that it is wired up as one).
