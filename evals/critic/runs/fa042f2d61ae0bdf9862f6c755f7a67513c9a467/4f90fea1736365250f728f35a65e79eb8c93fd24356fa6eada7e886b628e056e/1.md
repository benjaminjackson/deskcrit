**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and the three short lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. Then it hits the last line, "Design review: failed, everything on this page is centred". That line is set in body size, has nothing to do with a ferry, and isn't even true, because every block on the page is flush left. Overall it reads as a calm, clean, generic landing hero for a small ferry line, with all its weight in the left 60% and the right side empty white.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a ferry operator's website for day-trippers and locals. Its job is to make them want the crossing and send them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The headline and button are sound, but it isn't ready to ship. A stray review note sits in the page as if it were copy, and the page holds back the three facts a ferry passenger actually needs: when, where and how much.

**What would move it most**
1. **A line that isn't copy sits under the call to action.** "Design review: failed, everything on this page is centred" at the bottom is the same size and colour as the body text, so a visitor reads it as part of the page. It makes the company look broken right after the one action you want them to take, and it describes the layout wrongly. Remove it. I'm treating it as content, not as a judgement of the design. *Defect (common sense: unfinished copy, an element with no job).*
2. **The page makes the passenger go elsewhere for the facts that decide the trip.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no first or last sailing. "The north landing" assumes the visitor already knows where that is. No fare is shown, yet "Bicycles travel free" suggests people do pay. A tourist can't tell whether they could catch a boat in the next hour without clicking through. Put the real first and last times, the landing's location and the adult fare on this page, for example as the three short lines. *Defect (Nielsen 6, recognition rather than recall).*
3. **The right 40% of the frame is empty, so the page looks unfinished.** The whole column hangs off the left at x≈160 and ends around x≈790, with nothing on the right to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). It reads like a hero image that didn't load or was never placed. A ferry page is the easiest case for a picture: the harbour from the open deck that the paragraph describes. If an image is meant to go there, check that it loads. If not, either add one or widen and recentre the composition so the space looks chosen. *Defect if the image failed to load, convention otherwise.*

**Smaller things.**
- The three short lines ("Bicycles travel free" and the rest) have no markers and are spaced almost like the paragraph's lines, so they read as loose leftover sentences rather than a list of perks. Tighten their spacing, add a gap above, or give them markers (Arnheim on grouping by proximity).
- Those lines look about 17 px against a body of about 19 px. That's two nearly identical sizes for text with no different role. Use one size and set the list apart by spacing or weight (Bringhurst, 3.1.1).

**What's working.**
- The headline is concrete and speaks to the passenger. "Twelve calm minutes" gives a number and a feeling, and nobody else's page could use it. Keep the number.
- The call to action names one exact thing, the timetable, which is what a ferry visitor wants next. It is the only blue block on the page, so it can't be missed.
- The type is tidy. There's one family, the heading is set tight with clear weight contrast, the paragraph runs about 70 characters per line with comfortable leading, and the left edge is clean.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: let one picture of the crossing set the mood the headline promises. Blue water from the open deck makes the word "calm" believable in a way white space can't. *Taste.*
2. Let "twelve minutes" lead as a number. Pair it with the next departure ("Next boat 14:20") so the headline's claim becomes something the visitor can act on right now. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover and focus states, and how the column reflows on a phone.
