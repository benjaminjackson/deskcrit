Checks are done; here is the critique.

**First read.** The eye lands on "Planning a vegetable bed" in the top left, then moves down three subheads that are evenly spaced. It reads as a short, calm how-to article for a first-time gardener. It feels clean and quiet, and a bit bare: it's all black and grey on white, with nothing that says "garden" except the words.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a beginner's guide, on a web page, that someone reads once from top to bottom and follows step by step.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The structure is clear, the copy is good and the page is easy to read. The biggest thing in the way is the text column. It is too wide for comfortable reading, and the subheads are only bigger, not different, so the steps don't stand out as much as they could.

**What would move it most**
1. **The text lines are too long.** Defect / convention. The paragraphs run about 90 characters per line ("Dig to the depth of a spade and mix in compost from last year's heap. Rake the surface level"). That is well past the 45 to 75 range Bringhurst gives, with about 66 as the ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). On a long line the eye has to travel back a long way to find the next one, so a reader may lose their place. That matters more here because a gardener may be reading with dirty hands and only glancing at the page. Narrow the text column to about 65 characters, which is roughly 600 px at this body size. There is already plenty of empty space on the right, so nothing else has to move.
2. **The subheads differ from the text only in size.** Convention. "Preparing the soil", "Choosing what to plant" and "Watering" are set in the same weight and the same near-black as the body text, just larger. The title works the same way. Dair (*Design with Type*, ch. 8–9) says headings should change more than size. When size is the only change, the steps blur together as you scroll: the page reads as one even run of text rather than a set of steps you can find again. Make the steps smaller than they are now and change one more thing as well: a semibold weight, or a single colour (see "Where it could go further"). The structure will then show at a glance without the headings needing to get bigger.

**Smaller things.**
- Title: "Planning a vegetable bed" looks like it uses the same letter spacing as the body text, so at about 54 px it looks slightly loose. Tighten it a little (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 80; Bringhurst 2.1.1).
- The "Preparing the soil" paragraph uses a straight apostrophe in "year's". Use a curly one (’). No checked source covers curly quotes; this is common sense for finished text.

**What's working.**
- The spacing groups things correctly. Each subhead sits about 8 px above its paragraph and roughly 30 px below the paragraph before it, so every heading clearly belongs to the text it introduces. Keep this when you change the headings.
- The copy is concrete and specific: "six hours of sun", "the depth of a spade", "radishes, lettuce, and bush beans", "twice a week, rather than a little every day". Every line tells the reader exactly what to do, with numbers and a point of comparison. That is the best thing about the piece.
- The size steps are clear: about 54 px for the title, 32 for subheads and 18 for body text. Three levels with big gaps between them, and no two sizes that nearly collide.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: number the steps. "Preparing the soil", "Choosing what to plant" and "Watering" are steps done in order, and the first paragraph (choosing a spot) is really step one without a heading. Numbering them (1 Choose a spot, 2 Prepare the soil…) turns an article into a sequence the reader can follow and come back to. Taste.
2. Add one colour. The page has no hue at all, which feels clinical for a garden guide. One muted earth or leaf green, used only on the step headings or numbers, would make the page feel like it is about growing things and would give the headings the second difference finding 2 asks for. Taste.
