**First read.** The eye goes straight to the black bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers". Then it hits a wall of pale grey. The body copy is so light that it reads as secondary text, like a caption or a disabled state, not as the content you came for. This reads as a plain, calm information page for a small river ferry. It looks tidy but washed out, as if the page were half-loaded.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling passengers how often the ferry runs, how long the crossing takes, how bicycles work, and that winter times change.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound: a clear heading, a sensible column, short paragraphs. But everything the passenger came to read is set in pale grey that looks well under the legal contrast minimum. The one warning that could leave someone stranded is the smallest, faintest line on the page. Fix the text colour and the piece mostly works.

**What would move it most**
1. **All the body text looks too faint to read comfortably.** *Defect.*
   - **Where:** Both paragraphs and the winter note.
   - **What's wrong:** They are set in a light grey, by eye around #9CA3AF on white. That looks like roughly 2.5:1 contrast, against the 4.5:1 that WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 asks for normal-size text. Measure the actual colour to confirm.
   - **Who it hurts:** Anyone with low vision, anyone on a phone in sunlight on a landing stage, and older passengers. They have to strain for the only information on the page.
   - **What else it costs:** It also inverts the meaning. Grey says "less important", but this text is the whole point of the page.
   - **Fix:** Darken the body text until it clears 4.5:1. A near-black or dark slate close to the heading colour works. Let size and weight separate heading from body, not paleness.
2. **The most consequential line is styled as the least important.** *Taste, argued.*
   - **Where:** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." It is the smallest text on the page and just as pale as the body.
   - **Why it matters:** It is the only line that can actually cost a passenger something: a missed or non-existent crossing. Set as a footnote, it is the line most likely to be skipped.
   - **Fix:** Bring it up to body size and full text colour at minimum. Better, give it some mark of its own (bold lead-in, a rule, a placement nearer the top) so it reads as a warning, not small print.
3. **The copy leaves the passenger without the facts they act on.** *Defect, copy.*
   - **Where:** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening." There is no first or last sailing time and no departure minute.
   - **Why it matters:** A passenger can't plan a crossing from this. "Early morning" and "late evening" will be read differently by different people, and someone who guesses wrong about the last boat is stuck on the wrong bank.
   - **Fix:** Give the real times, e.g. "first boat 6:20, last boat 22:20, then every 40 minutes". The "twelve minutes in calm weather" line also leaves the passenger to work out what bad weather means for them. Say whether crossings slow down or get cancelled.
   - The winter note has the same gap. "Change" doesn't say from when, or whether sailings get fewer or stop. Say the months, and that sailings are less frequent (or whatever is true).

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- **The heading:** heavy, dark, and about twice the body size. There is no doubt where to start, and it says plainly what the page is.
- **The text column:** about 74 characters a line, flush left, with comfortable leading and even paragraph spacing. It is easy to read once the colour is fixed. Keep the measure and the spacing.
- **The restraint:** one typeface, no ornaments, nothing competing. That suits a practical notice.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: once the real times are in, set them as a small two-column schedule or a short list of facts (route, frequency, first/last boat, crossing time, bikes). A passenger checking from the landing can then find the next boat in a glance rather than reading prose. *Taste.* Tufte and Few both favour a table when the reader needs exact values.
2. Pull "every 40 minutes" out of the first sentence and let it lead, larger than the body, directly under the heading. Frequency is the first thing a ferry passenger wants to know, and the page currently hides it mid-sentence. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Narrow screens:** whether the column reflows cleanly on a phone, which is likely where passengers read this.
- **Exact colour:** the real text colour value. Measure it to confirm the contrast ratio.
