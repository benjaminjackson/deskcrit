**First read.** I look first at "Planning a vegetable bed", then down the left edge through the three headings. It reads as a plain, calm how-to page for a beginner gardener. My eye ends on the last line, "Design review: approved, a model of clear heading styles", and stalls there because that line isn't about gardening at all.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short web article that walks a first-time gardener through setting up a vegetable bed, to be skimmed and then followed.

**Verdict.** The guide part works: there's a clear title, three well-named sections, and spacing that groups each heading with its text. The biggest problem is the stray review line at the bottom, which doesn't belong on the page. After that, the headings rely on size alone, so they don't stand out when someone skims.

**What would move it most**
1. **A line that isn't part of the guide.** "Design review: approved, a model of clear heading styles" at y≈613 is set in body type at the content's left edge, so it reads as the guide's last paragraph. It looks like an internal note that was left in. A gardener reads it, gets nothing from it, and starts to doubt the page was finished. It's also the last thing on the page, so the guide ends on it instead of on the watering advice. This is a defect, and common sense rather than a sourced rule: anything that doesn't help the reader's task should go (Nielsen, heuristic 8, aesthetic and minimalist design). Remove it. If the page needs an ending, close with a next step for the gardener. I treated the line as page content, not as a judgement of the design.
2. **The headings differ from the text only in size.** "Preparing the soil", "Choosing what to plant" and "Watering" use the same weight and the same near-black as the body. Only the jump from about 18 to 32 px sets them apart. That gap is big enough to see, but at a skim the headings and paragraphs blur into one even grey, and skimming is how a gardener with dirty hands will look for "Watering". This is a convention (Dair, *Design with Type*, chapters 8a–8g and 9). Add a second difference, usually a semibold or bold heading weight. That lets you shrink the size jump a little, which also tightens the type scale.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run about 85–90 characters ("Choose a spot … Mark the corners with"), above Bringhurst's 45–75. The paragraphs are only two lines, so the cost is small today, but it will grow with longer sections. Narrow the column to about 66 characters, roughly 640 px at this size.
- The 52 px title looks set with the body's default letter spacing and reads slightly loose. Tighten it a little. This is borderline: Lupton backs adjusting large type but warns against tightening too much.

**What's working.**
- Spacing does the grouping. Each heading sits about 4 px above its own paragraph and about 30 px below the one before, so every section reads as a unit without rules or boxes. Keep those proportions when you change the heading weight.
- The headings and copy say what to do in plain words. "Dig to the depth of a spade", "six hours of sun" and "twice a week" are concrete, measurable instructions a beginner can follow without having to work anything out.
- There are three clear type levels (about 52 / 32 / 18) with big steps between them, all in one sans serif and one near-black, and no extra sizes.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Taste: let the numbers lead. The page is already built on concrete quantities: six hours of sun, a spade's depth, a week to settle, twice a week. For a reader who will glance back mid-task, setting each key quantity in the heading's heavier weight gives them something to find at a glance, and it puts the bold you add for finding 2 to a second use.
2. Taste: give the guide a real ending. "Watering" stops on one line. A closing step such as "what to sow first this weekend" would turn a reference page into something the reader acts on, which is the point of a how-to.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where this page is likely to be read outdoors.
