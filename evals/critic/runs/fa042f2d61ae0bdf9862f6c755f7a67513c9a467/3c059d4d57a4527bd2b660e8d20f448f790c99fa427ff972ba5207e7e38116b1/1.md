**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Plan the week with your whole team", jumps right to the white card, and stops on the blue "Create account" button. It reads as a standard SaaS sign-up page for a team planning tool called Tallyboard. It feels clean, calm and competent, and also anonymous: nothing on the page shows what planning in Tallyboard looks like.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the desktop sign-up screen for a team planning product, and its job is to get a first-time visitor to fill in the form and start a free workspace.

**Verdict.** The layout and hierarchy are sound, but the form makes people guess. No field has a label, so once someone starts typing, every prompt disappears, including the only statement of the password rule. That, plus a vague line about price, is what stands between this page and a confident sign-up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The fields have no labels; the placeholders are doing that job.** This affects all five inputs in the card. "Enter first name", "name@company.com", "At least 10 characters" and "Company name" vanish on the first keystroke. A user who gets distracted or goes back to check a field sees only their own text. The worst case is the fourth field: nothing ever says it is a password. Its only hint, "At least 10 characters", disappears just when the user needs it, while they are typing. This is a defect and breaks a well-established rule: labels stay visible above the field and the placeholder is never the only label (NN/g, "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful"; Nielsen heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Fix: put a short persistent label above each field (First name, Last name, Work email, Password, Company). Move "At least 10 characters" into helper text under the password field so it stays visible while the user types.
2. **The price line can be read two ways.** In the subhead, "Start a free workspace for up to five people. You can invite the rest of the team later." One reading is that the free plan covers five people and adding the rest will cost money. The other is that inviting the rest later is also free. Price is the thing people later hold you to. A visitor who assumes the second reading and hits a paywall at person six feels misled. This is a defect in the copy. Fix: say outright what happens after five people, e.g. "Free for up to five people. Add more on a paid plan whenever you're ready." Or, if it really is free, say that.
3. **The input borders look too faint.** The pale grey-blue outlines on the white card look well under 3:1, so a viewer with low vision or a washed-out screen sees the fields as vague shapes. This breaks a convention, WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.11 (non-text contrast). It costs less here because the placeholders hint where the fields are, but that support goes away once you fix finding 1. Measure the border colour against #fff. If it is under 3:1, darken it until it passes.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Sign in" link at the bottom of the card has the same grey and weight as "Already have an account?", so it does not look clickable. Underline it or give it the brand blue (WCAG technique G183).
- Required and optional fields are not marked. If Company name, for example, is optional, write "(optional)" after its label so people don't stop to wonder (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- There is no line about terms or privacy near the button. Common sense: a sign-up that takes a work email usually says what the user is agreeing to.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear. The headline is the one big element, the card heading is a clear second level, the body text is a calm third, and there are only about four type sizes, each with an obvious job. Keep that scale when you add labels: make labels small and medium weight, not a new in-between size.
- The colour is restrained. One blue is used only for the wordmark and the primary button, so the eye's last stop is the action. Don't add a second hue.
- The form is one column, with the only side-by-side pair (first and last name) being a short related pair, and there is one primary button with no competing action.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Let the left column show the week. Under the subhead, the left half of the screen is empty from the subhead down. The headline promises "plan the week with your whole team", and a glimpse of an actual week board with a few names on it would prove that. Right now the visitor has to take it on trust. This is taste: the job is to make a stranger want this workspace, and seeing their own kind of week beats reading about it.
2. Make the headline specific. "Plan the week with your whole team" could sit on any planner's page. A line that names what Tallyboard does differently gives the visitor a reason to choose it over the tool they already use, whether that is a shared spreadsheet or Monday's stand-up. This is taste; Krug's point that a page should make its value self-evident supports it.
3. Builds on finding 2: turn the five-person limit into the hook, e.g. "Free for your first five people". It is the most concrete offer on the page, and right now it is buried in the subhead. This is taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I can't see whether the fields show a visible focus ring when tabbed into. This matters a lot on a page that is all form.
- I can't see whether the email and password fields use the right input types (email keyboard, masked password with a show toggle).
- I can't see how errors appear and whether each one names its field.
