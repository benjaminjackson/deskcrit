**First read.** The eye lands on the serif "How can we help?" in the navy band, drops to the white search field, then moves down to the two rows of topic cards. Within two seconds it reads as a product's help center: calm, tidy, trustworthy, and very familiar. Nothing about it is surprising, which is mostly right for this job.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the home page of a product's help center, where a user arrives with a problem and needs to search or pick a topic fast.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, search comes first, the grid is clean, and there are no serious craft defects. The main weakness is in the copy, not the layout: the eight topics describe two different products, so a first-time visitor can't tell what Harbor is or whether they're in the right place.

**What would move it most**
1. **The topics don't add up to one product.** "Set up your workspace and invite your team", "Integrations: connect calendars, chat tools, and storage" and "Notifications" describe a team software tool. "Orders and shipping: track a delivery or start a return" describes a shop. Someone landing here with a problem first has to work out what kind of product this is, and someone who came for a parcel may wonder whether they're on the wrong site. That's a defect in the conceptual model (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2): the names and grouping should show what the product is and how its parts relate. If Harbor really does both (hardware with a companion app, say), the cards should say so: for example, "Orders and shipping: track a device order or start a return." If it doesn't, the card is leftover template copy and should go.

**Smaller things.**
- Card descriptions end with one word alone on the last line in five of the eight cards ("recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). Shorten each line so it fits on one line or breaks into two balanced lines (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- **One clear starting point.** The navy band holds only the question, one line of help, and the search field, so the user's first move is obvious. Keep the band this bare.
- **A strict grid.** The wordmark, "Browse by topic" and the first card column all start at the same left edge, and the card grid ends exactly where "Contact us" ends. The gutters are even in both directions, so the page feels orderly without any rules or dividers.
- **The type pairing.** The serif is used only for the one human question and the sans for everything else. That gives the headline warmth without fighting the interface text. Don't let the serif spread to the section headings.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Make the search field say what to type (taste). "Search for articles" could sit on any help center. Example queries from the user's own problems, such as "reset my password" or "where's my order?", and a search icon or button at the field's right end would show both that the field searches and what it can answer. This serves the impatient visitor the page exists for.
2. Give the "Status" link a place on the page (taste). People arriving with a problem often have an outage, not a question. A one-line strip under the search field, such as "All systems normal" or "Sync delays: we're on it", answers that before they search. The nav link is already there, so the page treats status as important but hides it.
3. Let the most common problem lead (taste). The eight cards all carry equal weight, so the user has to scan all eight. If one or two problems account for most visits, put a short "Most asked" row above "Browse by topic" so those users never need to browse.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Keyboard focus on the search field and cards, and how the four-column grid reflows on narrow screens.
- Whether popular articles or a contact route appear below the fold. The capture ends just under the second row of cards.
