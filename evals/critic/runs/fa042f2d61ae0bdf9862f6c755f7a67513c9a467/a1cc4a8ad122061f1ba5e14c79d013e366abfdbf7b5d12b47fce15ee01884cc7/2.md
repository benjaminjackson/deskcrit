**First read.** The eye lands on the white serif "How can we help?" in the navy band, drops to the search field, then moves to the grid of eight topic cards. It reads straight away as a product's help centre: calm, tidy, trustworthy. It also looks like every other help centre. Nothing here would tell you which company's it is.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you gave no brief: this is the landing page of a software product's help centre, and its job is to get a user with a specific problem to the right article as fast as possible, by searching or by browsing.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The layout is competent and well built, with no structural faults. The main weakness is that it waits for users to say what they need: the search field gives no hint of what to type, and the eight topics all carry the same weight, so a user in a hurry has to work out the categories before they find their answer.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page doesn't put the most likely answers in front of people.** In the visible area there's no "popular articles" list, no top issues, and no example searches. The eight cards are identical in size, weight and treatment, so "Getting started" counts for as much as "Notifications". Someone who arrives locked out or with a failed payment has to map their problem onto your category names ("is a failed card under Billing or Troubleshooting?") and then click through. This is common sense rather than a published rule (the one source the catalogue gives is off the approved list). Help-centre traffic is heavily skewed towards a few questions, so surfacing them is the cheapest speed-up you have. Direction: put the five or six most-read articles as direct links near the top, and/or put real example queries under or inside the search field ("Try 'reset password' or 'refund an order'"). If that block already exists below the 800 px cut, check that it sits close enough to the search to be seen without scrolling. *Defect (it slows the intended viewer).*

**Smaller things.**
- **Search field (centre of the hero):** there's no magnifier icon and no submit button, so the white bar reads as an input but gives no signal that pressing Enter searches. Add a magnifier inside the left edge, or a small submit button. *Convention, low cost.*
- **"Browse by topic" vs. the card titles:** the section heading looks about 18 px bold and the card titles about 16 px bold. Those sizes are too close to read as two levels, so the heading reads like a ninth card title without a card (Bringhurst, §3.1.1: build from a scale). Make it clearly larger, or change its form, for example a small all-caps label in a muted colour.
- **Card descriptions:** five of the eight end with one word alone on the last line ("recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). Rewrite those lines, or nudge the text width, so the last lines carry two or three words (Bringhurst, §2.4.2).
- **Topic set (copy):** "Set up your workspace and invite your team" and "Connect calendars, chat tools" describe a collaboration tool, but "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" describes a shop. A stranger can't tell what Harbor is or which customers this card is for. Either explain the link (if Harbor really does both) or rename the card so it fits the product. *Common sense.*

**What's working.**
- **The serif headline against the sans everywhere else.** It's the only serif on the page, so the welcome gets a human voice and the clear lead role while the rest stays functional. Keep it to that one line.
- **The card grid.** Four even columns share the margins of the nav and the "Browse by topic" heading. Every card has the same build: icon, bold title, one plain-language line. That makes the eight topics quick to scan.
- **The descriptions are written in the user's words** ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Fix sync problems and error messages"), not as category labels. Users can match their own problem to a card without guessing.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the search field lead the whole hero. Make it larger and wider and give it live suggestions, and shorten or drop the line "Browse a topic or search the guides.", which only describes what's already visible. A help centre's real interface is its search box, and a hero that commits to it says "type your problem here" without needing the subtitle. *Taste.*
2. Give the navy something to do beyond being a background. It's already the brand colour (the logo, the hero, the icons), but the rest of the page is generic SaaS grey and white. Using the navy as the accent for the most-used topics or for "Status" would give the page some identity of its own, so it reads as Harbor's help centre and not as a template. *Taste.*
