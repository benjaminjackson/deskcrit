**First read.** The eye goes straight to "How can we help?", the white serif on the navy band, then down to the white search field under it. Then it moves into the eight-card grid and scans the bold titles left to right. It reads at once as a support site's front page. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, and also familiar enough that it could be any company's help center.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the landing page of a product's help center. Customers who hit a problem should either search or pick the right topic within a few seconds.

**Verdict.** It does its basic job. The hierarchy is clear, the layout is clean and the search is impossible to miss. What holds it back is what it says, not how it's built. The topics describe two different products, and the page offers categories only, never the answers people most often come for.

**What would move it most**
1. **The topics don't add up to one product.** "Getting started: Set up your workspace and invite your team", "Integrations: Connect calendars, chat tools, and storage" and "Notifications" describe a team software tool. "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" in the top-right card describes a shop. A first-time visitor can't tell what Harbor is or which half of the help center applies to them. Someone whose parcel is late meets "workspace" in the first card and wonders whether they're on the right site. *Defect* (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model: the grouping and names should show what the product is). Fix: if Harbor really does both, say so, either in the subhead or by splitting the grid into two labelled groups such as "Using Harbor" and "Your orders". If it doesn't, the shipping card is wrong content and should go.
2. **The page stops at categories.** Below the hero, the only route to an answer is to guess a category and click through. There are no popular articles and no known-issue notice. "Status" is buried as the third nav link, even though "Is it down?" is one of the most common reasons people open a help center. Every visit costs at least one extra click before any answer shows. *Common sense* (no approved source states this). Fix: under the grid or in place of the subhead, add a short list of the five or six most-read articles. Show a one-line status strip ("All systems working" or the current incident) near the search, so the most frequent questions get answered on this page.

**Smaller things.**
- **Short last lines:** "data." (Privacy and security) and "where." (Notifications) sit alone on their second lines, and "receipts.", "recovery." and "messages." nearly do. Rewrite the blurbs to fill one line or two (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- **Near-identical sizes:** "Browse by topic" looks about 18 px bold, close to the ~16 px bold card titles and the ~20 px logo, so these read as near-misses rather than levels (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Make the section heading clearly bigger, or make it a small label in a different colour or weight.

**What's working.**
- The hero band. The navy field, the single serif headline (the page's only serif) and a wide white search box make search the obvious first move with nothing competing. Keep the serif to that one line.
- The grid discipline. The logo, "Browse by topic", the card edges and the right end of the nav share the same left and right edges (about 64 px and 1216 px). The gaps between cards are even, and the icons share one stroke weight. The page feels put together rather than assembled.
- The card blurbs mostly name real tasks ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Fix sync problems and error messages") rather than category labels, so users can match their problem to a card.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let the headline say who this is for. "How can we help?" sits on almost every help center. Naming Harbor's product or its customers in the headline or subhead would tell visitors they're in the right place before they read any card. *Taste.*
2. Make the search box do more of the work. Put two or three real example queries in or under it, like "reset two-step sign-in" or "where's my order", taken from the most-searched terms. This shows visitors the words that get results and also helps with the scope problem in finding 1. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the search box suggests results as you type and what its results page looks like, since a help center rises or falls on that. Also whether the cards show a hover state that signals they're clickable.
