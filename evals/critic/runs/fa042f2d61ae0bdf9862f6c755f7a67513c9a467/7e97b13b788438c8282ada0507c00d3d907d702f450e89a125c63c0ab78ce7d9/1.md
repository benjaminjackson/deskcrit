**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026", and then to the big navy wedge marked 38%. After that it goes back and forth between the pie and the legend, trying to match colours. It reads as a calm, well-made annual-report page. The top half lands in about two seconds. Everything in the left half of the pie, from 4% down to 1%, ends in a colour-matching chore.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is one page of a printed or PDF annual report for shareholders and the board. Its job is to show where the money went and what drives costs.

**Verdict.** This is a tidy, competent page. The way the chart encodes the data defeats its own purpose, though. A 14-slice pie with only a colour legend makes the reader decode colours for nine slivers that you can't tell apart. The headline names a topic but never says what the finding is.

**What would move it most**

1. **Fourteen slices is the wrong chart.** The left half of the pie is nine slivers of 1–4% (Travel through Postage). By eye you can't tell a 3% slice from a 2% one. The ordering only works because the legend is sorted, so the pie adds nothing the legend doesn't already say. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1) both say pies hold about five slices at most. The fix is one of two things. Keep Salaries, Rent, Cloud hosting and Marketing, and fold the other ten (25% in total) into one "Other operating costs" slice, broken out in a small table. Or drop the pie and show the shares as one sorted bar chart, where 4% vs 3% vs 2% is readable at once. *Convention, and here it costs the reader something real.*

2. **The categories are matched by colour alone, and the colours don't hold up.** None of the slices carries its name. The reader has to match 14 hues across about 300 px of page. Several pairs are close even with full colour vision:
   - teal Rent and sage Equipment repair
   - mid-blue Software licences and indigo Utilities
   - green Insurance and olive Bank fees
   - orange Cloud hosting and rust Training

   With a common colour-vision deficiency, red Travel, green Insurance and brown Training merge. Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*) and Wilke both put labels on the data, and WCAG 1.4.1 says colour can't be the only carrier. Fix 1 mostly solves this: with five slices, write each name next to its slice ("Salaries and benefits 38%") and drop the legend. Fourteen hues is also far past the point where categorical colour stays readable. That's common sense, since no approved source gives the number. *Defect.*

3. **The headline names the topic, not the finding.** "Where operating costs went" tells the reader what the chart is about. The reader still has to work out what it means. The figures support a real headline, for example "People and premises took over half of operating costs" (38 + 16 = 54%), or "Four categories made up three quarters of costs". Keep "4.2 Costs" as the eyebrow label, and let the headline say the thing a board member should remember. *Taste, argued: a report page is read once and quickly, so the line people remember should be the conclusion.*

4. **No total and no comparison, so the shares can't be judged.** "38%" of an unstated total, with no fiscal 2025 figure, tells the reader the mix but not whether anything moved. In an annual report, the year-on-year change is usually the point. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") asks for a comparison next to each headline figure. The fix is to state the total in the deck ("…of £X.Xm total operating costs") and add the prior-year share, or the change in points, as a column next to the legend percentages. *Defect for this audience: they can't tell good from bad.*

**Smaller things.**
- The legend's right edge (~1060 px) doesn't line up with the header's right margin (~1216 px). That leaves an unused strip down the right side. Either run the legend to the content margin or make the narrower measure an obvious column choice.

**What's working.**
- The type plan is clear. There's a bold serif headline, a lighter serif deck that says exactly what's measured and over what period ("twelve months to 30 June 2026"), and a sans for the data and legend. Each size has its own job.
- The legend is sorted by value, uses right-aligned figures and has hairline row rules. It's a good table, so keep it as the base if you go to bars or a breakout table.
- The source line is honest: it says the figures are rounded to whole percent, and they do sum to 100. Keep that.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: let the 38% lead. Salaries and benefits is the story. Colour only that bar or slice in the navy it already has, put the other categories in a quiet grey, and let the headline say the number. A shareholder then takes away one fact instead of fourteen. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Nothing that matters for this piece.
