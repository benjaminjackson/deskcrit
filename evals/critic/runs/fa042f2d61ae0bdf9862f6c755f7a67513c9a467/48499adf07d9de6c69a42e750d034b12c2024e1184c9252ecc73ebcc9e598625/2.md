**First read.** My eye lands on the big blue headline, drops to "Create your link", and then gets pulled straight into the row of four blue-outlined cards, which tug about as hard as the hero does. The right half of the hero is empty white. The row of names at the bottom reads as grey filler. It looks like a tidy, safe scheduling tool for small service businesses, and it looks like a template: nothing tells me why it's this product and not another.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the home page of a booking-link product, and its job is to get small service businesses (clinics, tutors, coaches) to sign up and make their first link.

**Verdict.** The page is competent and clear, but nothing on it stands out. One blue is used on almost everything, so the main button has no colour left to win with. The hero shows nothing of the product, so the visitor has to take "fill your calendar" on trust.

**What would move it most**
1. **The blue is spent on everything, so it marks nothing.** The headline, nav links, both filled buttons, all four 2 px card borders, the four card titles, four icons, four badges, four "Learn more" links and the rule above the name strip are all the same saturated blue. The result is that "Create your link" is one blue thing among about twenty-five. Below the fold line, the cards out-shout it because together they cover more blue area than the hero does. Keep the accent for the main action and one highlight, and draw headings, icons and borders in neutrals (Apple HIG and Material 3 on accent colour). The headline can stay dark, getting its rank from size and weight. The card borders can go to a light grey hairline or a pale fill. *Convention; the cost here is a weak call to action.*

2. **The hero tells but doesn't show.** The whole right half of the first screen, beside the headline, is empty. The left side is heavy (headline, subhead, two actions), so the page tips left (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). More importantly, a booking link is a visual thing: the visitor wants to see what their clients would see. "See a live example" admits this, but makes them click to find out. Put the actual booking page there: a guest's view of a week of open slots in their own time zone. That fills the space and lets the visitor judge the product for themselves instead of trusting the headline. *Defect: the visitor can't judge the value without leaving the page.*

3. **The copy could sit on any competitor's page.** "Booking links that fill your calendar" is the category's stock promise. "Fill your calendar" is also ambiguous for an audience that is often over-booked: do they want more meetings, or fewer gaps? The subhead is better, but "keep every meeting in the right place" doesn't say what goes wrong without the product. The name strip (Harbor Dental, Northfield Tutoring…) is the most specific thing on the page and gets no label and no story: it's grey text under a rule, so it reads as a footer. Lead with the concrete result for the viewer, such as fewer no-shows (the Reminders card already has that idea) or no more back-and-forth emails. Give the names a line that says who they are and what changed for them. *Defect: a stranger can't tell what's different here, so nothing pushes them to act.*

**Smaller things.**
- Card 1: the "New" badge pushes "Shared calendars" onto two lines, so its body text and "Learn more" sit about 30 px lower than in the other three cards and the rows don't line up across the set. Fix by shortening the title or moving the badge, so every card title stays on one line.
- Badges: "New" and "Beta" are statuses, "Auto" and "Email" are descriptions. The same pill means two things. Keep badges for status only and drop the other two.
- Four identical "Learn more" links: none says where it goes, out of context or to a screen reader (WCAG 2.4.4). Use "Learn more about reminders" and so on, or make the card title the link.
- Two buttons look like the main action: "Start free" in the nav and "Create your link" in the hero are the same filled blue with different labels. If they do the same thing, give them the same label. If not, make the nav one an outline button (Material 3, button emphasis levels).
- Name strip: no heading, so the viewer has to guess that these are customers. Add a short lead-in.

**What's working.**
- "Create your link" is a specific, concrete ask, and the text-link "See a live example" next to it is correctly lower in weight. Keep that pairing.
- The type scale is tight and clean: one sans family, one big headline size, a clear subhead, card titles and body. Every level is easy to tell from the next. Card body lines are short and easy to read.
- The card descriptions say who benefits ("so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows"). That is the right voice; the headline should borrow it.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: make the guest's booking view the hero, in the visitor's own world. For example, show "Harbor Dental — book a cleaning" with real slots in it. The visitor can then picture their own link before they make one. *Taste.*
2. Builds on finding 3: let one number lead. The Reminders card already promises fewer no-shows. If a customer in the strip has a real figure (no-shows halved, hours of email saved), make it the headline and turn the name strip into the proof under it. *Taste:* a number from a named peer is something these buyers already trust.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the buttons, the cards and the "Learn more" links. How the four-card row reflows on narrow screens: the title-and-badge wrap in card 1 will probably get worse there.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 5 yes, 3 no (main image and headline as one idea; every element does a job; each heading names its section)
What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (contrast from more than size)
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 9 yes, 4 no (no near-miss alignment; baselines across columns; space gathered; balance)
Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (headings break at phrase boundaries)
Color: 9 lines, 8 yes, 1 no (saturated colour kept to a small share)
Pictures: 12 lines, 12 yes
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 31 yes, 2 no (one primary action; link purpose), focus indicator unjudgeable from a still, the rest yes or not applicable
