**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big blue headline at top left, drops to the subhead and the blue "Create your link" button, then hits a row of four heavy blue-outlined cards that pull it sideways. The right half of the hero is blank. It reads as a scheduling SaaS for small service businesses, and it feels clean but generic: one shade of blue on nearly everything, and nothing to show what the product looks like.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the landing page for Slotwise, a booking-link tool, aimed at small practices and solo professionals (dentists, tutors, coaches). Its job is to get them to click "Create your link".

**Verdict.** It's competent and readable, and the ask is clear. But it gives the visitor nothing to see and nothing that sets it apart from other scheduling tools. The biggest problem is the empty right half of the hero: a booking product with no picture of a booking page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero shows no product.** The area to the right of the headline (about x 680–1216, y 100–380) is empty. Nothing shows what a guest sees when they open the link, so "fill your calendar" has to be taken on trust. The empty side also tips the top of the page to the left: everything heavy sits in the left column with nothing to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). *Defect.* If an image failed to load in this capture, check that first. Otherwise, put a real booking page there, with a week of open slots in the guest's time zone, so the headline and the picture make one claim together.
2. **Blue is on everything, so it stops meaning "click".** The headline, nav links, card borders, icons, card titles, badges, buttons and "Learn more" links are all the same blue. Two things follow. Nothing stands out against anything else, so "Create your link" has to compete with eight blue outlines and four blue titles. And the blue card titles ("Time zones", "Reminders") look like links but aren't, while the nav links look like headings. *Convention* (Apple HIG and Material Design on the accent colour). Fix: keep blue for the main button and real links, and draw the headline, card titles, icons and borders in neutrals.
3. **The four cards are boxed in heavy outlines.** The 2px blue borders around each card are the loudest shapes on the page after the headline. They break the feature row into four compartments, and space alone would already group them (Tufte on non-data ink; Lupton, *Thinking with Type*). *Defect,* because they take attention from the call to action. Remove the borders or replace them with a pale tint.
4. **Card 1 is out of step with the other three.** The "New" badge forces "Shared calendars" onto two lines. That drops the card's body text and its "Learn more" about 30px below the others (at y≈672 against 642), so the row stops reading as a set (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). *Defect.* Move the badges above or after the title, or shorten the title, so every card puts its body text on the same baseline.

**Smaller things.**
- The four "Learn more" links can't be told apart out of context, for example in a screen reader's list of links (WCAG 2.4.4). Name the destination: "How team routing works".
- The badges use one style for two different kinds of thing: status ("New", "Beta") and description ("Auto", "Email"). Keep badges for status and drop the other two, which add nothing the body text doesn't already say.
- The customer strip at the bottom (Harbor Dental … Orchard Vets) has no heading, so a visitor can't tell whether these are customers, partners or examples. Give it a short label, such as "Used by…", or show logos.
- The headline "Booking links that fill your calendar" could sit on any competitor's page. The subhead promises convenience, not more bookings, so the claim has no support. Say what is different here, or give a figure the reader can check.

**What's working.**
- The ask is unusually good. "Create your link" names one exact thing to do, and "See a live example" lets the visitor judge the product themselves rather than take the claim on trust. Keep that pair.
- The card body copy speaks to the reader and ends on why each feature matters to them ("so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful"). Keep that voice.
- The type scale is clean: headline, subhead, card title and body are distinct levels with no near-collisions, and the subhead runs about 60 characters per line.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the "live example" be the hero. The page already has a link to a real booking page, so put that page right in the hero, showing one of the named customers (say, Kestrel Physio's link) instead of a generic mockup. The viewer then sees a business like theirs with its slots open, and the customer strip turns into proof. *Taste.*
2. Builds on finding 2: once the blue is cut back, the one blue button carries the page. Give the hero more room around it so the path is headline → product → button, and nothing else on the page is blue except links. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the nav links, the two buttons and the "Learn more" links.
- How the four-card row reflows at narrower widths. The two-line wrap in card 1 suggests the badge layout will break further at smaller widths.
