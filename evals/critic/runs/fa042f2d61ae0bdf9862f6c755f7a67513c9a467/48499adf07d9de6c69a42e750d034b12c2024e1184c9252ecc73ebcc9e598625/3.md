**First read.** My eye goes first to the big blue headline, then the blue "Create your link" button, then into four identical blue-outlined cards. It reads as a booking-link tool for small appointment businesses like clinics, tutors and coaches. It feels tidy and competent, but generic. The whole right half of the hero is empty, and almost everything is the same blue, so after the headline nothing pulls harder than anything else.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page for a scheduling product, and its job is to get a small service business to click "Create your link."

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job, and the message is clear within seconds. Two things hold it back. Brand blue is spread across almost every element, so the call to action competes with the decoration. The hero has no picture, so the page never shows what a booking link looks like.

**What would move it most**
1. **Blue is on everything, so the button stops being the loudest thing.** The same saturated blue is used for the headline, the nav links, both buttons, all four 2px card borders, the icons, the card titles, the badges and the "Learn more" links. The four card outlines together cover more area than the button. After the headline, the eye spreads across the cards instead of settling on "Create your link." This is a convention: Apple HIG and Material both say to reserve the accent colour for the main action and interactive state. To fix it, keep blue for the buttons and links and maybe one highlight. Draw the card borders, icons and card titles in neutrals: a pale gray border or none, with dark titles. The headline can stay dark ink or stay blue, but then it should be the only large blue thing besides the button.
2. **The hero tips left and shows nothing.** From the headline to the button, everything sits in the left half, and the right half, roughly 560×340 px, is empty. The page looks unbalanced, as if something failed to load (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). It also costs the pitch. "Booking links that fill your calendar" is a visual promise, and the page never shows a booking page or a full calendar. The visitor has to imagine the product. If an image belongs there and didn't load in this capture, this finding becomes a check in a live browser. If not, it's a defect. The fix is to put the product in that space: a real booking page with open slots, the thing "See a live example" points to. Then the headline makes the promise and the picture proves it. That last part is taste and common sense, not a cited rule.
3. **The first card breaks the row.** In "Shared calendars," the "New" badge is pushed to the right edge and the title wraps onto two lines. That pushes its body text and "Learn more" about 30 px below the other three cards, so the row loses its common lines. This breaks two conventions: Müller-Brockmann on lines aligning across columns, and Gerstner on repeated elements following one program. The card looks broken rather than chosen, and it's the first one people read. Fix it by setting the badge inline after the title, as the other three do, or by shortening the title so all four cards share one layout.

**Smaller things.**
- **Badges mix two kinds of meaning.** "New" and "Beta" are status, while "Auto" and "Email" just restate the card title. They look identical, so they read as one kind of thing. Keep the status badges and drop "Auto" and "Email."
- **Customer strip has no label.** Harbor Dental, Northfield Tutoring and the rest sit under a rule in gray with no heading, so they could be read as footer links or categories. Add a short line like "Used by clinics, tutors and coaches" so they read as proof.
- **Headline breaks awkwardly.** "fill your / calendar" leaves "calendar" alone on the second line and splits the phrase "your calendar." Rebalance it so it breaks as "Booking links that / fill your calendar."

**What's working.**
- **The copy is concrete and talks to the reader.** Lines like "so nobody has to do the math," "cut down on no-shows" and "so the first minutes are useful" each end on what the visitor gets. Keep that voice when you edit.
- **"See a live example" is the right second ask.** It lets visitors judge the product themselves instead of trusting the headline. It's correctly styled lower than the primary button.
- **The type hierarchy is clean.** There are about four clear levels: headline, subhead or card title, body, and labels. Weight separates the levels, not just size.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Show their own customers' booking pages.** This builds on finding 2. You already name Harbor Dental and Kestrel Physio, so use one of their actual booking pages, with a full week, as the hero picture. A clinic owner sees a business like theirs with a full calendar, which beats a generic mockup. This is taste.
2. **Lead with a number.** The headline promises a fuller calendar but never says how much fuller. If you have a real figure, such as fewer no-shows from reminders or fewer back-and-forth emails per booking, putting it next to "fill your calendar" lets visitors compare it with their current week. This is taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half of the hero is a picture that didn't load. Check it in a live browser.
- Focus and hover states on the cards and links.
