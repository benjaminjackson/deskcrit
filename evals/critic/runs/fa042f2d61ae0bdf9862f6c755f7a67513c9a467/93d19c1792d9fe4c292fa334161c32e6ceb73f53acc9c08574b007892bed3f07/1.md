**First read.** The eye goes to the bold question, reads the one-line explanation, then reaches a row of four identical blue buttons and stalls. Nothing in the row says which button answers the question, so you have to read all four labels and decide for yourself. It looks clean and calm, but it makes you hesitate at the exact moment it should make you sure.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a confirmation dialog in a web calendar app. It asks someone who just edited a shared choir calendar whether to publish the new times to every follower, and it should make "save" a quick, safe click.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The question and the explanation are good, but all four actions get the same full emphasis. That includes the one that throws away the user's work. So the dialog gives no expected answer and does nothing to stop a mis-click that can't be undone.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no primary action.** *(Convention, and a defect here.)* Export, Discard, Keep editing and Save are all the same filled blue button with the same weight, size and colour. The viewer can't see an answer at a glance and has to read the row like a sentence. Over time, people learn to click by position, and that's how the wrong one gets hit. Material Design 3 ("Buttons") and the Apple HIG both say a view has one high-emphasis action, with the other actions styled lower. Make **Save changes** the only filled button. Make **Keep editing** an outlined or text button. Put the other two lower still (see 2 and 3).
2. **The destructive action looks like the safe one and sits in the middle of the row.** *(Defect.)* **Discard changes** throws away the new rehearsal times. It is styled exactly like **Save changes**, is just as large, and sits second in a row of identical targets, one button away from Save. Someone moving fast in a dialog like this can lose their edits, with no undo on screen. Apple HIG ("Alerts") and Material ("Dialogs") both say to set destructive actions apart, style them as destructive, and never give them the look of the default. Move Discard to the far left, away from Save, and make it a red text or outlined button. Better still, put an undo after it so the choice costs less.
3. **"Export as file" doesn't answer the question.** *(Defect: extra choice where a decision is wanted.)* The dialog asks a yes/no question about publishing. Exporting is a side task, but it gets first position and full primary styling, so it's the first button the eye reads after the sentence. It adds a fourth option to a decision that needs two or three, and it pulls attention away from the real answer (Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure"). Take it out of this dialog and put it in the calendar's own menu. If it has to stay, make it a quiet text link, clearly separate from the answer buttons.

**Smaller things.**
- The button row's left edge looks about 4 px left of the heading and body text, and the row also runs wider than the text on the right. It reads as a near-miss rather than a choice. Line the first button up exactly with the text edge, or offset it on purpose by a clear amount (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance").
- **Save changes** could say what saving does here: "Publish to followers", or "Save and notify". The body text already tells you the change goes to everyone, and the button that commits you to that should say it too.

**What's working.**
- The headline is a real question with a specific object, "the shared calendar", and the body line spells out the consequence for other people ("will be shown to everyone who follows this calendar"). That's exactly the right copy for a confirmation, so keep both lines.
- The two type levels are clearly different, the text is dark on white, and the line length is comfortable. Nothing in the text slows you down.
- The buttons are large targets, about 56 px tall with 16 px gaps, and their labels are verbs that name outcomes ("Discard changes", not "No"). Keep those labels when you restyle them.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: replace the Discard confirmation with an undo. Let Save and Discard both act at once, then show "Changes discarded — Undo" for a few seconds. That makes the dialog's job (getting a decision quickly and safely) much cheaper. Raskin's point that you should use undo instead of a warning is listed in the catalog entry, but it isn't on the approved reading list, so I'm arguing this as taste: one click and a way back beats a careful read of four buttons. *(Taste.)*
2. Since the risk is that other people see the change, show the change. A short summary under the body line, like "Thu rehearsal: 7:00 → 7:30 pm", lets the user check what they're about to publish, using times they already know, before they commit. *(Taste.)*

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Which button gets keyboard focus when the dialog opens. It should be Save or Keep editing, never Discard. Check this in a browser.
- The dialog sits on a plain white page with no dimmed backdrop. If that's just how it was captured, it's fine. If the live page shows the calendar behind it with no scrim, the dialog won't read as blocking.
