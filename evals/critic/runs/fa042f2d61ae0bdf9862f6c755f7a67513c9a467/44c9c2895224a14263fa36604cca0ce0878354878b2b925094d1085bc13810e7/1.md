**First read.** My eye goes to the bold sans heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three calm serif paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for a small local ferry. It feels quiet, well set and trustworthy, close to a printed notice. Then I want to know when the next boat leaves, and nothing on the page tells me.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells someone planning a trip on the river ferry when it runs, how long it takes, and what the rules are for bikes and winter.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound, but the page fails at its job. Every fact a passenger would plan around is vague or missing, and the facts it does give are buried in running prose. The biggest problem is the words, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on are missing or vague** (defect). Paragraph 1 says "between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". Neither is a time. A passenger can't tell from either line whether a 7:15 boat exists or whether a 6 pm crossing in December is safe to count on. "Before dusk" can mean 3:30 or 5:00 depending on the date, and the wrong guess leaves someone stranded on the wrong bank. "Every forty minutes" with no start time means they can't work out a single departure. The last sentence then sends them to "the shelter at each landing" for printed timetables, so the page sends them to the pier to find out when to go to the pier. **Fix:** state the first and last departures from each landing, with clock times for summer and winter, or link to the timetable right here. If dusk really decides the last boat, give the winter last departure as a time.
2. **The key facts are buried in prose instead of laid out for scanning** (convention: Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*, pages should be self-evident and scannable). Frequency, crossing time, the bike rule and the winter change sit mid-sentence in three similar-looking paragraphs, so finding one means reading all of them. **Fix:** pull the numbers a passenger looks up into a short list or a two-column summary at the top (route, how often, crossing time, first and last boat, winter hours). Keep the prose for the softer notes, such as the tide and how to board with a bike.
3. **The heading doesn't say what the page offers** (taste). "Notes for ferry passengers" names who the page is for but not what's on it, so it reads like an aside. Something that names the content, such as "Ferry times and travel notes: north landing to market quay", would tell a scanning visitor they're in the right place.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing. A bold, tight sans heading over a regular serif body gives two clear levels from size, weight and form all at once. Keep that split if you add a summary block: the summary can use the sans, the prose the serif.
- The text setting. Lines run about 68–72 characters, leading is about 1.6, the rag is gentle, and the gaps between paragraphs are clearly smaller than the gap above the first one. It reads comfortably, so don't loosen or widen it.
- Restraint. One hue (near-black on white), one left edge and no ornament suit a plain public notice.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the next departure lead. One large time in the heading sans, with the crossing time beside it, would make the page answer the one question every visitor brings, before they read a word of prose (taste).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, and whether the empty right side gets used at other widths.
