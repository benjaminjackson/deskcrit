**First read.** The eye goes first to the three big figures across the top: 3.8%, €64.20, 12.4k. Then it drops to the block of orange bars and runs left to right, up to the Saturday peak and down to Sunday. It reads straight away as the admin home page of a small bakery's online shop. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, much like a good off-the-shelf dashboard.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the shop owner's daily overview: are sales healthy, and which days does the bakery need to staff and bake for?

**Verdict.** As a piece of layout it does its job: it's clear, well set and easy to scan. The serious problem is the chart. It adds up raw orders for a month that has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of every other weekday, so the shape the owner would plan from is partly a calendar artifact.

**What would move it most**
1. **The weekday bars compare unequal numbers of days. Defect.** The source line says "orders delivered in September 2026". September 2026 starts on a Tuesday, so it has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays and four of everything else. Turned into orders per delivery day, the bars read roughly: Monday about 160, Tuesday about 142, Wednesday about 152, Thursday about 205. The chart shows a steady climb from Monday to Thursday. Per day, Monday is actually busier than Tuesday or Wednesday, and Thursday's jump is much bigger than it looks. An owner deciding staffing or batch sizes from this would put effort on the wrong days. The visual effect doesn't match the effect in the data, which is what Tufte's lie factor warns about (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2). The fix: chart average orders per delivery day, or count the same number of weeks for every weekday (say, the last four full weeks), and say which one in the subtitle or source line.
2. **The chart title names the topic, not what it shows. Taste and common sense, no approved source.** "Orders by delivery day" makes the owner work out the point from the bar heights. The two things worth acting on, the Friday–Saturday peak and the Sunday drop, aren't stated anywhere. A title that says the finding (once item 1 is fixed), such as "Saturdays are your busiest delivery day; Sundays about a third of that", turns the panel from something to look at into something to plan from. If you need the topic label for scanning, keep it as a small label above the title.

**Smaller things.**
- The time windows are worded three ways: "Last 30 days", "As of 30 September 2026" with "vs 31 August", and "September 2026" on the chart. If they all mean the same period, say it the same way. If they don't, the owner is comparing figures that don't line up.

**What's working.**
- The KPI cards are well built. Each one has the figure large, the unit (%, €, k) smaller and lighter so the number leads, then the period, then a change against a previous period. The change uses a ▲, a + sign and an explicit unit ("+0.4 pts" rather than an ambiguous "+0.4%"), so it doesn't rely on green alone and can't be misread. Keep that pattern.
- The chart is honest in its construction: bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, there's no 3D and no legend, the days run in their natural order, and there's a source line. Almost all the ink is data.
- There's one family, a short type scale (page title, figures, labels, chart small print) and one accent colour taken from the wordmark. That gives the page a restraint that suits an owner's tool.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Use the wordmark's orange for meaning, not for every bar (taste). Right now all seven bars carry the brand colour, so none stands out. Set the bars in a muted neutral and give orange only to the peak day, or to today's delivery day. The chart is for planning the week, and colour that singles out one day points the owner at the decision (Tufte's case for spending ink on what matters).
2. Builds on finding 1: put last month's per-day figure beside each bar as a faint tick or ghost bar (taste). The KPI cards already compare against a previous period. Giving the chart the same comparison lets the owner see whether Saturday is growing, not just whether it's big, and makes the page consistent from top to bottom.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bars show exact values on hover. Owners will want the actual number for a day, and nothing on the page gives it beyond the gridlines.
