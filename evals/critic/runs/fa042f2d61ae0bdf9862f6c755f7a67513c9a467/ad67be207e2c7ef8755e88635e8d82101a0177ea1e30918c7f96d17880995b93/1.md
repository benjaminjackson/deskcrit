**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", glances up at the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label, then reads three short paragraphs from top to bottom. It looks like a ferry operator's info page for passengers. It feels calm, orderly and quiet, and fairly anonymous.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page that tells a passenger when the river ferry runs and how to bring a bike on board.

**Verdict.** The typography and layout are sound, and you could ship them as they are. The piece falls short in its words. It skips the facts a passenger will act on (actual times, which months count as winter), so it sends people to a printed timetable instead of answering them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times are vague exactly where the reader will act on them.** This is in paragraphs one and three. "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first and last boats leave. Someone planning a 6:30 crossing or the last boat home can't tell whether it runs. "Every forty minutes" has no starting time, so they can't work out a departure. "In winter" could mean several different date ranges. "One boat each hour" could mean one an hour in each direction, or one round trip. The page then ends by saying "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing." That points the reader somewhere else for the answer when they already came to this page for it. Times and dates are what passengers remember and hold the operator to. If a line can be read two ways, someone will stand on the quay and miss a boat. *Defect.* Fix: put in real figures, such as "first boat 6:20 from North Landing, last boat 22:40 from Market Quay, departures at :00 and :40". Give the winter dates. Say whether "each hour" means in each direction. Link to the timetable rather than only telling people where the paper copy is.

**Smaller things.**
- Body text lines are slightly too long. The first lines look like they run about 83 to 88 characters across a column roughly 730 px wide. Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 characters. Because each paragraph is only two or three lines, this costs little, but narrowing the column to about 600 px, or 65 to 70 characters, would bring it into range. *Convention.*
- The bicycle paragraph only describes what riders do ("Riders wheel them on… and lock them"). It doesn't tell the reader what that means for them. For example, they need to wait until foot passengers are on, and maybe bring their own lock. Speak to the reader directly with "you". *Taste, from the copy.*

**What's working.**
- The order of importance is clear. There are three sizes with big steps between them: a small capitals label, body text around 18 px, and a bold heading around 36 px. Each size has one obvious job. Keep this scale as the page grows.
- Everything hangs from one left edge (x ≈ 120). The space between paragraphs is clearly bigger than the line spacing, so each paragraph reads as one topic at a glance.
- "Before you board" is a good heading. It speaks to the passenger at the moment they need the page, which is better than a label like "Service information".

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Make the frequency the thing that leads. "Every 40 minutes · 12-minute crossing" could sit as a large figure line under the heading, so a passenger glancing on a phone gets the one fact they came for before reading any prose. *Taste.* Refactoring UI's advice to make the key fact dominant supports this.
2. Each paragraph covers one separate topic: the schedule, bikes, and winter. Short run-in labels in the bold weight already used for the heading ("Bikes", "Winter") would let a rider jump straight to the bike rules without reading the schedule first. *Taste.*
