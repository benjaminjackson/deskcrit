**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue title, "Planning a vegetable bed", then drops down a clear ladder of three blue subheads. In about two seconds it reads as a short, friendly how-to for a first-time gardener. It feels calm and orderly but also bare and a bit corporate: plain black text and link-blue headings on white, with the upper-left 60% of the frame filled and the rest empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a beginner's web article or help page about setting up a first vegetable bed, meant to be read top to bottom.

**Verdict.** The typography mostly works: the hierarchy is clear, the headings sit with their text, and nothing gets in the way of reading. What holds it back is the content. The page is called "Planning", but it never tells the reader what size to make the bed or what goes where, and it ends on a single sentence. Second to that, the text lines run too long.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out the plan.** This is a defect. The title promises planning, but the steps skip the decisions a beginner can't make alone. "Mark the corners with sticks and a length of string" doesn't say how big the bed should be. "Sow in short rows" doesn't say how short or how far apart. "Watering" is one sentence and the page stops there, so the reader finishes without knowing when to plant or what comes next. Each step needs the number a beginner would otherwise have to guess, such as a bed size and a row spacing. The page should also end on a next step, like when to sow or what to expect in week one, rather than trailing off.
2. **The text lines are too long.** This breaks a convention, and it costs the reader something. The paragraphs run about 88 to 90 characters per line ("Choose a spot that gets six hours of sun and stays out of the wind. Mark the corners with"). That's well past Bringhurst's 45 to 75, with 66 as the ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). With so few lines it's survivable, but the eye has a longer trip back to the start of each line, and this column will hold any longer article written in this template. Narrow the column to about 65 characters, roughly 600 px at this body size. There's room to spare on the right.

**Smaller things.**
- "last year's heap": the apostrophe looks straight. Use a curly ’. This is common sense; at body size it's a small detail.

**What's working.**
- The spacing does its job. Each subhead sits about 6 px above its paragraph and about 30 px below the paragraph before it, so each heading clearly belongs to the text it introduces. Keep that ratio.
- The type has three clear levels, title (about 50 px), subhead (about 32 px) and body (about 17 px), with bold and blue separating the headings as well as size. Nothing collides, and the page needs no more levels than these.
- The voice is good. "Start with things that forgive mistakes: radishes, lettuce, and bush beans" is specific, kind, and names real choices. Write the rest of the page to that standard.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: give the page one concrete picture of the plan, such as a simple drawn plan of the bed with rows and sizes marked. A beginner reading a page called "Planning" wants to see the finished layout, and a sketch would do more than another paragraph. This is taste.
2. The blue in the headings is the standard web link blue. That reads as "software help page", not "garden". If this is a gardening publication, a single earthier colour for the title and subheads, chosen with the same contrast against white, would suit the subject without adding a second hue. This is taste (Itten, *The Art of Color*, on colour carrying mood).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, and whether any of the blue text is clickable.
