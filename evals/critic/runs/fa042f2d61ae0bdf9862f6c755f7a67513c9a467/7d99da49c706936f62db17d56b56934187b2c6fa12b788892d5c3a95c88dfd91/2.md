**First read.** My eye lands on the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time". Nothing pulls it there; that's just where reading starts. From there it slides down four pairs of grey lines that all look about the same, and the right third and bottom half of the frame are empty. It reads as a help or FAQ page for a bakery's pre-order service. It feels calm and neat, but flat, like a text file that was never styled.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section on a bakery's ordering site. Customers who already placed an order should be able to scan it, find their question and act on the answer.

**Verdict.** It doesn't quite do its job. It's tidy, but the questions can't be scanned because they look like the answers, and the two answers people will act on (cancelling and allergies) leave out the facts they need.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings are no different from the answers** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two headings look like regular weight, the same dark grey, and about the same size as the answers below them, maybe even a pixel smaller. Someone scanning for "can I cancel?" can't skim just the questions. They have to read every line, and on the first pass each pair looks like a two-paragraph block. Dair (*Design with Type*, ch. 8) says a contrast has to be obvious at a glance, and a one-step difference reads as an accident. The fix: make every heading clearly different from its answer through weight (semibold or bold), a clearly larger size, or both, so the four questions form their own column the eye can run down.
2. **The page has no title** (defect). There's nothing above the four pairs, so nothing is seen first and nothing tells a newcomer what page this is or whose bakery it is. They have to work it out from the answers. Krug (*Don't Make Me Think*) asks that a page explain itself within seconds, and Tschichold says one element should come first. The fix: add a title that names the page in the customer's words, for example "Your bread order: questions". Make it the one dominant element, a full step above the new headings.
3. **The cancellation answer can be read two ways** (defect, copy). "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say ahead of what (the pickup time, or the bake). "A day" could mean 24 hours before pickup or any time the day before. It also doesn't say how to cancel. This is a deadline, so customers will hold the bakery to it. Someone who reads it as "the day before" and cancels at 9pm for a 7am pickup will think they're within the rule. The fix: give one exact cutoff, for example "until 6pm the day before your pickup". Then say how, as the pickup-time answer does: "Open your order and choose Cancel."
4. **The allergy answer doesn't help someone ordering online** (defect, copy). "Every loaf has a card by the till" only works for someone standing in the shop. The reader is choosing bread online, before they ever reach the till. For a safety question, that means they either order blind or give up. The fix: say where the ingredients are listed on the order page, or how to ask before ordering. Keep the card as a second check at pickup. One more gap: "along with a name" in the collection answer doesn't say whose name. The direction is "your name, as on the order".

**Smaller things.**
- The answers run the full ~820 px column, about 95 characters a line ("Give the person your order number…"), well over Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Narrow the column to about 600 px.
- "anything." sits alone on the second line of the allergy answer. Rewording the answer (finding 4) or narrowing the column will move it.

**What's working.**
- The spacing already groups the content correctly. Each heading sits about 10 px above its answer and about 35 px below the previous answer, so the four pairs read as four units even without type contrast. Keep these spacing values when you style the headings.
- The answer to "Changing your pickup time" is the model for the others. It gives the action ("open your order and choose a new slot") and the consequence in concrete terms ("holds orders for two hours after the chosen time").
- One sans-serif typeface in one dark grey on white gives a calm, readable page with nothing competing.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Phrase the headings as the customer's own questions ("Can I change my pickup time?", "Can someone else collect it?"). That makes the list of headings work as the contents page someone scans to find their problem. Taste.
2. Put the cancellation cutoff in bold as its own figure. That way the one deadline on the page is what people remember, and the rule can't be misread at a skim. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "Open your order" is a link, or whether the questions expand and collapse. Check in a browser that the pickup-time answer actually links to the order.
