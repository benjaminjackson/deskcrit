**First read.** The eye has nowhere obvious to land, so it starts at the top-left line and reads down. It reads as eight lines of text in one weight, not as four questions and four answers. It takes until the second pair to see the pattern: heading, then answer. Once read, it's a bakery's pickup-order help section. It feels calm, plain and honest, but flat.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help or FAQ section on a bakery's online-ordering page. A customer scans it for the one question they have, mostly around pickup time.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The section headings look the same as the answers, so the page can't be scanned, and scanning is the whole point of an FAQ. Second, the two lines a customer will hold the bakery to, the cancellation deadline and the collection rule, can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings are no different from the answers. (Defect.)** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two headings look the same size, weight and colour as the text under them, perhaps a pixel smaller. Only the slightly tighter gap below each one tells you it's a heading. A customer who came to ask "can I cancel?" can't run their eye down the headings to find it. They have to read the page line by line. Dair's point (*Design with Type*, ch. 8) applies: a difference meant to be seen must be obvious at a glance, and a one-step difference reads as an accident. The fix: make the headings obviously different from the answers, by weight (semibold) or a clear step in size. Then the four questions stand out and the answers sit back.
2. **The cancellation and collection rules can each be read two ways. (Defect, copy.)** People act on these lines.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." A day ahead of what: the pickup time or the order date? Does it mean 24 hours before pickup, or by the end of the day before? A customer who reads "the day before, any time" and tries to cancel at 9 pm for a 10 am pickup gets refused, and feels cheated. State a deadline they can check against their own order, e.g. "You can cancel until [time] the day before your pickup."
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name." Whose name: yours or theirs? If the friend gives their own name and the staff expect the orderer's, the bread doesn't leave the shop. Say which name.
   - "Holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" is clear. Keep that standard for the other two lines.
3. **The lines are too long. (Convention.)** The full-width lines ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") look like about 90–95 characters, well past Bringhurst's 45–75 (*Elements*, 2.1.2). For short answers like these, the cost is mild but real: the eye has a long way back to the next line. Narrow the column to about 65 characters. The page has plenty of empty space on the right, so this costs nothing.

**Smaller things.**
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the Allergies answer. Narrowing the column will probably fix this; otherwise rebreak it (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- "Open your order" doesn't say where. There's no link and no hint of the order screen, so the customer has to remember where it lives. Link the phrase to their orders, or name the place ("in your confirmation email").

**What's working.**
- The answers are short, plain and in the customer's words. "the bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule instead of just stating it. That's the right tone for a small bakery; keep it.
- The headings are named by what the customer wants to do ("Changing your pickup time"), not by policy categories. Once they're styled as headings, they'll scan well.
- The text is near-black on white in a readable sans at a comfortable size. Nothing needs fixing there.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: put the one number each answer depends on in bold: "two hours", the cancellation deadline, "order number". These are the facts a customer comes back to check. Bolding them turns a re-read into a glance. (Taste.)
2. "Allergies and ingredients" sends the customer to the shop to find out what's in the bread. For someone with an allergy, ordering online, that's the wrong moment to find out. Link to the same ingredient list on each product, so they can check before they pay. (Taste. It's content, not layout, but it's where this section most lets its reader down.)
