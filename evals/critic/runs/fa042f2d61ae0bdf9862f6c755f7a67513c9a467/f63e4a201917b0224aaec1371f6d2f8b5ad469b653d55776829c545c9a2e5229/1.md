**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks", drops to the orange button, then gets pulled down to the purple "Keep your streak going" box. That box is bigger, more saturated and lower than the button, so it ends the eye's path. The teal block on the right registers as a colour field, not a picture. The page reads as a clean, friendly language-app landing page, competent but generic.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the logged-out home page of a language-learning app. Its job is to get a first-time visitor to start the free week.

**Verdict.** The headline and the offer are clear. But the page splits the visitor's attention across three loud colour blocks, and the loudest one is a message for people who are already learners. Removing or demoting the purple card is the single biggest fix.

**What would move it most**
1. **The purple "Keep your streak going" card is in the wrong place, aimed at the wrong person, and fights the CTA.** It sits directly under "Start your free week" and is wider and taller than the button, in a stronger, cooler saturated colour, with the same rounded filled shape. It reads as a second, bigger button. A visitor either reads it as an action and wonders what clicking it does, or the eye stops there instead of on the button. Its copy talks to someone who already has a streak. A first-time visitor has none, so the headline "Keep your streak going" makes no sense to them. The stat underneath ("finish the course twice as often") is the only useful part, and it works as proof, not as an instruction. *Defect* (two filled blocks of equal weight split the primary action: Material Design 3 and Apple HIG button emphasis; NN/g). Fix: take it off the logged-out page. If you keep the stat, make it plain text under the button in the neutral palette, rewritten for a newcomer: "Learners who practise five days in a row are twice as likely to finish." Then the orange button is the only filled shape in the left column.
2. **Three unrelated saturated hues: orange, purple, teal.** Each is a different strong colour with no shared role. Orange is the action, purple is a notice, and teal is decoration. Together they make the page look assembled from parts, and they dilute the orange, which should be the one colour that means "do this". *Convention* (Refactoring UI, "Define your shades"; Apple HIG and Material 3 on accent colours). Fix: keep orange for the main action and at most one key highlight. Draw everything else in neutrals or in tints of one supporting hue. The teal panel can stay only if teal becomes the brand colour and is used with restraint.
3. **The hero illustration repeats the headline instead of adding to it.** Two empty speech bubbles say "conversation", which the headline already says, and they don't show the product's real hook: a tutor that listens and gives feedback on each word. The large teal block gets the second-most attention on the page and tells the visitor nothing new. *Taste.* Direction: show the product doing its job, for example a real exchange in Spanish with word-level feedback. Then the picture proves the claim rather than restating it.

**Smaller things.**
- The illustration's right edge (about x=1076) doesn't line up with the nav and the third step column, which both end around x=1224. This leaves a dead strip on the right, and the block looks placed by eye. Extend it to the content edge or set it on the column grid (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- "A tutor that listens" can be read two ways: a human tutor, or software. Say which, because the visitor will hold you to it.

**What's working.**
- The headline names a concrete outcome and a time frame ("real conversation", "ten weeks"). It is the clear focal point, set large and tight in dark type.
- The three-step row (Listen / Speak / Use it) is a quiet, well-ruled explainer. The short verb headings each sit close to their one line of copy, and the copy names what actually happens.
- "Start your free week" is a specific ask with a clear cost (none).

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: commit to orange as Parlo's colour. Let it appear only on the button and one detail in the illustration, so on a page that is otherwise neutral the eye goes straight to the thing that starts the trial. *Taste.*
2. Let "ten weeks" lead. It's the claim that separates Parlo from every other app. Tie the three steps to it (e.g. "Ten minutes a day, ten weeks") so the visitor can picture fitting it into their own week. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button and nav links, and how the two columns reflow on a phone. That matters for where the purple card ends up if it stays.
