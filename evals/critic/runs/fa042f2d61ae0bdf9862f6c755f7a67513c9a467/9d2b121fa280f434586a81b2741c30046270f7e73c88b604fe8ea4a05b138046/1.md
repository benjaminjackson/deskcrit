**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the homepage of an independent garden centre. Its job is to get local people to buy shade plants, come to the shop, or book a workshop.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read, but it doesn't do its job yet: it says what the shop sells and then stops, with no action anywhere on the screen. After that, the headline is split from its own explanation, and the hero image is generic wallpaper rather than these plants.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's nothing to do.** *(Defect.)* The screen has no button or link apart from the four nav words. The headline promises plants for shade and small spaces, but there is no "See the shade plants" link. The Saturday workshops card says there are ten places per class but gives no date, price or way to book. The Delivery card makes an offer you can't take up from here. The eye ends on the cards with nowhere to go, so a visitor who is ready to act has to guess which nav word (Plants? Workshops?) gets them there. The fix: add one primary action to the hero, styled as a filled button and named for this offer (for example "Browse shade plants"), so it is clearly the strongest control on the page (Material Design, Apple HIG, Nielsen Norman Group on primary versus secondary actions). Then give each card one lower-weight link to its next step: book a workshop, get directions, check delivery. Adding the next step where people need it is common sense, not a cited rule.
2. **The headline and its explanation are cut apart.** *(Defect.)* "Plants for shade and small spaces" sits inside the hero, and the line that makes it concrete ("Ferns, hostas and compact shrubs… ready to pot this weekend") sits outside it, about 170 px lower, across the hard bottom edge of the image. A box groups whatever is inside it (Palmer; Nielsen Norman Group, "Common Region"), and here the box splits one message in two. That subhead is your best copy, and it reads like the start of the content below instead of the second half of the headline. Put the subhead in the hero under the headline, or bring the headline down out of the image. Either way, they belong in one group.
3. **The headline is out of line with everything else.** *(Convention.)* The logo, the subhead and the left card all start at about x = 56. The headline starts at about x = 72. A 16 px miss looks like a mistake, not a choice, and the eye notices the wobble (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on placements that are neither clearly aligned nor clearly off). Put it on the same left edge.
4. **The hero image says "plants", not "shade".** *(Taste.)* The 400 px band is a flat pattern of scattered leaves with red, orange and grey dots. It shows no ferns or hostas, no shade and no small space, so it only repeats the most general word in the headline. The grey dots look like dust on the screen. At half the first screen, the band is mostly mood. A photo of the actual shade stock, ideally in a small courtyard or on a balcony, would show what the headline promises and prove the plants are "grown at our nursery". That would set this shop apart, where the pattern can't.

**Smaller things.**

- Headline: "spaces" sits alone on line two, and the break splits the phrase "small spaces". Break after "shade", or narrow the text box so both lines are about the same length (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

- The type is under control: a dark-green serif for the logo, one sans for everything else, and a clear jump from headline to subhead to card titles to body text. Card titles are set apart by weight, not by another size.
- The three cards are even, share one grid and one style, and each holds exactly one topic. They're easy to scan.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Builds on finding 4.* Own the "Fernhollow" green. The logo's dark green is the only colour on the page with any character, so use it for the primary button and the card links, and let it be the one colour that means "do this". On a page this quiet, a single owned accent makes the action easy to find and gives the brand some recognition. *(Taste.)*
2. Make "this weekend" the hook. The subhead already has a time element. A stronger version would tie the hero to what's ready now, such as this week's shade plants or the next workshop date, so a repeat visitor sees something new and a first-time visitor has a reason to come on Saturday. For a local shop, a reason to visit now is worth more than a general claim about the range. *(Taste.)*
