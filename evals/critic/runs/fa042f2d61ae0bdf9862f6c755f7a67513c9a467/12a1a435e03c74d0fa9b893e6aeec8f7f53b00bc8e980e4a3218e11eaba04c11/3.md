**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and stops there because none has a unit. Is 3.8 a percent? Is 64.20 in pounds, dollars or euros? 12.4 subscribers can't be right, so it must be thousands, but that's a guess. Then the eye drops to the orange bars, where Saturday is highest and Sunday falls off. It looks like a calm, tidy admin overview for a small online bakery.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a quick health check of the shop for the owner and staff. They should be able to tell in a few seconds whether things are going well and which days are busiest.

**Verdict.** It's well built, but the most important part fails. The three headline numbers can't be read without guessing, and even read correctly they don't say whether things are good or bad. The chart is the only part of the screen that fully does its job.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units, no time period and no comparison.** *(Defect.)* This is the three cards across the top. "3.8" under "Checkout conversion rate" is very probably 3.8%, but the viewer has to supply the "%". "64.20" has no currency sign. "12.4" under "Active subscribers" reads as twelve and a bit people, so the viewer has to guess it means 12,400. A wrong guess here is costly: someone could quote "12 subscribers" or misread the order value in a meeting. None of the cards says what period it covers. Only the chart's footnote mentions September 2026, and the viewer has no reason to think it applies to the cards too. Nothing shows whether 3.8% is up or down, or above or below target, so the owner can't tell good news from bad, and telling them that is this row's whole job. The fix: put the unit in each number (3.8%, £64.20, 12.4k or 12,400). Add the period, either once above the row or on each card. Add one comparison per card, such as change against August or against target, in small text under the figure (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2).
2. **The chart title names the topic but not the finding.** *(Taste. I'm arguing this on its own terms because no approved source covers it.)* "Orders by delivery day" tells the viewer what the chart is. It leaves them to work out what it says: Friday and Saturday together take roughly twice Monday's volume, and Sunday is about a third of Saturday. That is useful for staffing and baking schedules. A title like "Friday and Saturday carry the week; Sunday is under 400 orders" gives a busy owner the point without reading the bars. The bars then become the evidence.

**Smaller things.**
- The current page in the top nav ("Overview") is marked only by a darker, bolder label. That's a weak signal on a four-item nav. Consider an underline or a background tint.

**What's working.**
- The bar chart is honest and quiet. The axis starts at zero, ticks are even, gridlines are faint, there's one colour and no legend, and the days run in their natural order. Keep that restraint (Tufte, data-ink).
- There's one accent colour. The brand orange in the wordmark is the same orange as the bars, and everything else is neutral, so the screen feels like one product rather than a template.
- The layout is clean. The KPI cards and the chart card share the same left and right edges, gaps and corner radius, and the type scale is clear at four levels: page title, big figures, labels and card title, axis text.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: the orange could carry meaning in the KPI row instead of only decorating the chart. Use it only for the change indicator on whichever metric needs attention, so the owner's eye goes there first. *(Taste.)* A health check is for spotting what changed, and colour used sparingly does that faster than reading three numbers.
2. Give the Sunday bar a direct annotation, such as "Limited delivery" or whatever the real cause is, if there is one. *(Taste.)* The sharp drop is the most striking thing on the screen. If the dip is expected, a note under it stops the owner wondering whether it's a problem. If it isn't expected, the dashboard has just shown the owner something to look into.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover tooltips on the bars (exact counts), the focus states in the nav, and how the three cards reflow on a narrow screen.
