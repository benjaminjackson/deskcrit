**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers across the top, then drops to the tall orange bars and stops on Saturday. It reads as the admin dashboard for a small bakery's online shop: calm, clean, and generic. But the second glance stalls. "3.8" what? "64.20" in which currency? "12.4" active subscribers, which can't be a fraction of a person? The biggest type on the page is the type I understand least.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner of a small bakery shop checks this overview to see at a glance how the shop is doing this period and when orders come in.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are tidy and competent, but the three headline numbers fail at the one thing they are for. They have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them with, so the owner can't tell what they mean or whether they are good. That is the thing to fix. Everything else is minor.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI figures have no units, so they can be misread.** *(Defect.)* In the top row, "3.8" is probably a percentage, "64.20" is probably money but in no stated currency, and "12.4" for "Active subscribers" can only make sense as thousands, which the card never says. These are the largest, heaviest marks on the screen. A viewer who has to guess the unit can guess wrong: they might read 12.4 as twelve people, or 3.8 as a rate out of 10. A dashboard number should never leave the reader guessing what it measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2). Fix: put the unit into the figure itself: "3.8%", "£64.20" (or whatever the currency is), "12,400" or "12.4k".
2. **No figure has a period or a comparison, so none of them can be judged.** *(Convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design".)* Even with units, "3.8%" is meaningless without a period (this month? all time?) and something to measure it against: is it up or down, above or below target? The only date on the page is in the chart's source line ("September 2026"). The cards may or may not cover the same month. The chart shows a single month with nothing beside it, so you can't tell whether Saturday's peak is normal or new. Fix: per Few, put the period and a comparison next to each headline number, such as "vs August: +0.4 pts" or a small trend line. Give the chart the previous month as a light grey reference series.
3. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding.** *(Taste. This is common sense, not a sourced rule.)* "Orders by delivery day" makes the owner work out the point. The point is plain once found: Friday and Saturday carry the week (about 1,050 and 1,130 orders), and Sunday drops to about 380. That pattern is what the owner would plan baking and staffing around. Fix: say it in the title or a one-line caption, e.g. "Friday and Saturday bring a third of the week's orders; Sunday is the quietest day", and consider marking the peak days.

**Smaller things.**
- The y-axis has no unit of its own. "Orders" is only in the title. Fine here, but "Orders" next to the axis, or "orders" in the tooltip copy, removes the last doubt.
- "Shop overview" works as a page heading, but it could carry the period ("Shop overview · September 2026"). That would fix the missing dates in finding 2 for every card at once.

**What's working.**
- **One-hue discipline.** The brand orange appears only in the wordmark and the bars, against white cards on a pale grey ground. The only thing in colour is the data, so the chart reads instantly. Keep it.
- **A clean grid with a clear order.** The three equal cards and the full-width chart share the same outer edges and gutters. The label-over-big-number pattern has two distinct type levels with real contrast between them, so the structure is plain without any borders or decoration.
- **An honest chart.** Bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, the days are in calendar order, and the source line names the system and the month. That last point is exactly what the cards are missing.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: let Saturday lead. Keep the orange for Friday and Saturday and drop the other days to a muted tint. For a baker deciding when to bake more, the chart then answers its question without being read. *(Taste. Tufte's case for spending ink on the data that matters supports it.)*
2. Make the cards say "good" or "bad" in the shop's own terms. "Active subscribers" next to a target ("12,400 of 15,000") turns the row from a readout into a status the owner can act on. *(Taste.)*
