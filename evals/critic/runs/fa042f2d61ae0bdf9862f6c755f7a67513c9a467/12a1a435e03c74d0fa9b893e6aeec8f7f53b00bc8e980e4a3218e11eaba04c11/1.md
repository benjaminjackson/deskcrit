**First read.** My eye goes straight to the three big numbers (3.8, 64.20, 12.4), then down to the orange bars, where Saturday is the tallest and Sunday clearly drops. This looks like the owner's home screen for a small online bakery. It feels calm, tidy and well made. But after the first glance I can't say what any of the three numbers means.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing screen where the owner of a small bakery that sells online and by subscription checks, in a few seconds, whether the shop is doing well and how orders spread across the week.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are clean and well built. The page still fails at its main job, because the three headline numbers have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them with. The owner can read them but can't tell what they mean or whether they're good.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three KPI cards don't say what their numbers are.** Top row. "3.8" is probably a percentage but could be a ratio. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" is an "Active subscribers" count, and a count of people can't be a decimal, so it must mean 12.4 thousand. The viewer has to guess that, and a guess of 12 or 124 is just as easy. None of the cards says what period it covers. The chart footnote says September 2026, but nothing ties the cards to it. With no previous period or target, none of the numbers tells the owner whether things are getting better or worse. These are the numbers the owner will act on and quote, so the ambiguity is a **defect**, not a style point. Direction: put the unit next to each number (3.8%, £64.20 or the right currency, 12,400). State the period once, above the cards or on each one. Add a comparison beside each figure, such as the change from last month or progress toward a target (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). **Defect.**
2. **The chart title names the topic, not what the chart shows.** "Orders by delivery day" makes the viewer read seven bars to find the story. The story is that orders climb through the week to a peak on Saturday (about 1,130) and fall to about a third of that on Sunday. That pattern is what tells the owner how to staff and bake, so the title or a short note should say it outright, for example "Saturday is the busiest delivery day; Sunday is a third of it". **Taste**, but it costs the owner a few seconds on every visit.

**Smaller things.**
- Nothing on the screen shows which period the whole overview covers, or offers a way to change it. Only the chart's footnote mentions September 2026. A period label or date control near "Shop overview" would fix this and support finding 1.

**What's working.**
- **The layout is built on one grid.** The three cards and the chart panel share the same outer edges and gutters, with a steady 48 px margin. That's why the page looks calm. Keep it when you add the comparisons.
- **The chart itself is honest and plain.** The bars start at zero, the gridlines are evenly spaced and faint, the days are in their natural order, there's only one colour, and there's a source line with its period. Nothing on it is decoration.
- **The type scale is clear.** There are about four distinct sizes: KPI numbers, page title, labels, axis text. The big numbers are heavy, and the labels above them are lighter and grey. Each level has its own job.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let one KPI lead. For a bakery that relies on subscriptions, active subscribers is probably the number that matters most. Giving that card more size or weight than the other two would tell the owner where to look first and what the business depends on. **Taste.**
2. The burnt orange is already the brand colour in the "Crumbwell Bakes" wordmark and in the bars. Once comparisons are added, use that orange only for what needs attention, such as Saturday's peak or a KPI that moved, and show the other bars in a muted tint. The chart would then point at its finding instead of only showing data (Tufte's case for spending ink on the data that matters). **Taste.**
