**Verdict.** It does not do its job well yet. The structure is sound, but the body text is set entirely in capitals, which slows every reader on the only part of the page that carries information. The copy also leaves out the one fact a passenger most needs: the actual times.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body paragraphs are set in all capitals** (defect). All three paragraphs, from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING.", are in capitals at body size. Capitals have no ascenders or descenders, so every word becomes the same rectangle. Readers lose word shapes and fall back to reading letter by letter. The three paragraphs blur into one dense block, and the tone turns from helpful to shouted. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says caps suit headings shorter than one line and that you should never capitalize whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals particularly hard to read. The fix: set sentences and paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for short labels like the "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow, which already uses them correctly. That change alone would make the page work.
2. **The copy gives no times** (defect, copy). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" sounds precise but isn't. A passenger planning a crossing can't tell whether the first boat is at 6:00 or 8:00, or the last at 19:00 or 23:00, and those times decide whether they make it home. "Thins to one boat each hour" has the same gap: from when to when? The page also sends people to printed timetables "kept in the shelter at each landing", which they can only read once they're already at the landing. Give the first and last sailing times, the winter dates, and ideally a link to the timetable itself.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 2.** Pull the numbers out of the sentences. The facts passengers act on are "every 40 minutes", "about 12 minutes" and "hourly in winter". Set them as a short row of figure-and-label pairs above the paragraphs, so someone glancing on the quay gets the service pattern without reading prose. That's taste, but it serves a page meant for quick lookup, and figures are what people remember and plan around.
2. **"Before you board" promises an action the page barely delivers.** Only the bicycle paragraph is about boarding. Either make the headline describe the whole page ("How the river ferry runs") or lean into the promise by ordering the notes as steps a passenger takes. That's taste: a headline that matches the content tells the reader what they'll get before they start.
