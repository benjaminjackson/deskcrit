**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Before you board", then hits a grey block of capitals underneath. Three paragraphs set entirely in caps make plain travel information read like a warning notice or terms and conditions. It looks like a passenger-information page for a small river ferry. The heading feels friendly, but the body text feels like it's shouting.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a web page that tells ferry passengers the practical facts before they travel (how often it runs, how long it takes, bikes, winter service), so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The page is well built: one column, a clear heading, sensible spacing and a good line length. Two things keep it from doing its job. The body is set in all capitals, which slows every line and makes the tone hostile. And the copy never gives a time, so a passenger can't plan from this page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in all capitals.** This is a defect, and it breaks a convention. All three paragraphs, about eight lines in all, are in caps at body size. Capitals all have the same height and a rectangular outline, so the word shapes that let us read fast are gone. The reader has to decode letter by letter. On a page whose job is to be read and then remembered on the quay, that's the wrong trade. It also changes the voice from "here's what to know" to "NOTICE". Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps": "Don't capitalize whole paragraphs." Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, says text in capitals is "particularly difficult to read." **Fix:** set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. Keep capitals for short labels like "PASSENGER NOTES", which already does this well.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on.** This is a defect. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing and no departure minutes. Someone deciding whether to walk down now can't tell whether they'll wait two minutes or thirty-eight. "One boat each hour" in winter has the same gap. It also doesn't say which months count as winter. The page then sends readers to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing". That is exactly where they can't use the information to plan. **Fix:** give the first and last departures from each landing and the minutes past the hour the boat leaves, plus the dates winter service runs. The general description can stay as the lead-in. These are the details people will hold the operator to, so they need to be exact.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing.

**What's working.**
- **The heading.** "Before you board" speaks to the reader and names the moment they're in. It's bold and big enough to clearly lead, and the small, spaced, grey "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it sets context without competing.
- **The measure and spacing.** Lines run roughly 65–75 characters with comfortable leading. The space between paragraphs is clearly larger than the space between lines, so the three topics separate on their own, without rules or boxes.
- **Restraint.** Near-black on white, one family and three type sizes with big steps between them. Nothing on the page is decoration.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 2.** Let each paragraph lead with its fact. This is taste. Start each one with a short bold lead-in in the body face: "Every 40 minutes", "Bikes ride free", "Winter: hourly". A passenger skimming on a phone at the landing then gets the answer from three words per paragraph. It uses the type's existing weight contrast (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*: bolder, not bigger) rather than adding a new size.
2. Put the timetable on the page itself. This is taste. The empty right-hand column is the natural place for a small table of departures from North Landing and Market Quay. That turns "Before you board" from a description of the service into the thing passengers actually check, and it fills the space with the one thing the page most needs.
