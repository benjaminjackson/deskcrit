It looks like an informational page for ferry passengers. The top half is calm and well ordered; the body feels like it is shouting at you.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for river ferry passengers, telling them how often the boats run, how bicycles work, and what changes in winter, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but setting every body paragraph in capitals makes the page harder to read and gives it a shouting tone. On top of that, the copy is vague on the times a passenger will actually plan around. Change those two things and this is a good page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body is set in all capitals.** All three paragraphs, about eight lines, are in full caps. Capitals take away the word shapes that ascenders and descenders give, so every line has to be read letter by letter. The text also reads as shouting, which clashes with the calm heading above it. This is the single biggest reason the page feels harder than it should. Convention and defect: Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says "Don't capitalize whole paragraphs", and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read". Fix: set sentences and paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions. The "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow already shows the right use of caps: short and tracked out.
2. **The times passengers will act on are vague.** "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone when the first or last boat leaves. That is the fact that decides whether someone gets home. "In winter" doesn't say which months, and "one boat each hour" doesn't say on the hour or at what minute. Every passenger will read these differently, and someone who guesses wrong misses the last boat. Defect (copy): give the actual first and last departure times from each landing, the dates winter service starts and ends, and the winter departure minute. If times change, say where to find the current ones (the line about the printed timetables does this partly, but only for people already standing at the landing).

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean: a small grey eyebrow tracked out in caps, a big bold heading, then body text. That's three clear levels with big steps between them, so nothing competes with the heading. Keep this type scale.
- The spacing groups things correctly. The heading sits close to its eyebrow and to the first paragraph, and the gaps between paragraphs are clearly larger than the line spacing within them. Each paragraph reads as one topic: timetable, bikes, winter.
- The measure is right. Lines run about 68–72 characters, which is within the 45–75 range Bringhurst gives. Lowercase text will be narrower, so check the column width again after the case change.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: once the first and last departure times are written out, let those times lead. Put them as figures in a short line above the paragraphs (for example "North Landing → Market Quay, every 40 min, first 06:20, last 22:40") so a passenger gets the answer they came for without reading anything else. Taste. This page exists to help someone plan, and the crossing times are the plan.
2. The three paragraphs each cover a separate topic (timetable, bicycles, winter). Give each a short bold lead-in, or a small subheading in the same style as the eyebrow, so a cyclist can jump straight to their paragraph. Taste. This builds on the scannability the layout already has (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*).
