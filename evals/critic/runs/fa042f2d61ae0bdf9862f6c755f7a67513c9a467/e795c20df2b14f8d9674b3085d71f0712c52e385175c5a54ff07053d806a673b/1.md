**First read.** The eye goes straight to the dark blue block in the middle of the grid, weekdays from about 09:00 to 16:00, and then up to the title. Within a couple of seconds it reads as a support-desk report on when tickets come in: busy weekday working hours, quiet weekends and nights. Then the eye moves to the three big numbers on the right. It feels calm, competent and restrained.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a report page for support leads deciding when to staff the desk. They need to see when demand peaks and trust the numbers enough to plan shifts around them.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made heatmap that mostly does its job. The one thing that matters is that the stat beside the chart contradicts the chart: "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" sits next to a grid whose darkest cells are at 11:00. On a page people use to plan rotas, that disagreement costs more trust than anything else here.

**What would move it most**
1. **The busiest-hour stat disagrees with the chart.** This is a defect. The hour labels sit centred over their columns, so the darkest cells (30+ on Monday and Friday) are in the 11:00 column, two columns right of "09:00". At 10:00, every weekday is 25–29. Tuesday to Thursday are 25–29 at both 10:00 and 11:00, so on the chart's own evidence 11:00 is at least tied and wins on Monday and Friday. A lead who checks one against the other has to decide which to believe, and if they believe the wrong one they put the extra staff in an hour early. Possible causes:
   - The stat may count a different thing, such as the median weekday or the start of a peak hour.
   - The labels may be meant as boundaries and are placed wrongly.
   - One of the two may simply be wrong.

   Fix whichever it is. If the stat is a different measure, say what it measures in the label. Also check the period: the subtitle says "1 to 28 September", but the first stat says "New tickets, September". Readers will assume both cover the same span, so either make the spans match or name the span in both places.
2. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** This is my judgment (common sense), not a published rule. "When new tickets arrive" makes every reader work out the pattern for themselves, and they may come away with different answers. The chart has a clear story that no text states: demand builds from 08:00, peaks late morning, dips a little around 13:00, holds until about 16:00, and weekends get roughly a third of the load. A title or one-line caption that says this in staffing terms, something like "Tickets peak at 11:00 on weekdays and stay high until 16:00", gives the reader the conclusion. The chart is then there to back it up. Once finding 1 is fixed, this title and the side stats should say the same thing.

**Smaller things.** None worth ranking. The two lightest ramp steps (0–4 and 5–9) are close together, but you can still tell them apart, as in Saturday's 13:00 gap.

**What's working.**
- **A single-hue blue ramp from light to dark, binned into seven labelled steps.** It reads as "more is darker" with no legend lookup needed, it works without full colour vision, and the binned legend gives exact ranges. Keep this.
- **Every headline number has a comparison.** "Up 4% on August", "Same as August" and "Down 6 min on August" tell the reader whether to worry, not just what the figure is.
- **The source line.** It states the time zone and that hours mean "opened, not answered". That pre-empts the most likely misreading of the chart. It's a careful detail and worth keeping.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 2: mark the peak on the grid itself.** This is my taste. Put a thin outline or a small label on the busiest weekday cells so the chart and the stat beside it point visibly at the same place. For a staffing page that turns the grid from a picture of demand into an answer, and the reader no longer has to match a number to a column by eye.
2. **Use the empty lower third for what staffing needs next.** This is my taste. Below the source line the page is blank. One plain row there comparing hourly arrivals with agents on shift, using the same hour columns, would let a lead see the gap directly instead of comparing this page with the Agents tab from memory.
