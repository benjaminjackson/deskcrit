**First read.** The eye goes straight to the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then down through the paragraph and three plain lines to the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top section of a small ferry company's homepage. It feels calm and tidy but bare. All the content sits in a column in the upper left, and the right half and the bottom third are empty white, so it looks like a template that hasn't been filled in yet.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the landing screen for a local passenger ferry. Its job is to get locals and visitors to trust the crossing and find out when the next boat leaves.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do the job yet. It's clean and the headline is good, but it holds back what a rider came for (when, where to, how much) and leaves half the screen empty. It could be anyone's page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page hides the departure details a rider came for.** The paragraph says boats leave "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". That's the line people will plan around, and both ends are vague: does "early" mean 6:00 or 8:00? Is the last boat at 21:00 or 23:00? The copy never says where the boat goes (north landing to where?) or what it costs. Everything concrete sits behind a click on "See the timetable". The viewer has to leave the page to answer the only question they had. **Defect** (common sense: these are the times and prices people hold you to). Fix: put the first and last sailing times, the destination landing and the fare on this screen, ideally as "Next boat 14:20". Keep the button for the full timetable.
2. **The weight tips into the top-left corner, and nothing makes the promise visible.** Every element is in a column about 630 px wide on the left. The right side and the bottom carry nothing, so the page looks unbalanced and unfinished rather than deliberately spacious (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). The page also promises calm, a harbour and an open deck, but shows none of them, so it has nothing that sets it apart from any other company's page. **Defect.** Fix: give the right side real weight. A photo of the deck and the bay would do it, or the departures panel from finding 1, which would fix both problems at once.
3. **The three-line list repeats the paragraph and blurs into it.** "Bicycles travel free" and "Seats on the open deck" repeat "Bring a bicycle" and "from the open deck" from the sentences just above. The list text looks about 2 px smaller than the paragraph, too close to read as a separate level. It has no bullets or marks, and the lines are spaced about as tightly as the paragraph's, so it reads as a stray end of the paragraph rather than a set of facts. **Defect** (repetition is common sense, Nielsen heuristic 8). The size clash is a **convention** (Bringhurst §3.1.1, sizes from a scale). Fix: use the list for things the paragraph doesn't say, such as the fare, step-free boarding or the crossing route. Set it at the same size as the paragraph and make it look like a list, with marks or a clearly larger gap above it.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "twelve / calm minutes" splits the number from its unit across lines. Rewrap or tighten it so "twelve calm minutes" stays on one line (Bringhurst §2.4.6).
- There's no header or navigation in the frame, just the "Coastal Ferry Co." label. If this is the whole page, riders have no way to reach fares, routes or contact details. That's fine only if a header exists outside this capture.

**What's working.**
- The headline pairs a number with a feeling ("twelve calm minutes"), so it's specific and has a mood. Keep that pattern.
- "See the timetable" names exactly what you get when you click. It's the one primary action, and the button is a comfortable size to tap.
- The colours are restrained. One blue ties the "Coastal Ferry Co." label to the button, all text shares one crisp left edge, and the paragraph runs at a comfortable line length (about 60 characters).

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let "twelve" lead. The figure is already the strongest claim on the page. Setting "12 min" as a big number beside the next departure time would make speed and frequency readable at a glance, which is what someone deciding whether to walk to the landing needs. Taste.
2. Builds on finding 2: own the blue. It's already in the label and the button. Carried into the picture or a band of water, it would make "calm" something the viewer sees, not just reads, and give a coastal operator a look of its own. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether a site header or navigation sits above this capture, and the button's hover and focus states.
