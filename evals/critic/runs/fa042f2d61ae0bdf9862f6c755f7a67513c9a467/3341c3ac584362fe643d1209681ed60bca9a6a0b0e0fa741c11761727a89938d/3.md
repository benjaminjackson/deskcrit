**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", moves down through the paragraph, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a small ferry company's website. It is clean and easy to take in, but bare. The right half of the frame is completely empty, and there is no water, boat or harbour anywhere, so it feels like a template waiting for its picture.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top section of a ferry operator's homepage. It has to make the crossing appealing to locals and day-trippers and send them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The copy and the type are good. As a design it falls short, because it describes a pleasant crossing and shows nothing of it. Half the frame is unused, and it looks like any startup's landing page instead of a boat across a bay.

**What would move it most**
1. **The empty right half (about 500 px of blank white beside the text).** All the content sits in the left 60% of the frame, and nothing balances it. The page tips left and looks unfinished, as if an image failed to load or was never placed. That space is exactly where the piece should show what it promises: the harbour from the open deck, a bicycle on board, calm water. A visitor would believe "calm" from a picture more readily than from an adjective. Arnheim's point in *Art and Visual Perception* (chapter I, "Balance") is that a heavy side needs a counterweight, and here the counterweight is the missing image. Fix: put one strong photograph or illustration of the crossing on the right, sized to hold its own against the headline block. If an image was meant to be there and didn't load, check the live page. *Defect* (the layout looks broken and the promise has no support).
2. **The page sends people away for what they came to find.** Anyone looking at a ferry page wants to know when the next boat leaves and what it costs. The paragraph gives a frequency ("every forty minutes from early morning until late evening") but no times or fare, and "early morning" and "late evening" are vague exactly where a traveller needs a number. They have to click to the timetable to plan anything. Fix: show the next one or two departures and the fare beside or above the button, and swap the vague phrases for actual first and last sailing times. Keep "See the timetable" for the full schedule. *Common sense* (no approved source covers this). It matters because times are what people act on and hold the operator to.
3. **The colours say software, not sea.** One saturated corporate blue on flat white, with an Inter-style sans throughout, carries none of the calm, light or outdoor feel that the words describe ("watch the harbour slide past"). Itten (*The Art of Color*) ties colour to the mood a piece carries, and here the mood is neutral. Fix: once the picture is in, take the accent and perhaps a soft background tint from it (sea, sky, painted hull), so the colour and the claim match. *Taste*, argued above.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes" gives a concrete number and a feeling in one line, and it lands on the reader: it says what *you* get. Keep it. It is the strongest thing on the page.
- **The type hierarchy.** One dominant heading in heavy weight, body text at a comfortable size with a measure of about 70 characters, generous leading, and a small blue brand label above. There are few sizes, each with a clear job, all on one strong left edge.
- **One clear, specific action.** "See the timetable" names exactly what you get, and it is the only primary button.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the image be the open deck itself, seen from a passenger's seat. Then "Seats on the open deck" and "watch the harbour slide past" are shown rather than listed, and the picture and headline together make the argument (the twelve minutes, felt). *Taste.*
2. Let "twelve" lead. The number is the most persuasive word on the page, and it is buried in the middle of the headline. Making the twelve-minute crossing the thing you remember (for example, set against how long the drive round the bay takes) would let visitors judge the value against something they already know. *Taste.*
3. Make the three perks look like perks. "Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board" are set like stray body lines. Small icons or a light visual treatment would make them scannable as reasons to go. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover, press and focus states.
