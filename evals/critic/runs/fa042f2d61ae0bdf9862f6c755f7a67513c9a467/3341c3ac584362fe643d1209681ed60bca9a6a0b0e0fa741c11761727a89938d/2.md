**Verdict.** It mostly works. The headline is specific and the ask is clear, but the page looks unfinished and anonymous. The empty right half reads as a missing picture, and the copy holds back the facts people need to plan a trip: where the boat goes, when it first and last runs, and what it costs.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half is empty, so the page tips left and reads as unfinished.** Everything sits in the left 60% of a 1280 px frame, and the area to the right of the headline and button is blank. Nothing balances the dark headline, so the layout looks lopsided rather than deliberately open, and most viewers will assume an image failed to load. Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance") says a heavy side needs a counterweight. That empty area is also the only place the page could show the bay, the boat or the open deck the copy describes. Put something with real weight there, ideally a photo of the crossing, or make the emptiness clearly intended, for example by widening and centring the text block. **Defect**: it costs trust and the sense of place.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts a traveller acts on.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no first or last sailing, so someone planning a 7 am commute or a late return still can't tell if the boat suits them. "The north landing" doesn't say what town or pier it is, and "cross the bay" doesn't say where you arrive. There's no fare, although "Bicycles travel free" suggests people pay. These are the lines the viewer acts on and will later hold the company to. Name both ends of the route and give the first and last departures. If fares are simple, add one fare line so "free" has something to compare against. **Defect**.
3. **The paragraph and the list are almost the same size.** The intro paragraph looks about 20 px and the three feature lines about 18 px, with the same weight, colour and typeface. They read as two slightly mismatched blocks of body text rather than two levels. Bringhurst (*Elements of Typographic Style* 3.1.1) says to keep to a small set of distinct sizes. Set the list at the paragraph size and tell it apart by something else, such as list markers, a small icon, or a darker or bolder first word. If the list isn't worth that, fold it into the paragraph. **Convention**: the cost is small but visible right under the headline.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete promise ("twelve calm minutes") that gives a number and a feeling together. It's the strongest thing on the page, so keep it.
- "See the timetable" is the one exact next step a would-be passenger needs. It's the only primary action and is clearly a button, so don't dilute it with a second button of equal weight.
- The type is tight and sensibly set: the headline is big and bold with tight tracking, paragraph lines run about 68 characters, and everything shares one left edge.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let the picture carry "calm": a wide, quiet shot from the open deck across the water would turn the claim in the headline into something the viewer sees for themselves. *Taste*.
2. Let the "twelve minutes" lead harder. Comparing it with what the viewer already knows, such as "twelve minutes by boat, forty around the bay by road", would let them judge the value on their own terms instead of taking "calm" on trust. *Taste*.
3. The blue is the only colour, and right now it's a stock corporate blue. Shift it toward a sea or harbour blue and use it a little more boldly, for example on the route or the times, and the page would stop looking like it could belong to any company. *Taste*.
