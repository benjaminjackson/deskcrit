**First read.** My eye lands on the serif headline, drops to the four pastel cards, and then jumps up to the dark "Book a visit" button in the top right. It reads as the home page of a local service that does cleaning, laundry and gardening, for busy households. It feels calm, tidy, friendly and trustworthy. It also feels interchangeable: a dozen other home-services sites look like this.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a home page for a local home-services company, meant to get a homeowner to choose a service and book a visit.

**Verdict.** It's well built and reads easily. But as a sales page it stops one step short. The four services are shown as a menu with prices you can't interpret and no way to pick one. The words also do too little to say why this company rather than any other.

**What would move it most**
1. **The service cards don't look like the first step, and booking is somewhere else.** The subhead promises a flow: "Pick a service, choose a day." But the four cards have no button, link, arrow or "Book" label. Nothing shows they can be clicked. The only action is "Book a visit" in the header corner, far from the services. A visitor who has just decided "I want deep cleaning" has nothing to press next to deep cleaning. They either guess that the card is clickable or travel up to the corner and hope the button remembers their choice. Norman (*The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 1) says the interface has to show where to act. The "put the next step where the user is" part is my own judgment. Direction: give each card a visible action tied to its service ("Book window cleaning →"), or make the whole card an obvious link with a hover state and an arrow. Then the header button becomes the general fallback rather than the only door. *Defect.*
2. **The prices can't be read the same way by everyone.** "From $89", "From $189", "From $24" and "From $65" have no unit. Is $89 per window, per visit, or per hour? Is $24 laundry per bag, per pound, or per week? A price is what people remember and hold you to. One visitor reads "$24 a week" and another reads "$24 a load", and whoever guessed wrong feels misled at checkout. "From" adds more doubt: from what, for how much home? Direction: name the unit and the base case ("$89 per visit, up to 10 windows", "$24 per bag"). Then the number works as a promise instead of bait. *Defect.*
3. **The copy could be on any competitor's page, and the name points the wrong way.** "Help around the house, on your schedule" names a category, not this company. Nothing says where they work, how fast they can come, or why to trust the "vetted crew" (no reviews, years in business, guarantee or insurance). In the US, "Home Care" usually means in-home elder or nursing care, so some visitors will first read this as a care agency until the cards correct them. Also, "choose a day" in the subhead conflicts with the laundry card's fixed "Monday… Wednesday". Direction: make the headline say something only Fernway can say, such as the service area, a same-week promise or a guarantee. Put one piece of proof by "vetted". Make the subhead and the laundry schedule agree. *Defect* (ambiguity and conflicting claims). The headline point is *taste*.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "on your / schedule" leaves one word alone and splits the phrase (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Widen the text box so it fits on one line, or break after "house,".
- Inside every card, the illustration panel's left edge (about x=70) and the text (about x=76) miss each other by roughly 6 px. That reads as an error, not a choice (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I). Line the text up with the panel edge, or inset it clearly further.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing. The serif in the logo and headline, set against a plain sans for everything else, gives the brand a warm, slightly established voice. It also makes the headline clearly the top level without needing extra size. Keep it.
- The card system. It has one illustration style, one palette (teal, coral, ochre on pale mint), and every price on the same baseline. You can compare all four services at a glance.
- The restraint. There's one primary button, a cream page and lots of air. It feels calm, which suits a company you're inviting into your home.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: let the "vetted crew" claim carry the page. A short proof line right below the subhead, with a review score, how long the crews have worked with Fernway, or a re-clean guarantee, would turn the softest line on the page into the reason to book. People booking strangers into their home need trust more than anything else. *Taste.*
2. Own the coral. It's already the warmest colour in the illustrations, and the page is otherwise teal and slate. Using coral only on the booking actions would make "book this one" the one warm thing the eye looks for in every card. Albers (*Interaction of Color*) shows how a colour gains force from what surrounds it. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are clickable or have hover and focus states. If they are, finding 1 is only about how they look.
- How the four cards reflow on a phone.
