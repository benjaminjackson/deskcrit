**First read.** My eye goes to the serif headline first, then drops to the big navy slice and its "38%". Next it jumps right to the legend to find out what 38% is, comes back for the grey 25%, and goes right again to learn that it is "Other". It reads as a calm, well-made annual-report page: warm off-white paper, sober colours, nothing shouting. It also feels a little inert. I come away knowing the split but not why I'm being shown it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one page of a company annual report, showing shareholders, staff or board members how operating costs divided up in fiscal 2026.

**Verdict.** It is clean and competently built, but it shows the split without telling the reader anything. The chart's second-largest slice is unnamed, there is nothing to compare against, and the names sit away from the data in a legend. The reader gets five percentages and has to work out the point alone.

**What would move it most**
1. **A quarter of costs is "Other", and it's the second-biggest slice.** The grey 25% at the top left is larger than rent, cloud and marketing. In an annual report, an unexplained quarter of spending is the first thing a careful reader (an investor or board member) will question. They'll wonder what is in there, or assume something is being hidden. Either way the chart loses trust exactly where it is meant to be transparent. Direction: break "Other" into its two or three largest parts so that no unnamed group is bigger than the smallest named one. If it has to stay, say what it holds in the footnote ("travel, legal, insurance, …"). *Defect.* This is common sense; no approved source covers it.
2. **The page gives the split but not the meaning.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" is a topic, not a finding. There is also no previous year anywhere, so 38% salaries or 12% cloud hosting can't be judged as high, low, rising or falling. For a yearly report, the change since last year is usually the whole story. Direction: add fiscal 2025 next to each share (a second column in the table, or "38% (↑4 pts)"). Then let the headline state the finding the comparison shows, such as "Cloud hosting overtook marketing" or whatever is true. *Defect* on the comparison (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a figure needs a period and a comparison). Stating the takeaway in the title is *taste*, and here the page is meant to report, so it should say something.
3. **The names are about 300 px from the slices they name.** Each slice carries only a percentage. To learn what "16%" is, the reader goes to the legend, finds 16% there, and reads across to "Rent and facilities". That trip happens for every slice, and every percentage is printed twice. It also leaves colour plus number-matching as the only link, which is shaky for colour-blind readers telling navy, teal and purple apart. Direction: put the category name with each percentage on or beside its slice, with short leader lines for the thin ones, and remove the legend. Or go the other way: the right-hand list is already a sorted table, so add a horizontal bar to each row and drop the pie. *Convention* (Tufte; Wilke: label at the data rather than in a legend).

**Smaller things.**
- The white bold percentages on the orange (12%) and teal (16%) slices look below 4.5:1. At this size (bold, under about 19 px) they don't count as large text. Fix: use dark text on the light slices, or deepen those fills (WCAG 1.4.3). The legend repeats the numbers, so nothing is lost, but it reads as careless.
- The right half is empty below y≈380. The legend stops at about x=1060, short of the 1216 px right edge set by the header, so the space next to the pie is leftover rather than shaped. Fix: once findings 2 and 3 change the right column, run it to the full content width, or centre the pie-and-labels group on the page (Arnheim, balance; Müller-Brockmann, one grid).

**What's working.**
- The type system is clear and well-pitched. A small spaced-caps kicker ("4.2 COSTS"), a heavy serif headline, a lighter serif dek and a sans for data and footnote give four distinct levels with no near-collisions. Keep it.
- The chart is honest and calm. It starts at 12 o'clock, runs largest to smallest clockwise with "Other" last, the shares add up to 100, and the footnote states the source and the rounding. That is the integrity an annual report needs.
- The palette is muted and grey goes to "Other", so the named categories stand out without any of them shouting.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the navy 38% lead. Salaries are the story of most cost pages, so making that one number and its change the visual anchor, with the rest supporting it, would turn a reference page into a statement the reader remembers. *Taste.*
2. Commit to the warm ochre already used in the "4.2 COSTS" kicker as the single accent for whatever changed most this year, and set the other categories in quieter tones. One highlighted category tells the reader where to look before they read a number (Wilke: highlight one series and grey the rest). *Taste.*
