**First read.** My eye lands on "Pricing", drops to the three big prices ($9 / $29 / $79), and then stops. There's nothing below the cards except about 230 px of empty white. It reads as a clean, competent pricing page for a calendar-booking SaaS, "Tidewell". It feels calm and trustworthy but generic, and it ends without telling me what to do.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: a desktop pricing page where small teams and businesses compare three plans and start a sign-up or trial on the right one.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The type and spacing are tidy, but none of the plan cards has a button, so a viewer who has picked a plan has nowhere to act on it. Nothing helps them pick either: no plan is recommended, and the card bullets don't show how the tiers relate.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cards have no call to action. This is a defect.** All three cards end at their last bullet ("Email reminders", "Reports", "Priority support"). The only action on the page is "Start free" in the top-right nav, about 300 px above the cards and tied to no plan. Someone who has just decided "Team, $29" has to go back up to the header and hope the button signs them up for the plan they chose. That breaks natural mapping: the control should sit next to the thing it acts on (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*). It also breaks the convention every pricing page sets, which is a button on each card (Nielsen, Jakob's law). The eye path ends in a dead stop, which is why the bottom third of the frame looks abandoned rather than calm. Fix: give each card a button at its foot that names the plan ("Start with Team"). Make the plan you most want people to choose the only filled button and the other two outline buttons (Material Design, button emphasis levels).
2. **No plan leads, so the choice is left to the viewer. This is a defect.** The three cards are identical in border, weight and size. Tschichold's rule that the order of importance be shown by difference doesn't apply to any of them. With nothing to anchor on, a viewer compares three prices cold, and many will default to the cheapest. Fix: mark one plan, most likely Team, since this is a "teams" product, as the recommended one. Use a tinted border or the brand blue, a short label, and its filled button. Keep the other two quiet.
3. **The bullets don't say what each tier adds. This is a defect.** Solo lists "Booking page" and "Email reminders", and Team lists "Shared availability" and "Reports". Does Team still include the booking page and reminders? The viewer has to guess or assume. They should be able to see what each tier adds without working it out (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Fix: start the Team and Business lists with "Everything in Solo, plus" and "Everything in Team, plus", or add a comparison table below the cards. That table is a natural use for the empty band.
4. **"Start free" doesn't say what is free. This is a defect, on the line the viewer will hold you to.** None of the plans is free, so "Start free" reads two ways: a free plan that isn't shown, or a trial of unknown length. If the viewer reads it the wrong way, the first charge comes as a surprise. Sitting next to "Change or cancel whenever you like", it starts to read as a promise. Fix: name the offer where people decide, for example "14-day free trial, no card" under each card's button, and make the nav button match.

**Smaller things.**
- Subtitle: "you like." sits alone on the second line, a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrow the text box or rebreak it so the two lines are closer in length, or trim it to one line.
- Copy: "Simple plans for teams of every size" could sit on anyone's pricing page. Say who each plan is for and what it saves them ("Solo — for one person taking bookings"), so the plan names do some choosing for the viewer. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- Price setting: the large bold "$29" with a small grey "/ month" makes every price readable at a glance, and the unit is never lost. Keep that size relationship when you add buttons.
- One restrained accent: the brand blue appears only in the logo, the active-tab underline and the one button. That's exactly why a blue "recommended" card will stand out once you add one.
- A clean, even three-column grid with plenty of room inside each card. The structure is sound; it's the content that's missing.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the recommended card break the row. Make it slightly taller or raised, with the blue as its border, so the decision is made visually before the viewer reads a price. This is taste. A pricing page exists to move people toward a choice, and a row of three equals makes them do that work themselves.
2. Use the empty band below the cards for the two questions that stop a purchase: what happens after the trial, and whether you can switch plans. "Change or cancel whenever you like" is already the strongest line on the page, and it's buried in the grey subtitle. Give it room to make its case at the point of hesitation. This is taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indication on the nav and button, and how the three cards stack on a narrow screen.
