**First read.** My eye goes to the bold title, then straight to the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%, then across the other three labels. The table on the right comes after the chart. It reads as a clean, calm analytics page for someone checking how each plan converts. It looks trustworthy and careful, but it doesn't say anything: I see four bars that are about the same height, and nothing tells me whether that's good news, bad news, or noise.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: an internal analytics view that tells product and growth people how each plan's trials converted in Q3 2025, so they can decide which plan needs attention.

**Verdict.** It's well made, and the figures add up: every bar matches Paid ÷ Trials in the table. But it shows the data without telling anyone what to make of it. There's no stated finding, nothing to compare against, and no sign of whether a 0.9-point spread between plans is real. A reader can't act on it without working it out themselves.

**What would move it most**
1. **The title names the topic, not the finding** (defect). "Trial to paid conversion by plan" just repeats the axis labels, so the title and the chart say the same thing twice. Meanwhile the one thing worth saying, that Growth converts best and Starter worst but every plan is within 3 to 4%, is left for the reader to work out from bar heights. Rewrite the title as the conclusion, in the viewer's terms. For example: "Growth trials convert best (4.0%); Starter, our biggest plan, converts worst." Keep the current line as the subtitle. This is common sense, since no approved source states it, but it's the difference between a chart and an answer.
2. **None of the rates has anything to compare against** (convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). 3.6% means nothing on its own. A reader can't tell whether Team got better or worse than Q2, or whether any plan hit its target. That's the question someone on a Conversion tab is actually asking. Put a comparison next to each value label, such as the change from Q2 or a target tick on each bar, so good and bad can be read at a glance.
3. **The gaps between plans may be noise, and the chart presents them as fact** (defect; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Enterprise's 3.4% rests on 38 conversions out of 1,120 trials. Growth's 4.0% rests on 118. Ranking plans on differences this small could send a team after a gap that isn't there. Show a confidence range on each bar, or at least note the conversion counts on the chart. The table holds them, but it sits apart from the bars it qualifies.
4. **You can read the definition two ways, and it decides the numbers** (defect, copy). The subtitle says "became paid accounts within 30 days". The definition says trials "still open on 30 October are counted as not converted". Reading one: every trial gets its own 30-day window. Reading two: there is one cutoff on 30 October for all trials, so a July trial gets three months and a late-September trial gets one. If readers pick the second, they will think the plans were measured unevenly and won't trust the comparison. Say which it is in one sentence, for example: "Each trial counts as converted if its first invoice is paid within 30 days of the trial starting; we stopped counting on 30 October."

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honest and restrained.** Bars start at zero, there's one colour, gridlines are faint, every bar has a direct value label, and plans run in tier order. Don't add anything to the chart itself.
- **The table is set properly.** It has right-aligned figures that look tabular, light rules, and headers that are small and visibly lower in the hierarchy. Readers can check exact numbers without the table competing with the chart.
- **The type scale is clear.** There's one title size, one heading size, one text size, and one small size for axes and source. Bold and grey do the remaining work.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Use the one purple already on the page as the highlight. Keep Growth (or whichever plan the story is about) in the purple and set the other bars in grey. Then the chart makes its point before anyone reads a label. Taste, along the lines of Tufte: when everything is emphasised, nothing is.
2. Give the empty lower right column a job. The right column stops at about one third of the height, below the definition, and the chart runs on to the source line. A "What this means" note with one or two sentences of interpretation or a recommended action would fill that space with the thing this page is missing. Taste.
