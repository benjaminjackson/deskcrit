**First read.** My eye lands on the bold title, drops to the tallest bar (Growth, 4.0%), then moves right to the table. It reads as an internal SaaS analytics page for a growth or product team. It feels calm, tidy and competent, and also generic: it shows the numbers but doesn't tell me what they mean.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal dashboard page that tells a growth or product team which plan turns trials into paying customers best, and whether that's good enough to act on.

**Verdict.** It's clean and honest but it doesn't explain anything. It shows four rates and leaves the viewer to work out whether they're good, whether the differences are real, and what the definition even counts. The biggest gap is that nothing on the page gives a point of reference: no previous quarter, no target, no overall rate.

**What would move it most**

1. **The numbers have no comparison, so the viewer can't tell good from bad.** The four bar labels (3.1%, 3.6%, 4.0%, 3.4%) stand alone, and the page covers a single quarter. A viewer who sees "Growth 4.0%" can't tell whether that went up, went down or missed a target, which is exactly the question a conversion page exists to answer. There isn't even an overall rate to compare each plan against; from the table it works out to about 3.4% (627 paid of 18,420 trials), so Enterprise sits right on average and Starter is the only plan below it. *Defect* (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a headline number needs a unit, a period and a comparison). Fix: put a comparison next to every bar label, such as last quarter's rate or the change from it. Draw the all-plans average as a reference line across the chart, and add a total row to the table.

2. **The ranking looks firmer than the data allows.** The bars show Growth beating Team by 0.4 points and Team beating Enterprise by 0.2, as clean steps. But Enterprise is 38 conversions out of 1,120, which is roughly ±1 point of sampling noise. Growth (118 of 2,950) is roughly ±0.7 points. Apart from Starter's clear deficit, the order may just be noise. Someone who reprices or pushes the Growth plan because "it converts best" may be acting on noise. The table gives the sample sizes, but nothing connects them to the bars. *Defect* (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16: show uncertainty for sampled rates). Fix: add error bars or intervals to the bars, or at least a line under the chart saying which differences are real.

3. **The title names a topic, not a finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" just describes the chart. The finding (Growth converts best and Starter worst, all within one point of each other) is left for the viewer to work out. *Taste* (no approved source states this rule, but on a page people visit to answer a question, the title is the cheapest place to give the answer). Fix: once findings 1 and 2 are settled, state what the data actually supports, e.g. "Starter trials convert least; other plans are within noise of each other."

4. **The definition can be read two ways, and it decides the metric.** The subtitle says "became paid accounts within 30 days". The definition says "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." Reading one: there's a 30-day window from each trial's start. Reading two: there's one cutoff of 30 October for every trial, so a July trial had about three months to convert and a late-September trial had about 30 days. Under reading two, the rates mix different windows, and anyone comparing against another quarter gets a different number. *Defect* (copy). Fix: state one rule plainly, e.g. "A trial counts as converted if its first invoice is paid within 30 days of the trial starting." Drop the 30 October sentence unless it means something different, and if it does, say what.

**Smaller things.**
- Right column top: "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" starts about 9 px below the top of the main title, which reads as an alignment mistake rather than a choice. Align it with the title's cap height or the subtitle's baseline.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. Bars start at zero, the gridlines are pale, there's one hue, and the data labels sit on the bars. Keep this restraint when you add the comparison and the intervals.
- Bars go in plan-tier order (Starter → Enterprise), which matches how the team thinks about the plans. Sorting by value would lose that.
- Showing trials and paid counts next to the rates, plus a source line with exact dates, means anyone can check the arithmetic (it does check out). Most dashboards hide this; keep it.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: the y-axis and the 5.0% gridline repeat what the bar labels already say. Taste: drop them, and use that vertical space to show last quarter as a ghost bar behind each plan. The chart then tells a story of change, which is what a conversion team acts on (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*: spend the ink on data).
2. The lower half of the right column is empty under "Definition". Taste: this is where a short annotated takeaway or a "what changed since Q2" note belongs. That gives the page one sentence a reader could forward, and it fills the space with purpose instead of leaving it looking unfinished.
