**First read.** The eye goes to the headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan", drops to the tallest bar (Growth, 4.0%), then moves along the other three. It reads as an internal SaaS analytics page for a growth or product team. It feels calm, clean and credible, and then it stops: nothing on the page tells you what to make of a spread from 3.1% to 4.0%.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal analytics view where a product or growth lead compares trial-to-paid conversion across plans for Q3 2025 and decides whether any plan needs work.

**Verdict.** The craft is good. The chart is honest, the numbers match the table, and the definition is there. But it shows the data without saying what it means, so the reader is left to judge on their own whether differences under one percentage point are real. On Enterprise's base of 38 conversions, they mostly aren't.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bars suggest a ranking the data can't support (defect).** In the four bars, Growth looks clearly best and Enterprise looks like it trails Team. Checked against the table, Enterprise is 38 paid out of 1,120 trials. A rough 95% range for that rate is about 2.3% to 4.5%, which covers every other plan. Growth (118 of 2,950) is roughly ±0.7 points. Only the Starter vs Growth gap probably holds up. With no ranges, a reader will act on an order that is mostly noise, for example by "fixing" Enterprise onboarding. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16, says to show estimates from samples with their uncertainty. Fix: add error bars or a light range to each bar, or at least put n on each bar, so the size of the sample sits next to the rate.
2. **Neither the title nor the figures say whether 3–4% is good (defect).** The headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan" only names the topic, and none of the four figures has a reference point: no Q2 value, no target, no change. A reader can't tell good from bad or up from down, which is the decision this page exists for. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") says to put a comparison next to each headline number, such as the last period or the target. Fix: give each bar a Q2 marker or a "+0.4 pts vs Q2" label. Then rewrite the headline as the finding, once the finding survives finding 1, for example "Growth converts best; other plans within noise". The point about the headline is my judgement, not a cited rule: a title that states the finding saves every reader from working it out.

**Smaller things.**
- The source line ("trial events, 1 July to 30 September 2025") covers when trials started, but the Definition says conversions are counted up to 30 October. Say "trials started 1 Jul–30 Sep, payments to 30 Oct" so the source and the window agree. This is common sense; no catalog entry covers it.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and calm: bars start at zero, gridlines are faint, there's one colour and no legend, and every bar carries its value. Keep that restraint when you add ranges and comparisons.
- The table beside the chart gives the raw counts, and they check out (286/9,210 = 3.1%, 38/1,120 = 3.4%). This is the right instinct, and it's the material for finding 1.
- The Definition block says exactly what "converted" means and how open trials are treated. Most dashboards leave this out. Keep it.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: commit to the Growth bar as the story. If the gap holds after the ranges go in, make Growth the only bar in full purple, grey the other three, and add a single annotation on that bar. The page is for a decision, and one emphasised bar lets the decision be read at a glance. This is taste.
2. Put the conversion rate in the table as a third column next to Trials and Paid. The right column would then read as a full summary on its own, and it would fill some of the empty space under Definition with something useful. This is taste.
