**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the opening screen of a feature article on a local-interest web magazine. Its job is to pull a casual visitor into a long read and keep them going.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and readable enough, but it doesn't sell the story. The writing is doing all the work. The page credits photographs and shows none, so the feature opens as a plain wall of grey text. The body lines also run long enough to tire the eye over a long read.

**What would move it most**
1. **The photographs the byline promises aren't on the screen.** This is a defect, unless the first photo sits just below the fold. Under the headline, "Photographs by the residents" tells the reader that pictures are part of this feature, yet the first screen is text only. The bottom quarter of the screen is empty too. The reader meets a promise the page doesn't keep, and the most persuasive thing this story has (pictures of beans and squash growing in a car park, taken by the people who grow them) does nothing to draw them in. The headline already sets up an image ("the end of the bus line"), and the picture should answer it. Fix: put a lead photo on the first screen, between the byline and the first paragraph or above the headline, wider than the text column. If the photos are meant to sit lower down, at least one has to show before the reader scrolls. Otherwise drop the credit from the opening.
2. **The body lines are too long for a long read.** This breaks a convention. The text column runs from about x=280 to x=1000. The first line ("Ten years ago … Today forty") looks like it runs about 80–85 characters, and the others are similar. Bringhurst puts the comfortable range at 45–75 characters, about 66 ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). On a three-paragraph screen that's minor. Across a full feature, the eye starts losing its place on the way back to the start of each line. Fix: narrow the column to about 620–660 px at this type size, or set the body a size larger in the same column. Measure the characters per line to confirm.
3. **The headline breaks badly, leaving "line" alone.** This is a defect at the one place everyone looks. "bus / line" splits a two-word noun phrase. The second headline line holds a single short word, so the title's shape is a long bar with a stub under it, and the stub draws the eye. It also weakens the headline's payoff, because "the end of the bus line" is the phrase that sets the scene. Fix: control the break so the phrase stays together, for example "The allotment at the end / of the bus line". Or adjust the size or measure so it breaks there naturally. A balanced-wrap setting on headings does this. (Bringhurst and Hochuli cover breaks in general; the heading case is common sense.)
