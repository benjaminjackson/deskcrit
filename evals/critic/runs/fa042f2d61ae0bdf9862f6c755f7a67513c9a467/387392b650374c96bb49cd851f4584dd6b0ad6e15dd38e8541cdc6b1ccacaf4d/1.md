**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "New sign-ups by channel", drops to the tall Organic search bar, and steps down the bars from left to right. Then it crosses to the side note. It reads at once as an internal growth report screen for a marketing team. It feels calm, plain and credible, with nothing shouting.

**What it's for.** No brief was given, so I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard page. Its job is to tell the growth or marketing team and leadership where Q3 sign-ups came from, and to justify a budget change for Q4.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made, honest chart that does most of its job. The one real gap is that the page's main claim depends on a change since Q2, and the chart shows only Q3. The reader has to take the most important number on trust.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart can't show the change the page is about.** The side note's main claim is "a third of all new accounts, up from a quarter in Q2". Its second claim justifies moving budget, and that is also a judgment relative to past performance. Yet the chart has one quarter, one bar per channel, and no Q2 value anywhere. The reader sees a big Organic bar but cannot see that it grew, how much the others fell, or whether Events was always this small. The figure that drives a budget decision sits in prose with nothing on screen behind it. Few's dashboard guidance is that a headline number needs a comparison, such as the previous period (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). *Convention, and here a defect, because the page asks for a decision.* Fix direction: bring Q2 onto the chart. A pale grey Q2 mark or bar behind each blue bar would work, or a small "Q2: 1,…" label under each value. Then the "up from a quarter" claim is visible, not asserted.

**Smaller things.**
- The side note heading "What changed this quarter" starts at about y 175, about 20 px above the chart's top gridline and level with nothing on the left. It reads as a near-miss rather than a choice. Align its top with the chart's top line (the 2,000 gridline) or with the subtitle (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).

**What's working.**
- **An honest, clean chart.** The axis starts at zero, the bars are sorted by value, every bar is labelled with its value, the gridlines are faint, and there is only one hue. The reader gets exact numbers and the ranking at a glance with no clutter (Tufte, data-ink). Keep it that restrained when you add Q2.
- **The numbers agree.** The bars sum to the 5,400 in the source line, 1,840 is in fact about a third, and podcast plus events is 670, under the 700 claimed. The source line gives the exact date range. That is why the page feels trustworthy, so protect it.
- **The side note says what the chart means and what happens next** ("The events budget moves to referrals in Q4"). That turns a report into a decision, which most dashboards never do.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let the title carry the finding. Rename "New sign-ups by channel" to something like "Organic search now brings a third of sign-ups", so someone who reads only the headline still gets the point. *Taste.* The side note does this job today, but it sits second in reading order.
2. Make the existing blue mean something. The note is about two things: Organic's rise, and Podcast ads plus Events falling short. Keep blue on Organic search, and set the other bars in a muted blue-grey, or tint Podcast ads and Events differently. The chart would then point at the same bars the text talks about. *Taste* (Few and Wilke on highlighting one series and greying the rest).
3. Show the Q4 decision on the chart itself. Mark Referral, the channel getting the events budget, with a small note at its bar. The reader would then see the source (Events, 260) and the destination (Referral, 1,210) side by side. *Taste.* This page exists to justify that move.
