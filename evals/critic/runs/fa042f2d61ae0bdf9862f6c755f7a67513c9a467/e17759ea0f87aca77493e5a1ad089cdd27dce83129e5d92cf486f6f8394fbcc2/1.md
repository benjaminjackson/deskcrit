**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Shipments peak each third quarter", drops to the row of eight identical blue bars, and settles on the tallest one (705). It reads at once as a clean, calm board-deck slide from a sensible company. It feels competent and quiet, and a little flat: nothing on the chart helps you find the peaks the headline talks about.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is page 3 of a 12-page board pack. It shows directors quarterly unit shipments, so they can understand the seasonal pattern and what is expected for Q4 2026.

**Verdict.** The slide is well made, and a director reads it in seconds. But it shows a forecast exactly like the actual results. It also states a finding (the Q3 peaks) that the chart doesn't point to. In front of a board, the unmarked forecast is the thing that will cause trouble.

**What would move it most**
1. **The Q4 2026 forecast bar looks exactly like the seven actual results.** It sits at the far right: the same solid blue, the same bold "560" label. The only sign that it's a forecast is a bracket at the end of the subtitle and a footnote in the smallest text on the page. A director scanning the chart reads 560k as something that has already happened. That is the number they will quote back, and later hold the team to, if Q4 comes in at 520. A projection should look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Draw the bar as an outline or a light tint of the blue, and put "forecast" on the bar or under its axis label. If the September forecast has a range, show it. *Defect.*
2. **The chart doesn't show the headline's claim.** The headline says "peak each third quarter", but the two Q3 bars are drawn like every other bar and sit four bars apart. The viewer has to scan the labels, find Q3 twice and check that each is a local high. The headline names one point, and the chart should hand it over. Keep the series in one colour, and save a second colour or a darker shade for the two Q3 bars (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Then the headline and the chart say the same thing at a glance. *Convention.*
3. **The headline states a pattern, not what it means for the board.** Seasonality is probably something these directors already know. The news is also in the data: every quarter of 2026 is up on the same quarter of 2025. Q1 went from 410 to 455 (+11%), Q2 from 520 to 590 (+13%), Q3 from 610 to 705 (+16%), and Q4 is forecast to go from 480 to 560 (+17%). As written, the headline leaves the board to work out the growth, and some of them won't. Make it land, for example by saying the peak keeps rising, or by adding the year-on-year growth to the subtitle. *Taste, but it decides whether the slide makes a point or just reports one.*

**Smaller things.**
- The subtitle says "Q4 2026" twice ("…to Q4 2026 (Q4 2026 is the forecast)"). Once the bar itself is marked as a forecast, cut the bracket.

**What's working.**
- The type plan is tight: a bold serif headline over a single sans for everything else. There are four clearly separate levels (headline, subtitle, chart title with unit, labels), so nothing competes with the headline.
- The chart is honest and light. Bars start at zero, gridlines are faint, values sit directly on the bars and the unit ("Thousands of units") is stated once. Keep this restraint when you add the forecast and Q3 marking.
- The frame is disciplined. Wordmark, headline, source line and page number all share the same left and right edges, which suits a 12-page pack where every page should feel like the same document.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: let one number lead. 705, the Q3 2026 peak, is already the tallest bar. A stronger version would put it, or its +16% on Q3 2025, in the headline or the subtitle. That gives the board one figure to remember, and the chart becomes the proof. *Taste.*
2. Put the two years side by side. The headline claims something repeats every year, and eight bars in a row make you compare across a gap of four bars. Grouping the bars by quarter, with 2025 and 2026 next to each other, would show the seasonal shape and the year-on-year growth in one view (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, on choosing the form that fits the comparison). *Taste.*
