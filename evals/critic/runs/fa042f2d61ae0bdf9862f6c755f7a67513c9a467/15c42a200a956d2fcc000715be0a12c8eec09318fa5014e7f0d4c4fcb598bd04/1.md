**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "River ferry timetable", drops to the grey subtitle, then runs down the two time columns. It reads as a calm, plain schedule for people who ride the ferry. Then the sentence under the table jars: it complains about "heavy black lines around every cell", but the dividers on screen are faint grey and only horizontal.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's there to let a rider at north landing find the next sailing that runs on the day they're travelling, and see when it gets to market quay.

**Verdict.** The table is clean and easy to read, and it mostly does its job. Two things hold it back. The sentence under it describes a table that isn't there. And the information that decides whether a rider can take a sailing, the days it runs, is in the last column, while the first column holds a label that only repeats the time.

**What would move it most**
1. **The caption contradicts the table (defect, common sense; no catalog entry).** The line at y≈695, "The heavy black lines around every cell make this timetable a mess", describes boxed cells with heavy borders. This table has light grey horizontal rules and no vertical ones. A rider reading it will look for a problem that isn't there, or wonder whether they're looking at an old version. If it's a leftover review note, delete it. If something is meant to sit under the table, put the information a rider needs there: where the times apply, the season's dates, or who to call when sailings are cancelled.
2. **The day it runs is buried, and the Period column says nothing the times don't (defect).** "Runs" is the column that tells a rider whether a sailing exists on their day. It sits at the far right, about 600 px from the departure time it qualifies. "Weekends" next to 19:30 is the easiest thing on the page to miss, and missing it means someone waits on a Tuesday evening for a boat that doesn't come. Meanwhile the first column, the first thing read on each row, spends its space on "Morning, Morning, Midday, Midday…", which 06:40 and 12:40 already say. This is the kind of thing that "carries no information [and] competes with the content" (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 4; Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, "Data tables"). Fix: drop Period, or turn it into a small group label over a block of rows. Then move the running days next to the departure time, and set Weekdays and Weekends apart from Daily, by weight for example, so the exceptions stand out.

**Smaller things.**
- Time columns: the figures look proportional. In the crop, 11:00 and 17:10 are visibly narrower than 06:40, so the column edges wobble. Use tabular figures (Bringhurst 4.4.3).

**What's working.**
- The ruling is right. Pale hairlines between rows, no vertical lines and no boxes. That's exactly what Lupton recommends for data tables, and it lets the times carry the page. Keep it, whatever the caption says.
- The hierarchy is clear and economical. A bold title, a smaller grey subtitle, column headers in bold at the same size as the body. That's about three sizes, separated by weight rather than near-identical sizes.
- The subtitle "North landing to market quay, spring season" answers direction and season before the first time is read.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Taste: give the arrival column a little less weight than the departure column. A rider decides by when they leave and only checks when they arrive. The leading time would then read as the leading number on each row.
2. Builds on finding 2. Taste: let the day patterns carry the structure. For example, put the Daily sailings first, then the Weekdays and Weekends exceptions, or add a slim day marker on each row. A rider could then answer "what runs today?" by scanning one band instead of every row.
