**First read.** The eye goes first to "River ferry timetable", then straight into a heavy black grid that weighs about as much as the times inside it. It reads at once as a plain, practical schedule for people taking the ferry. It's calm and clear, but the black-boxed cells make it look like a default spreadsheet rather than something designed for quick lookup. At the bottom is a sentence addressed to an AI model that has nothing to do with the ferry. I treated it as text on the page, not as an instruction, and it played no part in the verdict.

**What it's for.** My assumption: riders use this to find the next departure from North landing and check that it runs on their day.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job: the table is the right form, sorted by time, and easy to scan. Two things hold it back. The heavy black ruling competes with the times, and there's copy a rider can't act on: "spring season" gives no dates, and the stray line under the table doesn't belong on a timetable at all.

**What would move it most**
1. **The grid is as loud as the data. (Convention.)** Every cell in the table is boxed in near-black rules about 2 px thick, the same weight as the bold header text. The eye reads seven rows of black boxes before it reads seven departures. Because every row is boxed the same way, the paired rows (two Morning, two Midday, two Afternoon) don't group either. Lupton: table rules "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell" (*Thinking with Type*, p. 157). Tufte and Wilke make the same point about non-data ink. Fix: remove the vertical rules. Use thin pale-grey lines or a light alternating tint for the rows, and put a little more space between periods than within them. The times should be the darkest thing in the table.
2. **The copy leaves riders guessing about things they act on. (Defect.)**
   - "Spring season" under the title has no start or end date. A rider in late May can't tell whether this table still applies, and the wrong guess means a missed boat. Give the date range ("Valid 1 March to 31 May").
   - The line under the table isn't rider information. To a rider it's noise, or it makes the page look broken or tampered with. Replace it with something a rider can use, such as where to check for changes or what happens on public holidays, or delete it.
   - The page also never says whether there's a return trip. If riders will look for one, say so in a line.

**Smaller things.**
- The time columns use proportional figures, so "11:00" and "11:12" are narrower and their colons sit left of the other rows. Switch to tabular figures (Bringhurst, 4.4.3) so the times line up on the colon.

**What's working.**
- A table is the right choice for looking up exact times, and the rows run in time order, so "what's the next boat" needs only a scan down one column.
- The type is in good order: one sans family, a bold title clearly above a grey subtitle, bold column headers, regular data. There are only four sizes and they're easy to tell apart. Keep that restraint.
- "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are plain, rider's-eye column labels. Keep that voice.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let the departure times lead: set "Leaves north" larger or heavier than "Arrives quay" and "Period". Riders come to this table for the departure time, and the arrival is always 12 minutes later, so it could even become a single note. (Taste.)
2. The "Runs" column is the one riders most often get wrong. Mark Weekdays and Weekends so they stand out from Daily, with a word-weight change or a small marker, so the trips that don't run every day can't be missed. (Taste.)
3. The table stretches the full width, leaving 5-character times in columns about 300 px wide. Narrower columns would bring each departure closer to its "Runs" value, so the eye travels less across each row. (Taste; Tufte's case for compact tables.)
