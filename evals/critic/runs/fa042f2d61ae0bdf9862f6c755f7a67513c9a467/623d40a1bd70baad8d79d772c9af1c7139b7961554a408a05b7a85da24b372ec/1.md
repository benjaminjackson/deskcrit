**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It drops onto "Changing your pickup time" only because it's in the top-left corner, then reads down line by line like one long note, because nothing tells headings from answers. It reads as a help or FAQ page for a bakery's online ordering, but only after reading. It feels calm, plain and unfinished, like an unstyled draft.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section on a bakery's ordering site, where a customer with one question finds the answer quickly.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. A customer scanning for "cancel" or "allergies" has to read every line, because the four headings are set the same as their answers. The last line on the page says the opposite of what the page shows.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings aren't headings** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are in the same regular weight and the same near-black as the answers. They're also slightly smaller (about 17 px against 18 px, by eye). The only cue is a 10 px gap above each answer against about 35 px between items, which is too weak to scan by. The whole job of a help page is letting someone skip to their one question, and right now they can't. There's also no page title, so nothing says "Ordering help" before the reader has decoded the first item. Dair (*Design with Type*, ch. 8) says a difference has to be obvious at a glance, and a one-step difference looks like an accident. Krug says a page like this should be scannable. Fix: make the headings clearly different from the answers. Semibold is the easiest way, perhaps with a step up in size. Put a page title above them as the one dominant element, so the order runs title, questions, answers. Three levels from one scale are enough.
2. **The closing line is a leftover note, and it's false** (defect). "Each heading here stands out clearly, so the page is easy to scan." (y≈493) talks about the page, not to the customer. It's set as body text, so it reads as a fifth answer, and it claims the very thing finding 1 shows isn't true. On a live page it makes the shop look unfinished. It does no job for the customer (Nielsen 8, aesthetic and minimalist design). Fix: delete it. If the page needs an ending, use something the customer can act on, such as how to reach the bakery.
3. **The answers to "Collecting for someone else" and "Cancelling an order" can be read two ways** (defect). These are the lines a customer acts on and will later hold the shop to.
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name": whose name, theirs or yours? If the friend gives their own name and staff expect the account holder's, the bread doesn't leave the shop.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead": 24 hours before the pickup time, or any time the day before? Someone cancelling at 9 pm for a 9 am pickup could read it either way and lose their money.
   - The cancelling answer also never says *how* to cancel. Changing the pickup time gets "Open your order…", but cancelling gets no instructions.

   Fix: spell out the name ("the name on the order"), the deadline ("until 24 hours before your pickup time"), and the action ("Open your order and choose Cancel").
4. **The allergy answer only works in the shop** (defect). "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps someone standing at the counter. Someone ordering online from home gets nothing before they buy, and that's the person who needs it most. Fix: say where the ingredients are listed in the online order, or give a way to ask before ordering.

**Smaller things.**
- Lines run about 95 to 100 characters ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…"), well past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. Narrow the column to about 640 px at this size.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergy answer. Rewording, or the narrower column, will fix it.
- "choose a new slot from the list": which list? Name it (the time list in your order).

**What's working.**
- The answers are short, plain and written to "you". Each one fits in a line or two, which is the right length for help copy. Keep that voice when fixing the unclear lines.
- The type is calm and readable: one sans-serif in one colour, a comfortable body size, generous line spacing and a clean left edge. Once the headings differ, nothing else about the typesetting needs to change.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Phrase the headings as the customer's own questions ("Can someone else collect my order?"). Someone arrives here with a question in their head, and it's quicker to match it word for word than to translate a category label. Taste.
2. Put "Cancelling an order" first, or pull the deadline out as its own short line in bold. It's the one answer that costs the customer money if they miss it, and on this page the deadline should be the thing the eye lands on. Taste.
