**First read.** The eye lands on the purple Growth bar first, then the heading. It reads as an internal SaaS analytics view about which plans convert trials best. The immediate takeaway is "Growth converts far better than Starter, roughly two and a half times as well." The page is calm, clean and credible, and that is why the misreading in the chart sticks.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard page. It helps product and growth staff compare how well each plan turns trials into paying accounts, and decide where to act.

**Verdict.** The page is well made, but the chart misleads. Its bar axis starts at 2.5%, so a 0.9-point spread looks like a several-fold gap, and that wrong impression is the main thing a viewer takes away. Fix the scale first. Everything else is secondary.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bar chart's value axis starts at 2.5%, not zero (defect, and a broken convention).** Growth's bar rises about 1.5 points above the baseline and Starter's about 0.6. On screen Growth looks about 2.5 times as tall, but in the data it is 4.0% against 3.1%, about 1.3 times. That puts Tufte's lie factor at around 5. The labels on the bars are correct, but people judge bars by their length before they read the numbers. Anyone glancing at this in a meeting will repeat "Growth converts more than twice as well," which is false. Bar length carries the value, so bars start at zero (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 17.1; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2). Fix it in one of two ways. Start the axis at 0%, which honestly shows four similar rates. Or, if the small differences really are the story, switch to a dot plot on a scale that doesn't start at zero and label it clearly.

2. **The chart shows small differences as if they were certain, and Enterprise rests on 38 conversions (defect).** The table shows how thin some of the numbers are. Enterprise is 38 of 1,120 and Growth is 118 of 2,950. Rough 95% ranges are about ±1 point for Enterprise and ±0.7 for Growth, so the Team, Growth and Enterprise rates probably overlap. Only Starter vs Growth looks like a real gap. Because the chart gives no hint of this, a reader may move budget toward Growth or away from Enterprise on what could be noise (Wilke, ch. 16). Add error bars or ranges to the marks, or at least a note on sample size under the chart. That way the viewer can tell a real difference from a coincidence.

**Smaller things.**
- **Chart title:** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" names the topic, not the finding. Once the scale is honest, state what the chart shows. For example: "Growth converts best; Starter lags by about a point." This is common sense; the source for it isn't on the approved list.
- **No comparison to a previous period or target:** with no Q2 figure, target, or change shown, the viewer can't tell whether 3.1% is good or bad, or getting better. Put the previous quarter or the target next to each rate, for example as a faint tick or as a column in the table (Few, pitfall 2).

**What's working.**
- **Direct labels on every bar, no legend, faint gridlines, one hue:** the chart is nearly all data. Keep this restraint when you rebuild the scale.
- **The Definition block and the source line:** they say exactly when a trial counts as converted, how open trials are treated, and where the data comes from. That is unusual and worth keeping. It is what makes the numbers trustworthy.
- **The table beside the chart:** right-aligned, tabular figures with quiet row rules. It puts the raw counts beside the rates, which is how the reader can check the chart.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2. Taste. Add a conversion-rate column with ranges to the "Trials by plan" table, so each row reads trials → paid → rate. The table then becomes the lookup and the chart the overview. A reader checking the chart wouldn't need to divide 118 by 2,950 in their head, and the table would also justify having "Paid" in a section headed "Trials".
2. Taste. Let the purple pick out one bar and turn the others grey: the plan the team is acting on, or the outlier. The purple is already the brand colour, from the nav to the bars. With four same-colour bars, the chart says "here are four numbers." With one highlighted, it says "look here," and that is what a dashboard page is for.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bars or table rows have hover details or tooltips that show sample sizes or ranges. If they do, finding 2 is less serious, but still not fixed for anyone who only glances.
