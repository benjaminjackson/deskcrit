**First read.** The eye goes first to the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month". It then drops to the June bar, which towers over the others, and then crosses to $4.82M. It reads as a credit union's half-year report to its members. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, if a little generic. The striped bars are the first thing that feels off: they shimmer.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a page on the credit union's site that tells members how savings did in the first half of 2026, read at a glance on a desktop.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The layout is clear and the figures add up: the bars sum to $4.82M, and $1,150k divided by 5,374 accounts gives the $214 average. The biggest problem is that the June bar and all three June cards present a one-off bonus payroll as growth. The only place that says so is a grey footnote, so a member leaves believing something the report doesn't actually claim.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June spike tells a different story from the headline, and the bonus explanation is hidden.** January to May rises by about $40k to $80k a month. June then jumps $282k, or 32%, so the June bar becomes the visual claim of the page. The reason is the "mid-year bonus payroll", and that appears only in the 12 px grey source line below the chart. All three cards on the right measure June, the bonus month: average deposit, accounts that made a deposit, and the $1,150k behind them. So they show the bonus, not a trend. They are compared with June 2025 without saying whether June 2025 also had a bonus. A member will read "deposits surged in June" and "the average deposit is $214", which are both claims the report doesn't support. This is a defect: it misleads the main reader on the main number.
   - **The fix:** label the June bar on the chart itself, for example "includes bonus payroll", and give it a distinct treatment.
   - **For the cards:** either base them on a typical month or on the half-year, or say in the label that June includes the bonus and whether June 2025 did too.
   - **The headline:** it is still true without June, so it can stay.

2. **The diagonal hatching on every bar.** The blue-and-white stripes carry no meaning, because every bar has them. At this density they shimmer, and they make the bars look lighter and less solid than the numbers they stand for. A pattern fill on a chart also usually signals "estimate" or "partial". That would be a wrong reading here, and it is exactly the signal you could use for June. Patterned fills that show no data are chartjunk (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 11; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5). This is a convention break, and it costs something. Use solid flat blue, and keep a second treatment for the June bar if you do finding 1.

3. **Six months with nothing to compare against.** The chart shows only January to June 2026. A reader can't tell whether "rose every month" is real growth or just the normal pattern of every year. The 6.1% year-on-year figure is in a card, but last year's monthly shape isn't on the chart. My view, as common sense rather than a cited rule: add the first half of 2025 as a pale grey series behind the bars, or as a tick on each bar. Members could then judge the rise themselves instead of trusting the headline.

**Smaller things.**
- **Bar labels and axis:** "$1150k" and "$1200k" sit next to "$4.82M" in the first card, so the page mixes two units for the same quantity. Use $1.15M and $1.2M, or put thousands separators on all of them.
- **Card wording:** "Up $11 on Jun 2025" can be read as a date ("up $11 on [some day in] June 2025"). "Up $11 from June 2025" or "vs June 2025" reads one way only. This matters because these are the figures members will quote.
- **Right column:** the third card ends about 75 px above the bottom of the chart panel, which leaves a leftover gap. Either stretch the cards to the chart's height or move the source line up under both columns. Common sense.

**What's working.**
- The headline states a finding instead of a topic, and the subtitle says exactly what is being measured: new money, by month, and the date range. Keep that pairing.
- The bars carry direct value labels, the axis starts at $0, and the gridlines are pale. The chart is honest in scale and quick to read.
- The wordmark and headline are in a serif, and the figures are in a plain sans. That reads as a trustworthy institution without effort, and each card's label → number → change order is consistent and scannable.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: make the June bonus part of the story rather than a footnote. For example, add a short annotation that reads "Without the bonus payroll, June would have been about $X". Members then see both the underlying growth and the one-off, and the report earns trust by being straight with them. This is taste.
2. Let the $4.82M lead. It is the one number that sums up the half-year, but right now it competes with the headline and two other cards of equal size. Making it the clear hero of the right column, with the two June cards smaller beneath it, would give the page a single takeaway number. This is taste (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*, on emphasis by de-emphasising the rest).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bars have accessible text or table equivalents for screen readers, and how the two-column layout reflows on a phone.
