**First read.** The eye goes straight to the landscape, to the bright meadow and the bend of the river in the lower middle. Only after that does it find the headline, which is white type fading into a white sky. It reads as the home page of a small rural lodge, and it feels calm, open and unhurried. Then it stops: there's nothing to do next.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a desktop home page for a small cabin lodge, meant to make someone planning a quiet trip want to book.

**Verdict.** The photo and the copy create a real sense of place, but the page doesn't yet work as a lodge home page. The headline loses to the sky behind it, and the page never asks the visitor to book. Both have to be fixed before anything else matters.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline and the lead disappear into the sky (defect).** "Wake up above the river" is white 40 px Inter set over pale haze and cloud, which are nearly white. "the river" is the faintest part, and on a quick look the headline almost isn't there. The lead paragraph starts over the same haze ("porch facing east" sits on bright sky) and only firms up where it crosses the darker treeline. The facts file can't measure contrast over the image (`contrast: null`, "not measurable: background image" for `h1` and `div.copy > p`), but by eye the worst point behind the letters is near white on white. That's the case W3C's F83 describes. The result is that the page's one strong idea arrives late or not at all, and the photo takes over as the first read. The fix: put a dark scrim or gradient behind the top-left of the hero, or move the type over the dark band of hills, so that every letter stands out from what is directly behind it.
2. **Nothing on the page asks for a booking (defect).** "Book" is the third of three identical 15 px nav links, the same weight as "Rooms" and "Trails" (`nav > a:nth-of-type(3)`), and there is no button anywhere. The eye follows the river down and out of the bottom of the photo, reaches three info cards, and runs out of page. A visitor who has been won over has to go looking for the next step. Give the page one primary action, styled as a filled button under the lead text in the hero, and make the "Book" nav link visibly stronger than the other two (Material Design 3 and Apple HIG on button emphasis). Name what it does: "Check dates for a cabin" tells the visitor more than "Book".
3. **There's no price, season or dates, so the visitor can't judge the value (taste, common sense).** The copy is unusually good. It's concrete and specific: twelve cabins, porches facing east, breakfast from six, sleeps two to four, wood stoves, a shuttle from the train. But it never says what a night costs or when the lodge is open, and those are the two things a visitor needs to decide whether to click. A single line such as "From £___ a night, April to October" beside the button would turn the mood into a decision.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: four sizes, 40/19/17/15 px (`type_sizes`). The 19 px logo, the 17 px card headings and lead, and the 15 px body text sit only 2 px apart, so they read as near-misses rather than levels. Keep 40, body text and one heading size, and separate the card headings by weight, which they already are.
- Card text: "stove." and "station." are left alone on the last lines in the Rooms and Getting here cards (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrite slightly or adjust the measure so the lines end evenly.
- Headline tracking: the 40 px bold headline looks like it's set with text spacing and reads a little loose. Tighten it slightly (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*; Bringhurst 2.1.1).
- Nav: nothing marks the current page. That's minor on a home page, but add it before the other pages exist.

**What's working.**
- The copy has real specifics, like a porch facing east, breakfast from six, "in time to see the sun reach the far wall", and a shuttle that meets the morning train. It sounds like a real place, not a template. Keep that voice when you add price and the ask.
- The photo and the headline make one idea. "Wake up above the river" over a view looking down on a river from high ground is exactly right, so keep the image and the line and fix only the contrast.
- The layout underneath is clean. It sits on one 64 px margin and three columns (left edges at 64/461/859), body text is at 15.8:1 contrast, the lead runs at 65 characters a line, and the card headings sit tight to their text. Nothing needs restructuring.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus styling on the nav links, and how the hero crop and the text on it behave on a phone, where the type will probably land on even more sky.
