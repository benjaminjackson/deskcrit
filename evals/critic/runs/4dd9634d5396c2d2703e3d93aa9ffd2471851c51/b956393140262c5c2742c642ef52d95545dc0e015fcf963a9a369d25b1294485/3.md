**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is fainter than the byline.** The byline "By Ellen Marsh, March 14" is set near-black at a small size. The three paragraphs below it are a mid-grey that looks like about #666. That grey probably still passes the 4.5:1 contrast minimum (WCAG 1.4.3), so this is not an accessibility failure. But the levels are upside down: the meta line outranks the text by darkness, and the part people read for minutes looks washed out next to a line they glance at once. Over a long how-to, pale body text makes reading more tiring. This breaks a convention: each level should be distinct from the next, and in the right order (Müller-Brockmann). The fix is to set the body in the near-black of the headline, or close to it, and to move the byline down instead, either lighter grey or smaller. Grey belongs on the metadata, not the reading text.
2. **Nothing in the look says "stone walls" or "Field Notes."** This is taste. The wordmark, nav, sans-serif face and grey-on-white column are a default blog setup. That costs the piece its character: the writing has a real voice ("until one spring morning a whole section sags toward the lane"), but the page gives a reader no reason to remember this publication over any other. The direction is one deliberate choice, not decoration. For example, use a text face with some warmth for the body or headline, or a single earthy accent colour in the wordmark and links. Keep the restraint, but give it a point of view.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav (top right): nothing marks which section this article belongs to, so a reader can't tell whether they're in Stories or Guides. Mark the current item with one cue, such as weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Byline date: "March 14" has no year. For a repair guide, readers need to know how current the advice is. Add the year. (Common sense.)

**What's working.**
- **The reading column.** It looks like about 60–65 characters per line, generous leading, flush left, and paragraphs marked by space alone. This is the right setting for long instructional text; keep the width when you revise.
- **The headline.** It is set tight and bold, and it breaks into two lines that hold together. It is unmistakably the place to start, and its copy names a concrete situation the reader is in ("after a hard winter") rather than a topic.
- **The copy.** Each paragraph tells the reader what to do and why it matters to them ("the cheapest repair you will ever make"). That is exactly what a how-to should do.
