**First read.** The eye lands on the bold two-line headline, drops to the byline, then goes into the body. The body text is a mid grey and the byline just above it is near-black, so for a moment the author and date look heavier than the article. It reads as a calm, one-column how-to piece on a small editorial site. It's quiet and competent, and fairly anonymous.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an article page for a practical how-to, read start to finish by hobbyists or landowners who have a wall to fix.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The reading column is well proportioned, and the headline is clearly what you see first. The main thing in the way is the colour order: the body text, which is the whole point of the page, is set lighter than the metadata above it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is paler than the byline.** The three paragraphs are mid grey and "By Ellen Marsh, March 14" is near-black, so the byline is darker than everything below it. That flips the order of importance: the line that matters least looks firmer than the instructions. Over three paragraphs, the grey also makes long reading slightly harder work than it needs to be. The grey probably still passes the WCAG 1.4.3 minimum of 4.5:1, but measure it to be sure. Even if it passes, nothing is gained by greying it here. Each level of a page should be easy to tell from the next and come in the right order (Müller-Brockmann; Lupton). Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*, use lighter colour to push secondary text down, not the main text. **Fix:** set the body in the near-black of the headline or close to it. If the byline needs to sit lower, make it the grey one. *Defect:* it inverts the hierarchy and costs reading comfort.

**Smaller things.**
- **Headline line break:** "after / a hard winter" splits the phrase and leaves "after" hanging at the end of line one. Break before "after", or keep "after a hard winter" together with a non-breaking space. This is a convention, and my sourcing for it is thin: treat it as taste.
- **Navigation:** Stories, Guides and About all look the same, so a reader can't tell which section this article belongs to. Mark the current section with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Nielsen Norman Group, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist"). *Convention.*
- **Byline date:** "March 14" has no year. For seasonal repair advice, the reader can't tell how current it is. Add the year. *Common sense, about the copy.*
- **Generic look (taste):** Inter on white with a hairline header could be any blog. Nothing in the type or colour hints at stone, fields or notebooks, so the site doesn't stay with the reader. This isn't a defect for reading. It's a missed chance for character, most cheaply in the "Field Notes" wordmark.

**What's working.**
- **Line length:** the body runs about 60 characters a line with generous line spacing, which is right in Bringhurst's comfortable range. Keep this column width.
- **Headline setting:** a heavy weight, tight line spacing, and a clear size jump to the body. The two lines read as one unit, and the eye has no doubt where to start.
- **The copy:** concrete and in the second person ("walk the full run of the wall and mark every bulge with a stick"). Every sentence tells the reader what to do and why it matters to them. Don't edit that voice away.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Size of the nav links:** they are bare text links. Check that each is at least 24×24 CSS px (WCAG 2.5.8).
- **The rest of the article:** the screenshot ends after three paragraphs. Check that longer articles break up with subheads and end with a clear next step.
