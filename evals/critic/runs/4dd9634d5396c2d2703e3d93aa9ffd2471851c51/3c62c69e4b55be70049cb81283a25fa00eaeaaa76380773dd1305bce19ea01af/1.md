**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Why the second day of a stew is better", and then into the body text with nothing in the way. It reads as a short essay on a small cooking blog. It feels calm and easy to read, but it also feels bare: grey sans-serif on white, no picture, nothing that says *kitchen*. The page also stops abruptly, with about 150 px of blank white below the last paragraph and nothing in it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an article page on a personal cooking blog, meant to be read start to finish by home cooks who then go on to read or cook something else.

**Verdict.** As a page for reading, it works. The hierarchy is clear, the measure is comfortable and the text is set well. What holds it back is that it does nothing after the reader finishes and gives no sense of the site's character, so the page reads like a default template rather than "Slow Kitchen".

**What would move it most**
1. **The article ends with nothing after it (defect for the job).** After "…nothing catches on the pan." there is only white space. There is no recipe to cook it with, no related note and no way back to Recipes. A reader who just learned to "cook it a day early" is at the point where they are most likely to act, and the page gives them nowhere to go. The eye path should run headline → text → next step, and right now it stops at the end of the text. This is common sense, not a cited rule. Fix: put one specific next step directly under the last paragraph, such as a link to a stew recipe on this site, named by the dish. A generic "More posts" would be weaker.
2. **The look doesn't match what the page is about (taste).** Near-black and grey type in a system-style sans, with hairline rules, is the default look of a SaaS docs page. The text is warm and specific (shin bones, a cold pantry, fat lifted off the top), but the design around it has none of that warmth. To a first-time visitor it could be anyone's blog. The colour should suit the subject (Itten, *The Art of Color*). Fix: give the site one warm cue of its own and keep the restraint. For example, a warmer off-white or a single warm accent on the wordmark and links, or a serif for the headline. Don't add decoration.

**Smaller things.**
- Navigation, top right: Recipes, Notes and Contact all look the same, so the reader can't tell which section this article belongs to. Mark the current section with one cue, such as bolder weight or an underline (Nielsen Norman Group, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- The headline wraps so that "better" sits alone on line 2. That is a one-word runt, and the break separates the sentence from its point. Rebalance the lines, for example "Why the second day / of a stew is better" (Bringhurst names this for paragraph last lines; using it on a headline is my judgment).
- The byline date "January 9" has no year. On an article, the reader can't tell how old the advice is. Add the year.

**What's working.**
- **The reading column.** Lines run about 65–72 characters, the line spacing is generous, paragraphs are marked by space only, and the text is flush left with a soft rag. Bringhurst's 45–75 range is met. Keep this exact setting.
- **A clear three-step hierarchy:** a big bold headline, then a small grey byline, then the body text. The levels are far enough apart that you never wonder what's what.
- **The copy.** It is concrete and in a real voice. The last paragraph turns the explanation into steps the reader can actually follow.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page reflows on a phone, and whether the nav links show hover and focus states.
