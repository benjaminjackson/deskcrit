**First read.** My eye lands on the bold headline, "Why the second day of a stew is better", drops to the byline, then settles into three short paragraphs. It reads as a personal food essay on a small cooking blog. It feels calm, tidy and easy to read, and also anonymous: apart from the words, nothing says "Slow Kitchen" rather than any other blog template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one article page on a small cooking blog, where people read a short essay and come away with a technique they trust.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The text is easy to read, the order of importance is clear and nothing gets in the way. The biggest gap is that the page has no character, so the writing has to carry the brand alone. After that comes a headline that leaves "better" stranded on its own line.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page has no voice of its own (taste).** The whole look is a neutral sans-serif in near-black and grey on white, with a bold-text wordmark and a thin rule under the header. You could swap in any blog's text and the page would look the same. The writing has a warm, unhurried, kitchen-table voice ("the sauce coats the spoon instead of running off it"), but the visual design is the default for a software product's docs. A reader won't remember where they read it, and "Slow Kitchen" never becomes a place. The direction: give the page one or two choices that match the content. A text face with some warmth for the headline or body, a single warm accent colour, and a wordmark that's more than bold Inter would all do it. Keep the restraint, which is right for reading. Itten's point that colour should support the mood of the subject is the basis, but how far to go is my judgment.
2. **The headline breaks with "better" alone on line two (convention).** At the top left of the column, "Why the second day of a stew is / better" leaves the word that carries the claim hanging by itself. The headline reads as a long line plus a leftover, and the two lines look lopsided. Break it at a phrase boundary, for example "Why the second day / of a stew is better". Alternatively, narrow the headline's measure or set it a little smaller so it breaks into two balanced lines (Bringhurst §2.4; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*). `text-wrap: balance` will usually handle this.

**Smaller things.**
- Top-right nav: Recipes, Notes and Contact all look identical, so nothing shows which section this article belongs to. Mark the current section with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Byline "January 9" has no year (common sense). This essay will be read for years, and without a year readers can't tell how old it is. Add the year.

**What's working.**
- **The reading column.** Lines run about 65–68 characters, the line spacing is generous and the paragraphs are spaced without indents. Reading is effortless (Bringhurst's 45–75 range), and that's the most important thing on this page. Keep it.
- **A clear, small type scale.** There's a bold headline, regular body text, and a smaller grey size for the byline and nav. Every level is obvious at a glance and none of the sizes nearly collide.
- **The copy.** The headline is a specific promise, and the last paragraph turns the explanation into steps you can follow tonight. That's a better ask than any button would be.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus states and the actual hit areas of the nav links.
- Whether the empty band at the bottom is the end of the page or more content (a footer, links to related recipes) waiting below. If the article really ends there, give the reader somewhere to go next.
