I've reviewed it. The page reads well; the main fix is a narrower text column.

**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Why the second day of a stew is better", drops to the byline and then straight into the body. It reads as a personal food blog or essay site: calm, competent and quiet. In the first few seconds it also feels anonymous. Apart from the words, nothing says "kitchen" or "slow"; the same layout could be a startup's changelog.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one article page on a small cooking blog, read on a desktop by someone interested in home cooking. They should read it start to finish and then want more from the same site.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The headline clearly dominates, the type is clean and the copy is excellent. Two things hold it back: the text lines are noticeably too long for comfortable reading, and the page simply stops, so a reader who enjoyed the piece has nowhere to go.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body lines are too long (convention, with a real cost).** The text column runs from about x=290 to about x=980. The first line ("A stew that rests overnight… and the reason") is about 92 characters. On every line the eye has a long way back to the next line's start, and in an essay meant to be read slowly that's the one thing that makes reading tiring. Bringhurst (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2) gives 45 to 75 characters, with 66 as the ideal. The generous line spacing (looks about 1.6) softens it but doesn't fix it. **Fix:** narrow the column to about 600 px at this size, or raise the body size a step (17–18 px) and keep the width. Either way, aim for about 65–70 characters per line. Measure the actual width once you've made the change.
2. **The page has a dead end (defect, common sense).** After "catches on the pan." at y≈540 there is only blank white: no sign-off, no link to a stew recipe, no "more notes", no footer. The article ends with an instruction ("So cook it a day early…"), which is exactly when a reader wants the recipe, and the page gives them none. Visitors who liked the piece leave instead of reading on. **Fix:** close the article with one specific next step, such as "The shin-and-red-wine stew this is about →" or two related notes, then a footer to mark the end of the page.
3. **The look says nothing about the site (taste).** Default Inter, cool near-black on white, no accent colour, a hairline under the header: this is a template look. It costs you recognition. Nothing on screen tells a returning reader they're on Slow Kitchen rather than any other blog, and the warmth and patience the copy has don't show in the design. I'm not asking for decoration. A single choice would do it: a readable serif for the body or headline, a slightly warm off-white page or one warm accent colour on links and the wordmark.

**Smaller things.**
- The navigation (Recipes / Notes / Contact, top right) doesn't mark the current section. Underline or bold the active item, presumably "Notes" (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- The byline "January 9" has no year. On an evergreen article, readers can't tell how old it is. Add the year.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** It's a specific, intriguing claim in plain words, and the size and weight step from the 32-ish px bold headline to the 16-ish px body makes it the obvious place to start. Keep both the wording style and the large contrast between levels.
- **The copy.** It's concrete and goes against expectations ("The flavors do not really marry in the way cookbooks like to say"). It ends on what you should do, using "you". This is the strongest part of the piece. Keep it.
- **The spacing.** Space between paragraphs is clearly larger than between lines, the byline sits close to the headline, and the paragraphs are flush left with no indents. Nothing needs fixing here.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indication on the nav links. How the column reflows on a phone, which matters most for a reading site.
