**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Why the second day of a stew is better", drops to the byline, and settles into three short paragraphs. It reads as one article on a small food blog: calm, quiet and easy to start. It could be any tech company's blog, though. Nothing on the page feels like a kitchen, even though the writing does.

**What it's for.** This is my assumption: an article page on a personal cooking site. Its job is to get a reader through the piece, leave them with the advice, and bring them back for more.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the reading column is calm, and the copy is specific and finished. The biggest thing holding it back is a measure that runs too long for comfortable reading. After that, the page is so plain and generic that the writer's voice gets no help from the design.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text runs too wide.** The first line of paragraph one, "A stew that rests overnight … and the reason", is about 92 characters. The other lines are similar. That is well past the 45–75 range Bringhurst gives as comfortable (*Elements of Typographic Style*, §2.1.2). Over three paragraphs the reader feels it as mild drag: the eye has a long trip back to the left edge and sometimes picks up the wrong line. On a longer article this gets worse. The fix is to bring lines to about 65–75 characters, either by narrowing the column to roughly 600 px or by raising the body to about 18 px in the same column. The larger size also suits a leisurely read. *Convention.*
2. **Nothing in the look says "slow kitchen".** The whole page is grey Inter on white, with no hue, texture or typographic character. The writing has a distinct, opinionated voice ("The flavors do not really marry in the way cookbooks like to say"). The page has none, so a reader could not tell this site from a SaaS changelog, and nothing helps them remember where they read it. This is not a call for decoration. One move would be enough: a warmer text face for the headline or body, an off-white paper ground, or one warm accent colour in the wordmark and links. Itten's point that colour temperature carries mood applies here. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Recipes", "Notes" and "Contact" all look the same, so the reader can't tell which section this article belongs to. Mark the current section with one cue, such as weight or an underline (Nielsen 1; Laubheimer, NN/g menu checklist).
- End of the article: the page stops dead after "catches on the pan." with no link to a stew recipe, related notes, or a next article. A reader who has just been told to "cook it a day early" has nowhere to go. Add one specific next step. *Common sense.*
- Byline: "January 9" has no year. On an archive that grows, readers can't tell how old the piece is. Add the year.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean. The headline is about twice the body size and bold, the byline is small and grey, and the body is plain. That makes three distinct levels with nothing competing, so keep the scale.
- The leading is generous (it looks like about 1.6) and the paragraph spacing is clearly larger than the line spacing. Each paragraph reads as a unit, and the rag is gentle with no widows.
- The copy is specific and lands on the reader. The headline makes a claim, the middle paragraph corrects a common myth, and the last paragraph gives clear steps ("Lift the hard fat off… add a splash of water… lid on").

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where the measure problem may resolve itself, and whether the nav links have visible hover and focus states.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 7 yes, 1 no (color and shape carry the mood)
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 13 yes
Text: 29 lines, 28 yes, 1 no (lines of 45 to 75 characters)
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: skipped, no pictures
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 29 yes, 2 no (shows where the user is; puts what the user needs next in front of them), 2 unsure (hover/pressed states; focus indicator)
