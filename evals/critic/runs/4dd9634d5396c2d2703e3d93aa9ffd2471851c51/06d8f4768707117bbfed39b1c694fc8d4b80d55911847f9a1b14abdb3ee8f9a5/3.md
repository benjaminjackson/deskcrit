**First read.** The eye goes straight into the valley photo: the green flats, the line of trees, the bend of river at the bottom. The headline sits in the top-left corner of the photo, and it's the last thing I made out, because it's white type on an almost-white sky. Then I tried to read the two lines under it, where the treeline runs behind the letters. It reads as a quiet, outdoorsy lodge site, calm and plain, but I had to work to find out what it says, and nothing told me what to do next.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a small lodge, meant to make a visitor want to stay and then get them to check availability and book.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The headline is unreadable where it sits, and there's nowhere to book except a plain "Book" link in the nav, so the visitor can't read the promise and isn't pointed toward any next step. The page underneath is clean and the copy is better than most. These two problems are what's in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline and lead text disappear into the photo.** *Defect.* "Wake up above the river" is white bold type over the palest part of the picture, a hazy blue-white sky. The letters look close to the sky's own value, so the headline reads as a smudge, not a statement. The first line of the lead text ("Twelve log cabins… porch facing east.") runs along the bright horizon, so the letters break up wherever they cross it. The second line is a bit better against the darker treeline. A visitor who doesn't pick out the headline gets only "nice landscape" and loses the reason to stay. Text over an image has to hold its contrast at the worst point behind it, not on average (W3C, WCAG 2.2 Technique F83). Fix: put a dark scrim or gradient behind the top-left of the photo, or move the type to a dark, even part of the picture. You could also crop the photo so the sky isn't behind the text. Check every letter against what's directly behind it. In grayscale the top band of this hero is light on light right now.
2. **There's no call to action.** *Defect.* The only way toward a booking is "Book" in the top-right nav, the same weight, size, and colour as "Rooms" and "Trails". Nothing in the hero and nothing in the three columns below offers a next step. So the eye follows the photo down to the three short columns and stops in the white band at the bottom (Arnheim: the eye path should run from the focal point to the action without dead ends). One action per view should clearly dominate (Material Design, Apple HIG, Nielsen Norman Group on button emphasis), and here there's no action to dominate. Fix: put one filled primary button in the hero, under the lead text. Name the exact thing the visitor gets, e.g. "Check dates for a cabin", not "Book now". Even better, put something next to it that lets the visitor judge the value for themselves, like a price per night or the next open dates, so they're comparing it with what they already know a weekend away costs.
3. **One line of the lead copy makes the reader guess.** *Defect (copy).* "In time to see the sun reach the far wall" — which wall? The photo shows an open valley with rolling hills and no canyon wall. A stranger will picture a building wall, a canyon, or nothing. This is the one line that turns breakfast at six into a reason to come, so it has to land. Fix: name what the sun lights up ("the far side of the valley", or the rim by name if it has one) and end on the visitor: what they'll see from their own porch. Also, "above the river" appears in both the headline and the lead line. Cut it from the second one and use the space for that payoff.

**Smaller things.**
- The lower columns: "stove." under Rooms and "station." under Getting here each sit alone on a last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Widen the columns slightly or rephrase so each ends on a fuller line.
- There's a band of about 90 px of empty white under the three columns that looks left over rather than planned. Either close it up or give it the call to action or a link onward.
- The photo looks slightly soft and compressed at this size, especially in the tree detail. Check that the source is at least 2× the display width.

**What's working.**
- The concrete copy: "Twelve log cabins", "a porch facing east", "Breakfast is served from six", "The shuttle meets the morning train". These are specific facts a guest can picture and plan around, unlike the usual "escape to nature". Keep that voice when you fix the wall line and write the button.
- The structure under the hero: one left edge shared by the logo, the headline, and the first column. Three equal columns on a clear grid. A bold heading with plain text under it. It's quiet and easy to scan, and it suits a small lodge.
- The single photo, full width, with no decoration competing with it. It sets the mood on its own. It just needs to give the text a place to sit.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the nav links, and whether the header stays fixed and covers content as you scroll.
- How the hero crop and the text hold up on a phone, where the text will very likely land on even busier parts of the picture.
