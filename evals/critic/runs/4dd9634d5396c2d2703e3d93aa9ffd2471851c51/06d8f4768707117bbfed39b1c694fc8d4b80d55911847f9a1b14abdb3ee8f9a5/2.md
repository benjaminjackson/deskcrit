**First read.** The eye goes straight into the photo, along the river and into the big pine on the right, and only then finds the headline. "Wake up above the river" is white type on an almost white sky, so it looks like a faint watermark rather than a headline. The subtitle is easier to find because its second line sits on dark trees. It reads as a calm, unshowy small-lodge homepage. Once you have read it there is nothing to do next, so the page just stops.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the homepage of a small cabin lodge, and its job is to make a would-be guest want to stay and then get them to book.

**Verdict.** The photo and the copy are good and work together, but the page fails at its two main jobs. The headline nearly disappears against the sky, and there is no visible way to book. Right now it is a nice postcard, not a homepage.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline is lost in the sky (defect).** "Wake up above the river" is white on the palest part of the photo, the hazy sky at top left, and it looks close to 1.5:1 contrast. The first subtitle line ("…each with a porch facing east.") runs across the bright ridge and fades in places too. So the best line on the page is the last thing a visitor reads, and many will skim past it. What decides it is the contrast at the worst point behind the letters, not the average (W3C, WCAG F83). To fix it, put a dark scrim behind the top-left of the hero, or set the type dark on the sky, or move the text onto the dark tree band, so every letter stands clear of what is behind it. Take a contrast reading on the headline against the sky directly behind "river".
2. **Nothing dominates, and the page has no ask (defect).** With the headline washed out, the strongest things on screen are the river and the pine. Both pull the eye down and to the right, into the photo, and then nowhere. The only route to booking is "Book", a plain nav link the same size and weight as "Rooms" and "Trails". A visitor who is won over by the copy has to hunt for the one thing the page exists to get them to do. Make one element clearly first (Tschichold), and give the page one primary action that is visually dominant (Material Design 3, "Buttons"): for example a filled button under the subtitle. It should also look different from the other nav links.
3. **The ask that is there is vague, and the page leaves out what a booker needs (taste / common sense).** "Book" doesn't say book what, for when, or at what price. The three cards cover rooms, trails and transport, but nothing on screen gives a price, availability or season, which is what someone deciding whether to stay wants next. Name the action concretely, e.g. "Check dates for a cabin". Put a starting price or "from $X a night" next to "Cabins sleep two to four" so the visitor can judge the value on terms they already know.

**Smaller things.**
- Two of the three cards end on a single word on its own line ("stove." and "station."), which makes the row look ragged next to the one-line middle card (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Widen the columns slightly or rewrite so each card fits on one line or ends on a fuller second line.

**What's working.**
- The copy is specific and concrete in a way most hotel pages aren't: "Twelve log cabins", "a porch facing east", "Breakfast is served from six, in time to see the sun reach the far wall". It paints the morning you'd buy. Keep that voice when you add the call to action.
- The photo and headline make one idea: you look down on the river from above, exactly as the line promises.
- The restrained frame works: one typeface, a clear three-level scale (wordmark/nav, headline, card heading/body), a shared left edge at 64 px from the logo through the cards, and a neutral palette that leaves all the colour to the landscape.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover/focus states and target size of the nav links, especially "Book" if it stays the only route to booking.
- What sits below the fold. The empty strip under the cards suggests more content, which might include the missing booking block.
