**First read.** The eye lands on the photograph first: the valley, the winding river, the big pine on the right. Only on the second pass does it find the headline, which is white type half dissolved into a pale sky. From there it drifts down to three short columns and stops. Nothing points to a next step. It reads as a small cabin lodge's homepage and feels calm and honest, but underpowered.

**What it's for.** Assumed: the homepage of a small lodge. It should make a would-be guest want to stay and get them to check dates and book.

**Verdict.** The words and the photo are good, but the page doesn't yet work as a lodge homepage: the headline is hard to read over the sky, and there's no visible way to book. Both are fixable without touching the concept.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline disappears into the sky (defect).** "Wake up above the river" is white on near-white cloud, and the second line of the description below it, "Breakfast is served from six…", runs over a busy treeline. The largest type in the hero ends up with the least contrast, so the photo gets read first and the promise second, if at all. Text over a photo has to keep its contrast at the worst point behind it, not the average (W3C, WCAG 2.2 Techniques, F83). Fix: put a dark scrim or gradient behind the top-left of the hero, or move the type down onto the darker, quieter middle ground, so every letter stands clear of what's directly behind it. Measure the headline's contrast against the brightest cloud behind it once that's done.

2. **There's nowhere to go after the hero (defect).** The only action on the page is "Book", a small regular-weight nav word styled exactly like "Rooms" and "Trails". The page has no button, and the hero ends on the description. The eye path runs headline → description → the river, which carries the eye down and off the bottom edge, with no call to action at the end of it (Arnheim on the eye path; Material Design and Apple HIG on giving one primary action visible dominance). A guest who's sold by the photo has to hunt for the next step, and the page also hides what they most want to know: availability and price. Fix: add one clearly primary action in the hero, directly under the description. Name the exact action, such as "Check dates for a cabin", not just "Book". Make "Book" in the nav the strongest of the three items. If you can, show a price ("Cabins from £x a night") so guests can judge it against what they'd normally pay.

**Smaller things.**
- In the Rooms and Getting here columns, "stove." and "station." sit alone on their last lines (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Widen the columns slightly or rewrap so each paragraph ends on more than one word.
- The headline repeats "above the river" from the first line of the description. Use that line for something the headline doesn't say, like the number of cabins and the east-facing porches without "above the river" again.

**What's working.**
- The copy. "Breakfast is served from six, in time to see the sun reach the far wall" is specific, sensory, and could only belong to this place. Keep that voice in the call to action too.
- The photo and headline make one idea together: the picture shows the river view the headline promises, without simply restating it.
- The structure is clean and restrained. One 64 px margin governs the wordmark, hero text and three columns, the column headings are bolder rather than bigger, and the palette is left to the landscape. Don't add decoration when you add the button.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav; how the hero text and the three columns reflow on a phone; whether the nav targets reach 24 px.
