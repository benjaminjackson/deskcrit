**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, drops to the byline and runs down into the text. Nothing competes with it. It reads as a short food essay on a small personal cooking site. It feels calm and plain, and close to a default blog template.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: an article page on a small cooking blog, meant to be read start to finish by home cooks on desktop, and then to keep them on the site.

**Verdict.** It does its job. This is a clean, comfortable reading page with no real defects in hierarchy, type or contrast. Two things hold it back: the page stops dead after the last paragraph, and its look could belong to any site, so nothing here says "Slow Kitchen".

**What would move it most**
1. **The article ends at a wall.** After "…nothing catches on the pan." there is about 150 px of white space and then the page ends. The page is only 800 px tall, so there is no footer, no link to a stew recipe, no other notes and no way back except scrolling up to the header. The last paragraph tells the reader what to do ("cook it a day early"), so this is exactly when they want a recipe to do it with. Instead they have to go hunting in "Recipes". The fix should give the reader one obvious next step at the foot of the article, ideally a link to a specific stew recipe on the site ("Shin of beef stew, made to be eaten the next day"), and then a quiet footer. *Taste / common sense: the catalog has no approved source for this.*
2. **The page has no voice of its own.** The text colours are #1f2328 and #424a53 (`text_colors`), which look like a stock UI palette. The type is Inter throughout, and there is no colour, image or mark anywhere. The writing is specific and warm (shin, cold pantry, the spoon), but the frame around it says nothing about slow cooking or about this author. A reader who lands here from search will not remember which site they were on. The neutral, calm tone suits the essay, so this is not a mismatch; it is just anonymous. The direction is one deliberate identity choice that carries across the site, such as a text face with more character for the headline or body, or one warm accent colour for links and the site name. Keep the restraint. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- **Headline line break:** "Why the second day of a stew is / better" leaves the payoff word alone on line 2. Balance the two lines (for example "Why the second day of a stew / is better") with balanced wrapping or a slightly narrower headline measure. *Convention, though the reference source here is unverified.*
- **Navigation:** Recipes, Notes and Contact all look the same, so nothing shows which section this article belongs to (presumably Notes). Mark the current item with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist", Nielsen Norman Group).
- **Type sizes:** there are five: 32, 18, 16, 15 and 14 px (`type_sizes`). Nav at 15 px, body at 16 px and byline at 14 px are close enough to look picked one at a time. Folding the nav into 16 px, or the byline and nav into one small size, would tighten the scale. The byline already differs by colour, so this costs little (Bringhurst).
- **Byline date:** "January 9" has no year, so a reader can't tell how old the piece is. Add the year.

**What's working.**
- **Reading setting:** 16 px body text, about 67–68 characters per line (`characters_per_line`, `article > p`), 1.65 line height and 15.8:1 contrast. This is close to ideal for a measure this long, so the text reads without effort. Keep it.
- **Spacing:** the headline sits close to the byline, the byline about 24 px above the text, and paragraphs about 16 px apart. Space alone shows the groups, with no rules or boxes needed.
- **Headline and copy:** the headline is a specific question that the reader wants answered, and the last paragraph turns the explanation into concrete steps. The writing is the strongest part of the page.
