**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, drops to the small byline, then settles into the text column. It reads at once as a short essay on a food blog: calm, quiet, easy to read. It is also anonymous. Apart from the words "Slow Kitchen," nothing on the page says cooking or slowness, and it could be any Inter-on-white template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one article page on a small cooking site. Its job is to give a home cook a satisfying read and then keep them on the site, with the recipes as the obvious next stop.

**Verdict.** The reading experience is solid. The measure, leading and contrast are all right, and the headline clearly leads. Where the page fails is what comes after reading. The article ends in about 160 px of white space with no recipe, no next piece and no footer. The navigation also doesn't show where the reader is.

**What would move it most**
1. **The article stops dead with nowhere to go.** The last paragraph tells the reader what to do: cook a day early, lift the fat, reheat slowly with the lid on. Below it is blank page. A reader who has just decided to make stew tomorrow has to go up to "Recipes" in the header and hunt for one. The eye path ends in empty space instead of at a next step (Arnheim, on the eye path). The page doesn't offer what the reader obviously wants next; no approved source covers that part, so take it as common sense. *Defect.* The fix: end the article with one specific link from this reader's world, such as "A shin-and-red-wine stew to make a day ahead." That beats a generic "More recipes" or a related-posts grid. A plain footer would also tell the reader the page is over.
2. **The page has no character of its own.** Everything is Inter, the near-black #1f2328 with the grey #424a53 from the facts file, and a hairline rule. Calm suits a quiet essay, so the colour isn't wrong. But the writing has a voice ("The flavors do not really marry in the way cookbooks like to say"), and the design gives the reader no reason to remember this site over any other. The cost: a reader may enjoy the piece and never come back to the name. *Taste.* You don't need ornament. One deliberate choice would be enough, such as a text face with warmth for the headline or body, or one considered accent colour.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: the line breaks before "better," so the word that carries the claim sits alone on line 2 (Bringhurst 2.4.2 on short last lines; applying it to headings is my own judgment). Break after "stew" instead ("Why the second day of a stew / is better"), or adjust the width so the lines are more even.
- Navigation: "Recipes / Notes / Contact" look identical, so the reader can't tell which section this essay belongs to (Nielsen heuristic 1; Laubheimer, NN/g, "Menu-Design Checklist," guideline 5). Mark the current section with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline.
- Byline: "January 9" has no year. For food-science writing, a reader can't tell whether the piece is current or ten years old. Add the year.

**What's working.**
- **The reading column is very good.** The body is 16 px (`font_size`, `article > p`) at 67–68 characters per line (`characters_per_line`), with 1.65 line height and 15.8:1 contrast. That is close to Bringhurst's ideal measure and comfortable to read. Keep the 530 px column.
- **The hierarchy is clean.** The 32 px bold headline (`h1`) clearly leads, and the 14 px grey byline (`p.by`) sits close beneath it as its caption. Below that is one body style, with spacing that groups the byline with the headline and separates paragraphs by space alone.
- **The headline is specific and makes a claim** ("Why the second day of a stew is better"). It promises an answer, and the first paragraph delivers it.
