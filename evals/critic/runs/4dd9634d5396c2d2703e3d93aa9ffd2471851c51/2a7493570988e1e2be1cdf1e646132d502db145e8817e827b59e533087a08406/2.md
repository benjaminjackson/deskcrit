**First read.** The eye goes to the big headline because of its size, but it doesn't stay there. The headline is a soft mid-grey, so the small black "Winter timetable" label above it, the black body copy below and the black button all feel stronger, and the eye slides down to "See the timetable". It reads as a calm, competent ferry homepage that looks like a template, with a lot of empty white on the right and at the bottom.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the homepage of a small ferry operator. Visitors want to know when boats run, whether they need to book, and whether their car, bike or dog can come.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. It's clear, quiet and easy to scan, and the copy answers real questions. The biggest problem is that the most important message, the times, is set in the weakest tone on the page and written in words that leave the viewer to work out the actual hours. After that, the page never says which crossing this is.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline is the palest text on the page** (defect, hierarchy). The 46-ish px headline is mid-grey while the 15 px label, the 18 px body and the button are near-black. In greyscale it's the lightest text on screen, so it wins on size but loses on value, and the main message reads like a subtitle or a disabled state. The eye goes from the headline to the darker things around it instead of resting on it. A design should still show its hierarchy in greyscale (Albers, *Interaction of Color*; Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*). Its contrast looks like about 4:1, which passes WCAG 1.4.3 for large text, so this is about hierarchy, not compliance. Fix: set the headline in the same dark ink as the body text (or darker), and if you want a softer tone, use it on the "Winter timetable" label instead.
2. **The times are given in words that can be read two ways** (defect, copy). "From the first boat at six until the last at eleven" has no am/pm. It's almost certainly 6 am to 11 pm, but for the first and last sailing, the line people plan around, "almost certainly" isn't enough. Spelling the numbers out also slows the scan that a timetable visitor is doing. "Winter" gives no dates either, so the viewer can't tell whether these times apply to the day they're travelling. Fix: give the real times and dates, e.g. "Every 40 minutes, 06:00 to 23:00 · Winter timetable, 1 Nov to 31 Mar". You can keep the friendly tone, but the numbers must be unambiguous.
3. **The page never says where the ferry goes** (defect, copy). Nothing on screen names the route or the two ports. A first-time visitor arriving from search can't confirm they're on the right page, and a returning visitor can't tell which direction the times are for. Fix: name the crossing in the headline or the label above it ("Harbourtown ⇄ Isle of X"), and if there are two directions, say whether the times apply to both. This is common sense; no approved source covers it.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line 3 is the single word "eleven", and the break "the last at / eleven" separates the time from its phrase (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrite or rebalance so the last line carries a whole phrase, which the numeric times will probably fix anyway.
- The Dogs card ends with "lounge." alone on its second line. Trim the line ("Welcome on the open deck and rear lounge.") so it fits on one line like its neighbours.
- The nav's right edge ("Help") looks about 14 px inside the right edge of the card rules below. Align it to the 1160 px column edge, or offset it clearly, so it doesn't read as a near-miss (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*).
- The empty area right of the headline and below the cards looks left over, not used. One useful thing there, such as today's next departure, would fill it with what visitors came for. This is taste; no approved source covers it.

**What's working.**
- The three facts at the bottom (Cars and vans, Bikes, Dogs) are excellent. Each answers a question a real traveller has, in one plain sentence with a concrete figure ("2.4 metres high"). The short rule, bold heading and one line is a clean pattern, so keep it.
- "Foot passengers can simply turn up and buy a ticket at the gate" tells the viewer exactly what to do in their own terms. This is copy that removes a worry.
- One primary button, clearly styled, whose label names exactly where it goes. The type scale is tight: about four sizes, each with an obvious job.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The nav links' tap-target size and focus styling (WCAG 2.5.8, 2.4.7). Check that the padding gives at least 24 px targets.
