**First read.** My eye goes to the four big numbers in the top row, and it moves from one to the next without settling, because all four are the same size and weight. Then it drops to the order list. It reads straight away as the weekly admin screen for a small bakery called Larkspur. The feel is calm, clean and trustworthy, but impersonal: apart from the script logo, it could be any shop's back office.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home screen that the bakery's owner or counter staff open to check how the week is going and what needs making or sending out next.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and easy to read, and it doesn't make anyone work hard. But it's a report, not a working screen. The order list is the part staff actually act on, and it's ambiguous about when things are due and in what order. Nothing on the page says what needs doing first.

**What would move it most**
1. **The order list doesn't say clearly what's due when (defect).** "Recent orders" in the lower panel is a list of upcoming jobs, sorted in no order a baker can use: today 4:30 pm, then Saturday, then tomorrow, then Friday. It also mixes relative words ("today", "tomorrow") with weekday names, so the reader has to work out whether "Friday" is tomorrow. "Saturday morning" gives no time for a delivery someone will be held to. And no row names the customer, so staff can't follow up on an order from this screen. These are times and promises the shop gets held to, so a line that can be read two ways costs real mistakes, like a cake that goes out late.
   - Sort by due time, soonest first (Few, *Show Me the Numbers*: use the natural order, here time).
   - Write every due time the same way, with an exact time.
   - Add who each order is for.
   - Rename the heading to what the panel holds, e.g. "Due next" (WCAG 2.4.6: headings describe their content).
2. **Four tiles of equal weight, and none of them is the one that matters (defect).** Orders 412, Revenue $9,860, Pickups 287 and Refunds 3 are all set the same size and weight. So the eye can't tell which one to read first, and the Refunds tile, the only one with a problem in it ("All for late delivery"), gets the same treatment as good news. Make one element clearly the first thing seen and set the rest back by size (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). For this screen that's probably revenue, or today's workload. Give refunds a visual flag when there are any.
3. **Only one number has a comparison (convention).** "Up 38 on last week" under Orders tells you whether 412 is good. Revenue, Pickups and Refunds have no previous week or target, so $9,860 and 3 refunds can't be judged as good or bad. Put the change from last week next to each headline number (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").
4. **The screen doesn't say where you are or which week this is (defect).**
   - Orders, Menu and Staff in the top bar all look the same, so nothing marks the current page. This screen may well be a home view that isn't in the menu at all. Mark the current item with one cue, such as weight, an underline or a bar (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
   - "This week" gives no dates, so it's unclear whether it means Monday to today or the last seven days. Add the date range.
   - There's no way from the four rows to the rest of the orders. A "See all orders" link at the bottom of the panel is the obvious next step. This last point is common sense, not a cited rule.

**Smaller things.**
- Type sizes: the bold card labels (about 15 px) and the bold "Recent orders" heading (about 17 px) are too close to read as different levels. So are the 14 px tile footnotes and the 15 px order rows. Drop to one fewer size, or make the section heading clearly larger.
- The right edge of the top menu ("Staff" ends around x 1218) misses the right edge of the cards (x 1231) by about 13 px. Line them up.
- The pale grey-blue script logo looks faded, almost disabled, next to the near-black text. That's my taste, not a rule. If this isn't the brand colour at full strength, show it in its real colour.

**What's working.**
- The tile footnotes do real work. "Average order $23.93" and "70% of all orders" both check out against the totals, and "All for late delivery" explains the refund number instead of just reporting it. Keep that pattern and extend it to comparisons.
- The order descriptions are written the way a baker talks ("Birthday cake, lemon, serves twelve"), not as SKU codes.
- The grid is clean: one left edge at 48 px, even gutters, a quiet neutral palette, and empty space gathered at the bottom rather than scattered.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the order rows can be clicked to open the order.
- Whether the top menu links have click areas of at least 24 px.

**Checklist.**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 7 yes, 1 no (headings name their section)
- What matters first: 3 lines, 1 yes, 2 no (one element dominates; distinct text levels)
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 12 yes, 1 no (no near-miss alignment)
- Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
- Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
- Pictures: 12 lines, 11 yes, 1 no (logo in its own colours)
- Charts and data: 16 lines, 14 yes, 2 no (headline numbers have comparisons; data sorted in a natural order)
- Interface: 32 lines, 28 yes, 2 no (shows where the user is; puts the next need in front of them), 2 unsure (target size; rows look clickable)
