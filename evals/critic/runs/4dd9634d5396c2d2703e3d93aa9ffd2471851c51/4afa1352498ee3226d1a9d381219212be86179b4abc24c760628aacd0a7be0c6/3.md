**First read.** The eye lands on the bold black headline, drops to the byline, then reaches three paragraphs in mid-grey. The byline and the nav are set darker than the article, so the writing, which is why anyone is here, comes across as the faintest thing on the page. It reads as a quiet, tidy blog-style how-to, calm but anonymous.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a long-form how-to article on a small editorial site, meant to be read comfortably on desktop from start to finish.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The column is well set, with a good line length, generous leading and a clear headline. The one thing holding it back is the pale grey body text, which makes the article itself the weakest element on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is too pale, and it's lighter than the metadata around it.** All three paragraphs look about #777 on white. That sits right at WCAG's 4.5:1 minimum for normal text (SC 1.4.3), possibly just under. Measure the actual colour: if it's #777 or lighter, this is a contrast failure, not just a matter of style. Even if it scrapes a pass, the page's value order is backwards. "By Ellen Marsh, March 14" and the nav links are near-black, while the instructions people came to follow are grey. Over several screens of reading, grey text tires the eye faster, and readers with low vision or a dim laptop screen will struggle first. Make the body text near-black (in the #222–#333 range, close to the headline). If you want the byline to step back, lighten the byline instead. *Defect (WCAG 1.4.3), and the inverted order is taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "…wall after / a hard winter" cuts the preposition off from its object. Break before "after" (with a non-breaking space or a narrower measure) so each line is a whole phrase. *Taste; Bringhurst covers this only for numbers and units.*
- The nav (Stories / Guides / About) looks identical for every item, so a reader can't tell which section this article belongs to. Mark the current section with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- "March 14" has no year. On a practical guide, readers judge whether the advice is current from the date. Add the year.
- For a hands-on craft piece there's no picture of a bulge, a through stone or a rebuilt course. One photo near the top would show readers what they're looking for in their own wall. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- The measure and leading. Lines run about 65–70 characters with roughly 1.55 line spacing, which is right for long reading (Bringhurst). Keep the column width when you change the colour.
- The headline is tight and specific. It tells the reader exactly what problem the piece solves and when they'd have it. The spacing is right too: the byline sits close under it and there's a clear gap before the body.
- The copy is good. It's concrete and second-person, and each paragraph ends on something the reader does or gains ("the cheapest repair you will ever make").
