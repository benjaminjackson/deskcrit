**First read.** The eye lands on the heavy "Before you board" heading, glances up at the small gray "PASSENGER NOTES" label, then drops into three short paragraphs and reads straight down. It reads as a help or info section on a river ferry's website for people about to take the boat. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, like a sensible notice board, with nothing showy about it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page section that tells first-time ferry passengers what to expect before they travel: when the boat runs, how long it takes, and how bikes work. No claim is at stake; these are plain facts that ask the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** As a layout it does its job: one clear entry point, three distinct levels, generous space, and nothing in the way. What holds it back is the copy. It gives the frequency and the crossing time, but not the first and last boat, or when "winter" starts. Those are the facts a passenger plans around, and the page sends them to a shelter at the landing to find them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on are vague (defect).** "Between early morning and late evening" in paragraph one and "In winter" in paragraph three are the lines a reader will plan a trip on and hold the operator to.
   - "Early morning" could mean 6:00 to one reader and 8:00 to another, and "late evening" is just as open. Someone who reads it the generous way turns up for a boat that isn't running.
   - "In winter" doesn't say from when to when, so in November or March the reader can't tell which timetable applies.
   - The last sentence ("Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing") points to information the page could give itself. A reader looking at this before leaving home only learns the times once they're already standing at the landing.
   - Fix direction: state the actual first and last departure times, and the dates the winter hourly timetable runs. Either put them in the sentences already there or link the timetable where the shelter is mentioned.
   - Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times in each season, and the date range of the winter timetable.

**Smaller things.**
- **Body line length (convention).** The first two body lines run about 83 to 84 characters, above Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range. Narrow the text column by about 10% so lines land near 66 to 70 characters.
- **Shift in voice.** The heading talks to the reader ("Before you board"), but the bike paragraph switches to "Riders wheel them on… and lock them". An editorial edit such as "wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock it to the rail" keeps the meaning and keeps talking to the reader.

**What's working.**
- **A clean three-level type scale.** Small gray caps label, large bold heading, about 17 px body. Each level has an obvious job, and the heading's weight alone makes it the clear starting point. Keep the scale this tight if more sections get added.
- **Paragraphs that each hold one topic.** Schedule, then bikes, then winter, separated only by space, with relaxed leading. That makes the page scannable without any extra headings or boxes.
- **Restraint.** A neutral palette, one left edge and no decoration, which suits a practical notice and keeps the attention on the information.
