**First read.** The eye goes to "Plans for every size of team," then drops to the four big prices and moves left to right across $0, $12, $39, $99. It reads as the pricing section of a SaaS tool for collaborative work. It looks clean and calm, but all four cards look the same, so nothing tells you which one to pick, and the eye ends up skimming the feature rows to work out the differences on its own.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a pricing page for a collaboration product, meant to help a visitor (a freelancer, a studio lead, or someone buying for a company) pick a plan and start. That reader needs to see that each step up gets them more than the step below, and what each price actually covers. Right now they see neither clearly.

**Verdict.** The layout is tidy and conventional, but the offer doesn't add up when you read it. Solo costs $12 and gives fewer editors than Free. The page also gives the visitor no default choice. Both of these weigh more than any visual issue.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plan ladder contradicts itself (defect).** In the second feature row, Free gives "3 editors" and Solo, at $12/month, gives "1 editor". A visitor reading across sees that paying loses them two editors, and they either distrust the table or decide Solo is a bad deal. If it's intended (Solo is single-user, Free allows 3 editors on 2 projects), the page has to say so. Either the numbers or the tagline "For freelancers" needs to explain why a solo plan has one seat. If it's a typo, fix the number. Fact needed from the maker: whether Free really includes 3 editors and Solo 1.
2. **"/ month" and the Company price can each be read two ways (defect).** The amount is what the visitor commits to. "$39 / month" next to "15 editors" could be a flat price for the team or $39 per editor. A team buyer who guesses wrong will either walk away or be surprised by the bill. Company shows "$99 / month" but its button says "Talk to us", so the visitor can't tell whether $99 is the real price or a starting point before a sales call. Make the unit and the Company terms explicit. Fact needed from the maker: whether prices are per workspace or per editor, and whether $99 is a fixed price for Company.
3. **No plan is marked as the default, and all four buttons are the same primary blue (convention).** "Start free", "Choose Solo", "Choose Team" and "Talk to us" are identical filled buttons at the same weight. The page doesn't say which one most visitors should take, so each visitor has to compare all four columns row by row. Material Design and Apple HIG keep one filled primary per view, with the other actions set back. Pick the plan you want most people on, give it the only filled button and a visible lift (stronger border or tint), and make the other three buttons outline or secondary style. "Talk to us" should look different in kind anyway, because it starts a conversation, not a purchase.

**Smaller things.**
- The gaps between cards are uneven: about 25 px between Free/Solo and Solo/Team, but about 37 px between Team and Company. The row looks like it's slipping to the right. Use one fixed gutter (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- Every card has a dead band of about 70 px below its button. The cards are taller than what they hold, so the white space is left over rather than placed (Ruder). Trim the cards to their content, or use the space for something the card needs.
- The feature rows have no row labels, and "Full history", "Named contact" and "Priority help" are loose against the specific "7 days / 90 days / 1 year". The visitor has to guess what "Full" and "Priority" buy them. Make them as concrete as the other rows, in the maker's own terms.
- The headline "Plans for every size of team" could sit on any SaaS pricing page and tells the visitor nothing about this product. The subline ("Change or cancel at any time. Every plan includes unlimited viewers.") does the real work. Consider leading with what makes these plans different.

**What's working.**
- The feature rows line up across all four cards, row for row (projects, editors, history, help), so comparing across columns is easy once you start. Keep that strict row order.
- The type is disciplined: one family, bold prices clearly above everything else, gray for secondary lines like the taglines and "/ month". The hierarchy inside each card reads instantly.
- The subline answers two real objections up front (lock-in and viewer limits) in one plain sentence.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the four buttons, and how the four columns reflow on a phone (a stack of four identical cards makes the missing default pick worse).
