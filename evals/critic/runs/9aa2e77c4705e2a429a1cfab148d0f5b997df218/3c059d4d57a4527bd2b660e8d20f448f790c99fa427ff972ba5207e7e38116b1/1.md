Now writing up the critique.

**First read.** The eye lands on "Plan the week with your whole team," then on the blue "Create account" button, then on the form above it. It reads at once as the sign-up page of a team weekly-planning tool called Tallyboard. It feels calm, tidy and competent, and it looks like many other sign-up pages.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the web sign-up page for a team planning product, read by a team lead or manager on desktop who is deciding whether to start a workspace. To act, that reader needs to see what planning the week in Tallyboard actually looks like and why it beats the shared doc or calendar they use now. The page shows neither. It offers "free" and nothing else.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the main action is obvious, but the form fails the person filling it in, and the page gives a hesitant reader no reason to start. The biggest problem is the fields labeled only by placeholders: once someone starts typing, the form can't tell them what each field is. That matters most on the password field, which never says "password" anywhere.

**What would move it most**
1. **The fields have no labels, only placeholders. Defect.** All five fields in the card name themselves only with gray hint text that disappears on the first keystroke. The fourth field is the worst. It never says it's a password; it only says "At least 10 characters." Someone could read it as a username or a company code, and once they start typing, the 10-character rule is gone right when they need it. If the page comes back with an error, the user faces a column of filled boxes with no names. Persistent visible labels above each field are the established practice (Wroblewski, *Web Form Design*; NN/g, "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful"). Direction: put a short label above every field ("First name," "Last name," "Work email," "Password," "Company name"). Move "At least 10 characters" under the password field as helper text that stays visible, and keep the placeholders only as examples, like "name@company.com."
2. **The left column asks for a sign-up and gives nothing to believe. Defect.** The page asks the reader to accept that they should "Plan the week with your whole team" in Tallyboard rather than in whatever they use now. The only support is that it's free. There's no look at the product, no shown result, and no hint of how the week gets planned, so the obvious question, "how is this different from our shared calendar or task board?", goes unanswered. The headline itself could sit on almost any team tool's page. Meanwhile the left column ends at about the middle of the screen, and the lower half is empty. The space is there; it just isn't used to make the case. Direction: use that column to show the thing being sold (the planned week as the product shows it, or a real result), so the reader judges the value against their current tool instead of taking it on trust. I'm not supplying the content.
3. **"Whole team" vs "up to five people" can be read two ways. Defect.** The headline promises the whole team, and the subhead caps the free workspace at five. "You can invite the rest of the team later" can mean either "add more people for free whenever you like" or "pay once you go past five." A team lead with eight people will remember this line and hold you to it, and if they guessed "free" and later hit a paywall, they'll feel tricked. Direction: say plainly what happens past five. Fact needed from the maker: what inviting a sixth person costs or requires.

**Smaller things.**
- "Sign in" under the button is the same gray, size and weight as "Already have an account?", so returning users can't tell it's a link. Underline it or give it the link color (WCAG 1.4.1).
- The input borders look pale gray on white, probably under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks of control edges. Measure the border color and darken it if it falls short.
- Nothing shows which fields are required. "Company name" in particular could be optional. Mark the required fields, or add "(optional)" after the optional ones (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").

**What's working.**
- There is exactly one primary action. The full-width blue "Create account" is the only saturated block in the card, and it repeats the blue of the Tallyboard wordmark, so the page's one accent points to what you should do. Keep it that way.
- The headline sets a clear scale: one big, heavy two-line headline, a clearly smaller card title, and plain body text. There's no doubt where to start.
- The subhead says something concrete, "free," "up to five people," and it runs about 60 characters per line with comfortable line spacing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the inputs and button, the error states, and whether the password field masks its input and offers a show option. Check these in a live browser.
