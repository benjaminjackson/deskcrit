Below that, more than half the frame is empty. It reads as a calm community journal page about a boat restoration shed. It feels warm and literate, but it also feels unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or newsletter page for a community boat shed, meant to get local readers with no experience to come and volunteer. To act, the reader has to believe they'd be welcome and know exactly when and where to show up. The kettle and the spare apron make them feel welcome, but nothing tells them where or what time to go.

**Verdict.** It's pleasant but doesn't do its job yet. The invitation, which is the only thing a reader can act on, is set as the smallest, weakest text on the page, and it leaves out the details someone would need to come.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invitation is demoted below the scene-setting.** The left paragraph (the shed, the tide, the dinghies) is set at roughly 22 px with open leading. The right paragraph ("Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings…") is set at roughly 17 px and tighter. Both are body text of the same kind, but they look like two different designs, so the reader takes the right column for a sidebar or footnote and may skip it. That is the paragraph that asks them to do something. The sizes are also too close to read as a deliberate step down, so they look like a mistake rather than a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: a weak contrast is worse than none; Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind follow one rule). *Defect.* Fix direction: set both paragraphs in one body style. If the volunteer note is meant to stand apart, make it clearly an aside, for example with a short label or a clearly different setting, and give it at least as much presence as the description, not less.
2. **The ask can't be acted on.** "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings" gives no time, and "the end of the lane" gives no place. A stranger can't get from this page to the shed door. "Anyone who wants to help" is a warm invitation, but it doesn't say what to do next: turn up, write first, or sign up. Times and places are what readers act on, so a vague one here costs a visit. *Defect.* Fact needed from the maker: the start time on Thursdays, the shed's address or a findable location, and whether to just turn up or get in touch first.

**Smaller things.**
- The first lines of the two columns don't line up. The right column starts a few pixels above the left, and after that its baselines fall between the left column's lines. Using one body style (finding 1) fixes this; otherwise put both columns on one baseline grid (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- The page is top-heavy. All the content sits in the top 45% and the left column outweighs the right, so the frame looks unfinished rather than calm. If this is the whole page, move the block down or let the empty space frame it deliberately (Arnheim, ch. I, "Balance").

**What's working.**
- The headline is clearly in charge: one bold serif line, with one family used throughout. That gives the page a single, confident voice that suits a hand-craft subject. Keep it.
- The copy is specific and sensory ("clinker dinghies wait on trestles," "tar and cut cedar," "a spare apron on the hook by the door"). It sounds like this one shed and no other, and that is what makes "Nobody needs experience" believable.
- Line lengths of about 50 and 55 characters, set flush left in dark gray on white, make both paragraphs easy to read.
