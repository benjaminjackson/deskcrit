**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board" and then runs into three blocks of capital letters. Every line has the same height and shape, so the eye slows down and stops scanning. It reads as a page of passenger information for a small river ferry. It feels calm and official, but the capitals make it look like a notice on a post, not a page that helps you get on the right boat.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a page in a ferry's passenger guide or website, for people about to travel who want to know when boats run, how long a crossing takes, and what to do with a bicycle. No claim is at stake: it states plain facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** The layout and hierarchy are sound, but the page fails at its one job, which is being read quickly. All the body text is set in capitals. The times a passenger would act on are also too vague to plan a trip around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is in all capitals.** This covers all three paragraphs, from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING." In capitals every word is the same height and shape, so the reader can't recognize words by their outline and has to spell them out letter by letter. Seven lines of it on a page meant to be scanned at a landing slows everyone down, and it reads like shouting. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says don't capitalize whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) says text in capitals is particularly hard to read. Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for short labels like "PASSENGER NOTES." Nothing else on the page needs to change: the size, line length, and spacing already suit reading. *Defect.*
2. **The times a passenger plans around are vague.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat leaves. Someone who arrives at a "late evening" that has already ended misses the last crossing. "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" doesn't say which months, so in November or March the reader can't tell which timetable applies. Times and dates are what a reader acts on, so a line that can be read two ways is a defect. Pointing to printed timetables in the shelter only helps someone who is already standing at the landing. *Defect.* Fact needed from the maker: the times of the first and last boats, and the months when the winter timetable applies.

- **The heading does its job.** "Before you board" names who the page is for and when they need it.
