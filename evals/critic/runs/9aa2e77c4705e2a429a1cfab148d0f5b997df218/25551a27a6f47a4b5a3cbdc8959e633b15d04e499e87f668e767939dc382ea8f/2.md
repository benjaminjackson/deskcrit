I've finished the critique. It has one main finding: the three paragraphs are set entirely in capitals. The other two are gaps in the timetable wording.

**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board" and then runs into a block of capitals that reads like a notice shouting at you. It's clearly passenger information for a river ferry. The heading feels calm and modern, but the text below it feels loud and hard to scan. The empty right side and bottom half go unnoticed, as they should on a reading column.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or panel that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long a crossing takes, and how bikes board, so they can plan a trip and board smoothly. No claim is at stake: these are plain facts, and the reader isn't being asked to believe anything.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but it doesn't do its job well yet. Every sentence of the passenger information is set in capitals, so the part people came to read is the slowest part of the page to read. Behind that, the schedule wording leaves out the times a passenger actually plans around.

**What would move it most**
1. **All three body paragraphs are set in capitals** (defect). "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" through "…AT EACH LANDING." is about 125 words, all in capitals at text size. Capitals take away word shapes, so the reader has to decode letter by letter. Every line looks equally loud, so nothing like "every forty minutes" or "one boat each hour" stands out on a scan. The tone is also wrong: it reads as a warning, not friendly notes. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says not to capitalize whole paragraphs, and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals particularly hard to read. It also undoes the page's own type plan, where only the small label is meant to be in capitals. **Fix:** set the paragraphs in sentence case at the current size and spacing, and keep capitals for the "PASSENGER NOTES" label only.
2. **"Between early morning and late evening" gives no times on a timetable people will act on** (defect). A passenger reading this can't tell whether there's a 6:30 boat or a boat after 9 p.m. One reader guesses and shows up to a closed landing; another assumes there's no early boat and doesn't come. This is the line that decides whether someone makes their ferry. Fact needed from the maker: the times of the first and last crossings from each landing.
3. **"In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" doesn't say when winter is** (defect). Readers will guess different months. Someone who assumes the 40-minute service in a month that's already on hourly service waits up to 20 extra minutes or misses a connection. "One boat each hour" also doesn't say whether the hours change. Fact needed from the maker: the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, and its first and last crossings if they differ.

**Smaller things.**
- Once the text is in sentence case, decide whether "every forty minutes" and "about twelve minutes" should stay spelled out. Figures like "40 minutes" are faster to find when scanning a schedule. This one is my taste.
- The heading is fine as is, but under it the three paragraphs are each a separate topic: schedule, bikes, winter. If the page grows, short subheads like "Timetable" and "Bicycles" would make each topic easier to find. Common sense, not a rule.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale.** There are three clear levels: a small gray capitals label, a large bold heading, and body text. Each step is big enough that nothing collides. Keep this scale as is.
- **The reading column.** Lines run about 70 characters with comfortable line spacing, and paragraphs are separated by space only. Once the capitals go, this will read easily.
- **Spacing that shows the groups.** The label sits right on top of the heading, and there's a bigger gap to the body, so the label and heading read as one unit and the text clearly follows.
