**First read.** The eye goes to the bold "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then drops into three paragraphs of dark gray text. It reads as a plain info page for riders of a small river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and quiet. Below the headline nothing stands out, so you have to read every sentence to find the frequency, the crossing time and the winter hours.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells ferry passengers how the service runs (route, how often, how long, bikes, winter hours) so they can plan a trip. The page states plain facts, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The page is cleanly set and easy to read line by line, but it doesn't do its job well. A passenger can't plan from it, because the times are vague and the few hard facts are buried in running prose. The biggest problem is the content, not the styling.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page never gives the times a passenger plans around.** Paragraph 1 says the ferry runs "between early morning and late evening," and paragraph 3 says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk." It never says when winter starts or ends. A passenger reading this still can't tell whether a 7:00 or a 21:00 boat exists, or whether the hourly schedule applies next week. They have to go to the shelter at the landing to find the printed timetable, which is the very trip the page should save them. These are the facts people act on and later hold the operator to, so vagueness here is a defect, not a matter of style. The fix is to state the first and last departures, and the winter period, as real times and dates. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times for the regular and winter schedules, and the dates the winter schedule starts and ends.
2. **The facts that matter are hidden in the prose, so the page can't be scanned.** The only hierarchy is headline versus body. The three useful numbers ("every forty minutes," "about twelve minutes," "one boat each hour") are spelled out in words in the middle of sentences, at the same weight as everything else. The first paragraph is one sentence of about 60 words that runs from the schedule into the tide, the crew's course, the sandbank and the harbor wall. That buries the crossing time under a detail most riders don't need. Someone checking the page on the way to the landing gets no anchor. This is my common-sense view; no checklist line covers it. The fix is to make the schedule facts the first thing the eye finds:
   - Lead each paragraph with its fact, or pull frequency, crossing time and winter service into short labeled lines.
   - Set the numbers as figures ("every 40 minutes," "about 12 minutes").
   - Split the first sentence after "late evening" and give the tide and sandbank detail its own sentence after the crossing time.

   These are edits to words already on the page, not new content.

**Smaller things.**
- The first paragraph ends with "tide." alone on its last line, and the paragraph's ragged right edge makes it stand out. Fix it by rewrapping or with the sentence split in finding 2 (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The reading setup is right. The lines run about 65–70 characters, the line spacing is comfortable, and the dark text on white is high contrast. Keep this measure and spacing when you restructure.
- The page is restrained: one family, two clearly different levels, and the headline set apart by weight as well as size. There are no stock effects or ornaments. Add hierarchy with weight and figures, not with color or boxes.
