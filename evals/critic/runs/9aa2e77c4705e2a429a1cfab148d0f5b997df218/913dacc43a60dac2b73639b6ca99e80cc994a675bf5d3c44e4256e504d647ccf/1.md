**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title "Rainfall at the reservoir" and then drops to the three blue bars. I go looking for what the bars mean and find nothing: no numbers, no labels, no units. Next it slides to "Getting there", which turns out to be about buses and parking, not rain. It feels clean and calm but unfinished, like a page stopped halfway through layout.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an info page for visitors to a public reservoir or garden site, meant to show recent rainfall and also tell people how to get there. No claim is at stake. The chart reports measurements and the text gives plain directions, so nothing asks the reader to believe anything.

**Verdict.** It fails at its main job. The chart the title promises can't be read, because nothing says what the bars measure, which period each covers, or how much rain they show. On top of that, the travel text sits under a page title that doesn't cover it and is jammed against the chart.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart says nothing (defect).** The three bars in the framed panel have no category labels, no value axis, no numbers and no unit. A visitor can see that the middle bar is tallest, but not whether it means millimeters or inches, a month or a day, or this year or last. So the chart can't answer anything a reader would bring to it. It also means you can't tell whether the bars start at zero or what order they're in. Every chart needs labeled axes or direct labels, with units, a scale and a date (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). Fix: name each bar at its base, put its value and unit on or above the bar, and give a period. Then add a short line above the chart that states the takeaway. Fact needed from the maker: what each bar measures, the period it covers, the unit, and the values.
2. **The title and the image don't make one idea, and the title doesn't cover the page (defect).** "Rainfall at the reservoir" is the only heading above both the chart and the "Getting there" text, so the travel directions read as if they belong to the rainfall topic. A reader who came for the bus stop will skim past a page titled for rainfall, and one who came for rainfall gets a chart with no meaning. Headings should name what their section holds (WCAG 2.4.6). Fix: either make the page title cover both parts, or give the chart its own heading and set "Getting there" apart as its own section, with real space between them.
3. **The text is jammed against the chart (defect, common sense; proximity per Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*).** The "Getting there" heading and paragraph start right at the panel's right edge with no gap. The gap between two unrelated groups is smaller than the line spacing inside the paragraph, so the eye reads the text as a caption for the chart, which deepens the confusion in item 2. Fix: open a clear gutter between the chart and the text column, wider than the gaps inside either group, or move "Getting there" below the chart as its own section.

**Smaller things.**
- The dark gray frame around the tinted chart panel is the heaviest line on the page and carries no information. Drop the border and keep the pale tint, or use space alone (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*; Tufte, ch. 4).
- Everything sits in the top-left. The bottom third and the right quarter are empty, so the page looks top-heavy and unfinished (Arnheim, "Balance"). This will mostly fix itself once the content is complete and the sections are spaced. Otherwise, rebalance the layout on the grid so the empty space reads as chosen, not left over.

**What's working.**
- The type is plain and readable. The bold title, the semibold subhead and the regular body are three clearly different levels, and the paragraph runs about 45 characters a line with comfortable spacing.
- One restrained blue for the bars on a pale tint, with dark gray type, keeps the palette quiet. Once the bars carry labels and values, they'll stand out on their own without any decoration.
- The travel copy is concrete and useful: it names the bus number, how often it runs, when parking is free, and where the bike racks are. Keep that directness.
