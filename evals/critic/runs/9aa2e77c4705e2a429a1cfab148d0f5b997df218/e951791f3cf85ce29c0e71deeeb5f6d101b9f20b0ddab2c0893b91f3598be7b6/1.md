**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline at top left, then drops into three serif columns, and right away it catches on the gaps between words: "minutes   between   early", "In   winter   the   timetable", "bicycle   during   the". The gaps are what you see first, ahead of the words. It reads as a passenger information page about a river ferry. The mood is calm and plain, but the text looks damaged.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or print info page that tells ferry passengers how the service runs: when boats go, how bicycles travel, and what changes in winter. No claim is at stake: this is plain service information that asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** The page doesn't do its job well yet. The structure is sound, but the justified setting in narrow columns breaks up every paragraph, the headline promises a seasonal story the page doesn't tell, and the times a passenger needs aren't there.

**What would move it most**
1. **The justified narrow columns are full of holes and rivers (defect).** In all three columns the lines are about 28–32 characters long. That's well under the 40 to 50 that Bringhurst gives for text in several columns (*Elements of Typographic Style* 2.1.2). The browser has no room to spread the space, so it pumps space between words instead: "twelve minutes in calm / weather,   and   a   little / longer   when   the   tide" in column 1, "the   crew   must   hold   a" just below, and "Riders   wheel   them   on" and "bicycle   during   the" in column 2. Hochuli says that under about 50 characters, even word spacing "becomes difficult, if not impossible" and large holes appear (*Detail in Typography*, "The line"). Where the stretched lines stack, white streaks run down the column, so the reader keeps stopping on gaps instead of words. The fix: set the columns flush left, ragged right. If you must keep justification, widen the measure to at least about 45 characters and turn on hyphenation. With three columns at this width, ragged right is the simple answer.

2. **The headline promises something the page doesn't deliver (defect).** "The river ferry through the seasons" tells the reader to expect how the service changes over the year. Only column 3 is about a season (winter). Column 1 is the normal timetable and crossing time, and column 2 is the bicycle rules. A reader looking for summer hours, or reading the page as a seasonal story, ends up confused. The three columns are also three separate topics with no headings, so a cyclist looking for the bike rules has to read from the top to find them (WCAG 2.4.6 asks that headings describe their content). The fix: rename the headline to what the page actually covers, using its own words (the ferry between the north landing and the market quay). Then give each column a short heading drawn from its own text, such as the timetable, bicycles, and winter.

3. **The times a passenger acts on are vague (defect).** "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are what a reader plans a trip around. As written they can mean 6 a.m. or 8 a.m., and 9 p.m. or 11 p.m. A reader who guesses wrong misses the last boat. "In winter" also has no dates, so in a borderline month the reader can't tell whether boats run every forty minutes or once an hour. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last crossing times in the normal timetable, the last winter crossing time, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends.

**Smaller things.**
- There's a wide empty strip, about 270 px, to the right of column 3. It isn't a column and holds nothing. If the page is meant to fill its frame, either give that space a job or center the text block. (Taste.)

**What's working.**
- The three-column grid is clean. The columns are equal, the gutters are even, and the baselines line up across columns, so the page has a steady rhythm.
- The bold sans headline against the serif text gives a clear two-level hierarchy that differs in form, weight, and size, not just size. Keep that pairing.
- The copy is plain and concrete ("lock them to the rail along the side of the deck"). Each sentence says who does what.
