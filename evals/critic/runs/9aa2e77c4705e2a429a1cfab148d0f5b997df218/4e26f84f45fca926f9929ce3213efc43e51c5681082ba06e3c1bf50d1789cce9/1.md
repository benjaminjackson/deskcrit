**First read.** The eye lands on "Create your account", runs down six evenly spaced fields, and stops on the solid blue "Create account" button. It reads at once as the sign-up page of a note-taking product called Quillnest. It feels calm, tidy and competent, but also anonymous: it could be the sign-up page of almost any SaaS product.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the web sign-up screen for new users who have already decided to try Quillnest, and its job is to get them through the form with as little friction and as few failed submits as possible. No claim is at stake: the reader arrives already willing, and the page asks them only for their details.

**Verdict.** It works as a sign-up form, and the visual craft is sound. What holds it back is the form itself: it asks for more than an account needs, doesn't say which fields are optional, and gives no rules for the one field people most often get wrong.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form asks for six fields when an account needs three or four.** "Workspace name" and "How did you hear about us?" sit in the main path, between the user and their first note. Each one is another decision before any value shows up, and the attribution question only serves Quillnest. Nielsen's progressive disclosure says to show what the task needs first and move the rest behind it. Direction: cut the sign-up to the fields an account truly requires. Ask the survey after sign-up, or behind a visible optional control, and either default the workspace name or ask for it on the next screen. *Convention (Nielsen, progressive disclosure).*
2. **No field says whether it's required or optional.** All six labels look the same, so the user has to assume all six are mandatory, including the survey dropdown. Some will drop off, and others will fill in junk. Direction: put "(optional)" after each optional label, or an asterisk on each required one with a one-line key at the top of the form (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group, "Marking Required Fields in Forms"). *Convention.*
3. **The password field gives no rules, and "Workspace name" gives no hint about what it's for.** The user only finds out about a length or character rule when the submit fails, after typing everything else. And "workspace" is ambiguous on a notes product: is it their name, their team, or a URL? Direction: put short hint text under each of these two fields, stating the password rule and what the workspace name is used for (Nielsen heuristic 5, error prevention; heuristic 10, help near the step). Fact needed from the maker: the password rules and what the workspace name controls. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- The "How did you hear about us?" dropdown is blank, with no "Select one" prompt. It reads as an unloaded control or unfinished copy. Give it a prompt option.
- The "Sign in" link is the same gray as "Already have an account?" with no underline, so it doesn't look clickable. Underline it, or set it in the brand blue and also mark it with something besides color (WCAG G183).
- The input borders are a pale gray-blue on white and look below WCAG 1.4.11's 3:1 minimum, and they are the only cue that these are fields. Measure the border color against white and darken it if it's under 3:1.
- The centered card on a pale blue tint is the stock auth-page layout. It's acceptable for a single task, but nothing on the page (no image, line of copy or detail) belongs to Quillnest. Taste: one element that shows the product would help.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear: the 26-ish px bold headline, the bold labels, regular body text and one solid primary button. Each level has a single job, and the button is the obvious end of the eye's path. Keep this scale.
- Every label sits right above its field, and the spacing inside each label–field pair is clearly tighter than the spacing between fields, so every field reads as its own group.
- Only First name and Last name sit side by side, a short related pair; everything else is one column. Keep that rule as you change the field set.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the fields and button, which matter here because sign-up is often done by keyboard.
- Inline validation and error messages: whether they name the field and say how to fix it.
