**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading "Notes for ferry passengers" and then moves down through three even serif paragraphs. It reads as a plain web page of information for people who ride a river ferry. It feels calm and orderly, like a well-set printed notice. Nothing pulls the eye anywhere else, and nothing helps it either: after the heading, every line carries the same weight.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming a web page that tells passengers when the ferry runs and how to bring a bicycle, so they can plan a crossing. It gives facts and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and very readable, but as a page people plan trips from, it falls short. The facts a passenger acts on (when the boats run, when the last one leaves, when the winter timetable starts) are vague and buried in running prose. The most important one can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The last-boat line can be read two ways, and the wrong reading strands someone.** Third paragraph: "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk." One reading is that the last boat of the day leaves the market quay before dusk. The other is that the last boat in either direction does. A passenger who has crossed to the market quay doesn't know whether a boat still leaves the north landing later, or whether they need to be back at the quay by then. Dates, times, and last departures are the things readers hold the operator to, so this ambiguity is a defect, not a style note. Fix direction: name the landing that the last boat leaves from. If that boat is the last crossing in both directions, say so. (Defect; common sense, no catalog entry.) Fact needed from the maker: which landing or landings the last crossing leaves from in winter.
2. **The timetable is described, not given.** First and third paragraphs: "early morning," "late evening," "before dusk," "in winter." None of these is a time a reader can plan around. Dusk moves by hours over a season, and "winter" has no start or end date. The page then sends the reader to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing." That means a passenger only learns the real times after walking to the landing, which is the trip this page should help them plan. Reading as a stranger would, the gap sits exactly where the reader needs certainty. Fix direction: state the first and last departures and the dates the winter timetable runs, or link to a timetable they can see before they leave. (Defect.) Fact needed from the maker: first and last departure times for each season, and the start and end dates of the winter timetable.
3. **The facts people scan for sit inside paragraphs that look identical.** All three paragraphs share one size, weight, and form, and nothing marks which one covers times, which covers bicycles, and which covers winter. Someone checking "how often in winter?" has to read all three from the top. For a page this short that costs seconds, not minutes, but this is a page people come back to and scan. Fix direction: give each topic a short label in the heading face at a smaller size, or lead each paragraph with its subject in bold. Keep the prose; just let the eye find the right paragraph. (Taste. The checklist's chunking line is met at this length. I'm arguing it for a reference page people scan, not read once.)

**Smaller things.** The page shows no timetable, map, or fare information, so the "no extra charge" for bicycles has no fare to be "extra" to. If fares are covered elsewhere, a pointer would close that gap.

**What's working.**
- The measure and leading are right. Body lines run about 68 characters with generous line spacing, so the paragraphs read in one easy pass. Keep the column width if you add labels or times.
- The type pairing is clear: a bold sans heading over a book serif body, plainly different faces with plainly different jobs, so you never wonder which is which. The heading is evenly spaced and holds together as one unit.
- Restraint. One column, one ink color, and the empty space gathered to the right and below instead of scattered. That suits a calm, practical notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, and whether the page links to a timetable anywhere outside this view.
