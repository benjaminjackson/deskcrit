**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold two-line heading, "Update the card that pays for your subscription," and then down into calm, well-spaced body text. It reads at once as a help-center article for a billing product. It feels plain, trustworthy and quiet. The page reads easily, but it never says how to do the task its title names.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help article for a Ledgerly customer, usually an account owner or billing admin, who came to change the card on their subscription and wants to do it now. No claim is at stake: the page states policy and facts, and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** The page is well made, but it doesn't fully do its job. A reader who searched "update my card" learns the rules around changing the card and never gets the action itself: no steps, and no link to the page where they would make the change.

**What would move it most**
1. **The task in the title has no path.** The first paragraph says you can replace the card "from the billing page," but "billing page" is plain text, not a link, and no steps follow. Meanwhile, three links in the article send the reader somewhere else: billing history, roles, and invoices. A reader who came to act has to leave and find the billing page on their own, which is the one thing this article exists to prevent. This is a defect. It's common sense rather than a cited rule: put what the reader needs next right in front of them. Fix: make the action the first thing in the body. At minimum, turn "the billing page" into a link. Better, add a short numbered sequence of the actual clicks. Fact needed from the maker: where the card setting sits in the product and the steps to change it.
2. **The retry schedule can be read two ways, and it decides when someone loses their plan.** In the declined-card section, "We try a declined card again after three days and after seven days" doesn't say what the days count from. "If the card is still declined after the second attempt" can mean the second retry (day seven) or the second charge overall (day three, counting the first decline). A reader who takes the shorter reading may rush or give up. A reader who takes the longer one may get downgraded to the free plan earlier than they expected. This is a defect, because a date that triggers a plan change is what people hold you to. Fix: anchor the count and name the final attempt in the words already there, for example "after the second retry" or "after the retry on day seven." Fact needed from the maker: what the three and seven days count from, and which attempt is the last one before the downgrade.
3. **The page doesn't show where you are.** In the left "In this section" list, "Update a payment card" (the page you're on) looks exactly like its siblings. "Billing" in the top navigation isn't marked either. The breadcrumb ("Billing / Payment methods") is the only clue, and its label doesn't even match the sidebar's. A reader who lands here from search, or comes back from a linked article, can't see where this page sits among its neighbors. This breaks a convention (Nielsen, heuristic 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist," guideline 5). Fix: mark the current sidebar item, and "Billing" in the top bar, with one clear cue such as a bolder weight or a side bar. Then match the breadcrumb's middle label to the sidebar's wording.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run long: the first line of the first paragraph is about 83 characters, over the 45 to 75 range (Bringhurst 2.1.2). Narrow the text column by roughly a tenth.
- There is no search box in the header. On a help center, search is how most readers arrive and move around (Nielsen, heuristic 7), so add one to the top bar.

**What's working.**
- **The type hierarchy is clean and economical.** There's one family and about four sizes (title, section heading, body, small labels), with weight doing the work. The H1 clearly dominates, and the H2 "When the new card is declined" sits much closer to its own text than to the paragraph above. Keep this scale when you add steps.
- **The links are real links:** underlined, in one blue, with text that says where each goes ("roles and permissions," "paying by invoice," "restore your previous plan"). Keep that pattern, and make "the billing page" match it.
- **The copy is in plain, second-person sentences** that answer the follow-up questions a reader actually has: who is allowed to change the card, what happens to an open invoice, what happens if the card is declined. That anticipation is the page's strength. It just needs the main task added in front of it.
