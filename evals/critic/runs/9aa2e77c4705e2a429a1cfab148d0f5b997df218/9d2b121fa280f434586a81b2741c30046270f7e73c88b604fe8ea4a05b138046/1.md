**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big white headline, "Plants for shade and small spaces", on a dark field of scattered leaves. It drops to the gray sentence under the hero, then moves across the three bordered cards, and then the page stops. It reads as the homepage of a local garden center. It feels calm, trustworthy and competent, but quiet to the point of saying nothing more after the cards.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage of a local garden center, meant to bring nearby gardeners in, or get them ordering, for this weekend's shade and small-space plants. To act on that, a gardener needs to see the actual ferns, hostas and shrubs, and a way to get them. The page shows neither.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and the type is well handled, but as a shop window it doesn't work. It promises plants "ready to pot this weekend" and then gives the reader no plants to see and no way to go and get them. The missing next step is the biggest thing in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The offer has nowhere to go (defect).** The sentence under the hero says "ready to pot this weekend", which is an invitation. But the only things you can click are the four small gray nav links. After the cards there's an empty white band and the page ends. A reader who wants those shade plants has to guess that "Plants" in the nav leads to them, and may never connect the two. The path from headline to sentence to cards runs into a dead end where the action should be. Fix: add one primary action right under the headline that names the thing it opens, using words already on the page, such as "See ferns, hostas and compact shrubs". Style it clearly above everything else (Material Design and Apple HIG, button emphasis: one primary action per view).
2. **The hero picture shows nothing from this shop (template tell, and taste).** The hero is an evenly scattered pattern of flat leaf shapes. It isn't a fern, a hosta, a shady corner or a balcony. A generic picture as the hero is a stock template move (unslop, "Empty box or generic picture as the hero visual"). Shoppers have seen this kind of leafy wallpaper on many other sites, so the page looks like anyone's. It also wastes the strongest slot on the page: the headline promises shade plants for small spaces, and the picture could prove they exist and look good, but here it just repeats "green". Fix: use an image from this nursery's own world, meaning the actual plants named in the sentence, ideally in the kind of small, shady space the headline is about.
3. **The headline block is split and slightly off (convention, and common sense).** Three things go wrong here:
   - The headline is set about 16 px in from the left edge that the logo, the sentence and the cards all share. That's close enough to look like a mistake rather than a choice (Arnheim on ambiguous placement).
   - It wraps as "small / spaces", splitting the phrase that carries the message, while the right half of the hero sits empty. Bringhurst says headings should break at phrase boundaries.
   - The sentence that explains the headline sits outside the hero, about as close to the cards as to the headline. So it reads as the start of the info section rather than the second half of the pitch (Arnheim/Wertheimer on proximity).

   Fix: put the headline on the shared left edge and widen its box so it breaks at a phrase, or sits on one line. Then keep the explaining sentence with the headline, either inside the hero or with clearly more space between it and the cards than between it and the headline.

**Smaller things.**
- The leaf pattern is fairly high in contrast and busy, and pale gray dots sit behind parts of the headline. It still reads at this size, but darkening the pattern under the text area would quiet it.
- The cards are a row of three equal boxes, each with a bold title and one or two lines of text. It's a familiar pattern, though with no emoji or icons it doesn't tip into a stock template look.

**What's working.**
- The serif wordmark in deep green against plain sans-serif text gives the brand a clear, old-fashioned nursery voice without fighting the rest of the page. Keep that contrast.
- The three cards answer the questions a local shopper actually has (when you're open, what classes run, whether you deliver) in plain, specific sentences with real numbers: "until 8 pm on Thursdays", "ten places in each class", "orders over $60". That's good, concrete copy.
- The type scale is tight: a headline, a lead sentence, card titles and body text are all clearly different levels, and the card titles use weight rather than an extra size.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the nav links have hover and focus states, and whether the hero holds together at phone width, where the leaf pattern and the wrapping headline will be under more pressure.
