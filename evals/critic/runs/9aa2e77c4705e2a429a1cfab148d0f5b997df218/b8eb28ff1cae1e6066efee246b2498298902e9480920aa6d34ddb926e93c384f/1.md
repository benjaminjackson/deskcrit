**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif title, "Winter timetable at the harbour," then drops into two columns of calm, even prose. It reads as a ferry or harbour notice for passengers, and it looks quiet, trustworthy and a bit bookish. Then the scan stalls. I go looking for the timetable and there isn't one. The only subhead, "Fares and cards," sits on the right over text that has nothing to do with fares.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is a page that tells winter ferry passengers when boats run and how boarding works, so they can plan a crossing. No claim is at stake. It is plain practical information.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean, but the page doesn't do its job. It promises a timetable and gives no departure times, and both headings mislabel what's under them. A passenger who came for "when is the next boat" leaves without an answer.

**What would move it most**
1. **The title promises a timetable the page doesn't have (defect).** "Winter timetable" sits over three paragraphs of prose. The only timing facts are buried mid-sentence: "opens at seven," "every ninety minutes," "until the last boat has left." No departure time appears anywhere, not even the first sailing. A reader scanning for a time finds nothing to scan, has to read every line, and still can't plan a trip. This breaks the basic test that a page should be self-evident within seconds (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Fix: the times need to be the first thing under the title, set as a scannable list or table, with the prose moved below as notes. Fact needed from the maker: the winter departure times, at least the first and last sailing each way.
2. **"Fares and cards" labels a section about something else (defect).** The right-hand subhead sits over crossing times and wind, bicycles and prams, and dogs. There isn't one word about fares. The one mention of a season card is in the *left* column. A reader who looks for prices under that heading finds none and may assume the page has none. Headings should describe what their section holds (WCAG 2.4.6). Fix: either rename the heading for what the column covers (the crossing and what you can bring aboard) and move the season-card line under it, or keep the heading and put fares under it. Fact needed from the maker: the fares, if this page is meant to carry them.
3. **The two columns follow different rules, so the page has no structure to scan (convention).** The right column has a subhead and the left has none, though it's the same kind of content (office hours, sailings, waiting room). So the left column reads as an intro and the right as a section, which isn't what's going on. The extra heading also pushes the right column's text about 40 px lower, so the lines of the two columns don't share baselines (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*; Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*). Fix: give each column a subhead in the same style that names its topic, and set the subheads level so the body text starts on the same line in both columns.

**Smaller things.**
- The two gray rules at about y≈585 close off nothing: no footer, no next section. They look like leftovers. Remove them or put the content they're meant to introduce below them (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*; Lupton, *Thinking with Type*).
- Two right-column paragraphs end on a lone short last line, "thirty-five." and "bags." A small rewrap or a slightly different measure would pull them up (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The type is set well for reading: a sturdy serif, lines that look about 55 to 60 characters long, comfortable spacing between lines, and paragraphs marked by space alone. Keep this when the timetable goes in.
- The bold serif title clearly leads the page and matches the body face, so the page has one calm, civic voice that suits a harbour notice.
- The writing is plain and friendly ("the crew will always help with bags"). It's good material for notes under a real timetable.
