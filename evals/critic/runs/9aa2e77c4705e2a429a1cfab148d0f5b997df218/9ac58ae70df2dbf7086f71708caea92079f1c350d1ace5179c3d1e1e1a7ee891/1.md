**First read.** The eye lands on the "Pricing" title, drops to the three prices ($9, $29, $79), and runs across the cards comparing them. Then it stops. None of the cards has a button, so after the last feature line there's nowhere to go. The only action on the page is "Start free," up in the top-right corner. It reads as a neat, calm pricing page for a calendar or booking tool. It's competent, but it looks like a hundred others.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the pricing page of a scheduling SaaS, there to help a prospective customer pick a plan and start on it. To act, that reader needs to know exactly what each price buys (per what, including what) and how to start that plan. The page gives neither.

**Verdict.** It fails its main job: it shows prices but gives no way to act on any of them. The page ends in a dead end, and the main fix is a button in each card.

**What would move it most**
1. **No plan has a way to start it.** Each card ends after its feature list, with no button. The only action is "Start free" in the header, far from the prices and tied to no plan. A visitor who has just picked "Team" has to scroll back up, click a generic button, and hope it starts the plan they chose. The decision is split from the action: the card holds the choice, but the action sits in another box (common region: Palmer; Nielsen Norman Group). The eye path ends at the bottom of the cards (Arnheim). The empty band below the cards, roughly the bottom 230 px, makes this worse: the page looks like it stopped early. Fix: give each card its own action at the foot of the card, so choosing and acting happen in the same place. If one plan is the one most visitors should take, make that card's button the single primary and style the others as secondary. *Defect.*
2. **The prices can be read two ways.** "$29 / month" on a plan called "Team" doesn't say whether that's for the whole team or per person. A reader who assumes the team total and later sees a per-seat bill feels misled. The plan you'd pay for is exactly where a line can't be ambiguous. It's also unclear whether Team and Business include the plan below them: Team lists "Up to 10 calendars, Shared availability, Reports" but not "Booking page" or "Email reminders." A literal reader concludes Team lacks the booking page that the $9 plan has. Fix: state the billing unit next to each price, and make clear whether each plan builds on the one before it, for example a first line that says so. Fact needed from the maker: whether each price is per team or per user, and whether higher plans include everything in the lower ones. *Defect.*
3. **"Start free" isn't explained anywhere on the pricing page.** The header promises something free, but the cheapest thing on the page is $9. The reader can't tell whether there's a free plan, a free trial, or how long it lasts, and that is the first question at the moment they're looking at prices. Fact needed from the maker: what "Start free" means, whether a free plan or a trial, and for how long. *Defect.*
4. **The subhead is copy that could sit on anyone's page.** "Simple plans for teams of every size" tells the reader nothing about Tidewell, and the "Solo" plan isn't a team at all. The second sentence is the useful one: "Change or cancel whenever you like" answers a real worry about commitment. Lead with it, or cut the first sentence. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- The nav doesn't mark "Pricing" as the current page. Give it one cue, such as weight, an underline, or the accent color (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- The subhead breaks so that "you like." sits alone on its second line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rebalance the break, or shorten the line as finding 4 suggests.
- The three equal cards give no hint which plan fits whom. That's fine if it's deliberate, but if one plan is the usual answer, marking it would save most visitors the comparison. This is common sense, not a rule.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: title, plan name, price, and features are four clearly different levels. The big price figure with a small, light "/ month" makes the three prices easy to compare at a glance. Keep that.
- The restraint: one blue accent, neutral grays, thin card borders, and one primary button. Nothing competes with the prices.
- The cards line up exactly. Plan names, prices, and feature lines share baselines across all three, so comparing across a row takes no effort.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav and button, and how the three cards stack on a phone, in particular whether the price unit stays visible.
