**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into a column of serif text that reads easily. It reads as a short public information page from a small ferry service: calm, plain, trustworthy. On the second look you notice the deep indent at the start of every paragraph, the first one included, which makes the column's left edge stutter.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling riders and would-be riders how the ferry works: when it runs, how long it takes, bikes, winter service, and the landings. It states facts and asks the reader to accept nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job: readable text, a clear heading, a sensible column width. Two things hold it back. Each paragraph is marked twice, with both an indent and a gap, which looks fussy. And the copy never gives a passenger the one fact they came for, which is when the boat actually leaves.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are marked twice, and the first one is indented after the heading.** All four paragraphs have a deep first-line indent (about 47 px, roughly two and a half em) and also a gap above them. Either one is enough to show where a paragraph starts. With both, the left edge of the column breaks into notches every few lines, so the block looks busier than its content. The worst spot is the very first line under the heading, where the indent pushes the opening of the text away from the heading's left edge, so they no longer line up. Setting the first paragraph after a heading flush left is a published convention (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.3.1: after a heading "the indent is superfluous"). "Never both" is my judgment, not a sourced rule: the gap already separates the paragraphs, so the indent adds nothing. Fix: keep the gap and remove the indents, which suits a short web page of separate notes. Or keep a smaller indent of about one em and close the gaps. Either way, start the first paragraph flush left. *Convention (first paragraph) and taste (both marks).*
2. **The schedule gives a passenger no time they can plan around.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines a rider acts on. Read by a stranger, they can mean several things. Does "early morning" mean 6:00 or 8:00? Does the forty-minute cycle start from the north landing or from the quay? In winter, is "before dusk" 15:30 or 17:00? Someone who guesses late misses the last boat. Right now the page sends readers to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing," so they have to be standing at the landing to learn what this page could tell them at home. This is a defect: the copy is vague exactly where the reader will hold the service to it. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times from each landing, in summer and in winter.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading is a generic label. "Notes" says nothing about what the page holds. It is fine for a page this short, but the time facts above would lead better than the bike paragraph does if the order were ever revised.
- At 1280 px, the right 40% of the screen is empty. That is acceptable for a single reading column, but check whether the column should sit centered in the site's layout or stay on the left grid it shares with other pages.

**What's working.**
- **The measure and leading are right.** Lines run about 65 to 75 characters with generous spacing between them, so the body reads without effort. Keep the column width.
- **The type pairing is clear.** A bold sans heading over a serif text face gives two plainly different levels with no confusion. The heading is set apart by size, weight, and form, not size alone.
- **The plain, near-black-on-white color** fits a public information page and reads easily.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone. Check that the indent, if you keep it, doesn't eat a large share of a narrow line.
