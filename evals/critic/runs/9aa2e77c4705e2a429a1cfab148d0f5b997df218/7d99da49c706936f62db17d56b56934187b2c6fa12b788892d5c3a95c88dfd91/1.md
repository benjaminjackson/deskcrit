**First read.** The eye starts at "Changing your pickup time" only because it's at the top left, then slides down four blocks of near-black text that look almost the same. I had to look twice to see which lines are questions and which are answers: the topic lines look the same size as the answers, or a touch smaller. It reads as a small bakery's order-help or FAQ section. It feels calm and plain, but flat.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the help section of a small bakery's online ordering, for customers who have already ordered and want to change, hand off, check, or cancel it. It only gives practical answers, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The words are mostly clear and the setting is tidy, but it doesn't work yet as something to scan. The topic lines don't stand out from the answers, so a customer can't jump to their question. The cancellation deadline, the line people will hold the bakery to, can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The topic lines don't look like headings (defect).** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are in the same regular weight and near-black color as the answers, and look a pixel or so *smaller* than them. Nobody reads a help section top to bottom. They look for their own question. Here all eight lines look the same, so the reader has to read each one to find the four that are topics. The space does group each heading with its answer, but the type doesn't say which line is which. Dair says a difference has to be obvious at a glance in size, weight, form, or color, and a one-step difference looks like an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). Fix: make each topic line clearly different from its answer, most simply by a heavier weight, or by a clear size step up. Don't make it smaller or lighter than the answer. Also give the section a title above the list so the eye has somewhere to start. Right now nothing stands out on the page (Tschichold, *The New Typography*).
2. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways (defect).** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead." Ahead of what: the order date, the pickup time, the bake? And does "a day" mean 24 hours before the slot, or any time the day before? One customer reads "by 10 am Thursday for a 10 am Friday pickup". Another reads "any time Thursday". The second customer misses the cutoff, pays for bread they didn't want, and blames the bakery. Fix: name the event the deadline counts back from, and make "a day" exact. Fact needed from the maker: how the cancellation cutoff is counted (hours before the pickup slot, or a set time on the day before).
3. **"Along with a name": whose name? (defect).** "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name." It could mean the name on the order (yours) or their own name. If they give the wrong one, they're turned away at the counter. Fix: say which name. Fact needed from the maker: whether the person collecting gives the orderer's name or their own.

**Smaller things.**
- The answers run up to about 96 characters per line ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…"), well past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. They're short paragraphs, so the cost is small, but bringing the text column in to roughly 66 characters would fix it.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrowing the column or adjusting the break would bring it back up.
- Allergies: "Every loaf has a card by the till" tells someone ordering online that they'll find out what's in the bread only at pickup, after they've ordered. If the same list is available before ordering, the answer should say where. Fact needed from the maker: whether the ingredients and allergen list can be seen before ordering, and where.
- "Open your order": the reader may not know where their order lives (an email link, an account page). Naming the place would save a search, using the site's own label for it.

**What's working.**
- The spacing groups correctly: each topic sits about 8 px above its answer and roughly 28 px clear of the block before it, so the four pairs already read as four units. Keep those gaps when you change the heading type.
- The answers are short, in everyday words, and speak to "you", and most end with the consequence for the reader ("the bread has already been baked for you"). That voice fits a small bakery. Keep it.
- One plain face, one dark color on white, a single crisp left edge. Nothing here needs removing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether each topic is a link or an accordion that opens and closes, and how the section reflows on a phone.
