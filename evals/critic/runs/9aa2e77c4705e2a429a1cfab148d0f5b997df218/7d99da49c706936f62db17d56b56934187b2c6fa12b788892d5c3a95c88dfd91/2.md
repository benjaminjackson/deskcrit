**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It starts at "Changing your pickup time" only because that's the top-left line, then moves down a column of nine lines that all look the same size, weight and gray. It reads as a help or FAQ panel for a bakery's pickup orders, written for customers. It feels calm and honest, but flat. On a first look you're reading paragraphs, not scanning for your question.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help section where a customer who ordered bread for pickup finds their question and gets the answer quickly. No claim is at stake here; the piece only explains how things work.

**Verdict.** The writing is mostly clear, but the layout fails the one thing a FAQ is for: scanning. The questions look the same as the answers, so a reader can't pick out their question at a glance. One rule about cancelling, the line the reader will hold the bakery to, can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The questions don't stand out from the answers.** Each heading ("Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", and so on) uses the same typeface, weight and color as the answer below it. It even looks a pixel or two smaller than the answer. The only thing that sets it apart is the small gap underneath. A reader looking for "Can I cancel?" has to read every line, because the four questions don't form a column the eye can run down. That defeats a FAQ, which should be self-evident and quick to scan (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Headings that differ from the text in nothing, or only by a hair of size, give no contrast at all (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8–9). **Defect.** Fix: make the questions clearly a different level from the answers. Set them in a heavier weight, and/or a darker color than the answers, with the answers moved back slightly. A small step up in size can help, but don't rely on size alone.

2. **The cancellation rule can be read two ways, and it's the one the customer will act on.** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say ahead of what: 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time the day before. It also doesn't say how to cancel, while the pickup-time answer above it does ("Open your order and choose…"). If a customer reads it the generous way and is refused, they're left with bread they didn't want and a grudge. The same kind of gap shows in "show it at the counter along with a name": it doesn't say whose name, the person collecting or the person who ordered. A friend sent to collect could get turned away. **Defect.** Fix: say what the deadline is counted from and whose name is needed. Fact needed from the maker: the exact cancellation cutoff (24 hours before the pickup slot, or a set time the day before), how to cancel, and whose name must be given at the counter.

**Smaller things.**
- The one-line answers ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run about 95–100 characters, well past the 45–75 range (Bringhurst 2.1.2). Narrow the text column to around 600 px.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer (a runt, Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrowing the column will probably fix it; if not, rebreak by hand.

**What's working.**
- Grouping by space is right. Each question sits about 10 px above its answer, with about 35 px between pairs, so the pairs hold together without rules or boxes. Keep those proportions when you make the headings stronger.
- The answers mostly speak to the reader in plain, specific terms: "two hours after the chosen time", "a card by the till", "the bread has already been baked for you". That last one explains the rule instead of just stating it.
- The restraint suits the job: one typeface, one neutral color, nothing decorative.
