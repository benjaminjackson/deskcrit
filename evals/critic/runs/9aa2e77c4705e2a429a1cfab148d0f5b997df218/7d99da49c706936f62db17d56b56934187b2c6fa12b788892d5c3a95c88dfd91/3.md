**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It starts at "Changing your pickup time" only because that line is at the top left, then runs down eight lines of the same dark gray, same regular weight and nearly the same size, with no pause at the headings. It reads as help text for picking up a bread order. It feels calm and plain, but it also looks unfinished, like a text file more than a page someone designed. The bottom half and the right third of the frame are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help or FAQ section for a bakery's preorder service, read by a customer who has placed an order, or is about to, and has one specific question. No claim is at stake: the piece states policies and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't yet do its job. A customer with one question can't scan for it, because the four questions look the same as the answers. Two lines the reader will act on, the cancellation deadline and what to bring to the counter, can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The questions don't stand out from the answers** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are in the same face, weight and color as the answers below them. They also look a point or two smaller (about 18 px against about 19 px). Because everything looks alike, the eye reads eight equal lines instead of four labeled answers. A reader who came to find out how to cancel has to read from the top to get there. That breaks the main use of a help list: scan the questions, then read one answer (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Size alone wouldn't fix it anyway, and here the size step even points the wrong way. Dair (*Design with Type*, ch. 8–9) says to pair a size step with a change of weight or color. The direction: make the questions clearly stronger than the answers, for example a bolder weight with the answers set back in a lighter gray. That way the four questions form one column you can scan. The gap between groups (about 28 px) is already larger than the gap inside each one (about 10 px), so once the questions look different, the grouping will read.
2. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways** (defect). "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" could mean 24 hours before the chosen pickup time, or any time before a cutoff on the previous day. It's also unclear whether "a day ahead" counts from the order or from the pickup. This is the one line on the page that costs the customer money. Someone who reads it the generous way and tries to cancel the evening before can be refused, and they'll blame the bakery. Fact needed from the maker: the exact cancellation cutoff (hours before the pickup time, or a clock time on the day before).
3. **"Along with a name" leaves the person collecting guessing** (defect). In "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name", the name could be the customer's or the collector's. A friend sent to pick up the bread who brings the wrong one is stuck at the till. Fact needed from the maker: whose name the counter checks, the person who ordered or the person collecting.
4. **"Open your order" and "the list" point to places the reader can't see** (defect, common sense). The answer assumes the reader knows where their order lives (an email link, an account page, the app) and which list holds the slots. A reader who has just come from that screen will manage. One who arrives from a search won't know where to go. The fix is to name where the order is opened, or link the words "your order" to it. Fact needed from the maker: where the customer opens their order (the link, page, or email it lives in).

**Smaller things.**
- Two answers are too long a line. "Give the person your order number…" and "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead…" run about 95 characters, past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the text column to about 66 characters.
- Two answers end on a lone short last line: "anything." under the allergies answer, and the short "after the chosen time." under the first one. Reflowing the text to a narrower column will move these breaks. Check them after the column change.
- The piece has no title naming it as help or questions about pickup. Seen on its own, it doesn't say what it is until you read the first answer. If the page around it doesn't already give one, add a short title above.
- The allergies answer sends the customer to a card by the till. That's honest, but someone ordering online with an allergy can't check it until they're in the shop. If the ingredients are shown anywhere online, say where. (This is a content gap for the maker, not a layout issue.)

**What's working.**
- The copy is short, plain and in the second person ("Give the person your order number", "baked for you"). Keep that voice when you fix the two unclear lines.
- The spacing between groups is already set up correctly: more space between question pairs than inside them, all on one crisp left edge. Once the questions are made stronger, it'll read as four clean groups without any rules or boxes.
- "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" is a model line: who, what, how long, measured from what. The cancellation line should be this exact.
