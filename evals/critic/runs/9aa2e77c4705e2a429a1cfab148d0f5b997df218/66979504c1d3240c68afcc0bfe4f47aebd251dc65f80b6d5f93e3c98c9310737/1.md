**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "Send an invoice in the time it takes to pour coffee.", reads down to the subhead, and stops at the dark green button. Then it jumps right to the small white invoice card, and only after that drops to the three cards at the bottom. It reads as a calm, competent invoicing tool for freelancers and small studios. It feels tidy and friendly, if a little generic.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the first screen of a marketing site for an invoicing app, and its job is to get freelancers and small-business owners to start the 14-day trial. That reader must see the speed and the automatic chasing actually happen before they believe the headline, and here they see only a finished invoice, which shows neither.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the hierarchy is clear. But the one picture on the page doesn't prove the headline's promise, and the lower third reads as unfinished. Making the visual show the claim is the biggest single fix.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invoice card doesn't carry the headline's claim.** The headline asks the reader to believe two things: that an invoice takes "the time it takes to pour coffee", and that Tallyfold "fills in your clients, rates, and tax, then chases late payments for you." The only evidence is the card on the right, "Invoice 0142 · Harbor Print Co.", which is a finished invoice. Any invoicing tool could show that. It doesn't show anything being filled in for you, how quick it was, or a reminder going out, so the reader takes the main promise on trust. The card is also small, about 340 × 200 px in a hero roughly 1,230 px wide. It sits on its own at the far right with a wide empty gap between it and the headline, so it reads as a sticker, not as the other half of the idea. Fix: make the visual show the claim. Show the moment the product does the work, such as fields filling themselves or a sent reminder, using the product's real screens. Make it large enough to balance the headline. *(Defect: the reader's main doubt goes unanswered. Headline and visual as one idea is common sense here, not a cited rule.)*
2. **The three feature cards are mostly empty.** In "Reminders that sound human", "Pay by card or transfer" and "Reports for tax time", the text fills the top 90 px or so of each card, and the bottom 130 px or more is blank white with a border. Empty space inside a box doesn't read as breathing room. It reads as missing content, like an image that never loaded or a layout built for more copy. So the section looks unfinished just where the reader is deciding whether to scroll on. Ruder (*Typography: A Manual of Design*) argues for gathering empty space into deliberate areas, not leaving it in boxes. Fix: shrink the cards to fit their text and let the page's own background take the space, or fill each card with something that proves its line, such as the reminder email or the payment screen. If the cards are cut off by the bottom of the screenshot, check that at full height they aren't still empty boxes. *(Defect.)*

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks "in the / time it takes", which strands "the" at the end of line 1 and leaves "coffee." alone on line 3. Rebreak it at phrase boundaries, for example "Send an invoice / in the time it takes / to pour coffee." (Bringhurst on breaks in short text; that source is unconfirmed for headings, so treat this as common sense.)
- The gaps from headline to subhead and from subhead to button are both about 20 px, which is tighter than the headline's own line spacing, while 60 to 70 px sits empty above and below. Open up the gap before the button a little so the button stands apart as its own step.
- The headline looks extra-bold (around weight 800), a stock template move. Its size already makes it dominant, so a bold or semibold weight would keep the rank and look less like every other SaaS hero.
- The invoice card mocks the real product, and no real invoice or screen was given, so I couldn't check that the mock matches its format and labels.

**What's working.**
- The copy is concrete. "Start free for 14 days" tells the reader exactly what they're committing to, and the subhead says who does what ("fills in your clients, rates, and tax, then chases late payments for you"). Keep that plainness.
- The invoice figures are right: $640 + $45 = $685, 8% tax on that is $54.80, and the total is $739.80. They're right-aligned and separated from the total by a rule. A finance product's sample math has to hold up, and this does.
- One grid holds the page. The nav bar, hero panel and cards share the same outer edges (24 px and 1256 px), the text starts at the same inset, and the invoice card's right edge lines up with "Sign in". The restrained sage, cream and deep green palette, with one solid primary button, keeps the action obvious.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover and focus states, and whether the feature cards are this tall at real page heights or only at this viewport.
