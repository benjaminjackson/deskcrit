**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "About the trust" heading, drops into a single 11-line paragraph, and when the paragraph ends there is nothing else to look at. This is clearly the About section of a small local conservation charity's website. It feels calm, plain, and honest, and also like a wall of text set in the left half of an otherwise empty frame.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the About section of the trust's website, read by local people and walkers deciding whether to join a work day or become members. It asks nothing hard to believe (dates, staff counts, a free morning with tools provided), so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The type is set well, but the section hides its one useful offer. The invitation to come and help sits in the middle of a history paragraph, and the reader is given no way to act on it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The volunteer invitation is buried in the history (defect).** The paragraph does two jobs in one block. The first half (lines 1–7) is background: eleven miles of path, set up in 1987, four staff, two thousand members. The second half is an invitation: "Work days run every second Saturday… anyone is welcome… We provide tools, gloves, and a hot drink… nobody needs any experience." That invitation starts mid-line on line 7, after "moor.", so a reader who skims (and on the web most do) sees the opening lines of history and moves on before reaching it. Nielsen ("How Users Read on the Web") calls a long unbroken block like this a defect on screen. Fix: split the paragraph where the subject changes, and give the work-day sentences their own paragraph, ideally under a heading of their own. That way the most welcoming lines on the page can be found at a glance. Viewports: all
2. **"Every second Saturday" can be read two ways, and the reader acts on it (defect).** It can mean every other Saturday (fortnightly) or the second Saturday of each month. Someone who picks the wrong reading turns up on a day with no work party, or misses two. Fix: reword it so only one reading is possible, using whichever schedule is true. Fact needed from the maker: whether work days are fortnightly or on the second Saturday of each month.
3. **The invitation leads nowhere (defect).** "Anyone is welcome to join for the morning" is the only thing on the page the reader can do, but the eye path ends at "across the path." with no next step. The page gives no start time, no meeting point, no link, and no mention of whether to book. A reader who wants to come has to go and find all of that somewhere else, and many won't. Fix: end the work-day paragraph with the concrete details and one clear way to sign up or find out more. Fact needed from the maker: the start time, the meeting place, and whether people should book, and how.

**Smaller things.**
- The text column sits in the left half of the frame and the right half is empty, so the page looks lopsided, or looks as if something failed to load (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Either center the column on the page or give the right side a deliberate job. Check first whether this is only a partial capture.
- The voice switches from "It was set up… it has four staff" to "We provide tools". Pick one. "We" is warmer and fits the invitation.
- "About the trust" is a stock heading that says nothing about this trust. That's acceptable for site navigation, but a heading for the new work-day paragraph should say what happens there.

**What's working.**
- The reading measure. Lines run about 60 characters, with generous leading and a dark-gray-on-white contrast that is easy on the eye (Bringhurst's 45–75 range). Keep it when you split the text.
- There is a clear two-level hierarchy. The heading is roughly twice the body size, bold, and tightly set, so it is obviously where to start and nothing competes with it.
- The copy is concrete and modest: eleven miles, two small woods, the old quarry, drains, stiles, gorse. It sounds like real people and a real place. Keep these specifics when restructuring.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The empty right half and the empty band below the paragraph could be the design or a capture taken mid-load. Check in a live browser whether anything (a photo, a sidebar) is meant to sit there.
