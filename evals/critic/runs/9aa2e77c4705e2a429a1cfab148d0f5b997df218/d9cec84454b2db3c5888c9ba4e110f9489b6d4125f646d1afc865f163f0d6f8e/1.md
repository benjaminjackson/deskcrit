**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline at top left, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops straight into three even paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for people who use a small river ferry. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, a little bare. Nothing on it stops you or gets in the way.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for passengers planning a crossing: when the boat runs, how long it takes, what to do with a bicycle, and what changes in winter. No claim is at stake, because the page only gives facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is good and there is little to fix in it. The page falls short on its job because the facts passengers will plan around are vague, and they are buried inside sentences. It never says when the first and last boats leave, when "winter" starts, or what time "before dusk" means.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times people act on can be read more than one way. This is a defect.** In the first paragraph, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last departure. In the third, "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour, and the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" gives no dates for winter and no time for the last boat. "Before dusk" moves by more than an hour across a winter. A passenger reading "late evening" as 10 pm, when the last boat actually leaves at 8, is stranded on the wrong bank. These are exactly the lines people remember and hold the operator to. Right now the page sends them to the printed timetable in the shelter, which they only see once they're already at the landing. The fix is to state the actual first and last times for each season and the dates the winter schedule applies. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times for the regular and winter schedules, and the dates the winter schedule starts and ends.

2. **The key numbers are hidden mid-sentence, so you have to read the whole page to find them. This is taste.** A passenger opening this page mostly wants one thing: "when's the next boat, and how long is it?" The answers ("every forty minutes", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour") sit in the middle of running sentences, the same weight as everything around them. A skimming eye has no place to catch them. The prose works for the bicycle rules, which really are instructions in order. The schedule facts would serve the reader better pulled to the front of their paragraphs, or set as a short block of times above the prose. Once item 1 adds real times, there will be even more to find. Keep the calm voice and just change the order of the words. For example: "Every forty minutes, the river ferry runs from the north landing to the market quay…", putting the frequency first.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Notes for ferry passengers" doesn't say which ferry. The route is only named in the body ("north landing to the market quay"). Moving that route into the headline or a line under it would tell a visitor arriving from search that they're on the right page.

**What's working.**
- **The text setting is right.** Lines run about 65 to 72 characters, the line spacing is open enough for that length, the right edge is evenly ragged, and no paragraph ends on a lone word. The page is easy to read for as long as you stay on it.
- **The scale is restrained.** There are two sizes, a bold headline and regular body, in near-black on white with no decoration. For a practical notice, that plainness makes it feel trustworthy. Keep it when you add the times, and don't add a third or fourth size to show them off.
- **Each paragraph covers one topic**: schedule, bicycles, winter. That gives a clean structure to hang subheads or a times block on later.
