**First read.** The eye lands on the big serif headline "Help around the house, on your schedule", then drops to the row of four cards and reads them left to right, ending on the green "From $" prices. It reads at once as a local home-services company's homepage, for homeowners who want cleaning, laundry, or yard work done. It feels calm, warm, and friendly. After the prices the eye has nowhere to go; the only button, "Book a visit", sits in the top-right corner, outside that path.

**What it's for.** My assumption: it's the homepage of a local home-services company, and it has to turn a homeowner who's browsing into a booked visit. That reader is being asked to let a stranger into their home and to trust the price. To do that they need proof the crew is trustworthy and to know what the price covers, and the page gives them neither.

**Verdict.** The page is tidy and clear about what it sells, but it stalls where it should convert. The cards, which are what the viewer is told to pick, have no way to pick them or to book, and the page asks for trust that it never backs up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cards don't lead to booking (defect).** The subhead says "Pick a service, choose a day", and the four cards are exactly what the viewer reads last and wants to act on. But nothing on a card looks clickable: no button, no link styling, no arrow. The green "From $89" looks like a label. The only booking button is in the header's top-right corner, which the eye passed long before it reached the prices. So the path ends with nowhere to go, and the viewer has to guess whether to click a card or go back up to the header (Nielsen Norman Group, "Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention and Cause Uncertainty"; Arnheim on a clear eye path). Fix: give each card a visible action where the eye ends, such as a "Book" link or button under the price, or style the whole card so it plainly reads as clickable. Then add the primary "Book a visit" under the subhead, where the eye is right after the promise.
2. **"A vetted crew" is asked for on trust (defect).** The page asks the reader to accept that the crew walking into their kitchen or garden is "vetted". The only support is the maker's own word, "vetted", and nothing a homeowner would believe on sight: no reviews, no ratings, no insurance or background-check statement, no named people, no customer count. The reader will think "who are these people, and is my stuff safe?", and nothing on the screen answers that. Fix: put proof that shows the vetting near the headline or the cards. The page also never says where the company works, beyond a "Service areas" link in the nav, so the reader's other question, "do you come to me?", is also left open. Fact needed from the maker: what the vetting is and what proof of it (reviews, insurance, checks) they can show.
3. **The prices have no unit (defect).** "From $24" for laundry and "From $65" for garden care could mean per load, per bag, per hour, or per visit, and the reader will hold the company to whatever they assume. If they assume $24 a week and it's per bag, they'll feel misled at checkout. Restoring the unit is an editorial edit ("From $24 per …"), but only the maker knows what it is. Fact needed from the maker: the unit each "From" price is quoted in.

**Smaller things.**
- Sparkle shapes in the window and basket tiles, plus scattered confetti dots in all four tiles, are stock decoration (unslop, "Sparkle icon as decoration"). They make the tiles look like any template. Remove them and let the object in each tile stand on its own.
- The headline breaks inside "on your / schedule", leaving "schedule" alone on line 2. Widen the headline box or break after "house," so each line is a whole phrase (Bringhurst).
- Nothing in the nav marks the current page. It's minor on a homepage, but an active state would help once the reader moves on to Pricing or Service areas.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing: a sturdy serif for the wordmark and headline over a plain sans for everything else gives it a warm, trustworthy, domestic tone, with a clean three-level scale (headline, card title, body). Keep it.
- The illustrations each name their service at a glance (window, bucket, shirt, plant pot) in one flat style and one muted palette, so the row reads as a set and you can scan it in a second.
- The card copy is concrete and specific, for example "We collect on Monday and bring it back folded on Wednesday." That's the kind of line that makes a service feel real; keep that voice when you add the actions and the proof.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are already clickable and have hover states. If they are, they still need to look clickable before the reader hovers.
- The empty band below the cards may just be where the capture ends; check the full page in a browser.
