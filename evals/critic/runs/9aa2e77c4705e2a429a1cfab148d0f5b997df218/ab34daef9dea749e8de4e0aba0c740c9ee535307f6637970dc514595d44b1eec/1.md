**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading at top left and drops straight into the first serif column. This reads as a calm, plain, bookish information page about a local river ferry. Then it notices the shape: all the content sits in the top-left of the frame, with an empty strip down the right side and an empty bottom half. The three columns look like one block of equal weight, so nothing tells you which one holds what you came for.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells riders or would-be riders how the ferry works: the route and frequency, bicycles, and winter changes. These are plain facts, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** It's clean and readable line by line, but it doesn't do the job of a practical page well. The heading promises something the text doesn't deliver, the three topics have no labels, and the times a rider would plan around are too vague to act on. The biggest problem is that a rider looking for one answer, like "can I bring my bike?" or "when is the last winter boat?", has to read all three columns to find it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The heading names the wrong subject.** "The river ferry through the seasons" promises a story about how the service changes across the year. Only the third column is about a season (winter). The first column is the route and crossing time, and the second is bicycles. A reader expecting seasons reads two columns of something else and wonders if they're on the right page. This is a defect (WCAG 2.4.6: headings describe the topic of their section). Fix: make the heading name what the page actually covers (the ferry service and how to use it), or rebuild the content so it really is organized by season. Don't keep a heading that only fits one third of the text.

2. **Three separate topics with no subheads.** The columns are route and frequency, bicycles, and winter timetable. They're independent, and a rider usually needs only one. But they're set as three identical, unlabeled serif blocks, so the only way to find the bike rules is to start reading. Nielsen's work on how people read on the web is clear that skimmed text needs headings to scan by. This is a defect. Fix: give each column a short subhead naming its topic, in the heading's sans at a size clearly between the heading and the body. The three-column grid already gives you the slots, so the layout doesn't need to change.

3. **The columns are too narrow, and the page leans left.** Each column runs about 25 to 30 characters a line. That's well under the 40 to 50 that Bringhurst gives for multi-column text, so a three-sentence paragraph takes 12 lines with a line break every four or five words. Column 1 reads choppy, and the eye spends a lot of effort on returns. Meanwhile about 270 px on the right is empty, more than twice the 120 px left margin. The text block looks shoved left instead of placed (Arnheim on balance). This is a convention break with a real reading cost. Fix: widen the columns into that unused space, keeping one grid and one gutter, until lines reach roughly 40 characters. Alternatively, center or widen the container so the margins match.

4. **The times a rider would act on can't be pinned down.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat runs. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" changes all season, and "In winter" doesn't say which months. These are exactly the lines a reader plans a trip around and holds the operator to. Someone reading "late evening" as 10 pm when the last boat is at 8 gets stranded. This is a defect. Fix: state the first and last departure times and the dates the winter timetable runs. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times for the regular and winter timetables, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends.

**Smaller things.**
- Column 2 ends with "stairs." alone on its last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). This will probably go away once the columns are widened; if not, adjust the break so two words share the last line.
- Copy uses British spellings ("harbour", "towards"). That's fine if the audience is British; just keep it consistent across the site.

**What's working.**
- **Type pairing.** The bold sans heading against the regular serif body is a clear, deliberate contrast in both form and weight. The heading reads at once as a different level, and the serif suits the calm, local tone. Keep it when you add subheads.
- **A real grid.** The three columns have equal widths and gutters, the heading's left edge lines up with column 1, and the baselines line up across all three columns. That discipline is what makes the page look calm; keep it when you widen the columns.
- **Plain, readable setting.** Dark gray on white, comfortable leading, flush-left ragged text with a gentle rag, and no ornament competing with the text.
