**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks", then drops to the orange "Start your free week" button. It then goes to the gray "Keep your streak going" card before it reaches the picture on the right, which is a pale teal box with two chat bubbles. It reads as a clean, calm landing page for a language app. The hierarchy is clear, but the page gives nothing to look at or believe beyond the promise.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage for an app that teaches speaking in Spanish, French, Italian, or German, and that it's aimed at adult beginners deciding whether to start a free trial. To act, that reader has to see some evidence that this method gets someone talking in ten weeks: a real lesson, a real learner, or a sourced result. The page shows none of that.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and easy to scan, but it doesn't make its case. A big promise sits next to a placeholder picture and a statistic about something else, so the reader is asked to take the headline on trust.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline's promise has nothing behind it.** *(Defect.)* The page asks a beginner to believe they will "hold a real conversation in ten weeks" with "a tutor that listens". That reader doesn't already believe it, and most language apps they've tried made similar promises. Nothing on the page supports the claim:
   - The only number is in the gray card: learners on a streak "finish the course twice as often". It has no source and no baseline, and it's about finishing the course, not about being able to talk. It doesn't connect to the ask.
   - The reader's obvious questions go unanswered. Is "a tutor that listens" a person or software? What does "a real conversation" look like after ten weeks?

   The fix direction is to put proof next to the headline: something the reader can check for themselves, like a real lesson, a real outcome, or a sourced figure. Then say plainly what kind of tutor it is.
2. **The hero picture is a generic chat-bubble drawing in a tinted box.** *(Template tell; Sokolovsky, unslop.)* The right half of the hero, at about x 676–1076, is the area with the most visual weight after the headline. Two empty speech bubbles fill it. They just repeat the word "conversation" and say nothing about this product, so readers have seen the same picture on many other pages and Parlo looks like anyone's. It's also the obvious place for the proof in finding 1. Swap it for something from the product, such as the speaking exercise or the feedback on each word that step 2 describes.
3. **The streak card is aimed at the wrong reader, in the most valuable spot.** *(Defect.)* "Keep your streak going" is a message for someone who's already learning. Here it sits right under the call to action, where a first-time visitor reads it as the page's second message and stops to wonder what streak they have. In a box this prominent it pulls attention from the button without supporting it. Either cut it from this page or move it down to the section about how the method works. If the statistic stays anywhere, it needs a source and something to compare against. Fact needed from the maker: where the "twice as often" figure comes from, and what it's being compared with.

**Smaller things.**
- **The hero panel is off the grid.** It runs from about x 676 to 1076, so it lines up with none of the three step columns (56 / 461 / 867) and stops about 148 px short of the 1224 right edge that the nav and the steps share. Line it up with the right margin, or with a column edge (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- **The headline breaks after "in".** That leaves "ten weeks" cut off from its phrase. Break it as "Hold a real conversation / in ten weeks" so the time frame reads as one unit (Bringhurst, 2.4).
- **The button doesn't say what happens after the free week.** "Start your free week" commits the reader to something. It can be read as "free, then I decide" or as "free, then I'm charged automatically", and someone who guesses wrong will feel tricked. Fact needed from the maker: what happens when the free week ends (whether a card is needed and whether it renews into a paid plan).

**What's working.**
- **One clear primary action.** The orange button is the only warm, saturated color on a neutral page, so it's obvious what to do. Keep that color reserved for it.
- **The three-step row.** "Listen / Speak / Use it" is concrete, short, and set on an even grid with matching rules and baselines. It explains the method better than anything else on the page, and it's the material the hero picture should draw from.
- **A restrained type setup.** One sans-serif family, a dominant headline, and weight rather than extra sizes separating the headings from the body text.
