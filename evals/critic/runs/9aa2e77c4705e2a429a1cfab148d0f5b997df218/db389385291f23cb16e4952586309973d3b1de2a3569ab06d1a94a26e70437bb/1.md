**First read.** The eye goes straight to the huge black headline, drops to the gray subhead, and stops on the blue button. It reads as the top of a landing page for a team planning tool. It feels clean and confident, and also empty. Everything sits in the upper-left two-thirds, and the right half and the bottom of the screen hold nothing, least of all the product.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a marketing site meant to get team leads or managers to start a free trial. To act, that reader has to see that Weekplan really does merge their tickets, meetings and leave into one view they'd trust. They don't see it: the claim is made in words only.

**Verdict.** The type is well ordered, but the page doesn't yet do its job. It promises "one honest picture of the next five days" and shows no picture. The empty right half is where the proof should be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The promise has no proof, and the empty right half shows it** (defect). The subhead asks the reader to believe two things. First, that Weekplan turns "tickets, meetings, and leave into one honest picture". Second, implied by "the week your team actually works", that their current plan isn't honest. Nothing on the screen backs either one. There is no product view, no sign of which tools it pulls tickets and meetings from, and nothing showing the result. A lead's first objection will be "what does that picture look like, and will it read my tools?", and the page doesn't answer it. So the button asks for a trial on trust alone.

   It also costs the layout. All the visual weight sits upper left, and the right ~45% and bottom ~250 px are bare, so the screen feels lopsided and unfinished, not deliberately spare (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). A stock template also puts a product preview here, and leaving the slot empty reads as a missing hero visual (unslop, "Empty box or generic picture as the hero visual").

   Fix: put the actual product in that space: a real screen of one week where you can see tickets, meetings and leave together. Then the headline and the picture make the case together, and the picture balances the type. Sample data in the screen should look clearly made up.
2. **The button asks for a commitment without showing what the reader gets** (convention: Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). "Start a free trial" could sit on any SaaS page. Nothing ties it to the reader's own week or compares the result with what they have now. The headline already sets up that comparison, the plan versus the week your team actually works, and the button drops it. Fix: let the ask point to the reader's own case, such as seeing their own team's week. If a trial length or a "no card" condition exists, saying it near the button answers the next objection. Fact needed from the maker: the trial's length and whether a card is required.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline looks set at an extra-bold (800-level) weight, a stock template move. With this much size it would hold its rank at a medium-bold weight and look less like everyone else's hero (unslop, "Headings at weight 800 or 900").
- The headline's letter and word spacing looks no tighter than text spacing, so at this size "Plan the week your" looks airy. Tighten it slightly (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 80; Bringhurst §2.1.1).
- The headline breaks between "your" and "team", which splits the phrase "your team". Break after "week" ("Plan the week / your team actually works") so each line reads as a phrase (Bringhurst §2.4; break in headings unverified there, so treat this as common sense).

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear: one dominant headline, a distinctly smaller gray subhead, and one primary button. The eye never has to choose where to start.
- One strong left edge at x≈120 runs from logo to headline, subhead and button. It's a clean, decisive axis, and better than the stock centered stack.
- "Tickets, meetings, and leave" is concrete copy that names things from the reader's actual week. Keep that specificity and extend it to the picture and the ask.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button and nav links. Whether a product image was meant to load on the right and failed: check in a live browser. If it did fail, finding 1's layout half is a loading bug, but the proof gap remains if no image is planned.
