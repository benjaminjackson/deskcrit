**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline, and stalls there: its four lines are stacked so tight that they overprint. The descenders of "Quayside ferry" run into "crossings by daylight," and "through early" runs into "summer". You have to work to read it at all. Next the eye drops to the two-line paragraph, then to empty white space. It reads as the top of a spring service page for a small river ferry. The tone is plain and calm, but the crashing headline makes it look broken, not designed.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for people who plan to ride the ferry, telling them when and how often it runs this season. No claim is at stake: it states service facts and asks the rider to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't work yet. The headline is a layout bug that makes the most important line hard to read. Under it, a page labeled "Spring timetable" shows no timetable: no first sailing, no last sailing, no dates.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline lines collide (defect).** In all four lines of the headline, the line spacing is below the type size. The "y" and "g" descenders of one line cut into the capitals and ascenders of the next ("ferry" into "crossings", "daylight" into "through", "early" into "summer"). Readers have to untangle letter shapes before they can read words, on the one line everyone reads. At a glance it also says "broken page", which costs trust in a schedule. The heavy weight makes the overlap worse. Bringhurst allows tight leading for a title "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide" (*Elements of Typographic Style*, p. 37; also Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, p. 28). Fix: open the headline's line spacing until no letter of one line touches the next. It can stay tighter than the body text, as long as the lines stay clear of each other.
2. **A timetable with no times (defect).** The label says "Spring timetable" and the paragraph gives a frequency ("every forty minutes") and a duration ("about twelve minutes"). But a rider can't find out when the first or last crossing leaves, what hours "by daylight" covers, or on which dates "spring through early summer" starts and ends. Those are the facts they came for and will plan around. Read one way, "early summer" means June; read another, it means some specific end date they can't guess. Getting it wrong means standing at a closed landing. The bottom 40% of the frame is empty, so either the times are missing or they sit somewhere the viewer can't see. If a full schedule follows below this view, give it a cue in this frame so the page doesn't look finished at "weather.". Otherwise the label promises something the page doesn't deliver (WCAG 2.4.6 asks that a heading describe what its section holds; Krug: the page should explain itself within seconds). Fact needed from the maker: the first and last sailing times (or what "daylight" means in hours) and the start and end dates of the spring service.
3. **The headline breaks in the wrong places (convention).** "Quayside ferry / crossings" splits the noun phrase, and "early / summer" splits a time phrase and leaves "summer" alone on the last line. Fixing the leading means re-breaking the headline anyway, so break it at phrase boundaries ("Quayside ferry crossings / by daylight, / spring through early summer") or shorten it so it doesn't need four lines. This is an editorial reorder of the same words.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline looks like extra-bold or black weight. That's a stock template move and adds to the collisions. A bold weight (about 700) would still lead the page.
- The paragraph ends with "weather." alone on its last line. Widening the measure slightly or editing the copy would pull it up (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- A clear three-step hierarchy: small gray kicker, very large headline, body text at a comfortable size. Once the headline is fixed, nothing competes with it.
- The body copy is plain and specific: "every forty minutes" and "about twelve minutes in calm weather" give real numbers with units, and the caveat about weather is honest. Keep that voice for the missing times.
- Everything hangs from one left edge with a generous margin, and the paragraph runs about 68 characters a line, inside the comfortable 45 to 75 range.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether more content (the schedule itself) sits below this view or loads late. Check the live page.
- How the four-line headline wraps and collides at narrower widths, where it will likely be worse.
