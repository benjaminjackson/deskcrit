**First read.** The eye lands on "The Wexley Gazette", drops to the three headlines, and finds no reason to choose one over another, so it starts with the left one by default. Next it sees the gray of the body text broken by holes between words. Then it notices that the bottom ~45% of the frame is empty white. It reads as the front page of a small-town paper, probably online. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, but it also looks unfinished, as if the page stopped loading after the first row.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the front page of a local news site, where residents scan what's changing in town and pick a story to read. No claim is at stake: these are plain news items.

**Verdict.** The type and copy are good, but the layout isn't a front page yet. Three stories of equal weight sit in one row, with an empty half-page under them, so the reader isn't told what matters most and the page looks half-built. The other main problem is justified text in narrow columns, which leaves holes in every paragraph.

**What would move it most**
1. **No lead story; nothing dominates below the masthead.** The three headlines ("Bridge to reopen in spring", "Market moves indoors", "School wins garden prize") share one size, weight and column width. The masthead is only a modest step above them. So the reader gets a list, not a front page, and the bridge story (cars back in April, which affects the most people's daily lives) gets the same weight as the school garden prize. Tschichold asks that the order of importance be shown by differences of size, and Hofmann builds compositions on contrasts of much against little. *Convention.* Fix: pick one lead and make it clearly first, with a larger headline, a wider column, or both. Then set the other two back as secondaries. The masthead can stay where it is; what's missing is one dominant story.

2. **The bottom of the page is leftover space, not designed space.** Everything ends by about y≈415 on an 800 px frame. That leaves a dead block across the full width, and the page looks top-heavy and abandoned rather than airy. Ruder's point is that empty space works when it is shaped against dense areas. Here it's just where the content ran out. *Defect* (it reads as broken or mid-load) **and** *convention* (Ruder; Arnheim on balance). Fix: let the layout fill the frame on purpose. Finding 1 does most of the work: a lead story with more room, or a second tier of stories, gives the page a full shape. If the space is meant to stay empty, it has to look intended, framed by the content rather than trailing after it.

3. **Justified text in ~40–45-character columns leaves holes in every paragraph.** The worst are "months of repairs to its arches." and "a wet summer kept" in column 1, "the old corn exchange from next" and "a stage for local bands" in column 2, and "a regional prize for the vegetable" and "feeds the school kitchen two days a" in column 3. Each column's even gray breaks into patchy word spaces, so the reader keeps hitting gaps. The measure itself is fine for multi-column text: Bringhurst allows 40–50. The justification is the problem. Hochuli says that below ~50 characters, even word spacing "becomes difficult, if not impossible", and Bringhurst calls the result "white acne". There's no hyphenation, which makes it worse. *Convention*, with a real cost to the texture. Fix: set the columns flush left, ragged right. Or, if the newspaper look needs justification, turn on hyphenation and widen the measure.

**Smaller things.**
- The third column ends one line shorter than the other two, so the row's bottom edge is ragged. Most readers won't notice once finding 2 is fixed.
- "From next month" in the market story only means something against an issue date, and the page shows none under the masthead. A dateline would anchor it. Fact needed from the maker: the issue date.

**What's working.**
- **The type plan.** One sturdy text serif carries the masthead, headlines and body. Bold headlines contrast clearly with regular body text, and the body is a comfortable size and contrast. It reads like a paper, not like an app. Keep it.
- **The copy.** Each headline names its story in plain words ("Market moves indoors"), and each first paragraph says who, what and when ("will reopen to cars in April… Cyclists and people on foot will be able to cross from March"). That's good news writing, and the layout should give it more room rather than less.
- **The masthead.** The centered name over a double rule follows newspaper convention, and it signals "local paper" instantly without any decoration.
