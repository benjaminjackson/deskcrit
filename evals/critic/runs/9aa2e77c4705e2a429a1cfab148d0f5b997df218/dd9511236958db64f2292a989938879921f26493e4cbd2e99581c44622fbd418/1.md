**What would move it most**
1. **The illustration is aligned to the page, not to the text.** The square runs from about y 230 to 573, so it's centered on the 800 px frame. The text block runs from about 145 to 500. The image's top doesn't meet the headline or the first body line, its center doesn't meet the text's center, and its bottom hangs about 70 px below the last paragraph. Because nothing lines up, it looks placed by eye. The reader sees two blocks that happen to share a screen rather than one picture-and-caption unit, and the image's low position pulls the eye downward away from where the reading starts. Müller-Brockmann (*Grid Systems*) wants every block's edges to come from one grid, and Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*) explains why a near-miss makes the eye waver. The fix is to give the image a clear relationship to the text: align its top to the headline's cap height (or to the first body line), or center it on the text block. Then move both together as one unit within the page. *Defect.*
2. **The visiting details are vague and buried.** The only practical facts, "open to visitors on summer weekends" and tickets "at the cottage by the car park", sit mid-paragraph at the end. "Summer weekends" can be read as school-holiday weekends or as a fixed season, and it gives no hours. Someone who drives out on the wrong Saturday, or arrives in the afternoon after reading "a clear morning", is let down. That's the one line a visitor will hold the page to. The fix is to give the visiting line its own short sentence or paragraph so it can be found at a glance, keeping the warm prose. Fact needed from the maker: the months and hours the tower is open. *Defect.*
