**First read.** My eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", at the top left, then drops into three gray paragraphs that are centered under it. The heading is set flush left and the text is centered, so the left edge I expected to follow doesn't exist and each line starts somewhere new. It reads as a plain, calm information page for a small river ferry, more like a notice than a timetable.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells passengers how the ferry runs: route, frequency, bicycles and winter service. They'll read it to plan a trip, often while scanning for one fact like when the last boat leaves. No claim is at stake. The page asks the reader to believe nothing, it only informs.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and readable line by line, but two things get in the way of its job. The centered body text has no steady left edge, so every line takes extra work to find. And the facts a passenger acts on, which are times and seasons, are given only as vague prose.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraphs are centered under a flush-left heading.** All three paragraphs (four lines each) are centered on an axis near the middle of the column. The heading's left edge is at about x=120, while the body lines start anywhere from about x=125 to x=136. That makes two problems. First, with no fixed left edge, the eye has to search for the start of each new line, and that cost repeats on every line of every paragraph. Second, the body edges almost line up with the heading but miss by a few pixels, which looks like a mistake rather than a choice. Centering is for a few short lines, not paragraphs of several lines (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst 2.1.3). An almost-aligned edge reads as an error (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Fix: set the paragraphs flush left on the same edge as the heading, so the page hangs from one line. *Convention, with a real reading cost: defect.*
2. **The times a passenger acts on aren't there.** The text says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "one boat each hour" in "winter", and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger plans around a departure time, and each of these phrases can be read several ways. "Late evening" could mean 8 pm or 11 pm. "Before dusk" moves by more than an hour over the season. "Winter" could start in November or in December. A wrong guess leaves someone stranded on the wrong bank. The page sends readers to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which helps only someone already standing on the landing, not someone planning ahead on this page. *Defect.* Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times in summer and winter, and the dates when the winter timetable starts and ends.

**Smaller things.**
- Each paragraph covers its own topic (crossing times, bicycles, winter service), but nothing labels them. A passenger looking only for the bicycle rule has to read from the top. Short subheadings using words already in the text would make the page scannable. This is taste, not a broken rule.

**What's working.**
- The body text reads easily: it's a plain sans-serif at a comfortable size, the lines look about 60–68 characters long, the line spacing is generous, and it's dark gray on white. Keep all of that when you change the alignment.
- The heading stands clearly above the body by both size and weight, and nothing else competes with it. The page has exactly two levels, which is right for content this short.
- The prose is plain and complete, with real verbs: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail". A first-time passenger knows exactly what to do with a bicycle.
