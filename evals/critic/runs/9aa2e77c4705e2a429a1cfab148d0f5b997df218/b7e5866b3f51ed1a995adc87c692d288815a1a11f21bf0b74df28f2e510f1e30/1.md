**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers" heading, then drops into three gray-black blocks that look smeared, like a misprint. Each line of the body text sits on top of the next, so descenders run into the ascenders below ("forty minutes" runs into "takes about twelve"). It reads as a passenger information page for a river ferry. It feels broken before you've read a word.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling ferry riders, including first-timers and cyclists, when the boat runs, how long the crossing takes, and the bicycle rules, so they can plan a trip. No claim is at stake: it states facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** It fails right now. The body text's line spacing has collapsed below the type size, so every paragraph is hard to read, and that's the one thing the page has to do. Fix that first. After that, the vague times are the next problem.

**What would move it most**
1. **The lines of every body paragraph overlap.** All three paragraphs are affected. The distance from one baseline to the next looks to be about 15 px, while the text looks about 19 px, so descenders and ascenders collide: "every / forty", "them / on after", "kept in / the shelter". The reader has to untangle each line from its neighbors, re-reading where letters merge. Some words are partly hidden, which hurts people with low vision or dyslexia most. This is a defect. Line spacing has to suit the type and the line length, and longer lines need more of it (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 2.2.1; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*). These lines run to about 70 characters, so they need generous spacing, not negative spacing. This looks like a CSS bug, such as a fixed pixel `line-height` smaller than the font size or a unitless value under 1. Set the body line spacing to roughly 1.4–1.5 times the type size, then check that the paragraph gap is still clearly bigger than the gap between lines. Measure the computed `line-height` against `font-size` on the paragraphs to confirm.
2. **The times a passenger acts on can each be read more than one way.** "Between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are what a rider plans around. "Early morning" could mean 5:00 or 8:00. "Before dusk" moves through the winter and doesn't say how long before. A rider who guesses wrong misses the last boat home. "In winter" doesn't say which months, so a reader in late October can't tell which timetable applies. This is a defect, because these are the lines people hold the operator to. The page sends readers to printed timetables at the landings, which doesn't help someone planning from home. State the first and last departures and when the winter timetable starts and ends, or link to the timetable. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times in each season, and the dates the winter timetable runs.

**Smaller things.**
- Once the spacing is fixed, check that the gap between paragraphs is still clearly bigger than the line spacing. Right now the three paragraphs only read as separate because the lines inside them are crushed together.

**What's working.**
- The heading is the only bold, large element, so there's no doubt where to start. Keep that single level above a single body size.
- The copy is short and split into one topic per paragraph: schedule, bicycles, winter. A rider can find the part they need. That will be easy to scan once the type is legible.
- One plain sans serif in near-black on white, set flush left at a measure of about 70 characters. The basics are sound. Only the line spacing is broken.
