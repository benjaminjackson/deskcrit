**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Help for ferry passengers," then splits evenly between the two bold subheads, "Before you board" and "If something goes wrong." It reads as a plain, calm passenger-information page or notice. It feels practical and trustworthy, with no noise and nothing trying to sell.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help page or posted notice that tells ferry passengers how to board smoothly and how to report a problem, read by ordinary passengers, some of them in a hurry or upset. It gives facts and steps and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the text is easy to read and the steps are concrete. What most gets in the way is the right column: its heading promises help with anything that goes wrong, but what it gives is a slow written-report process, and that process ends by sending the reader to another sign for the address.

**What would move it most**
1. **The heading "If something goes wrong" promises more than the section gives (defect).** A passenger who is hurt, has lost a child on the quay or has missed the last boat will read "something goes wrong" as meant for them. Under it they find three steps for writing a note and leaving it in a box. That is a reporting process, not help in the moment. The wrong reading costs a stressed person time when they need a crew member, and leaves them unsure whether anyone will act. WCAG 2.4.6 says a heading must describe what its section holds. Fix: reword the heading so it says this is how to report a problem in writing, using words already on the page ("note," "describe what went wrong"). If urgent cases have a different route, that route is the maker's to add.
2. **Step 3 sends the reader somewhere else to finish the task (defect).** "Send it to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter" means anyone reading this away from the shelter, at home or on a phone after the crossing, can't complete the one task this column exists for. The address is the one detail they would act on, and it is the one detail the page leaves out. Fix: print the address in step 3 itself. Fact needed from the maker: the postal or email address that reports should go to.

**Smaller things.**
- Across the two columns, the lines match only for the first list item. After that, the space between list items moves the left column off the right column's line rows: left item 2 starts at about y 298, right item 2 at about y 271 (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Make the gap between items a whole number of line heights if the page is meant to read as one grid. It costs the reader little here.

**What's working.**
- **Three sizes with big steps between them:** title about 40 px, subheads about 22 px, body about 18 px. They all share one weight plan, so the order of reading is clear at a glance. Keep this scale when adding content.
- **Each step says who does what and why**, for example "lock them to the rail along the side of the lower deck" and "so that the gangway is kept clear." The reason is attached to the instruction, so passengers are more likely to follow it. Keep that pattern in any new items.
- **The list types fit the content:** bullets for the unordered tips, numbers for the steps that happen in order. Bullets hang cleanly, and the lines run about 55–60 characters, which is easy to read.
