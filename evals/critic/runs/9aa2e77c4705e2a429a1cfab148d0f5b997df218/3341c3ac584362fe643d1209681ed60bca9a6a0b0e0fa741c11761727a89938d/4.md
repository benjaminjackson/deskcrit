**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops into the paragraph, skims three plain lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a small ferry operator's website. It's calm and tidy, but very bare. The whole right half of the frame is empty white, so the first impression is that a picture didn't load.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a ferry company's website, for locals and day-trippers deciding whether to take the boat across the bay right now. It states plain facts and asks the reader to believe nothing they would doubt, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** It's clean and easy to read, but it doesn't do its job yet. It answers "why" (calm, bikes are free, coffee) and leaves out what a traveler needs to decide: where the boat goes, when the next one leaves, and what it costs. Meanwhile half the screen is empty where the bay should be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The screen leaves out what a traveler came for.** The headline says "Cross the bay" but never names the other side. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives no first or last sailing, and there's no fare anywhere. The "north landing" is named but not placed. So the one question every visitor has ("can I make the next boat, and to where?") is pushed behind "See the timetable", and the hero is spent on extras like hot drinks. This is a defect, argued from common sense (the catalog entry for this point has no approved source). The fix: bring the deciding facts up into the hero, either in the paragraph or in place of one of the three perks, and let the button lead to the full timetable. Fact needed from the maker: the destination landing, the first and last departure times, and the fare.
2. **The right half of the page is dead, and the page tips left.** All the type sits in the left 60% of a 1280 px frame. Nothing on the right weighs against it, so the composition looks unfinished rather than airy (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). It's also the stock template's empty hero: a ferry ride sold on "watch the harbour slide past" shows no harbour, so the strongest selling point is left to words alone (unslop, "Empty box or generic picture as the hero visual"). The plain gray-and-system-blue look adds to it: it's calm, but the same palette could sell accounting software, with nothing of the sea or the bay in it (taste). The fix: put something from the crossing itself on the right, such as the boat, the deck, the view, or a map of the route. Or, if there will be no picture, change the layout so the text owns the frame on purpose, rather than sitting in a column next to a hole.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "in twelve / calm minutes", splitting the number from "minutes". Keep "twelve calm minutes" on one line with a non-breaking space, or reflow the headline (Bringhurst, 2.4.6).
- The three perk lines look about 2 px smaller than the paragraph above them (roughly 18 vs 20 px, by eye), close enough to look like a slip rather than a separate level. Set them at body size, or set them clearly apart with weight or an icon (Bringhurst, 3.1.1 "Don't compose without a scale").

**What's working.**
- There is one clear place to start. The heavy headline is far above everything else in size and weight, and it carries the brand's best line, a specific number ("twelve") tied to a feeling ("calm").
- The paragraph speaks to the reader as a person: "Bring a bicycle, a pram or a picnic" names real things a reader might carry, which is far better than generic ferry copy. Keep that voice.
- There is a single primary action with a specific label, all on one clean left edge, with comfortable line length and line spacing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's focus and hover states, and whether the empty right half is a hero image that didn't load in the capture. Check the latter in a live browser before treating finding 2 as a layout choice.
