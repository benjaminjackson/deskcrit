**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline. It moves right to the small invoice card, then drops back to the green button. It reads as an invoicing tool for freelancers and small studios. It feels calm, warm and tidy, close to a well-made template.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of an invoicing app, meant to get freelancers and small-business owners to start a trial; correct me if not. To start, that reader has to believe invoicing really gets that fast and that the app does the filling-in and the chasing for them. Right now they see only a finished invoice, which proves neither.

**Verdict.** It's clean and ordered but doesn't make its case. The only picture shows the result of invoicing, not the speed or the automation the headline and subhead promise. The button doesn't say what the reader is committing to.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invoice card doesn't show what the headline claims** (defect). The page asks the reader to accept that they can "send an invoice in the time it takes to pour coffee" and that "Tallyfold fills in your clients, rates, and tax, then chases late payments for you." The only evidence is the card at the right: a static, finished invoice for Harbor Print Co. Any invoicing tool, or a spreadsheet, could produce that. Nothing shows speed, nothing shows a field being filled for you, and nothing shows a reminder going out.
   - The proof the reader needs is a shown result of the claim itself: the few steps or the elapsed time to that invoice, or what was filled in automatically.
   - The reader's obvious objection, "I still have to type my clients and rates in first", gets no answer.
   - The card is also small and floats in a wide empty band, so the right side looks light against the heavy headline (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance").
   - Direction: make the visual carry the claim. Show the invoice mid-creation, with the prefilled parts marked, or next to the reminder it will send. Give it enough size to balance the headline.
   - The headline–picture pairing is my judgment (taste). The balance point is a convention.
2. **"Start free for 14 days" leaves out what happens on day 15** (defect). This is the line the reader commits on, and it reads two ways:
   - a trial that simply stops;
   - a trial that turns into a paid plan, maybe on a card taken up front.

   A reader who guesses wrong either hesitates at the button or feels tricked later. The button also names no task from the reader's own week. It invites them to try the product, not to compare it with how they invoice today. An editorial direction that uses words already on the page is to lead with the task, as in "Send your first invoice", and put the trial terms beside the button. Fact needed from the maker: whether the trial needs a card and what happens when it ends.
3. **The reminder copy contradicts the subhead** (defect). The subhead says Tallyfold "chases late payments for you." The first card says "Three gentle nudges before an invoice is overdue." A reader can't tell whether anything happens once a payment actually *is* late, and that's the pain they came to fix. Direction: make the two lines describe the same behavior. If reminders continue after the due date, the card should say so. Fact needed from the maker: whether reminders go out after an invoice is overdue, and how many.

**Smaller things.**
- **Headline break:** "time it takes to pour / coffee." leaves "coffee." alone on the third line and splits the verb from its object. Break at phrase boundaries instead, such as "Send an invoice / in the time it takes / to pour coffee."
- **Near-miss alignment:** the invoice card's left edge (~820 px) and the third feature card's (~824 px) are about 4 px apart. They read as meant to line up and missed (Arnheim). Align them or separate them clearly.
- **Feature cards:** all three are fixed-height, with their lower halves empty, which spreads small dead gaps instead of gathering space (Ruder). Size them to their content or give that space a job.
- **Headline weight:** it looks like extra-bold (around 800). That's a stock template move that makes the page look like many others (unslop, "Headings at weight 800 or 900"). Size already makes it dominant, so a lighter weight would do.
- **Card title "Pay by card or transfer"** doesn't say who pays. The body clears it up ("Clients pay…"), but the title alone could be read as the reader paying Tallyfold.
- **The invoice card is a mock of the product's real invoice.** No real invoice was given, so I couldn't check its format and labels against the real thing.

**What's working.**
- **One strong left edge.** Logo, headline, subhead, button and first card all hang on a 120 px line, and the right edges of "Sign in", the invoice and the third card share a line too. The page feels ordered without trying. Keep that frame.
- **Restrained color.** Warm off-white ground, near-black type, and one dark green kept only for the single primary button. Its rank is unmistakable.
- **Concrete numbers in the invoice.** $640 + $45 + 8% tax = $739.80 adds up, with right-aligned figures. It reads as a real document, not lorem ipsum.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the text links in the nav ("Product", "Pricing", "Guides", "Sign in") have hit areas of at least 24 px.

**Checklist.**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 7 yes, 1 no (main image and headline make one idea).
- What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes.
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 10 yes, 3 no (exact alignment or clearly off; space gathered, not scattered; balance).
- Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (headings break at phrase boundaries).
- Color: 9 lines, 9 yes.
- Pictures: 12 lines, 12 yes.
- Charts and data: skipped, no chart.
- Interface: 33 lines, 32 yes, 1 unsure (target size).
- Template tells: 10 lines, 9 yes, 1 no (type without stock moves: extra-bold headline).
