**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing page of a pool or swim school, and that its main job is to get adults who can't swim to sign up for the Tuesday class (correct me if it's for something else). That reader is nervous and needs to see who runs the class, where and when it is, what it costs, and how to sign up. Right now the page gives them reassuring words but none of those facts.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The headline is strong and the subhead says the right thing, but the page stops before the reader can act: there's no way to sign up and no who, where, when or cost. The two blocks below the hero then send the eye toward offers that aren't for this reader.

**What would move it most**

1. **There's no next step, so an interested reader has nowhere to go (defect).** The page makes its offer in the headline and subhead, then ends. There's no sign-up or booking button, no link, and nothing in the empty lower half. An adult beginner who has just been reassured has to find the booking route somewhere else, and many won't bother. The eye path ends in empty space (Arnheim's point that the eye should travel from the focal point to an endpoint, usually the action). The fix: put one clear primary action, for the Tuesday class, directly under the subhead, so the hero ends in something the reader can do. Make the button text name that one thing, not something generic like "Get in touch".

2. **It asks a nervous adult to trust an unnamed class (defect).** "Small adult classes on Tuesday evenings, with a coach in the water beside you" speaks to the right fear. But it asks the reader to believe they'll be safe and won't be embarrassed, and it shows nothing that backs that up. There's no pool or club name anywhere on the screen, no place, no class time, no price and no coach. The reader's obvious objections ("Where is this? What time? How much? Who's teaching?") get no answer, so the offer reads as generic. The direction: add those facts near the subhead, briefly, as plain details rather than more promises. Fact needed from the maker: the venue name and place, the class time on Tuesdays, the price, and who coaches.

3. **The section below the hero is aimed at other people (defect).** The headline addresses adults learning to swim. The two blocks below advertise open lane swimming ("three speeds to choose from") and a Sunday family hour. People who use lanes can already swim, and the family hour is for parents. A beginner who reads on gets nothing more about their class, and the page stops looking like it's about them. The direction: either give the space under the hero to the beginner class (what happens in a lesson, what to bring), or present the lanes and family hour clearly as the pool's other sessions, set below the class offer and its action.

4. **The layout sits in the top-left corner and leaves the rest looking unfinished (convention).** All the content is in the top 55% and the left three-quarters. The bottom ~350 px and the right edge are blank. That isn't a deliberate pause, because nothing below or beside it gives the space a purpose. The page looks like it stopped loading or the content ran out. Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance") says a heavy side needs a counterweight. Here, the call to action from finding 1 and the class details from finding 2 are the natural counterweight. Place them so they fill the lower part of the screen.

**Smaller things.**
- Type sizes: the subhead (~22 px gray) and the block titles "Join the lanes" / "Family hour" (~20 px black) are nearly the same size, so they don't read as separate levels. Their only difference is color, and color reverses the order (the lower level is darker). Make the block titles clearly smaller, or bolder at body size, so the scale steps down: headline, subhead, title, body (Bringhurst, "Don't compose without a scale").
- Mood (taste): the page is entirely neutral gray on white. For something about water and an anxious beginner, that feels cold and generic. One color or image that belongs to this pool would make it its own place. Keep it calm, not loud.
- "Every morning from six" relies on "morning" to mean 6 a.m. It's probably clear, but a time the reader acts on should leave no doubt. Restore the unit, as in "from 6 a.m.", if that's what it means.

**What's working.**

- The subhead answers the beginner's real fear. "Small", "adult", and "a coach in the water beside you" are specific promises in the reader's terms, not filler. Keep that wording when you add the facts.
- The two columns share a clean left edge and baseline, the spacing between text blocks is clearly larger than the spacing inside them, and the gray body text looks dark enough to read comfortably.
