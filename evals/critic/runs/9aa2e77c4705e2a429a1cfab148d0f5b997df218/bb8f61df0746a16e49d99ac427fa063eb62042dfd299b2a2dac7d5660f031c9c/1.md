**First read.** The eye lands straight on the big serif headline "The finest bread in the valley", drops to the two-line sans line under it, and then has nowhere to go. **What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of Hartley's Bakery's website, for local people deciding whether to go there and buy bread. It asks them to believe "the finest bread in the valley". They'd believe that on sight from the bread itself, or from a name they trust saying so. They get neither, only the bakery's own word about its oven and its schedule.

**Verdict.** The typography is handsome and the hierarchy is clean, but the piece doesn't do its job yet. It makes a claim it can't back up and gives a hungry visitor no picture and no next step.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline makes a claim the page doesn't back up, and it could belong to any bakery.** The largest thing on screen asks the reader to accept "the finest bread in the valley". That's a superlative every bakery could print, so a stranger reads it as filler, not as a reason to come. The reader will ask "says who?", and nothing on the page answers. "Since 1962" and "wood-fired" describe the bakery's history and process. They're believable, but they don't show that the bread is the finest. What's missing is proof the reader would accept on sight, such as a picture of the loaves or a source outside the bakery. The fix is either to show that proof or to stop leading with the superlative. The concrete line underneath ("Sourdough, rye, and white tin loaves, baked every morning in a wood-fired oven on Mill Street") is the most convincing copy on the page, and right now it sits at body size. One way to fix this is to promote that concrete line, or part of it, and make the superlative secondary or cut it. *Defect* (it fails at persuading the reader); the generic-copy part is *taste*. Fact needed from the maker: what supports "finest" (an award, a review, or a well-known stockist), if anything does.
2. **The visitor has no way to act.** The reader learns there's a bakery on Mill Street, but there's no button, link, opening hours, full address or order route anywhere on screen. The eye finishes the subhead and stops. For a shop whose whole aim is getting people through the door, the next step ("when can I come, and where exactly?") is the most important thing missing. Add one specific action that tells the reader what to do next. If the hours and location live further down the page, at least point to them from this first screen. *Defect.* Fact needed from the maker: opening hours and the full address or ordering method.
3. **Bread is something people buy on sight, and there's none on the page.** All the weight sits in the top-left. The right half and bottom third are a blank cream field with nothing to balance the type (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). An empty area can be a deliberate pause, but here it's where a reader would expect to see the product. A photograph of the actual loaves would prove the claim, give the composition its counterweight and make people hungry, all at once. *Defect* for the job; *convention* for the balance.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "The finest bread in the / valley", which leaves "valley" alone on line 2 and splits "the valley". Change the measure or the size so the break falls at a phrase boundary, e.g. "The finest bread / in the valley" (Bringhurst, 2.4). (convention)
- The eyebrow "HARTLEY'S BAKERY · SINCE 1962" is the stock small-caps, letterspaced line above a hero (unslop, "Uppercase labels with wide tracking"). It makes the page look like a template, and it's also the only place the bakery's name appears. Set it in sentence case, or give the name a proper wordmark so it reads as the brand and not a label.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing: a classical serif headline at real display size over a plain sans for reading. It feels traditional and crafted without looking fussy, which suits a bakery that has been around since 1962. Keep the serif, and keep the large size gap between headline and text.
- The single strong left edge (label, headline and subhead all hang from the same line), with a clear three-level hierarchy. It's quiet and orderly, so new elements have a structure to sit in.
- The palette: warm cream ground, near-black text, one brown accent. It's restrained and fits the subject. Don't add more colors when the photo goes in.
