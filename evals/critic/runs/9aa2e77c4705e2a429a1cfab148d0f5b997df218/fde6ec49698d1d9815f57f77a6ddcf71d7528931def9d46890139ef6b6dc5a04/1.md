**First read.** The eye lands on the big white headline, "See where every dollar goes this month," then drops to the subhead and the white "Try it free" button. That takes about two seconds and it's clear: a personal budgeting app. Then the eye drifts right into a large field of empty blue, finds nothing, and falls to a thin row of three text-only features that sits close under the hero above a blank white band. It reads as clean and confident, but it looks unfinished, like a hero still waiting for its picture.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the marketing home page of a consumer budgeting app, meant to get people who manage their own or their household's money to start a free trial. To act, that reader has to believe two things: that the app really shows where their money goes, and that it's safe to connect their bank accounts. The page shows neither. It never shows the product, and the safety point is only the maker's own word.

**Verdict.** The copy and type hierarchy are good, and the core message comes across in seconds. But the page asks a stranger to connect their bank accounts without showing the product or any proof of safety, and the empty right half of the hero makes that gap visible. Putting the product in the hero would fix most of what's wrong here.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero has no picture, and the right 40% of the blue band is empty** (defect, plus a template tell). The headline promises something you can see ("See where every dollar goes"), but nothing on the page shows it. The viewer gets a claim with no picture of what using the app looks like, and the empty block makes the page look half-built. It also throws the balance off. All the weight sits on the left, and the right has nothing to counter it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). An empty or generic hero box is also a stock template move (Sokolovsky, unslop: "Empty box or generic picture as the hero visual"), so the page looks like anyone's. Fix: put a real product preview in that right column, ideally the monthly category view with limits and a heads-up, so the picture finishes what the headline says.
2. **The page asks for trust it doesn't earn** (defect). It asks the reader to "Connect your accounts" to an app they've never heard of. The only thing addressing the obvious objection ("is it safe to link my bank?") is "The app can see balances and history but can never move money." That is the maker describing its own product, and it sits as the third of three equal feature notes below the fold line of the hero. Nothing a stranger would accept on sight is there: no named way the connection is made, no outside proof, no shown result. The "Security" nav link suggests the answer exists somewhere, but not where the decision gets made. Fix: put the proof this reader needs next to the "Try it free" button, and give the read-only point more weight than a feature-card blurb. Fact needed from the maker: how accounts are connected and what outside proof of security can be named.
3. **The feature row is an afterthought** (convention, with some taste). It sits about 45 px below the hero, with roughly 200 px of empty white under it, so the space below is larger than the space above. The empty areas don't line up with any intent: one sits right of the headline, one at the bottom. The space isn't shaped on purpose (Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*, "Contrasts"), and the three blurbs read as leftovers instead of the page's second chapter. Fix: give the row more air above it than inside it, so it reads as its own section, and let the hero's product picture carry the page's weight instead of blank space.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline weight looks extra-bold (about 800). Size already makes it dominant, so a 600–700 weight would keep the rank and look less like a stock template (taste; Sokolovsky, unslop: "Headings at weight 800 or 900").
- "Watch the tour" doesn't say how long it is or what it shows. That's a small gap for a hesitant reader, and the runtime is a fact only the maker has.

**What's working.**
- The headline and subhead are specific and spoken to the reader. "Set a limit for each category, and get a heads-up before you go over" names three concrete things that happen. Keep that copy.
- The buttons have a clean primary/secondary split. The filled white "Try it free" clearly beats the outlined "Watch the tour", and both are large, high-contrast targets.
- The type system is tight: one sans family in about four clearly separate sizes, everything aligned to one left margin and a three-column grid.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the two hero buttons and the nav links, and how the hero reflows on a phone (with a product image added, the stacking order matters).
