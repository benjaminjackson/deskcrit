**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Shipments peak each third quarter," drops into the chart, and goes to the tallest bar, Q3 2026 at 705. It reads at once as a slide from a board sales review. It feels calm, plain, and trustworthy. But all eight bars are the same flat blue, so the eye has to hunt for the peaks the headline names, and nothing tells it the last bar is a guess.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is one slide in a board deck that shows two years of quarterly shipments to retailers and makes the point that volume is seasonal. The headline's claim rests on numbers that are shown in full, with their source, so no belief is at stake beyond reading the chart correctly.

**Verdict.** This is a clean, honest chart that mostly does its job. The one real defect is that the Q4 2026 forecast is drawn exactly like the actuals, and the chart doesn't point at the third-quarter bars its own headline is about.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast bar looks like a result.** The last bar, Q4 2026 at 560, has the same solid blue and the same bold label as the seven quarters that already happened. The only sign that it's a forecast is a parenthetical at the end of the subtitle and a note in the source line. A board member scanning the chart, or someone who sees the slide later without the talk, will read 560 as a closed quarter and may compare it with Q4 2025's 480 as real growth. Projections should look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Draw that bar so it reads as an estimate at a glance, for example as an outline or a lighter tint, and put "forecast" right under or on the bar. If the September forecast has a range, show it there. *Defect.* Fact needed from the maker: whether the September forecast has a range around 560.
2. **The chart doesn't show the headline's point.** The headline says shipments peak each third quarter, but Q3 2025 and Q3 2026 look the same as every other bar. The reader has to check eight labels to confirm the claim. Keep one color for the series and a second, stronger color for the points the reader must notice (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Here that means the two Q3 bars, with the other six muted. The headline and the chart would then make the point together instead of the chart only backing it up. *Convention.*

**Smaller things.**
- The subtitle says "Q4 2026" twice: "…eight quarters to Q4 2026 (Q4 2026 is the forecast)." Once the bar itself is marked as the forecast, the parenthetical can go. If it stays, shorten it to "…to Q4 2026, the last a forecast."
- The year-over-year change from Q3 2025 to Q3 2026 (610 to 705) is probably what a board will ask about. Right now they have to work it out from two labels. If that's a point you want to make, label the difference between the two Q3 bars, using the figures already on the slide. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, the values are labeled directly on the bars, and the unit ("Thousands of units") sits under the chart title. Nearly all the ink is data, and nobody has to read anything off the axis. Keep this.
- The headline states the finding in words instead of naming the topic. That's the right habit for a board slide, so keep it as you rework the chart to match it.
- The source line names the system and says the forecast date. That's exactly the provenance a board expects. Keep it.
