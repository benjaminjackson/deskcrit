**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline, "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into four serif paragraphs of the same size and color. This is a plain information page for people who ride a small river ferry. It feels calm, quiet and a little bookish. Nothing in the body stands out, so a passenger who wants to know when the boat runs has to read every paragraph to find out.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells passengers how the ferry runs, read while planning a trip or while standing at the landing. It informs and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The type is clean and easy to read, but the page doesn't yet do its job. The facts a passenger acts on, when the boat runs and when the last one leaves, are buried in running prose and given only as vague times of day. A rider can't plan a trip from it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule has no real times** (defect). The first paragraph says the ferry runs "between early morning and late evening," and the third says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk." These are the lines a passenger acts on, and each can be read more than one way. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00, and dusk moves by more than an hour over the winter. A rider who reads it wrong misses the last boat and is stuck on the wrong bank. The fix is to give clock times for the first and last departures, in summer and in winter. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times in the regular season and in winter, and the dates when the winter timetable starts and ends.
2. **Four topics run as one even block of prose** (convention, Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). The paragraphs cover the schedule, bicycles, the winter timetable and the rebuilt landings. They are all the same size and weight, with no heading between them. A passenger skims for one thing ("can I bring my bike?", "when's the last boat?") and has no way in except reading from the top. The winter change sits in the third paragraph, apart from the schedule it changes. The fix is to put a short subhead over each topic, using words already in the copy (Timetable, Bicycles, Winter, Landings), and to move the winter timetable up next to the regular one. The subheads should come from the sans face of the headline, at a step between the headline and the body, so the page keeps its two-face plan. Once the clock times exist, setting the timetable as a short list rather than a sentence would make it scannable at a glance.

**Smaller things.**
- In the second paragraph, "crossing." sits alone on its last line, a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrap the line or edit it so at least two words carry over.
- The last paragraph, about the rebuilt landings, is news rather than instructions. With subheads in place it can stay last, but it shouldn't sit between a rider and the schedule.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing is clear: a bold sans headline over a plain text serif. The two faces are plainly different, and each has an obvious job.
- The body is set well for reading: flush left, lines about 68 to 72 characters long, roomy line spacing and dark text on white. Keep all of this when the subheads go in.
- The space between paragraphs is clearly larger than the space between lines, so each paragraph reads as its own group.
