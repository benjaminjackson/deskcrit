**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading at top left, "How the river ferry runs in winter", then down two paragraphs to the pale gray-blue box. It reads as a plain, calm information page for riders, likely one section of a transit or town site. It feels tidy and generic. Everything is pushed against the left edge of the frame, and the right third and bottom half are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling local riders when the winter ferry runs, what can delay it, and what vehicles pay, so they can plan a crossing. It states plain facts and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy is clean and the page mostly works, but the things a rider acts on are hard to find. The times are spelled out inside running sentences, one crossing time can be read two ways, and the text has no left margin with lines that run too long. Fix the timetable copy first.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable is buried in prose, and one line can be read two ways.** In paragraph 1, "every forty minutes", "seven in the morning" and "six in the evening" are written as words in the middle of sentences. A rider glancing back to check a time has to read the whole sentence again. "The last at six in the evening" can mean the last boat leaving the north landing or the last crossing in either direction. A rider on the far bank who reads it the second way and arrives at 6:15 may find no boat home. That's a defect, because these are the times people plan around. The direction: put the times in numerals and break them out of the prose, for example "First crossing: 7:00 am · Last crossing: 6:00 pm · Every 40 minutes", and say which landing each time applies to. Fact needed from the maker: whether 6:00 pm is the last departure from the north landing only, and when the last return crossing leaves.

2. **The text has no left margin.** The heading, both paragraphs and the box all start at x = 0, against the left edge of the frame. Text that touches the edge looks cropped or broken, and the eye has no frame to settle into. This is a convention (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "Margin proportions"). It also makes the page look tipped, with all the weight at the top left and a dead band on the right (Arnheim, "Balance"). If this is a column cut from a larger page, check it in context. As shown, it needs a real left margin, or a centered reading column. Defect.

3. **The lines are too long.** Paragraph 1 runs about 100 to 105 characters per line ("From November … The first crossing is at"), well past Bringhurst's 45 to 75, so the eye can lose its place moving back to the start of the next line. This is a convention, and on a page this short the cost is moderate. Narrow the column to about 65 to 70 characters. The empty right side has room for it, and moving the column in also helps fix finding 2.

**Smaller things.**
- The box has more padding below the text (about 45 px) than above it (about 24 px), so it looks like a line is missing. Make the padding equal (common sense).
- The heading promises how the ferry *runs*, but the box covers fares and vehicles, a different topic with no label of its own. Give it a short subhead, or widen the heading's scope (WCAG 2.4.6).
- "Cars pay the standard fare" gives no amount or link, so a driver still doesn't know the cost. Fact needed from the maker: the standard car fare, or where it is listed.
- "Vans over two metres high use the cargo deck" doesn't say whether they pay more. Fact needed from the maker: the fare for vans on the cargo deck.

**What's working.**
- The type is clean: one sans-serif family, a bold heading clearly above regular body text, and only two text sizes. There's no doubt where to start.
- The pale tinted box separates the fares from the schedule using tone only, with no border or rules. That's the quiet way to make a group, so keep it.
- "Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue" names a specific place and a specific action. It's the clearest instruction on the page.
