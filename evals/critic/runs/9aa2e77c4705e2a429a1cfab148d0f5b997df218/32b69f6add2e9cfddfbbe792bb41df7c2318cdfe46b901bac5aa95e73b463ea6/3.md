**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers in the top row, 184,220 first, then drops to the three colored lines in the chart, which all rise. It reads as a calm, tidy web-analytics overview for a small marketing or growth team. Then it stalls, because the chart never says which line is which channel, what the values are, or which weeks are on the x-axis. The biggest element on the page answers no question.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a weekly glance for a marketing or growth team that wants traffic, sign-ups and conversion over the last 12 weeks, and which channels drive visits. It's a dashboard that reports measured numbers, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The stat cards do their job well. The chart, which takes up more than half the screen, doesn't: it has no channel names, no axis values and no dates, so "Visits by channel" shows three nameless shapes. Until the chart can be read, this is three numbers and a decoration.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart can't be decoded.** This is a defect. The "Visits by channel" panel promises channels but names none. The blue, orange and green lines have no labels and no legend, the gridlines carry no values, and the x-axis has no week marks. The reader can see that something rose, but not which channel, by how much, or when the dip in week 8 happened. They can't answer the one question the title raises. Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*) and Few both require axes with values and units, and series labels, so the reader never has to guess what a mark measures. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*) and Tufte prefer labels placed at the data over a legend.

   The empty band about 70 px tall under the plot and the blank strip about 50 px wide to its left look like room kept for axis labels that aren't there. They may have failed to render, but in this image they're missing.

   Fix: put the channel name at the right end of each line, add a few y values (visits) on the gridlines, and add week or date marks along the x-axis. If the labels exist in the live page and didn't render in this capture, the fix is to make sure they render.
2. **Color is the only thing that tells the channels apart.** This is a convention (WCAG 1.4.1, *Use of Color*). Even with a legend, the orange and the mid-green get close for viewers with red-green color blindness, and nothing else separates the lines. Labels at the line ends (finding 1) solve most of this. Also check the orange and green in a color-blindness simulator, or vary line style or lightness, so the two lower lines stay distinct.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title names a topic, not a finding. Once the lines are labeled, a short caption with the main trend would make the chart readable at a glance. This is taste: no approved source states the rule.
- The green delta lines ("+6.1% vs previous 12 weeks") are small, mid-green text on white. They look like they pass, but measure them against 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3).
- The chart breaks down visits only, while sign-ups and conversion rate, the numbers a growth team acts on, get no breakdown. This is taste. Consider whether visits is really the chart this page should lead with.

**What's working.**
- The stat cards are well built. Each has a label, then a big figure, then a comparison to the previous period, in three clear steps. "+0.1 pts" correctly treats a change in a rate as points, not percent. Keep this pattern.
- The type scale is tight and disciplined: roughly the "Overview" heading, the large figures, the panel title, the labels and the small deltas, each with a clear job. Everything sits on one grid with even gutters.
- The chart uses very little ink: faint gridlines and no effects, with the data lines doing the work. Once labels are added, it needs nothing else.
