**First read.** The eye lands on the big dark-blue 38% wedge, then goes up to the serif headline. After that it splits: it either climbs into the fan of ten thin slices with tiny gray labels at top left, or crosses to the 14-row legend on the right. It reads as a cost-breakdown page from a company annual report. It feels calm and well set, but the left half is busier than the content needs.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one page of an annual report, telling shareholders and staff where fiscal 2026 operating costs went, so a reader comes away knowing the few categories that matter and roughly how big they are. No claim is at stake: these are sourced figures, not a case the reader has to be won over to.

**Verdict.** The page is clean, but the chart is the wrong kind for the data. A 14-slice pie with a separate color legend makes the reader match colors back and forth, and the pie tells them only one thing the legend table doesn't already: salaries are over a third. Fix the chart and this page is close to done.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart doesn't suit 14 categories** (convention). Ten of the 14 slices are slivers of 1–4%, fanned into the top-left quarter with leader lines and small gray labels. No one can compare a 3% sliver with a 2% one by angle. Their labels say "3%" or "2%" but not which category, so the reader has to go to the legend for every one. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1) both say a pie works up to about five slices. Meanwhile the legend on the right is already a sorted table with exact values, so the pie mostly repeats it less precisely. Two ways to fix it:
   - Turn it into a sorted horizontal bar chart, with the category name and percentage at each bar. That merges the chart and the table into one thing.
   - Or keep the pie, but show the four or five largest slices and merge the rest into one gray "Other" slice, with the table listing what's inside it.

   Either way the reader reads the chart directly instead of matching colors.
2. **Fourteen hues, with matching by color alone** (defect). The legend links each row to its slice only by color, and several colors are near pairs: teal (Rent) vs. light teal (Equipment repair), mid-blue (Software licences) vs. indigo (Utilities) vs. purple (Marketing), orange (Cloud hosting) vs. salmon (Postage). Travel (red) and Insurance (green) sit next to each other, which is the classic pair a red-green color-blind reader can't tell apart. So for most readers the small slices can't be identified reliably, and for some they can't be identified at all. Tufte and Wilke both say to label at the data rather than in a legend. Common sense says no palette keeps 14 categories apart. The fix in #1 solves this: with direct labels, color no longer carries the category, so it can drop to one or two colors, for example the leading category in the accent and the rest in gray.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding** (taste). "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" tells the reader what the chart is about but leaves them to work out the point. The point is plainly that salaries and benefits, rent, and cloud hosting take about two-thirds of costs, and everything else is small change. Stating that, using figures already on the page (e.g. "Salaries and benefits took 38% of operating costs"), turns the page from something to decode into something to confirm. Keep the current line as the subhead if you want the scope stated too.

**Smaller things.**
- The white "12%" on the orange wedge looks well under the 4.5:1 that bold text at this size needs (WCAG 1.4.3); "16%" on teal is borderline. Use dark text on the lighter wedges, or move those labels outside. Measure all four in-wedge labels.
- The shares have no point of comparison. An annual-report reader will ask what changed since last year, and the page doesn't say (Few, pitfall 2). Add the prior-year share or a change column to the table. Fact needed from the maker: the fiscal 2025 share for each category.

**What's working.**
- The type is well judged: a serif headline and subhead over a plain sans for labels and figures, with a clear three-step scale (headline, subhead, table and source). Keep it.
- The table is good: sorted by value, light rules, bold right-aligned percentages, and slices ordered clockwise from 12 o'clock to match it. If you go with bars, keep this order and the right-aligned figures.
- The source line states the source and the rounding, and the shares add to exactly 100. That's the kind of honesty a report page needs.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the gray leader-line labels are real text or part of a chart image, which matters for screen readers and for the report's text alternative.
