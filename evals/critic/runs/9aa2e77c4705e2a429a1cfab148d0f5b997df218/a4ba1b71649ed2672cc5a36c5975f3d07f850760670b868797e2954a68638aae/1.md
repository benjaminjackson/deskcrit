**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Shipments peak each third quarter" and then drops to the row of blue bars. The tallest bar, Q3 2026 at 705, wins at once. It reads as one page of a board sales deck. The page feels orderly and sober, but the extruded 3D bars look like an old office-chart default sitting under modern, carefully set type.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is page 3 of a board deck in which the sales team shows directors that shipments are seasonal, with a third-quarter peak, so they can plan around it. To believe that, a director needs to see the Q3 high repeat in actual results and to tell actuals from the forecast. The chart shows the first, but it never visually separates the second.

**Verdict.** The page mostly works: the headline states the finding, the chart backs it, and the source line is honest. Two things hold it back. The forecast bar looks exactly like the seven actual bars, and the 3D drawing makes every bar harder to read than it needs to be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The Q4 2026 forecast looks like an actual result.** The last bar (560) has the same blue, the same 3D form and the same bold value label as the seven actual quarters. The only signs it's a forecast are a parenthesis at the end of the subhead and the source line at the foot. A director scanning the chart reads 560 as a result. That's the number most likely to be quoted back in the meeting, and the claim that 2026 peaked in Q3 depends on it. This is a defect (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16: forecasts must look different from observed data). Fix: give the forecast bar a different treatment that still shows without color, such as an outline or lighter fill plus a "Forecast" label right at the bar, or a range if the forecast has one.
2. **3D bars hide the values.** Every bar has a dark side face and a lighter top cap, and the value labels sit above the caps. The reader can't tell whether a bar ends at its front edge or its back edge, so the gridlines at 400 and 600 stop being reliable. The depth also adds ink that carries no data, and it's the one dated element on an otherwise clean page. This breaks a convention, and it costs something here, because the bar heights are the whole argument (Wilke, ch. 26.1, "Avoid gratuitous 3D"; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*). Fix: draw the bars flat, so each value is read from one length.
3. **The layout makes the reader do the seasonality comparison themselves.** This one is taste. The headline is about a pattern across years, but the eight bars run as one sequence in which year-on-year growth is the strongest visual signal. To check "each third quarter", the eye has to jump from bar 3 to bar 7 and compare each with its neighbors. Putting the two years side by side, so that Q1–Q4 line up for 2025 and 2026 (paired bars, or one line per year), would turn the headline into something the reader sees at a glance.

**Smaller things.**
- "Peak each third quarter" rests on two cycles, and the 2026 one is checked against a forecast. "Each" claims a little more than two years show. This is common sense, not a rule: either show more years or soften the headline to what the two years show.
- The empty band of about 120 px below the footer leaves the page sitting high and makes the chart smaller than it could be. The chart could take that height. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding as a sentence, and the subhead says what was counted, where it went and over what span. A director knows what to look for before reaching the bars.
- The chart labeling is complete and quiet. The title and unit ("Thousands of units") sit above the plot, values are labeled directly on the bars, the axis starts at zero, the gridlines are faint, and the source line names both the order system and the September forecast. Keep all of this when the bars are redrawn.
- There's a clear type plan: a serif headline, a sans for everything else, and a short scale of distinct sizes.
