**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Update the card that pays for your subscription," drops into the first paragraph, and then picks out the blue links. It's a help-center article for a billing product. It feels calm, sober, and trustworthy: one typeface, one accent color, plenty of air. Only on the second pass do I notice that the page never tells me how to update the card.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help article for an account owner or billing admin who came here to swap a payment card and wants the steps and the consequences, quickly. No claim is at stake. This is instructions, not persuasion.

**Verdict.** It looks clean and well built, but it fails its main job. The title promises a how-to, and the body gives policy (who may do it, what happens to invoices, what happens on decline) without ever giving the steps or a way to reach "the billing page." Second to that, the decline paragraph states the one deadline a reader will hold the company to in words that can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**

1. **The how-to has no "how."** The title is an instruction ("Update the card…"), and the first sentence only says it can be done "from the billing page." There's no path to that page, no steps, and no link, even though four lesser destinations in the same text are linked. A reader who came to do this task has to leave the article and hunt through the product, which is exactly what a help page exists to prevent. *Defect* (Nielsen 10, help that is short and task-focused). Direction: make the action the first thing after the title. Link "the billing page" (those words are already there), and put the steps in a short numbered list just under the title, before the invoice and role caveats. Fact needed from the maker: where the billing page sits in the product and the steps to replace the card there.

2. **The decline paragraph's deadline reads two ways.** "We try a declined card again after three days and after seven days… If the card is still declined after the second attempt, the subscription moves to the free plan." First, "after three days" after what: the first decline, or the previous retry? Second, which attempt is "the second"? If the first charge counts, the second attempt is the day-3 retry and the plan drops after three days. If only retries count, it drops after day 7. These are the dates when a team loses its paid plan, so a reader who picks the wrong reading may not fix the card in time. *Defect.* Direction: name the starting point and say which retry is the last one in plain words, e.g. "…after the retry on day seven." Fact needed from the maker: the day the downgrade happens, counted from the first decline.

3. **Nothing shows where you are.** In the left list, "Update a payment card" looks exactly like its siblings, and "Billing" in the top nav isn't marked either. The breadcrumb helps, but someone scanning the section list for the next related task can't see where they are in it. *Convention* (Nielsen 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist", guideline 5). Direction: mark the current page in the side list with one clear cue, such as a bolder weight, a bar, or a background, and mark Billing in the top nav the same way.

**Smaller things.**
- Inline links ("check your billing history," "roles and permissions," "paying by invoice," "restore your previous plan") are marked by color alone. Mid-blue against near-black body text likely falls short of the 3:1 difference WCAG G183 asks for, and some readers will see plain words. Underline them by default.
- Body lines in the main column run about 80–85 characters, over Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the text column a little. The empty right side has room to absorb it.
- A help center with no visible search box makes every reader browse the nav (Nielsen 7). Add search to the header.
- "If you are not sure which invoice is next, check your billing history before you make the change" doesn't say why it matters. Spell out the consequence, which the paragraph already implies: an open invoice will still charge the old card.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is tight and honest: headline, one subhead, body, nav, and a small label size, each clearly its own step, so the page scans without effort. Keep it.
- One grid governs the page. The 96 px margins line up the wordmark and sidebar, the nav ends on the matching right margin, and "In this section" sits on the same line as the breadcrumb. It feels orderly without any rules or boxes.
- The subhead "When the new card is declined" is written as the reader's own situation, not a category label. Do the same for the steps section you add.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the inline and sidebar links, and whether an underline appears on hover or focus.
- The click area of the sidebar links: about 31 px per row looks fine if the whole row is clickable.
