**First read.** My eye goes to the serif headline "Savings deposits rose every month". It then drops straight to the June bar, which is nearly twice as tall as January's and much taller than May's, and from there to "$4.82M" on the right. This reads as a credit union's half-year report to its members: calm, trustworthy, and well made. What I take from the first few seconds is "June was a breakout month". Only when I reach the small gray source line at the bottom do I learn that June includes a one-off bonus payroll.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a public report page that tells Brookhollow members how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026. It reports measured figures with a stated source, so no claim is at stake that members would need persuading of.

**Verdict.** This is a clean, honest, well-built report page and it mostly does its job. The one real problem is that the biggest thing on the page, the June bar, tells a story the page then takes back in its faintest line of text. Members leave believing deposits surged in June when a one-off payroll did most of the lifting.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June spike is explained only in the footnote.** Defect. The June bar ($1150k) is the tallest, darkest, most eye-catching mark on the page, and the headline invites the eye to read the rise. The only thing that says June is unusual is the end of the 12 px gray source line under the chart, "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll." Most readers will never get there, so they take a one-off as growth. The same issue reaches the right column: "Average deposit, Jun 2026" and "Accounts that made a deposit, Jun 2026" are both June-only figures, and the reader can't tell whether "Up $11" and "Up 402" compare a bonus month with a bonus month. Fix direction: put the note on the chart, as a short label beside the June bar or a visibly different treatment of that bar with a label. Repeat it, or point to it, on the two June cards so the comparison reads as like-for-like or plainly not. (This is my own judgment on chart annotation; no approved source states it.) Fact needed from the maker: whether June 2025, and January to June 2025, also included a mid-year bonus payroll.

2. **"Up … on …" can be read two ways.** Defect, and minor. "Up $11 on Jun 2025" can read as "compared with June 2025" or as "up $11 on a date in June 2025". "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025" is clunkier still. These are the comparison figures readers will remember and repeat. An editorial fix keeps the meaning and removes the ambiguity: "Up $11 from June 2025", "Up 6.1% from Jan to Jun 2025".

**Smaller things.**
- Mixed number formats. The chart says "$1150k" and the axis "$1200k", while the card says "$4.82M". Four-digit thousands are hard to read at a glance. Pick one style, such as "$1.15M" and "$1.2M" on the chart, so the June bar and the total read the same way (unslop, "Numbers in mixed formats").
- The chart title ("Deposits into savings accounts, US dollars per month") restates the subhead ("New money paid into member savings accounts, by month…"). Keep the unit and drop the repeated topic from one of them. (Common sense.)
- The mock of the real thing: there is none to check. The page itself is the report.

**What's working.**
- Honest chart construction. The bars start at $0k, the gridlines are pale enough that the bars are the darkest ink, and every bar carries its value, so nobody has to read values off the axis (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*).
- The headline states the finding rather than the topic, and the dates and source are spelled out. Members get the point and where it came from in one pass.
- The type plan is clear: a bold serif for the wordmark and headline, a plain sans for everything else, and roughly four sizes with clear jobs. Keep it.
