**First read.** The eye goes straight to the red and orange block in the middle of the heatmap, then up to the title and across to the three big numbers on the right. Within a few seconds it reads as a support-desk report: tickets pile up on weekday late mornings and fade out by evening. It feels calm and competent, apart from the traffic-light colors, which make the busy hours look like an alarm.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a support lead or team manager reads this Reports screen to decide how many people to staff at each hour of the week. It reports measured counts and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The structure is good: the right chart, clear labels, a source line, and stats that each come with a comparison. But the rainbow color scale hides the very thing this page exists to show, how much busier one hour is than another, and the "Busiest hour 10:00" stat seems to contradict the chart next to it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The rainbow scale doesn't read as an order (defect).** The seven bins run purple, blue, teal, green, yellow, orange, red. Nothing about those hues says "more." The darkest, heaviest color on the grid, the purple used for 0–4 tickets, covers about two-thirds of the cells, so the quiet hours carry the most visual weight. Yellow (20–24) is the lightest color, so the middle of the scale stands out most. To tell lime green (15–19) from teal (10–14), or yellow from orange, a reader has to keep checking the legend. In grayscale, or for a red-green color-blind reader, the green, yellow, orange, and red steps mostly merge, and the 10:00–14:00 shape turns into mush. For someone planning staff by the hour, that is the whole job of the chart. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*) gives the fix: use one hue that goes from light to dark, so the empty night hours sit near-white and the peak sits darkest. The order of the colors then matches the order of the values, and the busy block shows up without the legend. This also stops the page using red and green, which usually mean error and success, to mean "30+" and "15–19."
2. **The busiest-hour stat seems to contradict the chart (defect).** The card on the right says "Busiest hour, weekdays 10:00." But under the column labels, the only red (30+) cells are at 11:00 on Monday and Friday, and 10:00 and 11:00 are both orange on Tuesday to Thursday. A reader who checks the chart against the card finds 11:00 and starts to doubt either the card or the column labels. Both may be right, since a weekday average can put 10:00 a ticket ahead while the 5-ticket bins hide the difference. But a staffing decision should not depend on the reader working that out. The fix: make the chart show the peak the card names. For example, mark the 10:00 weekday column on the chart, or show values in the peak cells so the difference between 10:00 and 11:00 can be read. If the number is wrong, fix the number. Fact needed from the maker: the average weekday count at 10:00 and at 11:00.
3. **The title names a topic, not the finding (taste, common sense).** "When new tickets arrive" tells the reader what the chart covers. It leaves them to find the takeaway themselves, which the colors make hard to do (see finding 1). The subtitle is a good, exact description of the data. The headline is where the page could tell a support lead the one thing to act on: when the peak starts, when it ends, and that weekends are lighter. Any sentence like that must come from the data. Fact needed from the maker: the peak window the team wants this page to state.

**Smaller things.**
- Exact counts appear only as color bins, with no numbers anywhere on the grid. A reader who can't tell the hues apart can't get the values (WCAG 1.4.1). Tooltips may cover this in the live product, but a still can't show them. Fixing finding 1 removes most of the problem.
- The rainbow cells are a stock template move (unslop, "Rainbow, glowing, or pill-shaped chart bars"). Readers have seen this look on many generic dashboards, so this report looks like anyone's. The fix in finding 1 also removes the tell.

**What's working.**
- The chart type fits the question. A day × hour grid with days in natural order and labels every three hours answers "when" at a glance, and the cell gaps are thin enough not to compete with the data.
- Each stat comes with a comparison ("Up 4% on August", "Down 6 min on August"). The source line also clears up the two ambiguities a careful reader would raise: which time zone, and opened versus answered.
- The type is restrained: one family, a clear title, subtitle, and body scale, and big numerals that lead each stat card without shouting.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether hovering a cell shows its exact count. If it does, the missing-numbers point above matters less for mouse users, though not for keyboard or print.
