**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers," drops into the paragraph, and then has nowhere to go. It's one dense block of about 16 lines with no breaks, no times, and nothing to scan for. It reads as a plain info page for people who ride a river ferry. It feels like a memo pinned to a wall, not something a person standing at a landing can use in five seconds.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is a web page telling riders when the ferry runs, how to board (with or without a bicycle), and how to buy tickets. They come to it with one question each, like "when is the next boat?" or "can I bring my bike?". No claim is at stake: the page gives practical facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The type is clean and easy to read line by line, but every answer a rider needs is buried mid-sentence in one paragraph, and the answers that matter most (when the boats run, when the last one leaves) are given as vague phrases, not times.

**What would move it most**
1. **Five topics are packed into one paragraph** (defect). The paragraph holds the schedule and crossing time, the boarding order, bicycle rules, tickets and passes, and winter hours plus where to find timetables. A rider with one question has to read all 16 lines to find their sentence. Someone with a bike has to get past the timetable and boarding order before "Riders with bicycles" turns up in line 5. Someone asking about winter hours finds them in line 12. People skim screen text, and a long unbroken block is the clearest case where skimming fails (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Fix: split it into short sections under plain headings named for the rider's question: running times, boarding, bicycles, tickets and passes, winter, changes. Set the bicycle rules as a short list, since they're steps in order (wait at the gate, wheel to the lower deck, lock to the rail, keep bags on). The text can stay almost word for word. It just needs to stop being one paragraph.
2. **The times riders will act on are too vague to plan around** (defect). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone when the first or last boat goes. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can be read two ways: the market quay or both landings, and any time before dusk. A rider who guesses wrong misses the last boat home in winter, which is the worst case this page exists to prevent. "In winter" also has no dates. These are the lines people will hold the operator to, so they need exact values, and the schedule deserves its own block near the top, not a clause mid-paragraph. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times from each landing in summer and in winter, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends.
3. **The title names a format, not what the page is about** (convention, Krug on self-evident pages). "Notes for ferry passengers" tells the reader this is a set of notes, not which ferry or which crossing. The text right under it names "the river ferry" and "the north landing to the market quay," so the title can be edited to use those words. Keep the title as the one bold anchor it already is.

**Smaller things.**
- The right ~55% of the frame is empty at this desktop width. That's fine for a reading column. But once the text is split into sections, consider whether the schedule could take a short block of its own so the times are found first.

**What's working.**
- The body text is set well: a plain sans at a comfortable size, near-black on white, lines around 60–70 characters, and generous line spacing. Each line reads easily, so the fix is only about structure, not about the type.
- The heading stands clearly above the body in both size and weight, with just two type levels on the page and no stock decoration. Keep that restraint when you add section headings: make them a third level between the two, not a new style.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone, which is where riders standing at a landing will most likely read it.
