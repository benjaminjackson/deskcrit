**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Rainfall at the reservoir," drops to the three solid blue bars, then moves right to "Getting there" and a paragraph about buses and parking. It reads as a visitor page for a park or reservoir, aimed at the public. It looks tidy but unfinished. The chart has no words at all, and the bottom third of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a public information page or panel for visitors to the reservoir: it tells them about recent rainfall and how to get there. No claim is at stake here. It reports facts and directions and doesn't ask the reader to believe anything.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The only thing on the page about rainfall is a chart that can't be read: three bars with no values, units, periods or source. The other half of the page is about something else, so the title promises a topic the page never delivers.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart tells the reader nothing.** The three bars in the boxed panel have no value axis, no numbers, no unit (mm? inches?), no labels for what each bar is (months? years? sites?) and no date or source. A visitor can see that the middle bar is tallest and nothing more. Without an axis they can't tell whether the bars start at zero, so even that comparison can't be trusted. Defect (Tufte, *Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). To fix it, label each bar directly with its period and value and state the unit. Show the zero baseline and add a source and date line. If three bars don't cover enough time to show anything, add more periods. Fact needed from the maker: the rainfall values, their unit, the periods the bars cover and the data source.
2. **The title and the page don't match.** "Rainfall at the reservoir" sits over two unrelated blocks: a chart and travel directions. The page reads as two scraps placed side by side, not one piece, and a visitor looking for directions has to get past a heading about weather. Defect (WCAG 2.4.6: headings describe their section). There are two ways to fix it. Either give the page a title that covers both (a reservoir visitor page) and let "Rainfall" become a section heading over the chart, the way "Getting there" heads its own block. Or split the directions onto their own page or panel.
3. **The chart says no finding in words.** Even once it's labeled, nothing tells the visitor what to take from it: wetter or drier than usual, and does that matter for their visit? This is common sense, not a cited rule: a caption that states the takeaway turns three bars into information. Keep the caption an edit based on the data the maker supplies, and don't invent a trend.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart frame is a dark 2–3 px gray border around a tinted panel. It's as heavy as the data and holds the chart together without saying anything. Remove the frame and the tint, or make them pale, so the bars are the darkest thing in the chart (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*).
- The bottom ~35% of the frame and the area under the text column are leftover space, not a shaped open area. The page looks like it stops early, not like it's resting. Once the chart has its labels and caption, give the empty space a deliberate shape, for example by letting the content set the height (Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*).

**What's working.**
- The type hierarchy is clean: a bold title, a bold section heading and a regular body in one family, with three clearly separate sizes. Keep it as you add chart labels, and keep those labels at one small size.
- The directions copy is concrete and complete. It names the bus number, the stop, how often it runs, when parking is free and where the cycle racks are. A visitor can act on every sentence.
- The restraint is right: one blue, neutral grays, no decoration. The palette is ready for a real chart.
