**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers," then reads down two even paragraphs. The last line, "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel.", is set smaller than the rest, so it reads as a footnote. The page feels calm, plain and trustworthy, like a notice from a local operator. It also feels a little bare: a single text column in the top-left of a mostly empty white frame.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web information page for people about to take a local river ferry, who need to know when it runs, how long it takes and how to bring a bike. No claim is at stake: the page gives facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read, but the page does its job only partly. The two things a traveler most needs to act on are its weakest parts: when the ferry actually runs, and the warning that times change. One is vague and the other is set as small print.

**What would move it most**
1. **The warning is set as the least important line, but it's the one most likely to cost someone a crossing.** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." is the line that changes what a reader does. It sits in a third text size, a few pixels smaller than the body (about 16 px against about 19 px by eye; measure both), with the same weight and color. Two problems follow. First, the size step is too small to read as a separate level, so it looks like a mistake rather than a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: a contrast must be obvious; Bringhurst §3.1.1: sizes come from a scale). Second, it sends the wrong signal. Small print tells people they can skip it, and a winter traveler who skips it misses the boat. Set it at body size, and if you want it to stand out, use weight or a separate position, not a smaller size. That also brings the page down to two sizes, heading and body, which is all it needs. *Defect* (it misleads the reader about what matters); the size collision is a *convention*.
2. **The schedule copy has no times, so the reader can't plan a trip from it.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't give a first or last sailing. Someone deciding whether they can catch a 10 pm crossing home can't tell. "Every forty minutes" can also be read two ways: forty minutes between departures from the north landing, or forty minutes between any departures from either side. Read the first way, a traveler at the market quay may expect a boat that isn't coming. The noticeboard line suggests the times exist and live at the landing. A traveler reading this page at home has no way to see them. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times from each landing, and whether the forty-minute interval applies at each landing. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- "Timetables change in winter" doesn't say when winter starts or ends, so a reader in early November or March can't tell which schedule applies. Fact needed from the maker: the dates the winter timetable runs.

**What's working.**
- **The text is set for reading.** One plain sans family, near-black on white, lines of about 74 characters, comfortable line spacing, a clean ragged right, and space between paragraphs rather than indents. Keep all of it.
- **The heading is bold as well as large,** so it stands clearly apart from the body (Dair: contrast from more than size), and it plainly says who the page is for.
- **Each paragraph covers one topic,** first the schedule and then bicycles, and the bike paragraph spells out who does what and in what order ("Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). Every sentence should be that concrete.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I couldn't judge how the column reflows on a phone, or whether the empty right and lower parts of the frame are on purpose or a piece of a larger page. A left-aligned reading column on a wide screen is normal, so I haven't counted the space as a defect.
