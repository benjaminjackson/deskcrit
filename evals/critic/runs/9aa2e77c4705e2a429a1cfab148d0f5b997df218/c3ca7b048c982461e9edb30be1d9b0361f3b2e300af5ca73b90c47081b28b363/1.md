**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and the three short lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a local ferry operator's website: clean, quiet and competent. It also feels like a stock template, all type on white, and nothing on screen shows the bay, the boat or the calm the headline promises. The bottom third of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the landing hero for a small passenger ferry, meant to get locals and visitors to check times and take the boat. It mostly gives facts and asks the reader to believe very little, so no real claim is at stake beyond whether the crossing is as pleasant as it says.

**Verdict.** It's clear and does the basic job: you know what it is and what to click. But it's a generic centered text stack selling something you'd want to see, and it leaves out where the boat actually goes. As a result it informs without making anyone want to ride, and the first thing a visitor needs to know takes a click.

**What would move it most**
1. **The crossing is never shown, and the layout is the stock centered stack.** The copy sells an experience: "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck", "twelve calm minutes". But the whole hero is type stacked on one centered axis on a white ground (eyebrow, headline, paragraph, list, button), with nothing in the empty lower third. The viewer has seen this layout on countless product pages, so this ferry looks like anyone's, and the "calm" lives only in an adjective. This is a template tell (unslop, "Hero stacked on one centered axis"), and it's my taste that this product in particular needs a picture. Direction: let a real view of this crossing, such as the deck or the harbor from the water, carry the mood. Set the text beside it in an asymmetric split, or over a large image, and use that space for the picture instead of leaving it blank.
2. **The page never says where you end up.** "Cross the bay" and "leave the north landing" name only a starting point. A visitor deciding whether this ferry suits them has to click through to the timetable to learn the destination. The opening hours ("early morning until late evening") are also vague enough that the first question, "can I make one today?", stays unanswered. This is a defect: it slows the exact person the page is for. Direction: name both ends of the crossing in the paragraph, where "north landing" already sits. Fact needed from the maker: the name of the landing the boat arrives at.

**Smaller things.**
- The three-line paragraph is centered, so each line starts somewhere new and it reads more slowly than it should. Set it flush left, or cut it to one or two lines if it has to stay centered (Tschichold; Bringhurst 2.1.3).
- The three feature lines ("Bicycles travel free" and the rest) look only about 2 px smaller than the paragraph and have no markers. They read as a slightly smaller paragraph, not as a list. Either match the body size or make them clearly a list.
- The headline breaks as "twelve / calm minutes", which separates the number from "minutes". A break after "bay" ("Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes") keeps the promise in one line.
- There's no header or navigation in the frame. If the live page has none, visitors lose the usual way to find fares or contact details (Nielsen, Jakob's law).

**What's working.**
- The headline is specific and concrete: a number, a feeling and a place in one line. Most ferry pages don't manage that, so keep it as the lead.
- There's one primary action, it says exactly what happens ("See the timetable"), and it is clearly the only button.
- The palette is restrained: near-black type plus a single blue shared by the brand name and the button, so the eye connects the two.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button. Whether content follows below this first screen, which would change how much the empty lower third matters.
