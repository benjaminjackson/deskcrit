**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "River ferry timetable" at top left, then drops into three paragraphs of prose. The title promises a grid of departure times, so I scan for times and find none. The page looks calm and tidy, but it feels like something is missing, as if the timetable itself never loaded.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells foot passengers and cyclists when the river ferry leaves and how to ride it, read by someone planning a trip or standing at the landing. No claim is at stake. These are plain service facts, and the reader only needs to find them.

**Verdict.** It does not do its job. The page is called a timetable but gives no departure times. A rider can't learn when the next boat leaves, which is the one thing they came for.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable is missing.** The heading says "timetable" but the page below it holds three paragraphs of prose. The only times it gives are "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "one boat each hour" in winter, and "before dusk". A rider can't plan around "early morning" or catch a boat that leaves "before dusk". They have to guess the first and last sailings, or go to the landing and read the printed timetable the page sends them to. That defeats the page. This is a defect: the heading doesn't name what the section holds (WCAG 2.4.6, Headings and labels), and the copy leaves out the facts riders act on. The fix: put the real departure times in a table, one for summer and one for winter, with the first and last crossings from each landing, right under the heading. Move the prose below it as notes. If there is no table, rename the page so it doesn't promise one. Fact needed from the maker: the actual departure times from each landing in each season, and the date the winter timetable starts and ends.
2. **The three topics run together as undifferentiated prose.** The paragraphs cover how often the boat runs and how long it takes, the rules for bicycles, and winter changes plus where to find notices. They have no subheads, so a cyclist looking for the bike rule or a winter rider looking for the last boat has to read every line to find it. This is a defect, from Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web": people skim, and text they skim needs headings or lists. The fix: give each topic a short heading built from words already in the text ("Bicycles", "Winter"), and pull the key numbers out where the eye can find them (forty minutes, about twelve minutes per crossing, one boat each hour in winter).

**Smaller things.**
- "A little longer when the tide turns against the boat": a rider can't plan with this. Fact needed from the maker: how much longer the crossing takes against the tide.
- "Lists any changes for the coming week": the page doesn't say where online changes are posted, or whether this page shows them. Fact needed from the maker: whether service changes are published anywhere besides the noticeboards at the landings.

**What's working.**
- The type is plain and easy to read. There's one sans-serif face, the bold heading is clearly a step above the body, the lines run about 70 to 75 characters, and the line spacing is comfortable. Keep all of this when the table goes in.
- The copy reads like a person speaking and is specific where it can be ("lock them to the rail along the side of the deck"). Keep that voice for the notes under the table.
- The single left column with one open area to its right is calm and has room for a table without crowding.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page reflows on a phone, which matters for a rider checking times at the landing.
