**First read.** The eye lands on the bold near-black heading, "Notes for ferry passengers". It then drops into gray body text so pale that you lean in to read it. It reads as a plain information page for people about to take a small river ferry, and it feels calm, tidy and a bit washed out. The one line that could change your trip, "Timetables change in winter", is the smallest and last thing on the page, and the eye nearly skips it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long the crossing takes, and how bikes board. No claim is at stake, so the reader only needs to find and trust the facts.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The text people came to read is too faint, and the facts they will act on are vague or hidden at the bottom. The layout is sound; the problems are contrast and what the words actually say.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is too pale to read comfortably.** The two paragraphs and the winter note are a light gray on white, roughly #9CA3AF by eye, which is about 2.5:1 contrast. WCAG 1.4.3 asks for 4.5:1 for text this size. That is a defect: people with low vision, anyone reading on a phone at the landing in daylight, and anyone glancing quickly will struggle with the very facts the page exists to give. It also leaves the page with only two values, near-black and pale gray. In grayscale, everything except the heading reads as one faint block (Albers; Refactoring UI). Fix: darken the body text until it reaches at least 4.5:1, and measure it to check. Keep the gray step for the secondary note only if that note also passes.
2. **The schedule doesn't give times a passenger can act on.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" can be read two ways. One reader takes "early morning" as 6:00, another as 8:00, and the second one misses the first boat. The same goes for the last boat home, which is the one that strands people. On a ferry page, times are what people plan around and hold you to, so this is a defect, not a style note. Fact needed from the maker: the times of the first and last crossings.
3. **The most important caveat sits lowest in the hierarchy.** "Timetables change in winter" can cancel everything above it, but it is the smallest, palest and last line on the page. A passenger who skims the schedule paragraph and stops will think they have the full answer. "Winter" is also vague: a reader can't tell whether their trip date counts. Fix: raise this note to the level of the schedule. Put it right after the times it changes, at body size, rather than as a footer. Fact needed from the maker: the dates or months when the winter timetable applies.

**Smaller things.**
- The body lines run about 72 to 75 characters, at the top of the comfortable 45 to 75 range (Bringhurst). The measure can stay as it is, but don't widen it.
- The heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", names a category rather than what's in it. It could sit on any transit page. That's taste: something like "Ferry times and bikes" would tell people at once whether this page has their answer. That edit only uses words already on the page.

**What's working.**
- There is one clear place to start. The heading is much larger and heavier than the body, so nobody wonders where to begin.
- The single flush-left column, with more space between paragraphs than between lines, makes three topics read as three groups: schedule, bikes, winter. Keep that structure when you reorder.
- The bike paragraph is good copy. It tells the reader who does what and in what order: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail". Write the schedule line with the same plainness.
