**First read.** The eye lands on the big white headline at left, and the brightest part of the screen, the lime-green speckles on the right half, pulls it straight off. Those speckles turn out to be nothing in particular. It reads as a local plant nursery's homepage. It feels fresh, green and friendly, but anonymous: nothing on the screen shows this nursery or a single plant it sells.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage hero of a small nursery's website, and its job is to get local gardeners who have shady spots to browse the stock and come in. That reader is being asked to believe the nursery has ferns and shade plants "for every corner", "ready to plant this weekend". To believe it they need to see the actual plants, and the screen shows none.

**Verdict.** The hero doesn't do its job yet. The type and layout are clean, but the half of the screen meant to show the product is filled with a generic pattern of random green ovals, so the main promise is never shown, and that pattern also pulls the eye away from the one button.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero picture shows no plants: defect.** The right side and the whole background are scattered flat ovals in many greens. You can't find a fern frond or a hosta leaf, and nothing looks like a shady corner, a greenhouse or a hill. The headline names a specific product ("ferns and shade plants") and the subhead names a specific place and time ("grown on the hill", "ready to plant this weekend"). The picture backs up none of it, so a visitor deciding whether to make the trip has to take the words on trust. It also goes against the message. The brightest ovals are sunlit lime and yellow-green, the color of full sun, on a page selling plants for shade. A generic pattern where the product's picture should be is also a stock template move (Sokolovsky, unslop: the hero picture should be the product or something from its world). A viewer has seen this decorative leafy background on many sites, so this nursery looks like any other. The headline and picture should make one point together (taste, because no approved source states it). Fix: replace the pattern with a real photo of the nursery's own ferns and shade plants, ideally in their actual greenhouse or on the hill. Keep its tones in the deep, cool greens of shade so it supports "shade" instead of arguing with it.

2. **The bright right half outweighs the text, and the one action is the weakest kind of button: defect.** The left side is darkened behind the type. The right side is the lightest and busiest part of the frame, so it carries most of the visual weight (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). After the headline the eye drifts right into the speckles and out at the edge. It doesn't drop to "Browse the greenhouse", which is a thin white outline with no fill. Material Design and Apple's Human Interface Guidelines treat an outline-only button as a secondary action, so here the one thing to do is styled as the least important thing on screen. The busy pattern makes it worse: half the screen is saturated, high-contrast texture competing with the focal point (taste, because no approved source states it). Fix: calm the right side so it sits behind the type in value and busyness, which a real shade photo, color-graded down, will mostly do. Then make the button a filled, high-contrast button so the path runs from headline to subhead to action.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline ends with "corner" alone on its third line, a short runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Widening the text box a little or rebalancing the breaks would make it two lines, or three more even ones.
- "Browse the greenhouse" works as a label, but nothing tells a first-time visitor whether it opens the online stock list or directions for a visit. The menu has both "Plants" and "Visit". Make sure the button leads where the subhead points, to plants ready this weekend.

**What's working.**
- **The copy is specific and sounds like a person.** "Grown on the hill and ready to plant this weekend" gives a place, a time and a reason to act, which is a lot better than a generic welcome line. Keep it, and show it in the picture.
- **The type is restrained.** It's one sans-serif family with a clear scale: a large headline, a subhead about a third its size, then the button and the menu. The headline is set tight, bold but not black, so it reads as one block with an obvious place to start.
- **The header is quiet and plain.** The dark-green name and four plain menu labels with generous margins stay out of the way and match the green theme.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the background moves or is animated, and how the text block and button reflow on a phone, where the pattern would sit directly behind the type.
