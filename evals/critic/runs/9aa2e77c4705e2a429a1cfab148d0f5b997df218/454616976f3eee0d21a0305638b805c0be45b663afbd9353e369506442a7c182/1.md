**First read.** The eye goes to "$4.82M" first, moves across the three numbers in the top cards, then drops into the large blue block of bars and follows them climbing to December. It reads as the revenue home screen of a subscription business, made for founders or a finance lead. It feels calm and trustworthy: gray, navy, one blue, and nothing loud.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal desktop dashboard that shows a SaaS team how its revenue did in 2025, by month and by plan. It's a dashboard of measured figures, so no claim is at stake: it asks the reader to believe nothing beyond the numbers.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made dashboard and it mostly does its job. The one real problem is that its numbers don't agree with each other. The table called "Revenue by plan, 2025" adds up to the ARR figure in the first card, not to the 2025 revenue the chart shows. A finance reader who checks will stop trusting the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plan table's label and its total contradict the chart (defect).** The right-hand table is headed "Revenue by plan, 2025", and its Total is $4.82M. That is the same figure as the first card, "Annual recurring revenue, Dec 2025". But the chart beside it is 2025 recognized revenue, and its twelve bars add up to about $4.49M by eye (roughly 312 + 328 + … + 447 thousand). So the page shows two different "2025 revenue" totals 300 px apart. There's a second mismatch too. December's bar is about $447K, which annualizes to well above the $4.82M ARR. That can be legitimate (one-off fees, usage), but nothing on the page says so. The reader who adds things up is exactly who this page is for, and they can't tell whether the table is ARR split by plan, revenue split by plan, or a mistake. Once they wonder, they start doubting the other figures too. The heading has to name what the table actually holds (WCAG 2.4.6, headings describe their content). If it's ARR, say "ARR by plan, Dec 2025" to match the card. If it's 2025 revenue, the total has to match the chart. Which one it is, and whether December revenue includes non-recurring income, are facts only the maker has. Fact needed from the maker: whether the plan table shows Dec 2025 ARR or 2025 recognized revenue, and what makes December's monthly revenue run above ARR.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Revenue by plan" heading sits about 4 px below "Monthly revenue, 2025". It's a near-miss, not a deliberate offset, so the two column heads don't line up (Müller-Brockmann, Arnheim). Put them on one baseline.
- The plan rows have no comparison: no share of the total and no change from 2024, even though every card above them has one (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Add a prior-period change or a share column so the mix can be read as getting better or worse.
- The Total row is styled like the plan rows above it, with the same bold value and the same thin rule. A heavier rule above it or a different weight would make it read as a sum, not a fourth plan.
- The chart title "Monthly revenue, 2025" names the topic but not the finding. This is my taste, not a published rule: the climb from about $312K to $447K is the story, and a caption could say it in words already on the piece.
- The "Jan to Dec 2025" chip in the top right looks like a static label. That's fine if it's fixed, but if it's the date picker it needs a chevron or a border so it looks like a control.
- The nav has "Overview", whose page is titled "Revenue overview", directly above a separate "Revenue" item. The reader can't tell how the two differ (Norman, conceptual model). Either rename the page or make clear what "Revenue" adds.

**What's working.**
- Every top card gives a period and a comparison ("Dec 2025", "+18% vs Dec 2024"), and the change is marked by its sign, not just by green. Keep that pattern.
- The chart is honest and quiet: one blue, bars from zero, faint gridlines, and the units spelled out in the subtitle ("Thousands of US dollars, recognised revenue"). The source line underneath ("closed invoices only") tells a finance reader exactly what's counted. Don't decorate it.
- The type stays restrained: one family, a few sizes, and bold used only for the numbers that matter. That's why "$4.82M" gets the first look without anything shouting.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date chip opens a picker, and what the hover and focus states of the nav look like.
