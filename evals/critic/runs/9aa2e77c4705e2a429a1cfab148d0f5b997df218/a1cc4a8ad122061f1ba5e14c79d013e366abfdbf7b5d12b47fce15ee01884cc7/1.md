**First read.** The eye lands on "How can we help?" in white serif on the navy band, drops to the white search field, and then spreads across the eight topic cards below. Within a second it reads as the help center for a software product with workspaces, teams, and integrations. It feels calm, orderly, and trustworthy, and also familiar: this is the standard help-center layout, done cleanly.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of Harbor's self-service help center, for existing customers who arrive with a specific problem and want to find the answer without contacting support. The page states facts and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** It works. The search is the obvious first move, the topics are named in the reader's own words, and nothing gets in the way. The one gap that matters is that the first screen offers only broad categories and none of the specific answers most visitors come for, so everyone who doesn't search has to guess a category first.

**What would move it most**
1. **The first screen doesn't show the answers people most often need.** Below the search there are only eight category cards ("Getting started", "Account and sign-in", and so on). A visitor whose question is "I can't sign in" or "where's my order" has to pick a bucket, open it, and then scan again. Some buckets overlap: a failed payment could sit under "Billing and invoices" or "Troubleshooting", and a sync error under "Integrations" or "Troubleshooting". Help centers earn their keep by putting the most common answers in front of people. Direction: add a short list of the most-asked articles (a handful of links), either just under the search or right after the topic grid, so people with a common problem skip the category step. Which articles go there is the maker's call, from real search and ticket data. This finding stands unless the page continues below the frame with such a list (see "Couldn't judge"). Taste and common sense: the reference entry for this point has no approved source.

**Smaller things.**
- In five of the eight card descriptions, a single word sits alone on the last line: "your team." (Getting started), "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages.". That gives the grid a ragged, unfinished look. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.4.2) says a paragraph shouldn't end on one short word. Fix with balanced line wrapping, or with small editorial trims so each blurb fits on one line or breaks evenly over two.
- The hero says "search the guides" but the field says "Search for articles". Pick one word for the content, so the reader isn't left wondering whether guides and articles are two different things. (Common sense.)

**What's working.**
- **The page has one clear entry point.** The navy band holds only the question, a one-line instruction, and a wide white search field with strong contrast against the navy. Visitors know at once that they're meant to type. Keep the hero this bare.
- **The topic labels are written in the user's words, not the system's.** "Track a delivery or start a return" and "Fix sync problems and error messages" name tasks people actually come with, not internal module names. Keep this voice when you add more topics.
- **The grid is disciplined.** The logo, "Browse by topic", and the left card column share one left edge. The right card column lines up with "Contact us". The icons are one line set with one stroke weight, in one spot on every card. The quiet borders let the content carry the page. Keep the four-column grid and the single icon style.

- Focus styles on the search field and the cards, and whether a whole card is the click target or only its title.
