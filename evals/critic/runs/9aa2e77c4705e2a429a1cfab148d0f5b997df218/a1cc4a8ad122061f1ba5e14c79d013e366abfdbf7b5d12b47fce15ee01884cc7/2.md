**First read.** The eye lands on the white serif "How can we help?" in the navy band, drops to the search field, then scans the eight topic cards. It's clearly a product's help center home page. It feels calm, tidy, and trustworthy, and also familiar: it looks like most help centers.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the help center home page for customers of a team workspace product, who arrive with a specific problem and want the answer fast. No claim is at stake: the page asks the reader to believe nothing, only to find something.

**Verdict.** It's competent and does its basic job: a visitor can tell where to search and what the topics are. But it stops at a table of contents. Nothing on the page points to the problems people most often arrive with, and the search field, which is the main action, has no visible way to submit.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing points to the most common problems.** Below the search there are only eight cards of equal weight. Someone who arrives with "my calendar stopped syncing" has to guess whether that's under Integrations, Notifications, or Troubleshooting, then click through to find out. The page has room below the grid and nothing in it. The fix: give a band to the few articles people actually open most, such as popular articles or current known issues, and put it between the search and the topics or right under the topics. Then the most likely answer is one click away and the categories are the fallback. The "Status" link already exists in the header, so a current incident could show up here too. *Taste / common sense:* the "anticipation" principle has no approved source, so I'm arguing it on its own terms. Help visitors come with one question, not to browse.
2. **The search field doesn't look like a complete search.** The white box in the hero has no magnifying-glass icon and no submit button. Its only cue is the placeholder "Search for articles", which disappears as soon as someone starts typing. Most people will press Enter, but anyone who looks for a button finds none, and on a touch device there is less to tap. This is the main action on the page, so it should be unmistakable. Add a search icon inside the field, and either a submit button or an icon that works as one, so the field still reads as search once it holds text. *Convention:* placeholders shouldn't be the only label (NN/g, "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful"). The headline and subhead carry some of the labeling here, which is why this is ranked second rather than first.

**Smaller things.**
- **Type scale is muddled below the headline.** I count about six sizes between 14 and 20 px: logo about 20, "Browse by topic" about 18, card titles and subhead about 16, nav about 15, card text about 14. "Browse by topic" sits barely above the card titles, so it doesn't read as its own level. Keep about three steps under the headline, and set the section heading apart by size or color, not by 2 px (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1).
- **Lone words end several card descriptions** ("where." under Notifications, "data." under Privacy and security, also "recovery.", "receipts.", "messages."). This makes the bottom edge of each row ragged. Rebalance the line breaks or tighten the wording so the last lines aren't single words (Bringhurst, section 2.4.2).
- **The copy is generic.** "How can we help?" and "Browse a topic or search the guides." could sit on any help center, and the subhead just describes the two things the page already shows. This costs little here, since familiarity helps people find the search box. Still, cutting the subhead or making it specific to this product would tighten the hero.

**What's working.**
- **One clear focal path.** The dark band holds the headline and search field, and the light area below holds the browse grid. Search comes first and browsing second, which is the right order for this page.
- **The topic cards are well written.** Each title names a category in the reader's own words, and each description gives concrete examples ("Passwords, two-step sign-in, and recovery", "Track a delivery or start a return"). That's what lets someone pick the right card without opening the wrong one. Keep this level of specificity.
- **The icons are consistent.** They are one line set with the same stroke and size, in the navy of the band, and sit in the same spot on every card. The grid reads as one system.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the cards and the search field. Since the cards have no other sign that they're clickable, check that they show a clear hover and focus state.
