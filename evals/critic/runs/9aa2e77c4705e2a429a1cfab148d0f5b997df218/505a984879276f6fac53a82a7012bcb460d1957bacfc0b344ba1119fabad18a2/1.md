**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, "How the river ferry runs in winter", then reads down two plain paragraphs and stops at the gray box. It reads as a public-information page for people who take the ferry. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, close to a council notice done well.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells winter passengers when the ferry runs, what can delay it and what they pay, read on a desktop screen. No claim is at stake: the page only gives facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The page is clean and the headline is clear. Three things get in the way. The schedule is written in words, so readers can't scan it. The one box on the page highlights fares instead of the ice delay, which is the line that can actually leave someone stranded. And the lines are too long.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule can't be scanned.** In the first paragraph, "every forty minutes", "at seven in the morning" and "at six in the evening" are spelled out as words. Someone checking whether they can make the last boat looks for a time and finds a sentence, so they have to read the whole paragraph to pull out three numbers. It also gives only departures from the north landing, so a reader on the other bank can't tell whether these times apply to them. Fix direction: set the times as numerals the eye can pick out, as an editorial edit of the same facts (for example "every 40 minutes… first crossing 7:00, last 6:00 pm"). Also say whether the times are for both landings. *Defect (copy): it slows the reader at the one thing they came for.* Fact needed from the maker: the departure times from the other landing, or confirmation that the north-landing times cover both directions.
2. **The box emphasizes the wrong thing.** The gray box, the only emphasis on the page, holds the fare rules for bicycles, cars and vans. Those are routine. The line that can actually cost a passenger their crossing is "the crew may hold the boat at the landing until the channel is clear. Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue." That line sits in plain body text. A reader skimming the page sees the box and skips the warning. Fix direction: move the emphasis to the ice and delay paragraph, and let the fare rules be a normal paragraph, ideally under their own small heading. *Taste/common sense, argued: emphasis should follow consequence, and the delay is the only line here that changes what the reader does.*
3. **Lines run far past a comfortable length.** The body paragraphs span the full 880 px column at what looks like about 100 to 105 characters per line ("From November to March … The first crossing is at"). On the way back to the start of each line the eye loses its place, which is worse on a page people read standing up, in a hurry. Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 characters, about 66 ideal (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). Fix direction: narrow the text column to about 600 to 640 px at this size and keep the headline on the same left edge. *Convention, and here it costs the reader.* Measure the actual characters per line to confirm.

**Smaller things.**
- Gray box, lower edge: the text has about 24 px of padding above it and about 45 px below, so the box looks like it lost a line. If a line is missing, restore it. If not, make the bottom padding match the top.
- Box copy: "Bicycles ride free on weekdays" leaves the weekend fare to inference, and "the standard fare" gives no amount. A reader can't tell what they will pay. Fact needed from the maker: the standard fare amount and the bicycle fare on weekends.

**What's working.**
- One clear type hierarchy: a bold headline and one body size, with nothing competing. Keep it this sparse when you add anything.
- The copy talks to the reader in plain, complete sentences that tell them what to do ("Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue"). That is exactly the voice a notice like this needs. Keep it when you change the times to numerals.
- The palette is quiet: near-black on white plus one pale tint. The tint is enough to mark the box without a border, which is the right way to set off a block (Lupton, pale tone over rules).
