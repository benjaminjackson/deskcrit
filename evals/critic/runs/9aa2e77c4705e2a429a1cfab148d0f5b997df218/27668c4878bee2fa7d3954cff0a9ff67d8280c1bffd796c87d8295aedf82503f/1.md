**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three blocks of heavy brush script and slows right down. Each word has to be worked out, and the long flowing lines blur into a texture. It reads as a plain information page for people who ride a river ferry. It feels like a café chalkboard, not like something you check before catching a boat.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or posted notice that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long the crossing takes, and what to do with a bicycle. No claim is at stake. The reader needs facts, not persuading.

**Verdict.** It fails at the one thing it is for. Reading is the whole job, and every sentence is set in a decorative brush script that makes passengers decode the schedule letter by letter. Under the typeface, the copy also never gives a time a passenger could plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in a brush script.** All three paragraphs, about ten lines of running text, are in a heavy connected script. Joined, slanted, uneven letters make each word take longer to read. The parts you act on, "every forty minutes", "twelve minutes", and "one boat each hour", are the hardest to pick out, because the script gives numbers and words the same swashy texture. Scripts are for a few words of display, and paragraphs belong in a face made for reading (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1). The script also clashes with the neutral grotesque of the heading: a 1950s brush face under a plain modern sans makes the page look like two templates put together. That last point is my own judgment as a designer, not a cited rule. Fix: set the paragraphs in a plain text face. The heading's sans family at regular weight would do and would also join the two halves into one piece. If the hand-lettered feel matters to the brand, keep the script for a few display words at most, never for the schedule. *Defect* (convention plus consequence).

2. **The times a passenger acts on are vague, and one line can be read two ways.** "Between early morning and late evening", "before dusk", and "In winter" are the facts people plan around and later hold the operator to, and none of them is a time or a date. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" is ambiguous. "The quay" could mean the market quay only, as in the first paragraph, which would leave a passenger at the north landing unsure whether a later boat comes back for them. It could also mean either landing. Read it the wrong way and someone is stranded on the far bank at nightfall. The copy also sends people to "printed timetables… in the shelter", which admits the times live somewhere else. Where the piece already uses its own words, keep them and make them exact, for example by saying which landing "the quay" means. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times from each landing in normal service and in winter, and the months the winter timetable runs. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.** None found.

**What's working.**
- The structure is right for the job. There is one clear heading and three short paragraphs, one topic each (schedule, bicycles, winter), set flush left on a shared 120 px left edge with sensible gaps between them. Keep this when you change the face.
- The heading is clearly dominant by size and weight, so the reader knows at once what the page is. Keep it that strong.
- The plain dark-on-white palette keeps contrast high and adds nothing that competes with the information.
