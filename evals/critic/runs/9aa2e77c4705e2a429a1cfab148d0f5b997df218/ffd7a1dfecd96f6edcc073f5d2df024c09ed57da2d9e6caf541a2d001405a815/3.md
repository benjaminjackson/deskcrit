**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading at top left, "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three even paragraphs of dark gray text. It reads as a plain information page for people taking a river ferry. It feels calm and orderly but bookish: it reads like a letter, not a notice. Nothing stands out below the heading, so to find out when the boat leaves you have to read sentences.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for people about to take the ferry who want to know when it runs, how long the trip takes, and whether they can bring a bike. It gives plain facts and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The setting is clean and easy to read, but the page fails its main job. A passenger who wants to know "when is the next boat?" finds no times at all, only "early morning," "late evening," and "before dusk," hidden mid-sentence in one paragraph. The biggest problem is that the facts people need to act on are vague, and they're written as prose that can't be scanned.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable has no times, and the wording can be read more than one way. (Defect.)** The first paragraph says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening," and the third says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk." These are the lines a passenger plans around. "Early morning" could mean 5:00 or 8:00, and "before dusk" moves by an hour or more across the winter. Anyone who guesses late gets stranded on the wrong bank. "Leaves the quay" is also unclear, since "the market quay" is one end of the route: does the last boat leave from the quay only, or from both landings? "In winter" names no dates, so a reader in early November can't tell which timetable applies. The fix is to state the first and last departure from each landing as clock times, and to give the winter timetable's start and end dates. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times from each landing, in summer and in winter, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends.
2. **The facts are buried in three undivided paragraphs. (Convention: Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web.")** Times, bike rules and winter changes all sit in the same look of gray prose, with no subheads and no list. Someone checking the page on the landing has to read all three paragraphs to find the one fact they need. The frequency ("every forty minutes"), the trip length ("about twelve minutes") and the winter frequency ("one boat each hour") are written as words in the middle of sentences, so the eye can't catch them as numbers. The fix is to split the page under short headings that use words already there (timetable, bicycles, winter) and pull the frequency and trip length out where they can be scanned, ideally as a small table of times once item 1 provides them. Writing the numbers as digits ("every 40 minutes," "about 12 minutes") is an edit that adds no new content.

**Smaller things.**
- The right ~40% of the frame and the bottom third are empty. For a single reading column that's fine, but if a timetable is added it has room to sit beside the text instead of below it.
- The page says printed timetables and changes are posted "in the shelter at each landing," but not where to find them online. If a web version exists, linking to it from here is the obvious next step for this reader.

**What's working.**
- The text setting is solid. The body is a plain sans at a comfortable size, the lines look about 70 characters long, the leading is generous, the rag is even, and the dark text on white is high-contrast. Keep all of this when adding structure.
- The hierarchy is clear and unforced: one bold heading in the same family, with no second size competing with it. It's restrained and doesn't use template styling.
- The bicycle paragraph is good instructional copy. It says who does what and in what order ("Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"), which is the standard the timetable lines should meet.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where passengers read this at the landing.
