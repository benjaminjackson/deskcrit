**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading at top left, "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three paragraphs of dark gray prose and stays there. It reads as a plain information page from a local ferry service. It feels calm, clean and unbranded, a little like a draft. The right 45% of the frame and the bottom third are empty.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a desktop web page for people about to take the river ferry, who come to it to check when boats run and what the rules are. No claim is at stake: the page tells riders things and asks them to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** It's set well, but it fails at the one job a ferry notice has: a rider can't find out when the boats actually run. The times are vague prose ("early morning," "before dusk") buried in undivided paragraphs.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a rider acts on are vague or can be read two ways (defect).** In the first paragraph, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last departure, so a rider can't plan the one boat that matters to them. In the third paragraph, "In winter" names no months, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can be read two ways. "The quay" could mean the market quay, or whichever landing the rider is standing at, since the page names two landings. If a rider at the north landing reads it the wrong way, they may wait for a boat that has already gone, and in winter the boat could be the last one of the day. The page itself says the real answer is on the printed timetable in the shelter, so the page sends people somewhere else for its own key fact. Fix: state the actual departure times and the winter dates, and say which landing each last crossing leaves from. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times from each landing, in summer and in winter, and the dates when the winter timetable starts and ends.
2. **Three separate topics are run together as unlabeled prose (convention: Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** The paragraphs cover the timetable, bicycles and winter changes, but nothing marks that, so a cyclist or someone checking the last boat has to read all three to find their part. Fix: give each paragraph a short subhead in words already on the page, such as "Timetable," "Bicycles" and "Winter." Once you have the times from finding 1, set them as a small table under the timetable heading rather than in a sentence, so a rider can find a time at a glance.

**Smaller things.**
- The text column hugs the left edge of a 1280 px frame and nothing sits on the right, so the page looks left-heavy and unfinished at this width (taste; Arnheim on balance). Either center the column in the frame, or use the right side for the timetable table from finding 2.
- The last sentence says changes are listed "for the coming week" on noticeboards only. That's fine as a fact, but a rider reading online gets no way to check them from here. Say on the page where changes are posted, if there's such a place.

**What's working.**
- The reading setup is right. The body text looks about 19 px with roughly 1.5 line spacing, lines run about 65 to 70 characters, and the text is near-black on white, set flush left with a calm rag. Keep all of it when you add the subheads.
- The hierarchy is simple and clear: one bold heading above body text, differing in both size and weight, with no extra levels and nothing decorative. Subheads should be a third step between these two, set in the body size and bold, not a new size.
- The copy is plain and humane, for example "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat." Keep that voice and add the facts it's missing.
