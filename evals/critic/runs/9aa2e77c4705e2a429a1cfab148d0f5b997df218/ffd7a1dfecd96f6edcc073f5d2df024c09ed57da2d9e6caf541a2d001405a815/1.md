**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three even paragraphs of body text. It reads as a calm, plain info page from a small local ferry service: clean, quiet, trustworthy. Nothing else competes, and the right half and the bottom of the frame are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for people planning a crossing who want to know when the boat runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike. No claim is at stake: the page gives facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** The typography and layout do their job well. The page fails on the words. The facts a passenger acts on (when the first and last boats run, which months count as winter, and where the last boat leaves from) are vague or can be read two ways, and they're buried mid-sentence in running prose.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger plans around are not times.** In paragraph 1, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing. In paragraph 3, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" gives no clock time, and dusk moves by more than an hour across a winter. A passenger reading this can't tell whether a 4:30 pm crossing in December is safe, and the person who guesses wrong is left on the wrong bank. This is a **defect**: these are the lines people will hold the operator to, so vagueness here costs more than anywhere else on the page. The direction is to state the first and last departures as clock times. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times in each season, and the months the winter timetable covers.
2. **"The last crossing leaves the quay" can be read two ways.** Paragraph 1 names two ends, "the north landing" and "the market quay", but paragraph 3 names only "the quay". It could mean the last boat from the market quay, with the last boat from the north landing at another time. Or it could be loose wording for the last sailing of the day. Someone waiting at the north landing who takes the second reading may miss the boat. This is a **defect**. The fix is an editorial one: name the landing, using names already on the page ("leaves the market quay", or say the last boat from each side). Fact needed from the maker: which landing the last winter crossing leaves from, and the last time from the other side.
3. **The key facts are buried in prose, so nobody can scan for them.** "Forty minutes", "twelve minutes", "no extra charge", and "one boat each hour" each sit in the middle of a sentence, and nothing under the heading tells a returning reader where the bike rules or the winter changes are. Each paragraph covers one topic, so the structure is already there. It just isn't shown. This is **taste**, argued on how people use the page: they come back to check one fact, not to read it through. The direction is to put a short heading over each paragraph, using words already in it (the crossing, bicycles, winter), or to pull the frequency and duration into a short lead line. The copy doesn't need rewriting.

**Smaller things.** None. Everything on the checklist passed.

**What's working.**
- The type is well set. The body text is a plain sans at a comfortable size, with lines of about 65 to 75 characters and generous line spacing, so it reads easily. Keep that measure if you add headings.
- The hierarchy is clean: one bold heading in the same family and color as the text, standing clearly above it by size and weight. It has two levels and no muddle.
- The palette is restrained: near-black on white with no ornament. That suits a public-service notice and keeps the attention on the information.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone (likely fine for a single column), and whether the empty right side is waiting for a map or picture that didn't load. If something should be there, check it in a live browser.
