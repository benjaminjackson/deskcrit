**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then drops into three even paragraphs of identical weight. It reads as a plain information page for people using a river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, like a parish notice. But once past the headline there is nothing to scan for: no time, no word or line stands out, so you have to read every sentence to find the one fact you came for.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for someone planning a crossing who wants to know when the boat runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bicycle. It states plain facts, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and readable. As a timetable page it falls short: the facts a passenger acts on are given as vague prose, and the three topics look the same, so a reader can't jump to the one they need.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger relies on can't be acted on (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and, in winter, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines people use to decide whether they'll make the boat. "Early morning" can mean 6:00 to one reader and 8:00 to another. "Before dusk" moves by an hour across the season and gives no departure time at all. If someone reads them the wrong way, they're left on the quay after the last boat. "In winter" has the same problem, because the reader can't tell whether this week counts. The first paragraph also says the ferry "runs from the north landing to the market quay" every forty minutes but never says whether that covers both directions or only one. The fix is to state the first and last departures from each landing and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, then let the prose explain. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times from each landing in summer and in winter, whether the forty-minute and hourly intervals apply in both directions, and the dates the winter timetable applies.
2. **Three separate topics are set as one undifferentiated run of text (convention: Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** The paragraphs cover the regular service, bicycles, and the winter timetable, but nothing on the page marks that. A cyclist has to read the service paragraph to learn that the second one is theirs. A winter visitor has to get to the third paragraph to find out the first one doesn't apply to them. The paragraphs are short, which helps, but each still hides its topic. The fix is to give each paragraph a short subhead built from its own topic (service, bicycles, winter). Then pull the times out of the prose into a small list or table, so the numbers sit where the eye lands first.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing. The empty right side and bottom read as a normal left-aligned reading column on a desktop browser, not as a balance problem.

**What's working.**
- The body measure is about 65–72 characters at roughly 19 px, with generous leading and a soft rag. That's a comfortable reading line, so keep this column width when you add structure.
- The headline steps up clearly from the body through both size and weight, with only two sizes on the page and no stray ones. Any subheads you add should sit between these two in weight, not add a third size near either.
- The text is plain and concrete ("lock them to the rail along the side of the deck"), written the way a crew member would say it. Keep that voice when you pull the facts out into a list.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where most people would check this page at the landing.
