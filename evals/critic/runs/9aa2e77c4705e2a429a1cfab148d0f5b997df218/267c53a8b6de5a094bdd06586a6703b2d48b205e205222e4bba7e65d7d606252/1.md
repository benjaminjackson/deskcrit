**First read.** The eye lands on "Landing stage rebuild" and then jumps to the boxed "Get timetable changes" card on the right, pulled there by the blue Sign up button. In between, it stops on the flat drawing, where a blue rounded bar the same color as the button looks like a second control. It reads as a ferry operator's news page with an alert sign-up next to it. It feels calm, plain and public-service, and mostly well built.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a page on a ferry operator's site. It tells passengers about the rebuilt landing stage and asks regular riders to sign up for notes when the timetable changes. To give their name, a rider has to see what they will get and how it will reach them. They see the "what" (one short note per change, and they can stop any time) but not the "how", because nothing on the page says where the note is sent.

**Verdict.** The article half works. The sign-up, which is the only thing the page asks anyone to do, can't do its job: it asks for a name and a landing, never asks for an email or phone number, and never names a channel. Fix that first. The figure that stands in for the news comes next.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sign-up form has no way to reach the person who signs up.** Fixing this changes the most. The card on the right promises "one short note when the timetable changes", but its only fields are "Your name" and "Home landing". There is no email or phone field, and the copy never says whether the note comes by email, text or app. A careful rider will stop and wonder where it goes. A less careful one will press Sign up and never hear anything. In both cases the page's one call to action fails. This is a defect (common sense; no catalog rule covers it). The fix is to add the contact field the service actually uses, and to name the channel in the button or the helper line so the ask says exactly what happens. Fact needed from the maker: the channel the timetable notes are sent through (email, SMS or other).
2. **The figure doesn't show what the text says, and part of it looks like a button.** Figure 1, under the first paragraph, is three flat blocks on a pale blue band. The caption calls it "the new north landing, seen from the water at low tide", but nothing in it shows a ramp, a tide, the covered waiting area or the deck meeting the boat. A reader gets nothing from the picture that the text hasn't already said, so it reads as a placeholder. Worse, the blue rounded bar at the right of the figure has the same hue and corner radius as the Sign up button, so for a moment it reads as a control (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*: things that look alike are read as belonging together). This is a defect. The fix is a picture (photo or drawing) of the ramp meeting the deck. That is the page's real news. Whatever goes there must not use the action blue.
3. **The news that matters most is buried under a label.** "Landing stage rebuild" is a noun-stack label that tells riders a project happened, not what it means for them. The line that does speak to them, "The ramps now meet the deck at every tide, so prams and wheelchairs roll straight on", sits last, under the figure, where many readers won't get to it. This is taste, but for anyone with a pram, a wheelchair or a bad knee, that sentence is the reason to read the page. Move it up so it leads: into or right under the headline, and ahead of the benches and the kiosk. Use only the words already on the page.

**Smaller things.**
- The small text is set in a light weight: the field labels "Your name" and "Home landing", the helper line under Sign up, the figure caption and the footer. At this size the thin strokes look frail and grayish. Use the regular weight for everything under about 14 px (Bringhurst, section 6.2.3).
- "Home landing" is a free-text field, so riders will type spellings that don't match a real stop. Use a picker listing the landings (Nielsen, heuristic 5: error prevention).
- Neither field shows whether it is required. If both are required, say so once; if not, put "optional" after the optional one (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- The figure mocks a real place, the north landing, and no real reference was given, so it could not be checked against the real thing.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean. There's one bold headline, a clearly smaller card heading, readable body text at a comfortable line length, and a separate caption size. Keep that scale when you rework the copy.
- The helper line, "We send one short note when the timetable changes. You can stop the notes at any time.", answers the two objections people have before signing up (how often, and how to leave) in plain words. Keep it, and add the channel.
- The layout is calm and has room to breathe: content on the left, action in a card on the right, a shared top line and generous margins. It suits a public-service page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The focus rings and error states on the two fields and the Sign up button, and what happens after someone presses Sign up.
