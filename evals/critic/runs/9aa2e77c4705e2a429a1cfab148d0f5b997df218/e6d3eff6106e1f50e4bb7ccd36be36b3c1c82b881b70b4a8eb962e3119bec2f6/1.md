**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline, "The allotment at the end of the bus line", then drops into the first paragraph, which reads easily. In a few seconds it's clear this is a long-form feature on an independent local magazine site. It feels calm, plain, and serious. Then the eye trips on the second headline line, where "line" sits alone, and nothing ever draws it lower: there's no picture anywhere, even though the byline credits the photographs.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is an article page on a small community or city magazine, for readers who come to read a feature to the end on a desktop browser. No claim is at stake. This is reporting, and the reader is asked to read, not to believe a pitch.

**Verdict.** The page mostly does its job. The column is quiet and readable, and the opening paragraph is strong. What lets it down is that a feature credited to "Photographs by the residents" shows no photograph, so the most personal part of the piece is missing. After that, the headline break and a slightly long line are fixable craft issues.

**What would move it most**
1. **The credited photographs aren't there.** The byline under the headline says "Photographs by the residents", but there's no image anywhere on the screen. The page goes from byline to three paragraphs to empty white. A reader is promised the residents' own pictures and gets a wall of text. For a story about one plot, its beds and its people, a picture is what would make a reader stay and believe the place is real. If the images load further down or failed to load in this capture, this is fine as long as a lead image appears near the top in the live page. If the piece really has no photographs in the opening view, put a lead image between the byline and the first paragraph (or above the headline), so the credit points at something on arrival. *Defect* (common sense; no approved source covers it). It may also be a capture problem: see "Couldn't judge from a still".
2. **The headline breaks with "line" alone on line 2.** The second line of the H1 is the single word "line", so a short phrase is split in the middle. It reads as "The allotment at the end of the bus" followed by an afterthought, and the heading looks lopsided right where the eye first lands (Bringhurst, *Elements*, §2.4.2, on a short word alone on a last line). The fix is to balance the break so "bus line" stays together, e.g. "The allotment at the end / of the bus line". Use a balanced text wrap or a non-breaking space between "bus" and "line", or narrow the headline's measure a little. *Convention*.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run long: the first paragraph's lines look like about 80–85 characters, above the 45–75 range (Bringhurst, *Elements*, §2.1.2). Narrow the text column a little, or bump the body size, to land nearer 66 characters. The headline can keep the wider measure.
- The nav ("Features · Food · Places · Subscribe") doesn't mark where the reader is, though the kicker says "Feature". Mark "Features" with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline, so the reader can see their place on the site (Nielsen Norman Group, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").

**What's working.**
- The reading column is disciplined. The kicker, headline, byline, and body all share one left edge, the body leading is generous without drifting apart, and paragraphs are marked by space alone. Keep that column and that single edge when you add images.
- The hierarchy is clear and restrained. There are about four levels (headline, body, byline/nav, kicker), with big steps between them, and the only color is the small green "Feature" label. The headline wins without any help.
- The opening paragraph does the work a standfirst would. It moves from a concrete before-and-after ("a car park nobody used" to "forty households") to the queue at the water tap, and it lands the story in four sentences. Don't bury it under a separate dek.
