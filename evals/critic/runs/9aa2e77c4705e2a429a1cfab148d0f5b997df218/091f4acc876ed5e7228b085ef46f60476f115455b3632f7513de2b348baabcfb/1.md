**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, then drops through the italic standfirst into the body. It reads at once as the opening of a long-form magazine feature, about a community garden built on an old car park. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, but a little anonymous. The headline ends on "line" sitting alone on its own line, and you notice that before you start reading.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the first screen of an online feature, and its job is to pull a general reader into a long piece. No claim is at stake, because it's a story, not a pitch.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The order of reading is clear, the copy is specific and good, and the page stays out of the way. What holds it back is reading comfort: the body lines are too long for a piece meant to be read end to end, and a one-word runt weakens the headline.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set too wide.** The two body paragraphs run about 85 characters a line. "It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to" is 86. Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 as comfortable and about 66 as ideal (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). On a short page that costs little. On a feature the reader has to get back to the start of the next line thousands of times, so wide lines mean lost places and a tiring read, and that is when readers give up partway. Narrow the text column to bring body lines to roughly 65 characters, or raise the body size a little within the same column. You can leave the headline wider than the body if you want it to stretch. *Convention, with a real cost here.*
2. **The headline ends on a one-word runt.** "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" splits the phrase "bus line" and leaves the second line nearly empty. The headline is the page's one strong shape, and it reads lopsided, with "line" looking stranded. Bringhurst warns against a short word alone on a last line (2.4.2). Breaking headlines at phrase boundaries is a common practice, not a rule from a source. Rebalance the break so the lines are closer in length, for example "The allotment at the end / of the bus line". You can do that with balanced headline wrapping, a slightly narrower headline box, or a manual break. *Convention.*
3. **The standfirst isn't a level of its own.** The four-line intro looks the same size as the body, and only italic and a slightly grayer tone set it apart. So it reads as a slanted first paragraph rather than the deck that sells the story. Four lines of sans italic is also slower to read than the upright text below it. Give it its own step in size, about one clear step between body and headline, and consider setting it upright. Then size does the work and the italic isn't needed. *Taste, argued from hierarchy: Müller-Brockmann's distinct levels.*

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Subscribe" is styled exactly like "Features · Food · Places". The site's one real action reads as just another section. Give it a quieter but distinct treatment, such as a weight change or an outline. That's enough to set it apart without competing with the headline.
- Nav links: about 14 px text separated only by middots. The tap targets look smaller than 24 × 24 px (WCAG 2.5.8). Measure them in the browser and add padding if they fall short.
- The page shows a mock of nothing, so there's nothing to check there. But the byline credits "Photographs by the residents" and no photograph appears on this screen. If the photos come later, that's fine. If the first one can sit above the fold, it would also give the page a picture that belongs to this story rather than to any blog.

**What's working.**
- **The copy.** The standfirst is concrete and human: "the queue for the shared water tap is the best place in the district to hear the news". The opening line, "It started with a letter.", pulls you in. Keep that voice.
- **The restraint.** One centered text column, one small green "Feature" kicker as the only color, and plenty of margin. Nothing competes with the words, which is right for long-form reading.
- **The headline's dominance.** It's clearly the largest and heaviest thing on the page, so the eye knows where to start without help.
