**First read.** The eye lands on "Harbour Lights festival", a bold serif title at the top left. Then it falls into three gray walls of text of equal weight and finds nothing to stop on: no date, no time, no place stands out. It reads as a short local newspaper article, calm and literate. The lower 40% of the frame is blank, which makes the page look unfinished rather than airy.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling local residents and families when and where the festival is, what's on, and that volunteers are needed. No claim is at stake. The page asks only that readers turn up or help, and it backs that with plain facts (dates, places, times).

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The text is pleasant to read, but every fact a visitor needs is buried mid-sentence in unbroken prose, so a reader who skims leaves without the when, where, or how.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts are buried in prose (defect).** "First weekend of September", "old market square", "Saturday night… at dusk", "Sunday afternoon", "by one o'clock", "at four", and "ten each morning" all sit inside running sentences across the three columns. Someone scanning for "when do I need to be there?" has to read every word, and most won't (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Two things are missing that a visitor plans around: the year and the exact dates. "First weekend of September" leaves the reader to work out which days. Direction: pull the when and where out into a short, scannable block near the title, and split each event's times and places out of its paragraph (a list or a short facts line per event). Fact needed from the maker: the exact dates (day, month, and year) of the festival weekend.
2. **The three columns have no headings, so the page has only two levels (defect).** Below the title, everything is the same size and weight. The lantern walk, the pie contest, and the general overview look interchangeable, and a reader can't jump to the event they care about. The page needs a third level, short subheads that name each column's topic (Müller-Brockmann; Lupton and Phillips on distinct levels of hierarchy; WCAG 2.4.6, which asks headings to name what their section holds). For example: "Lantern walk" and "Pie contest", which are words already on the page.
3. **The volunteer ask is hidden, with no way to act on it (defect).** "Volunteers are still needed…" is the fifth sentence of the first column, and nothing says whom to contact or how. This is the one place the page asks something of the reader, and it carries the least weight. The entry rules for the pie contest ("baked at home and brought to the hall by one o'clock") have the same problem: they are a deadline a reader will be held to, and they're buried. Direction: set the volunteer ask apart with a heading of its own and give it a single, specific next step. Fact needed from the maker: how volunteers sign up (contact or link).

**Smaller things.**
- The bottom 40% of the frame is empty, and all the weight sits in the top band. The space reads as leftover, not chosen (Ruder; Arnheim on balance). Fixing items 1 to 3 will likely fill it. Otherwise, size the frame to the content.
- The mood is plain black serif on white for a lantern-and-boats evening festival. Nothing in the color or imagery suggests light, water, or evening (taste; Itten on color matching the message).
- "The steps" in the pie-contest column doesn't say which steps (the church's? the hall's?), and a winner waiting at four needs to know. Restore the place the line already implies.

**What's working.**
- The serif text face, with generous line spacing and a column width of roughly 40 to 48 characters, is comfortable to read and right for a multi-column page (Bringhurst gives 40 to 50 for several columns). Keep it.
- The thin vertical rules and the consistent gutters give the three columns a clean, even grid with a shared top line.
- The copy has a warm local voice ("the pie that makes the judges laugh", "guard the cake tent against hungry seagulls"). It is specific to this festival and couldn't sit on anyone's page, so keep it when you restructure.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the three columns reflow on a phone.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 6 yes, 2 no (each heading names what its section holds; colors carry the mood)
What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (distinct levels below the first element)
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 11 yes, 2 no (empty space gathered; weight balanced)
Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (long text broken into chunks)
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: skipped, none
Charts and data: skipped, none
