**First read.** The eye lands on "Walking routes," then moves across three identical cards from left to right, reading each bold name and then hitting three identical blue buttons. Then it falls into the empty lower half of every card. That empty area is the strongest impression on the page: it looks as if a map or photo failed to load. Overall the page reads as clean and calm, but unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page where someone planning a walk picks one of three local routes and clicks through to its details. Nothing here needs the reader's belief: the cards state plain facts about each route.

**Verdict.** The page is tidy and readable, but it doesn't yet help anyone choose. The main problem is the empty bottom third of every card, which makes the page look broken. Close behind is that the facts a walker compares are buried in sentences that don't line up from card to card.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cards are about 440 px tall, but their content stops at the button around 395 px down the page. That leaves roughly 200 px of empty white inside each border (defect, common sense).** A boxed empty area reads as "something should be here." The viewer assumes an image or map failed to load and stops trusting that the page works. The space isn't framing anything; it's left over. Ruder's point (*Typography: A Manual of Design*, "Contrasts") is that empty space should be gathered and shaped on purpose. Here it's trapped inside three boxes and again below the row. To fix it, either let the cards end about one padding below the button, or fill the area with what the empty slot seems to promise, such as a route map or photo, if that's planned. Either way, the space should stop looking like a gap.
2. **The facts a walker uses to choose aren't in the same place on each card (taste/common sense).** Each blurb mentions distance, but as a spelled-out word in the middle of a sentence. After that, each card lists different things: climbs and a cafe, shade and benches, paving and bicycles. To compare effort, the reader has to read all three sentences and rebuild a table in their head. Cliff path mentions steepness, but the other two never say they're flat or easy in the same terms. "Flat" appears only on Woodland loop. One fix is to pull the distance out as a figure in the same spot on every card, "6 km", "4 km", "10 km", using the unit the copy already gives. Then let the sentence carry the character of the route. If you want readers to compare difficulty or surface, every card has to state it the same way. Fact needed from the maker: the terrain and surface of each route, stated the same way on every card.

**Smaller things.**
- "end." sits alone on the last line of the Cliff path blurb (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Adjust the measure or the wording so the line doesn't end in a single short word.
- All three buttons say "View route." Visually that's fine because each card gives the context, but check that screen-reader users hear which route each button opens (for example, the button's accessible name should include the route).

**What's working.**
- The type is in clear steps. The page heading, card titles, gray body text, and white-on-blue buttons each have a distinct size or weight, so the hierarchy reads instantly. Keep that scale.
- Everything sits on one grid. The heading's left edge lines up with the first card, the gaps between cards are even, and the padding inside the cards is consistent. The page feels orderly without trying.
- The card borders are light and the palette is limited to gray plus one blue, so the only color on the page marks the action.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty lower part of each card is meant to hold an image or map that didn't load in this capture. Check in a live browser. If an image is supposed to be there, item 1 becomes a loading or placement problem, not a spacing one.
- Hover and focus states of the buttons, and how the three columns reflow on a narrow screen.
