**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then drops straight into one column of black text and reads down it. It reads as a plain public info page for a small river ferry. It feels calm, clear and a little bare: the text sits in the left 45% of the screen and the right side is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how to board and how to pay, read before or during a trip. No claim is at stake: it states plain facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read, but it doesn't do its main job. A passenger can't tell from it when a boat leaves. The section breaks also get less space than they need, so the page reads as one long run of paragraphs.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable gives no times** (defect). The opening paragraph says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening." That's the fact people come for, and it can't be used. Without a first departure, "every forty minutes" doesn't say whether a boat leaves at 9:00 or 9:20. "Late evening" could mean 9 pm or 11 pm, and someone who guesses wrong misses the last boat. That is the one mistake this page has to prevent. "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes" works because it has a number. The schedule needs the same. The fix is to put the actual first and last departures in that first sentence, or in a short timetable directly under it, so the opening lines answer "when can I go?" Fact needed from the maker: the time of the first and last departures from each landing.
2. **Sections don't separate from each other** (convention). The space above "Boarding," "Tickets" and "Winter service" is about the same as the space between two paragraphs inside a section: roughly 52 px from baseline to baseline in both cases. Each heading sits only a little closer to its own text than to the text before it. The bold weight is the only thing marking a new section, so when you scan, the page looks like one continuous list instead of four topics. The principle is proximity: the gap between groups must be clearly larger than the gap inside a group (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II). Bringhurst §2.2.2 also sets subheads with more space above than below. To fix it, roughly double the space above each heading and keep the tight gap below it, so each heading plus its paragraphs reads as one block.

**Smaller things.**
- Under "Tickets," the paragraph ends with "kiosk." alone on its last line, a runt (Bringhurst §2.4.2). Rebreak the line or adjust the measure slightly so at least two words carry over.
- "A season pass covers any number of crossings for a month" can mean a calendar month or 30 days from purchase. People pay based on this line, so the wrong reading costs them days of travel. Fact needed from the maker: whether the season pass runs for a calendar month or for a set number of days from purchase.
- The page never mentions fares, though "Tickets" is a section. If prices belong on this page, they're missing. Fact needed from the maker: the price of a single, a return and a season pass, if they belong on this page.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: the title, the section headings and the body text are three clearly separate sizes, and both heading levels are also bold. You never have to wonder what level a line is. Keep this.
- The column is a comfortable length, about 60 to 65 characters per line, set flush left in a plain sans with relaxed line spacing. It's easy to read and needs no changes.
- The bulleted list under "Boarding" is the right move: three short, separate instructions pulled out of the prose, each one an action. Do the same for the timetable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The capture ends mid-sentence in "Winter service." That's the screen edge, not the design. Check the rest of the page, especially whether the winter last-boat time is stated, in a live browser.
- How the column reflows on a phone, where most passengers will read this at the landing.
