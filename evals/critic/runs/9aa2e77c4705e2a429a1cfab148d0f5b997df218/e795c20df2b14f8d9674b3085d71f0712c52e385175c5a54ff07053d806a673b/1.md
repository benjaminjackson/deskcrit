**First read.** The eye goes straight to the heatmap's dark band: weekday late mornings, with the darkest cells on Monday and Friday. Then it moves right to the three big numbers. It reads as an internal support-ops report for whoever plans staffing, and it feels calm, quiet and trustworthy. The one snag comes when you check the chart against the "Busiest hour" figure: they don't seem to agree.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a report page for a support lead deciding when to put agents on shift, looked at by people who already trust their own ticket log. It reports measured facts, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, well-made report page. The one thing in the way is that its single stated finding, "Busiest hour, weekdays 10:00", looks like it contradicts the chart beside it. On a page that exists to answer "when?", that undercuts everything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **The busiest-hour figure and the chart disagree, and the hour axis makes it hard to settle.** The hour labels sit at the left edge of their cells: 00:00 over the first column, 09:00 over the cell starting at about x 430, 12:00 over the one at about x 544. That puts the darkest cells (Monday and Friday, the "30+" color) in the 11:00 column, the third cell after 09:00. The 10:00 column is one step lighter on every weekday. A lead reading "Busiest hour, weekdays 10:00" and then looking at the chart will either distrust the number or staff the wrong hour. Labeling only every third hour makes it worse: to name any one peak column you have to count cells, which is how both the reader and possibly the maker got here. This is a **defect**. Fix the data first: either the stat or the column mapping is wrong. Then make single hours readable at the peak, for example by labeling every hour, or every other hour, in the working-day range (Tufte, *Visual Display of Quantitative Information*: the reader should never have to guess what a mark measures). Fact needed from the maker: which hour actually has the most new tickets on weekdays, 10:00 or 11:00.

**Smaller things.**
- The title "When new tickets arrive" names the topic but not the answer. A reader has to dig the finding out of the chart or the side column. Once the peak hour is settled, consider putting it in the subtitle or annotating the peak column. This is taste: on a staffing report the answer is the point.
- Period mismatch: the chart covers "1 to 28 September" but the side stat says "New tickets, September". A reader can't tell whether 3,412 includes the 29th and 30th. Make the two periods match, or name the period on the stat.
- The two lightest ramp steps (0–4, 5–9) are hard to tell from each other and from the page ground, and so are the middle steps (15–19, 20–24). That is fine for spotting the peak but weak for reading quiet early-morning or weekend hours. Widen the lightness gaps between those steps. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- The heatmap is the right form for day × hour. It uses one blue lightness ramp, so darker reads as more without effort, it works without color vision, and the grid is light enough that the data carries the page.
- Every headline number in the side column comes with a comparison ("Up 4% on August", "Same as August", "Down 6 min on August"), in a clear three-level stack: label, figure, delta. Keep that pattern.
- The source line says exactly what an hour means: local time, when the ticket was opened rather than answered. That heads off the most likely misreading.
