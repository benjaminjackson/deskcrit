**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "Travel lighter with the Wayfarer pack", drops to the gray line below it, then stops on the black "Shop the pack" button. It reads as the hero of a store page for a carry-on backpack. It feels clean and competent, and then oddly empty: the right half and the bottom third are bare white, and you never see the bag.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a landing page for a new travel backpack, meant to make frequent flyers want to buy it or compare sizes. To act, that reader has to see the bag and believe it really fits under the seat on their airline. They see neither: there's no picture, and "fits under an airline seat" comes with no dimensions.

**Verdict.** The type is well set, but the hero doesn't do its job. It sells a physical object without showing it, so it asks for a purchase on words alone. The missing product picture is the one thing in the way, and fixing it would also fix the empty, lopsided layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no bag on a page that sells a bag (defect).** The whole right side of the frame, from about x 700 to 1280, is empty, and nowhere on the page is the Wayfarer shown. A backpack buyer judges with their eyes first: size, shape, how it opens, whether it looks like them. Here they get three claims and a "Shop" button, so the decision gets put off until a click they have no reason to make yet. The subhead's best claim, "opens flat like a suitcase", is exactly what a picture proves in a second and words only assert. The headline and picture should make one idea together. Right now the headline is doing both jobs and can't. If an image was meant to fill this space and didn't load, check it in a live browser. Fix: put the bag in the open right side, and show it doing the thing the copy claims, such as open flat or under a seat, so the picture proves the line instead of repeating it. An empty or generic hero visual is also a stock template tell (unslop): a text-only hero with no product reads like anyone's landing page.
2. **The layout tips left and the white space reads as missing, not chosen (convention).** Everything sits in a block in the upper left, from about y 120 to 495, and nothing on the right or below balances it. By Arnheim's account of visual weight, a heavy side needs a counterweight. Here the dark headline and button have none, so the page looks unfinished rather than calm. Ruder's gathered white space works because it's set against something dense, and these empty areas aren't set against anything. Fix: the product image from finding 1 is the counterweight. Once it's in, the remaining space will read as deliberate.
3. **"Fits under an airline seat" is a promise the reader can't check (defect).** This is the line a carry-on buyer will hold the brand to at the gate. As written, it can mean "fits under any airline's seat" or "fits under some seats", and the reader who assumes the first and is wrong pays for it at boarding. The page asks them to accept the fit on trust and gives no dimensions or named size standard to judge it against their own airline's rule. That comparison is what would let them see the value for themselves. "Compare sizes" hints that the answer is somewhere, but behind a click. Fix: state the bag's measurements next to the claim, or state the size limit it meets. Fact needed from the maker: the Wayfarer's external dimensions, or the cabin-bag size standard it is built to meet.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline looks like an extra-bold weight (around 800). That's a stock template move (unslop), and the size alone already makes it dominant. Try a medium or semibold weight and let the size do the work.
- "Travel lighter" is a line any bag brand could run. The specific case is in the subhead (22 litre, under a kilo), so consider pulling "under a kilo" up into the headline so it says something only this pack says.
- There's no logo, navigation, or cart, which a store hero normally has. If this is a crop of the hero component, ignore this. If it's the whole page, the reader can't tell whose store this is.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean. There's one dominant headline, a clearly secondary gray subhead, and one filled primary button with an underlined text link as the lesser action. Each level is obvious at a glance, and everything hangs from one shared left edge at about x 120. Keep that structure when you add the image.
- The subhead does real work. Three concrete, checkable claims (22 litre, under-seat fit, under a kilo) in one sentence of about 70 characters per line, set comfortably. It's the most persuasive thing on the page.
- The palette is restrained: near-black, gray, and a single blue for the "New for spring" label. It will leave room for a product photo to carry the color.
