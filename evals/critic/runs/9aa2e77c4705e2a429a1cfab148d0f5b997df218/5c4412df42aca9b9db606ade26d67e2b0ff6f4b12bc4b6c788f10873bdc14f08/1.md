**First read.** The eye goes to "Pricing", then drops to the three big prices, $9, $29, $79, and reads them left to right as a ladder. It's a pricing page for a calendar or booking tool (Tidewell), aimed at small businesses and teams. It feels clean, calm and competent, but anonymous: it could be any SaaS pricing page. The read stops at the bottom of the feature lists, and nothing tells you what to do next.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public pricing page that should get a visitor, probably someone booking meetings for themselves or a small team, to pick a plan and sign up. To act, that reader has to see which plan fits them, what each one costs in practice (per what, and whether it includes the cheaper plan's features), and how to start on that plan. The page shows the first only partly and the other two not at all.

**Verdict.** The page doesn't do its job. It shows three prices and then gives no way to choose one. With no button on any card, no recommended plan, and unclear terms, the reader can compare but can't act.

**What would move it most**
1. **No card has an action.** Each of the three cards ends at its feature list. A reader who has decided on Team has to go back up to "Start free" in the nav. That button doesn't name a plan, and no free plan or trial appears anywhere on the page, so they can't tell whether clicking it starts Team, a trial, or something else. The decision point and the action are a full screen-width apart, and the link between them is broken. *Defect.* Fix direction: give each card its own action, labeled for that plan, and make the nav button's label say what it starts. That label is a fact only the maker has. Fact needed from the maker: whether "Start free" means a free plan, a free trial (and how long), or something else.
2. **The three cards are equal, so nothing helps the reader choose.** Same size, same border, same weight, same type. None stands out as the plan most readers should take, so the reader has to work out the fit alone from three-line lists (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance through differences). *Convention.* Fix direction: make one plan clearly first, the one the business wants most people to choose, using emphasis (a stronger border or fill, or a label), and set the other two back.
3. **The terms are ambiguous on exactly the lines the reader commits to.** "$29 / month" can be read as per account or per user. Since Team is "Up to 10 calendars", a reader who assumes per-seat pricing estimates up to $290 and may leave, and one who assumes flat pricing may feel misled at checkout. The feature lists also don't say whether Team includes Solo's "Booking page" and "Email reminders", or whether Business includes "Shared availability" and "Reports". As written, Business looks like it lacks a booking page. *Defect* (Nielsen 6, recognition rather than recall: the reader shouldn't have to infer what's missing). Fix direction: say what the price is per, and show that each higher plan includes the one below it, for example by opening each list with a line that points back to the cheaper plan. Fact needed from the maker: whether prices are per account or per user, and whether each higher plan includes all features of the plan below it.
4. **The copy could be on anyone's page.** "Simple plans for teams of every size" doesn't fit the Solo plan, which is for one person, and says nothing about what Tidewell does. The page never says what a "calendar" is in Tidewell's terms, even though it's the unit that sets the price. The reader can't tell why this tool rather than any other. The second sentence, "Change or cancel whenever you like", is good and carries real reassurance, but it's set as an afterthought. *Taste.* Fix direction: lead the subhead with the reassurance that's already there and cut the generic half. Each plan name could say who the plan is for, using the reader's own words.
5. **Stock layout.** A centered heading, a centered subhead, and three identical bordered cards in a row are the most common pricing template there is. Readers have seen it many times, so Tidewell looks like a theme rather than a product (unslop, "Hero stacked on one centered axis"). *Convention.* Fixing 1 and 2 already breaks most of the sameness. Beyond that, put something that belongs to this product where the generic subhead is.

**Smaller things.**
- Subhead: "you like." sits alone on the second line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrow or widen the measure so the two lines balance, or shorten the line as in finding 4.
- The large empty band below the cards (about the bottom 230 px) has nothing after it. If the page really ends there, it's the natural place to answer pricing questions such as billing period, payment, and what counts as a calendar. If it's just where the capture ended, ignore this.

**What's working.**
- Price type: the large bold figure with a small regular "/ month" makes the three prices easy to compare at a glance. Keep that size and weight contrast when you add emphasis to one card.
- Restrained color: the brand blue appears only on the wordmark, the active nav tab, and the one button, so whatever you make blue next (a per-card action, a recommended plan) will stand out immediately.
- A clear "you are here": the underlined Pricing tab shows the current page without fuss.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**

- Hover and focus states of the nav and "Start free" button.
