**First read.** The bold serif heading "Notes for the summer fete" catches the eye first. The eye then skips the sentences and jumps between the six italic, underlined phrases: *arrive before ten*, *labelled with their ingredients*, *three o'clock sharp*, *collected by four*, *on the field only*. They look like links, so for a moment this reads as a page of links rather than a set of instructions. It is a short web note for a village summer fete. It feels calm, plain, and friendly.

**What it's for.** My assumption: it tells stallholders and helpers the times and rules they must follow on the day of the fete, read once ahead of time and maybe checked again that morning. These are plain instructions, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read. It falls short in three ways. Every key phrase gets a double treatment that makes it look like a link. One of the two deadlines can be read two ways. The page never says which day the fete is. Time-bound instructions are the whole job here, so a reader could act on the wrong day or the wrong rule.

**What would move it most**
1. **The prize deadline can be read two ways (defect).** In the second paragraph, "all tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box" does not say who collects what. One reading: winners must claim their prizes by four. The other: whoever sold raffle tickets must hand them in by four. If a winner takes the second reading, they leave at half past three and lose their prize. If a helper takes the first, they never hand the stubs in. Deadlines are what readers act on and later hold you to, so the line needs a subject and a clear object. Keep the words already there and add who does it. Fact needed from the maker: who must collect what by four (winners claiming prizes, or helpers gathering tickets).
2. **The emphasis is doubled on every key phrase, so it looks like links and stops working as emphasis (convention + defect).** Each of the six phrases is both italic and underlined. Underline is how the web marks a link, and here it is the only signal readers know means "click". So the page looks like six links, and a reader who taps one gets nothing. Stacking two signals also breaks the rule that emphasis inside text changes one thing (Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style* 3.5.1; Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 94: "usually requires only one signal"). And since every rule in the note is marked, none stands out over the others. The eye hops from phrase to phrase instead of reading the sentences in order, which is the "every element highlighted" template tell (Sokolovsky, unslop). Fix: drop the underline and keep italic. Better still, mark only the one or two deadlines that cost something if missed, such as the arrival time and the prize deadline, and set the rest plain.
3. **The page never says which day the fete is (defect).** "Arrive before ten" and "three o'clock sharp" are times with no date anywhere on the page. A note sent out ahead of an event is often the one place people check the date, and a reader who has to ask is a reader who might guess wrong. The heading or a first line is the natural place for it. Fact needed from the maker: the date of the fete.
4. **Four separate rules are run together as prose (convention).** The arrival time, the cake labels, the raffle times, and the parking rule are tucked mid-sentence across two paragraphs. A reader checking the night before has to read every sentence to find the one rule they need. Notes that people skim and come back to work better as a short list, one rule per line (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). A list would also make the emphasis from finding 2 unnecessary. Fact needed from the maker: none. This is reordering words already on the page.

**Smaller things.**
- "only." sits alone on the last line of the second paragraph, a short word as its own last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrap it, or let the list in finding 4 remove the problem.
- "Arrive before ten" doesn't say who must arrive: stallholders, helpers, or everyone. Visitors who read it may think they can't come in before ten. Name who it is for in the heading or in that line, using words already on the page, such as "stall".

**What's working.**
- The type is simple and right for the job. A bold serif heading over a regular serif body in one family, with a clear step in size and weight, so it's obvious where to start. The body is a comfortable size with generous line spacing, and the lines run about 74 characters, at the upper edge of a good measure.
- The color is restrained: dark gray text on white with no other color, so nothing competes with the words.
- The left margin and the text block share one clean left edge, and the empty space sits together on the right and at the bottom, not scattered.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the underlined phrases really are links. If they are, the italic on them is still a second signal to remove, and their destinations need checking.
