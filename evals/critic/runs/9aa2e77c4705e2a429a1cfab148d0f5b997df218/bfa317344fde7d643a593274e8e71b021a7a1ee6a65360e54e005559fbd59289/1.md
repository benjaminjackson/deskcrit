**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline, drops to the big navy 38% slice, then moves right to the legend to find out what navy means. It reads at once as one page of a company annual report that shows how operating costs split. It feels sober, well made, and trustworthy. Then the eye is left in an empty lower-right quarter with nothing to do.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a page in an annual report (section 4.2) for shareholders, staff, and analysts, and that it shows how fiscal 2026 operating costs split across categories. It reports sourced figures and doesn't ask the reader to believe anything, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and honest, and the chart itself is built correctly. Its weakness is information: a quarter of all costs is filed under "Other", and the shares stand alone, with no total and no earlier year, so the reader learns proportions but can't tell whether anything is large, small, better, or worse.

**What would move it most**
1. **A quarter of the money is unexplained.** The gray "Other" slice in the upper left of the pie is 25%, the second-largest category on the page, bigger than rent, cloud hosting, and marketing. In an annual report, the reader's first question is "what is in that quarter?", and nothing on the page answers it. It also weakens the whole chart: a breakdown whose second-biggest part is "everything else" hasn't really broken costs down. Few's guidance on pies is to group only the small leftovers into Other, and here Other isn't small. Name the largest categories now inside it, or add a short note under the chart that lists what it holds, until Other is clearly the smallest slice. *Defect.* Fact needed from the maker: what the 25% "Other" costs consist of, by category and share.
2. **The shares have no scale and nothing to compare against.** Every number is a percentage of a total the page never states, and none has a prior year next to it. The reader can't tell whether 38% on salaries is normal, rising, or falling, or how much money any slice stands for. Few (Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design) and Tufte both ask for a reference point next to a key number. The usual fix is to state the total operating cost in the subtitle or the legend, and to add a prior-year column to the legend table so each share has something to be read against. That table already has room, and the empty lower right has more. *Convention.* Fact needed from the maker: total operating costs for fiscal 2026, and the fiscal 2025 shares for the same categories.
3. **The labels are split between the slices and a legend, and the layout leaves a hole.** The percentages are printed twice, once on each slice and again in the legend, while the category names appear only in the legend at the top right. To read one slice, the reader matches its color across the page. Tufte and Wilke both say to label the data directly. Meanwhile the legend is short and pinned high, so the pie sits heavy on the left and the lower right is empty, and the page looks like it's tipping (Arnheim, on balance). Choose one place for the labels. Either put the category names on or next to the slices and turn the right column into the comparison table from finding 2, or keep the table and remove the numbers from the slices. Either way, use the right-hand column to carry information that's missing now, so the empty space has a purpose. *Convention for the labels, taste for the balance.*

**Smaller things.**
- The white "12%" on the orange slice looks like it falls below 3:1 contrast. At about 18 px bold it's also probably too small to count as large text, so it may need 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3). The white "16%" on teal is borderline. Measure both. If they fail, use dark text on those two slices or darken the fills.
- The headline "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" names the topic rather than the finding. In my view (taste), a report page does more when its title or subtitle says what the chart shows, built from words already on the page, for example that salaries and benefits are the largest share at 38%.

**What's working.**
- The chart is drawn correctly. It has five slices that add up to 100%, in angles that match the numbers. It starts at 12 o'clock and runs largest first, and the legend follows the same order with Other at the end. There's no 3D, shadow, or decoration. Keep this discipline.
- The type does its job. A serif headline over sans-serif data text separates the levels by more than size. The subtitle gives the exact period ("twelve months to 30 June 2026"). The legend numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures. A source line gives the origin and explains the rounding.
- The running header ("Annual report 2026 · Section 4 of 9") and the "4.2 COSTS" kicker tell the reader where they are in the report, in the usual editorial form.
