**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Notes from the boat shed", a confident bold serif with plenty of room around it. It then drops into two short, even paragraphs, reads left then right, and stops at "anyone who wants to help". The lower 60% of the frame is blank white. It feels calm, literate, and warm, a bit like a small club's newsletter, and the empty bottom makes it feel unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a web page for a community boat-restoration group, read by local people who might come and volunteer. No belief is at stake: the piece asks for no leap of faith. It's a plain, friendly invitation, and "Nobody needs experience" already answers the obvious worry.

**Verdict.** As typesetting it's clean and nearly faultless. As an invitation it fails, because it raises the wish to help and then gives the reader nothing to act on: no time, no place they could find, no way to sign up or ask. The empty lower half makes that dead end obvious.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invitation has no next step (defect).** The right-hand paragraph makes the ask, "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings… for anyone who wants to help". Then the page ends. A reader who wants to go still doesn't know:
   - What time "Thursday evenings" means.
   - Where the shed is. "At the end of the lane" only works for people who already know the lane.
   - Whether to just turn up or contact someone first.
   
   Krug's test is whether a page shows within seconds what you can do next. Here the reader has to go and find out, and most won't. The eye path ends on the strongest line of the page with nothing after it. The fix direction is to give the ask a concrete next step right under that paragraph: the meeting time, the address or a map, and either "just turn up" or one contact route. Set it as the third level of the page so it's the last thing the eye lands on.
   
   Fact needed from the maker: the Thursday meeting time, the shed's address, and how a newcomer should get in touch or whether they can just turn up.
2. **The page is top-heavy and looks unfinished (convention, Arnheim on balance).** All the visual weight sits in the top 300 px: the headline and two 4-line paragraphs. About 500 px of empty white sits below with nothing to balance it. The white space isn't wrong in itself. It's gathered, not scattered. But with nothing under it, it reads as a page that failed to load or a section with its content missing, not as chosen calm. If more content follows below the fold, this goes away. If not, use the space for what the piece lacks. The next step from finding 1 is the natural thing to fill it. A picture of the shed or the dinghies would also give the evocative left paragraph something to point at, if the maker has one.

**What's working.**
- **The type is well set.** It's one serif family, with a bold headline clearly separated from the regular body by both size and weight, and only two sizes. Keep the scale this spare.
- **The two columns are well proportioned.** Each runs about 55 to 60 characters a line with comfortable leading, the baselines line up across the gutter, and the rag is gentle with no lone short words. They read easily and look composed.
- **The copy is specific and lands on the reader.** "Three clinker dinghies wait on trestles", "the smell of tar and cut cedar", and "a spare apron on the hook by the door" belong to this place and no other. "Nobody needs experience" heads off the main reason someone would hold back. Keep this voice when adding the practical details.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the page continues below this screen. If it does, finding 2 shrinks. Check the full page live.
