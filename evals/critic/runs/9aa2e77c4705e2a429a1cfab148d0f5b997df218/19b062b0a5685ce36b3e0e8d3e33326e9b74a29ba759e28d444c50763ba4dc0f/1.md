**First read.** The bold serif headline "Winter timetable at the harbour" is the first thing you see, then the eye drops into two calm, evenly gray columns of prose. It reads as a public notice from a small ferry service, quiet and trustworthy in tone. Then comes the letdown. I went looking for a timetable and found paragraphs. The page then trails off into a "Fares and cards" heading with nothing under it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling island ferry passengers when boats leave in winter and what to expect on board. No claim is at stake. This is practical information, not persuasion.

**Verdict.** This page does not do its job. It promises a timetable and gives none, so someone who came to find out when the next boat leaves can't. The typesetting is good, but that matters little until the departure times show up as times.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable isn't there** (defect). The headline names a timetable, but the page gives no departure times at all. All it has is "every ninety minutes during the winter" and "the office opens at seven". With no first departure time, the reader can't work out any sailing, and can't tell whether "seven" is also the first boat. Someone planning a trip leaves with nothing to act on. WCAG 2.4.6 (Headings and labels) asks that a heading describe what its section holds, and this one doesn't. The fix is to put the departures at the top, under the headline, as a scannable list or table of times, with the prose below as supporting notes. Fact needed from the maker: the departure times, or at least the first and last sailing of the winter day.
2. **Everything is one undifferentiated stream of prose** (defect). Five paragraphs on unrelated topics sit under one heading at the same weight: office hours, sailing frequency, weather cancellations, crossing times, the waiting room's paperbacks, bicycles and dogs. The one fact that can ruin a trip is buried mid-paragraph in the left column: in rough weather a sailing may be skipped, and the notice goes up by the gate. It gets the same weight as the shelf of paperbacks. Readers who skim (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web") won't find it. The direction is to sort the copy into a few headed groups, such as sailings, crossings and weather, and on board, using the sentences already there. Let the cancellation line stand out on its own. It also only reaches people already at the gate, which is worth flagging to the maker.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Fares and cards" rule is split by the gutter, and the right half has no heading over it. It reads as if a second heading is missing, or as if "Fares and cards" belongs to the left column only. Either run one rule across both columns or give each half a heading (Arnheim, on unintended grouping).
- Short last lines: "thirty-five." and "bags." each sit alone on the last line of their paragraph in the right column (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rebreak or re-edit so at least two words carry over.
- After the paragraph gaps, the lines in the two columns no longer line up across the gutter. "Dogs on a lead…" sits level with the space between two lines of the left column (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Set the paragraph space to a whole line, or a half line in both columns, so the rows match.
- Copy: "a rough one closer to thirty-five" drops the unit. Restore "minutes", which the same sentence already states.

**What's working.**
- The text setting is good: a sturdy reading serif at a comfortable size, lines of roughly 60 characters, easy leading, flush left with a soft rag. Keep it when you add the times.
- The hierarchy is clear and economical: one bold headline, one bold section head, body text, and only black and gray. The quiet, civic tone suits a harbour notice.
- The copy speaks plainly and kindly: "the crew will always help with bags" and "Dogs on a lead are welcome on every sailing". Keep that voice in the notes under the timetable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Nothing appears under "Fares and cards", and the bottom of the frame is blank. This could be a cut-off capture or content that hadn't loaded. Check in a live browser that the fares section actually renders.
