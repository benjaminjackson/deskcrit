The walk is done and the entries are open; next I'm writing the critique.

**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline "Booking links that fill your calendar", drops to the subhead and the blue "Create your link" button, then slides down into a row of four equal cards. It reads at once as a scheduling-link product for small businesses and solo practitioners. It feels clean, calm and competent, and also generic: nothing on the screen shows the product, and the right half of the hero is blank white.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the marketing home page for a booking-link tool, aimed at small service businesses (clinics, tutors, coaches) who might sign up. That reader has to see what a booking page looks like to their clients, and some proof that it works for businesses like theirs. They see neither: there is no product view, and the proof is an unlabeled row of names in gray text.

**Verdict.** The page is tidy but doesn't do its job. It says the product fills your calendar and shows nothing that backs that up. The biggest problem is the empty right half of the hero, where the reader should be seeing the product.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero has no picture of the product, and half the hero is empty** *(defect)*. The headline and subhead take up the left 650 px. From x≈700 to the right edge, between the nav and the cards, there's nothing. All the weight sits on the left and nothing on the right balances it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). More important for the job, the reader is asked to sign up for a booking page they have never seen. "See a live example" admits the product is the proof, but it hides it behind a click. **Fix:** put a real view of the product in that space, such as the booking page a guest sees or the "pick a time" step, so the hero balances and the reader understands the offer without clicking.
2. **The page asks for trust and doesn't earn it** *(defect)*. It asks the reader to believe the links will "fill your calendar." The only thing offered in support is the row at the bottom: "Harbor Dental, Northfield Tutoring, Kestrel Physio, Lumen Coaching, Orchard Vets". These are plain gray text with no label and no logos, so the reader can't tell whether they are customers, examples or partners. Nothing ties them to the claim either: no result is shown for any of them. The objection this reader will surely raise is "I already have a calendar tool, so why switch?", and nothing on the page answers it. **Fix:** the proof needs to be something the reader would accept on sight: who these businesses are and what changed for them. The page also needs a direct answer to why this tool and not the reader's current one. At the very least, the name row needs a label that says what it is.
3. **The headline can be read two ways, and one of them overpromises** *(taste, copy)*. "Fill your calendar" can mean "you'll get more bookings" or "bookings land in your calendar without the back-and-forth." The subhead promises only the second. A reader who takes the first meaning expects new demand the product can't deliver. **Fix:** make the headline promise what the subhead delivers, for example by pulling the subhead's "pick a time that works" and "every meeting in the right place" into it, so the claim is one the product actually keeps.
4. **Badges on every card, placed inconsistently** *(template tell + convention)*. All four cards carry a pill: "New", "Auto", "Email", "Beta". Only New and Beta are real status. "Auto" and "Email" just repeat what the card text already says. A badge on every card is a stock template move, so none of them stands out and the page looks like everyone else's (Sokolovsky, unslop). On top of that, the "New" badge is filled blue and pinned to the card's right edge, while the others are gray and sit right after the title. That squeezes "Shared calendars" onto two lines, so card 1's body text and "Learn more" sit about 30 px lower than in the other three cards and the row's baselines break (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: repeated elements follow one program). **Fix:** drop "Auto" and "Email", keep at most one quiet status badge, and place it the same way the others are, so all four titles sit on one line and the cards line up.

**Smaller things.**
- Four identical "Learn more" links can't be told apart out of context (WCAG 2.4.4). Make each link name its feature, using the card titles already on the page, e.g. "Learn more about time zones".
- "Start free" in the nav and "Create your link" in the hero are both solid blue buttons with different labels. If they do the same thing, use one label so the reader doesn't wonder whether they lead to different places.
- The row of customer names has no heading and no separation from the footer area, so it reads as stray text rather than a proof section. (This is covered under finding 2; it needs a label at least.)

**What's working.**
- The subhead is concrete and well said: "Share one link, let people pick a time that works, and keep every meeting in the right place" tells you who does what. Keep that plain, verb-led voice.
- The card descriptions land on the reader, e.g. "so nobody has to do the math" and "so the first minutes are useful". Each one ends on why the feature matters to you rather than stopping at the fact.
- The hierarchy is clear: one dominant headline, one filled primary button with a text-link secondary beside it, and a restrained one-blue palette on neutral gray.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I can't see what lies below the fold, i.e. whether a product view, pricing or real proof appears further down. If it does, the hero still needs it, because the reader decides in the hero.
