**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and then stops, because none of them says what it counts. "Active subscribers: 12.4" makes you look twice, since nobody has 0.4 of a subscriber. Then it moves down to the orange bars, where the story is clear right away: orders climb through the week, peak on Saturday and drop on Sunday. It reads as a clean, calm internal dashboard for a small bakery. The tidy layout makes the vague top row feel more careless than it is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's or staff's own overview screen for a small online bakery, checked often to see how the shop is doing. Nothing is being argued here, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are well made, but the top row doesn't do its job. The three headline figures have no units, no time period and nothing to compare against. A reader can't say what they measure or whether they're good, and the row is the most prominent thing on the screen.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI values have no units, and one of them reads as nonsense** (defect). All three cards in the top row have this problem. "3.8" under "Checkout conversion rate" is probably a percent but never says so. "64.20" under "Average order value" names no currency. "12.4" under "Active subscribers" is the worst: as written it's a fractional head count, and the reader has to guess at a dropped "k" or some other scale. These are the biggest type on the page, so the first thing the reader takes in is a puzzle. Every value needs its unit or scale next to it, in the same place on each card (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few). The percent sign may be an obvious edit, but the currency and the subscriber scale are facts only the maker has. Fact needed from the maker: the unit or scale for each of the three values (percent for conversion, which currency for order value, and whether 12.4 means thousands or something else).
2. **The headline numbers have no period and no comparison** (convention). Even with units, "3.8%" alone can't tell the owner whether this month is good or bad. That judgment is the reason to open the page. The chart caption gives its period ("orders delivered in September 2026"), but the cards give none, so the reader can't tell whether they cover the same month, today or all time. Put the period and one comparison next to each number, such as the change from the previous period or a target (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). The cards have plenty of empty space to the right of each number for a small secondary line.

**Smaller things.**
- **Chart title:** "Orders by delivery day" names the topic, not the finding. A title or caption that states the Saturday peak and Sunday drop would save the reader the look (my own judgment, common sense; no approved source).

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and plain: bars start at zero, ticks are evenly spaced every 300, the days run in their natural order, there's one series in one color, gridlines are faint, and a source line gives the system and month. Keep all of that.
- The type scale is tight. I count four clear levels (page title, number, card or chart label, and small axis and source text), with weight and gray doing the finer work rather than extra sizes.
- The grid is consistent. The logo, page title, cards and chart all share the 48 px left edge, and gutters between the cards are even. The current page is marked in the nav by a darker, bolder "Overview".

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the nav links' click areas reach 24 × 24 px. Check the padding in a live browser.
