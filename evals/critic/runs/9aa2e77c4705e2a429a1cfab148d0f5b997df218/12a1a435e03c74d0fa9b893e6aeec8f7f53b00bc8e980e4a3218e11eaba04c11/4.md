**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three large numbers across the top, 64.20 first because it's the widest, and then down to the orange bars, where Saturday's peak and Sunday's drop stand out. It reads as the internal sales dashboard for a small bakery that sells online and runs subscriptions. It feels calm, tidy, and on-brand. Then you look back at the numbers and can't say what any of them mean.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard where the bakery's owner or staff check how the shop is doing and plan delivery days. It makes no claim the reader has to be persuaded of; the numbers are facts.

**Verdict.** The layout, chart, and hierarchy are solid. The dashboard still fails at its main job, because the three numbers at the top of the screen have no units, no time period, and nothing to compare them against. A glance tells the reader nothing they can act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The stat cards are bare numbers with no unit (defect).** "Checkout conversion rate 3.8", "Average order value 64.20" and "Active subscribers 12.4" each leave the reader to supply the unit. 3.8 is probably a percent. 64.20 is money in an unnamed currency. 12.4 is the worst case: a subscriber count can't be fractional, so it must mean thousands, a rate, or something else, and the card doesn't say which. The person scanning this is the person who decides on staffing and baking, and a misread here (12.4 subscribers vs. 12,400) changes the decision. Tufte and Few both say the reader should never have to guess what a number measures. Put the unit right next to each value: %, the currency symbol, and the count written out or marked "k". Fact needed from the maker: the unit of each of the three stat values, especially whether "12.4" means thousands of subscribers.
2. **No card gives a time period or a comparison (convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2).** The chart's source line says September 2026, but the cards say nothing. Is 3.8 for this month, today, or all time? Is it up or down? Without a period and a comparison, the reader can't tell a good number from a bad one, and that's the whole reason for putting these numbers at the top. Add the period and one comparison, such as the change from the previous period or a target, under each value. Keep it to a short secondary line in the gray already used for the card labels, so the big number stays dominant. Fact needed from the maker: the period each card covers and the comparison value to show.

**Smaller things.**
- The y-axis has no label. "Orders by delivery day" in the title carries the unit, which is enough here. If the chart is ever reused without its title, add "orders" to the axis.
- The axis tick labels and the source line are small and light gray. They look like they pass, but check them against 4.5:1 in the live page.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and easy to read. The bars start at zero, the days run in their natural Monday-to-Sunday order, the gridlines are faint, and one brand orange carries every bar. The weekend peak and the Sunday drop are clear in a second.
- The source line is short and plain ("Crumbwell order system, orders delivered in September 2026"). It's the only place the page gives a period, and it's what the stat cards need too.
- The hierarchy is clear. There are three sizes (page title, stat values, labels), with weight and gray doing the finer work. The nav marks the current page in bold, and everything sits on one grid with even gutters.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the stat cards or bars show exact values on hover or click, and whether there's a way to change the date range. If the period can be picked, the cards still need to show which one is active.
