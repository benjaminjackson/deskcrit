**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, then down to the orange bars, where the Saturday peak and the Sunday drop stand out. It reads as a calm, tidy shop overview for a small bakery's back office. A second later the numbers fall apart: is 3.8 a percent, is 64.20 dollars, and how can a shop have 12.4 active subscribers?

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the internal overview screen for the bakery's owner or staff, who use it to see at a glance how the shop is doing and to plan around delivery days. No claim is at stake: it reports figures to people who already trust their own system.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are clean and well judged, but the three headline numbers, the most prominent thing on the screen, can't be read as they stand. They have no units, no period and nothing to compare against. The screen leads with the part that tells the reader least.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three KPI cards have no units.** The cards across the top show "3.8", "64.20" and "12.4" under their labels and nothing else. "Checkout conversion rate 3.8" is almost certainly a percent, but the reader has to supply that. "Average order value 64.20" has no currency. "Active subscribers 12.4" can't be a head count, so it must be thousands or some other unit the reader has to guess, and a guess that is off by a factor of 1,000 on subscribers changes every decision made from this screen. These are the largest and heaviest type on the page, so they are what people read first and remember. A number the reader has to decode before trusting is worse than a smaller number they can take at face value (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few). The fix: set the unit right next to each value, in the same line and lighter or smaller than the figure, so the number stays dominant. Defect. Fact needed from the maker: the unit of each value (whether conversion is a percent, which currency the order value is in, and whether subscribers are counted in thousands).
2. **The headline numbers have no period and no comparison.** Even with units, "3.8%" alone doesn't tell the owner whether today is good or bad. The cards say neither what time span they cover nor how they compare with last period or a target. Only the chart's source line mentions a period ("orders delivered in September 2026"), and nothing on the cards says they share it. This is the dashboard failure Few names directly: a bare big number gives the reader no way to tell good from bad, so the screen informs without ever prompting action. Following Few's fix, add the time period and a comparison next to each headline number, such as the change from the previous period or the target, kept quieter than the value itself. The current hierarchy, label then big figure, has room for one small line under each value. Defect. Fact needed from the maker: the period each card covers and which comparison (previous period or target) the shop wants to see.

**Smaller things.**
- Chart title: "Orders by delivery day" names the topic but not the finding. The chart's own message is that Friday and Saturday are the biggest days and Sunday drops off sharply. A title or a short note that says this saves the reader a step. This is common sense, not a cited rule.
- Chart y-axis: the tick values (0 to 1,200) carry no unit. The title implies "orders", so this is minor, but "orders" on the axis or in the title's wording would remove any doubt.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear. The page title, then the three equal KPI cards, then one wide chart card, all on one grid with the same gutters and left edges, so the screen scans in a single pass from top to bottom. Keep this structure when you add units and comparisons.
- The chart is honest and restrained. Bars start at zero, there's one brand color with no legend, the gridlines are faint, the weekdays are in their natural order rather than sorted by value, and a source line names the system and the month. Keep all of that.
- The color is disciplined. One warm brand orange is used for the wordmark and the data, and everything else is neutral, so the orange marks exactly what matters.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The focus indicator on the top navigation links, and their hover states.
