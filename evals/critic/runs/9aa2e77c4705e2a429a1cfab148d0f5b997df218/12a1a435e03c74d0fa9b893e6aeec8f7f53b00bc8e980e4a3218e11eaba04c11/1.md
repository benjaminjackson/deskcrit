**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers across the top (3.8, 64.20, 12.4), then drops to the orange bars, where the tall Saturday bar and the short Sunday bar stand out. It reads as the internal dashboard of a small bakery that delivers orders. It is calm, tidy and well organized, but the numbers raise questions as soon as you try to use them.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the screen the bakery's owner or staff open to see how the shop is doing and decide what to do, such as how to staff and bake for each delivery day. It reports figures and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are well made. But the three headline numbers, which are the first thing anyone reads, can't be interpreted: they have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them against. That is the biggest problem, and the page shouldn't ship until it's fixed.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three KPI numbers have no units, so they can be read the wrong way.** In the top row of cards:
   - "Checkout conversion rate 3.8" is presumably a percent.
   - "Average order value 64.20" is presumably money, but in an unknown currency.
   - "Active subscribers 12.4" can't be a head count, so it is probably thousands, but nothing says so. A reader could fairly take it as 12 people.

   These are the largest type on the page, and a wrong reading here leads to a wrong decision. Every number should state what it measures (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few). The fix: put the unit right on each number (%, a currency sign, a "k" or the full count), at a smaller size and lighter weight than the figure so the number still dominates. Defect. Fact needed from the maker: the unit of each figure, including whether "12.4" is in thousands and which currency "64.20" is in.
2. **The numbers have no period and no comparison, so the reader can't tell good from bad.** None of the three cards says what time span it covers. The chart below says "orders delivered in September 2026", but nothing ties the cards to that month, so the reader has to guess or remember it from elsewhere (Nielsen, heuristic 6). With no comparison, a 3.8 conversion rate means nothing on its own: you can't tell whether it is up, down, or on target. Few lists this bare big number as a standard dashboard pitfall. The fix (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"): put the period and one comparison, such as the change from the previous period or a target, under each number in the gray label style already used. Defect. Fact needed from the maker: the period each KPI covers and the comparison value for each.

**Smaller things**
- The chart title "Orders by delivery day" names the topic but not what it shows. The pattern a reader would act on is Friday and Saturday at the top and Sunday at the bottom. A title or one note on the chart should say that in words already on the page, such as naming Saturday as the peak. Taste or common sense, since no approved source covers it.
- The y-axis has no label. The title implies it counts orders, which is enough here, but "orders" next to the scale would remove any doubt.

**What's working**
- **The chart itself is honest and clean.** The bars start at zero, the gridlines are hairline, days run in their natural Monday-to-Sunday order, and the bars are labeled directly with no legend. It is one series in one brand color, with a source and date line underneath. Keep all of that.
- **The hierarchy is clear.** The gray label sits above a large, heavy number on every card. The page title, card labels and chart title each have a separate job. The layout uses one grid: the cards and chart panel share the same left and right edges and the same gutters.
- **The color is restrained.** One warm orange is used only for the wordmark and the data, everything else is neutral gray, and the current nav item is marked by weight and darkness rather than by color alone.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Hover and focus states on the nav links (Overview, Orders, Subscribers, Products), and whether the bars show exact values on hover. That matters here because the chart has no value labels.
