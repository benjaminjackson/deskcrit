**First read.** My eye goes first to the bold headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan", then drops to the four purple bars and lands on the tallest one, Growth at 4.0%. Next it crosses to the small table on the right and then the definition. It reads as a tidy, calm internal analytics tab. It's clean and easy to trust at a glance, maybe too easy: everything looks settled, but you can't tell what any of it means.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard tab where a growth or product team compares trial-to-paid conversion across plans and decides where to act. It's a dashboard of measured numbers, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid and the chart is honest. But the tab shows four rates with nothing to judge them against and no sign of how firm they are, so a reader can rank the plans without being able to tell whether the ranking means anything. That missing context is the biggest problem. Second is a conversion definition that can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing to compare the rates to.** Each bar label (3.1%, 3.6%, 4.0%, 3.4%) stands alone. There's no previous quarter, no target and no trend, so the reader can't tell whether Growth's 4.0% is good, normal or worse than last time, or whether Starter is slipping. The tab answers "which plan is highest" and stops before "is this a problem." Few's dashboard pitfalls name this exactly: a metric with no comparison can't be read as good or bad. Fix: put a comparison next to each rate, such as last quarter as a quiet gray bar beside each purple one, a change label under each value, or a target line across the chart. *Defect.*
2. **The ranking looks firmer than the data.** The whole spread is under one point (3.1% to 4.0%), and the table shows Enterprise rests on 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials and Growth on 118. The clean bars and bold labels make a gap of a few tenths of a point look like a real difference between plans. A reader may move budget on noise. The trial counts are in the table, but nothing links them to how far each bar could swing. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) asks for sample results to show their uncertainty. Fix: give each bar an interval or error bar, or at least mark the small-sample plans so the reader knows which gaps can't carry a decision. *Defect.*
3. **The conversion rule says two different things.** The subhead says trials "that became paid accounts within 30 days". The Definition block says "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted". The source line covers trial events only "1 July to 30 September 2025". There are two readings:
   - Each trial gets its own 30-day window.
   - Anything paid by 30 October counts, which gives a July trial roughly three months to convert.

   These give different rates, and the second favors plans whose trials started early in the quarter. Someone who rebuilds the number, or compares it with another quarter, will get a different figure and stop trusting the tab. Fix: make the subhead, the Definition and the source dates state one rule. Fact needed from the maker: whether a trial counts as converted only when its first invoice is paid within 30 days of its start, or whenever it is paid by 30 October. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- The title names the topic rather than the finding. That's fine for a live tab whose numbers change, but a short caption on the one thing worth noticing would save the reader working it out. *Taste:* a takeaway line suits a reviewed report more than a live view.
- The right column's heading "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" sits a few pixels off the headline's cap line and baseline. Either line it up with one of them or drop it clearly lower.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and lean. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, the plans run in their natural tier order, and each value sits on its bar, so there's no legend to decode. Keep all of that when you add comparison bars or intervals.
- The table repeats the chart's plan order and right-aligns its numbers, so you can check a bar against its counts in one sweep. It's already the right place to send the eye for sample size.
- The page states its definition and its source on screen. That's the right habit; it only needs the wording made consistent (finding 3).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The nav links ("Acquisition", "Conversion", "Retention", "Revenue") look short. Measure their click areas against the 24 px minimum (WCAG 2.5.8).
