**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, drops to the tallest bar (Growth, 4.0%), then runs across the other three value labels before jumping right to the table. It reads as an internal SaaS analytics report: trial conversion by pricing plan, for a growth or product team. It feels calm, plain, and credible. The doubt comes a second later: the bars differ by fractions of a point, yet they're drawn as if the ranking were settled.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's for a growth or product team comparing Q3 trial-to-paid conversion across plans, to decide where to act. The page shows measured figures to people who already trust their own data, so no claim is at stake. I checked the arithmetic: 286/9,210, 185/5,140, 118/2,950 and 38/1,120 match the bars.

**Verdict.** This is a clean, honest chart that nearly does its job. The biggest problem is that the page says two different things about what "converted" means, and that is the one number everyone will quote. After that, it presents differences of under a point, one of them resting on 38 conversions, as a firm ranking with nothing to compare it against.

**What would move it most**
1. **The subtitle and the Definition disagree on what counts as a conversion (defect).** The subtitle under the headline says "became paid accounts within 30 days". The Definition block on the right says a trial counts "when the first invoice is paid" and that trials "still open on 30 October" don't count. There are two readings:
   - (a) a fixed 30-day window from each trial's start;
   - (b) anything paid by 30 October.

   A trial started on 1 July that pays on day 60 counts under (b) and not under (a). Because of that, the July cohort may be inflated against the September cohort. Someone who repeats "Growth converts at 4.0% within 30 days" may be quoting a number calculated another way. The "Paid" column header in the table has the same gap: paid within what window? The fix is to make the subtitle, the Definition, and the "Paid" header state one rule in the same words. Fact needed from the maker: which rule the figures were actually calculated with, a 30-day window per trial or paid by 30 October.
2. **The chart reads small, noisy differences as a firm ranking (defect).** The four bars span 3.1% to 4.0%. Enterprise's 3.4% rests on 38 paid accounts, so each conversion moves that bar by about 0.09 points. A handful more or fewer and Enterprise passes Team or falls below Starter. The chart shows no range and no sample size at the bars. The sample sizes sit only in the table on the right, so the eye ranks the bars without seeing how thin the evidence is. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says to show uncertainty for samples. The fix is to put a range on each bar, or at least show the trial and paid counts at each bar, so a reader can see that Enterprise and Growth may not really differ.
3. **No comparison, so no figure can be read as good or bad (convention).** Every rate stands alone. Is 4.0% for Growth up or down from Q2? Is it on target? The reader can't tell whether there's anything to act on. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") says to put a comparison, such as the previous period or a target, next to each key figure. The fix is to add the previous quarter or a target for each plan, as a marker or a second value. Fact needed from the maker: the comparison figures, such as Q2 2025 rates per plan or the target rates.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan" names a topic, not a finding. Once the first two findings are fixed, a title that states what the reader should take away would save the step of working it out (taste, common sense).
- The Definition sits on the right, well away from the subtitle it qualifies. A reader who stops at the chart never sees it. Once it agrees with the subtitle, consider moving it under the chart, beside the source line.
- The table and the chart are split: the table has counts but no rates, and the chart has rates but no counts. One combined view would save the reader from matching them up by eye.

**What's working.**
- The bars start at zero with even ticks and one flat color. Small differences look small instead of being blown up by a cut-off axis. Keep that honesty when you add ranges.
- The value labels sit directly on the bars and the gridlines are faint, so nobody has to read values off the axis. Keep it if you add markers.
- The source line names the system and the exact dates, and the table's figures are right-aligned. That's the restraint a reporting page needs.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the nav tabs ("Acquisition", "Conversion", etc.) have clear hover and focus states.
