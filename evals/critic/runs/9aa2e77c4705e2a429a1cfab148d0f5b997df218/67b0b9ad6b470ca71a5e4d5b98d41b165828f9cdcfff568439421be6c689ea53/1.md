**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Trial to paid conversion by plan", drops to the four purple bars, and stops on the tallest one, Growth at 4.0%. It then goes to the table on the right. This reads as an internal analytics dashboard for a product or growth team. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy. I could read every number in a few seconds, but I couldn't tell whether any of them is good news.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an internal dashboard panel where a product or growth team checks Q3 trial-to-paid conversion by plan and decides where to act. It's a dashboard reporting measured numbers, so there's no claim at stake.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made panel that answers "what was conversion?" but not the question a team acts on: "is that better or worse, and is the gap between plans real?" The numbers have no comparison and no sense of how sure they are, so the reader can't tell good from bad, or a signal from noise.

**What would move it most**

1. **Every number stands alone, with nothing to compare it to.** The four bar labels (3.1%, 3.6%, 4.0%, 3.4%) are the panel's headline numbers. They have a period and a unit, but no previous quarter, no target and no trend. A reader can't tell whether Starter at 3.1% is a problem or a record. The only thing they can compare is the plans against each other, so they will treat Growth as the "winner" whether or not that's what matters. Few's dashboard pitfall is exactly this: a figure without a comparison can't be read as good or bad. Because the piece shows only Q3, it also can't show whether any plan is moving. Direction: put a comparison next to each bar, such as a previous-quarter marker or a target line, so each plan reads as up, down or on target. *Convention (Few).* Fact needed from the maker: the comparison values, either earlier-quarter rates per plan or the target rate.

2. **The chart presents gaps of less than a point as clean differences, and the sample sizes sit apart from it.** The spread from lowest to highest is 0.9 points. Enterprise's 3.4% rests on 38 paid accounts and Growth's 4.0% on 118 (table, right). At those counts, the order of Starter, Enterprise and Team could easily flip next quarter. But the bars are drawn with equal confidence, and the counts that would warn the reader are across the page in a different block. Someone scanning the chart will rank the plans and may act on a gap that's within noise. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says to show samples with ranges or notes about sample size. Direction: put the sample size where the reader looks, for example "n = 1,120" under each plan name on the axis, or show a range on each bar. That way a thin bar like Enterprise looks as uncertain as it is. *Defect.* It can mislead the decision.

3. **The definition can be read two ways, on the number the reader acts on.** The subtitle says "within 30 days". The definition says "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." One reading is a fixed 30-day window per trial, with data pulled on 30 October. The other is a calendar cutoff, so a July trial had about three months to convert and a late-September trial had one. If the second is true, the plans are measured on different windows wherever their trial lengths or start-date mix differ. If "still open" means trials longer than 30 days, such as longer Enterprise trials, then Enterprise is undercounted by design. Direction: make the two lines say the same thing, either by dropping the 30 October sentence or by saying plainly that each trial gets 30 days from its start date. *Defect.* An ambiguity on the figure being judged.

**Smaller things.**
- The title "Trial to paid conversion by plan" names the topic, not the finding. On a dashboard that's acceptable, but a short line stating what the chart shows would let a skimmer leave with the point. This is taste; no approved source covers it.
- The right-column heading "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" sits a few pixels above the line of the left title and doesn't align with it or with the subtitle. Put it on the title's baseline, or clearly below it (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- Each bar has a value label and there's also a full 0–5% axis with gridlines, so the scale is said twice. The gridlines are pale enough not to compete. This is taste: with labels on every bar, the axis could thin to just the 0% baseline.
- There's no all-plans rate. The table holds everything needed for one, and a total row or reference line would show which plans pull the average down.

**What's working.**
- The chart is the right form, and honest. The bars start at zero and come in tier order (Starter → Enterprise). Each is directly labeled, so the reader needs no legend and no squinting at the axis. Keep this when adding comparisons.
- The table shows the raw counts behind each rate, and they check out (286 / 9,210 = 3.1%, and so on). This is what makes item 2 easy to fix: the data is already there, it's just in the wrong place.
- The type and color are restrained: one hue, grays for structure, a clear three-level scale (title, section heading, body/labels), and right-aligned figures. The page has no template moves, and it looks like a tool rather than a pitch.
