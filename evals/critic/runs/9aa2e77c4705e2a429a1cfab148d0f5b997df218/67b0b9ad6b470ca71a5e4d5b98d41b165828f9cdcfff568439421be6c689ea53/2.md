**First read.** The eye lands on the title "Trial to paid conversion by plan", drops into the four purple bars, and stops on Growth at 4.0%, the tallest. It then moves right to the "Trials by plan" table. It reads as an internal analytics page for a growth or product team, and it feels calm, orderly and trustworthy.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard that product or growth staff use to compare trial-to-paid conversion across plans for Q3 2025, and then act on the gaps. It shows measured figures and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the chart is honest. The page still invites the wrong decision: it ranks the plans as if a 0.9-point spread were settled. The counts in its own table don't support that, and its definition of "converted" says two different things.

**What would move it most**

1. **The bars present differences as firm that the sample can't separate.** The table shows Enterprise converting 38 of 1,120 trials and Growth 118 of 2,950. A rough binomial check puts Enterprise's 3.4% within about ±1 point, so the gap to Growth's 4.0%, and arguably to Team's 3.6%, could be noise. The chart shows four solid bars with bold value labels and nothing to mark that range. A reader will take "Growth converts best, Enterprise underperforms" and act on it. This is a defect, because it misleads. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says observed rates from samples should show their uncertainty. Fix: add an interval to each bar, or at least a note beside the chart tying each rate to its paid count, so small samples read as tentative. Keep the zero baseline.

2. **The definition of "converted" can be read two ways.** The subtitle says "became paid accounts within 30 days". The definition says "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." Reading one: each trial gets its own 30-day window. Reading two: there is one cutoff on 30 October, so a July trial had about three months to convert and a late-September trial had 30 days. Under reading two, the rates mix different windows, and they aren't the "within 30 days" figure the subtitle promises. The source line adds to the doubt: it covers "trial events, 1 July to 30 September 2025", but conversions are counted until 30 October. This is a defect in exactly the part readers will quote back later (common sense; no catalog entry). Fix: make the subtitle, the definition and the source line describe one rule. If the 30 October date is a data cutoff rather than the window, say that in so many words. Fact needed from the maker: whether each trial gets its own 30-day window or every trial is cut off on 30 October, and the date range of the invoice data behind the paid counts.

3. **Each rate stands alone, so nobody can tell whether 3.1% is good.** There's no previous quarter, no target and no trend on the chart or in the table. The page can show a ranking but not whether conversion is getting better or worse, which is usually why someone opens a conversion tab. This is a convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2; Tufte, *Visual Display*. Fix: put a comparison next to each plan's rate, such as the change from the last period or the target. Fact needed from the maker: the previous quarter's conversion rate per plan, or the target rate per plan.

**Smaller things.**
- Title: "Trial to paid conversion by plan" names the topic but not the finding. That's fine for a dashboard tab, but once uncertainty is shown, a caption that says what the reader may conclude would help (taste; the source for this rule is off the approved list).
- Balance: the chart fills the left column from top to bottom, while the right column ends at the Definition paragraph around a third of the way down. That leaves the bottom-right quadrant empty, so the page leans left (Arnheim, "Balance"). Finding 3's comparison, or the uncertainty note, could fill it.
- Table: it gives Trials and Paid but no rate column. To match a row to its bar, the reader has to divide in their head. The rates already appear on the bars, so adding a rate column (or a rate under each count) is just rearranging figures the piece already has.
- Period: the page is fixed to Q3 2025 and no period or plan control is visible. If one exists elsewhere, ignore this; if not, the reader has nowhere to go for another quarter (Nielsen, heuristic 7).

**What's working.**
- The bar chart is honest and quiet. The axis starts at zero, the gridlines are faint, each bar carries its value label directly with no legend, and the plans run in their natural tier order. Keep all of that when you add intervals.
- The table puts the denominators on the page, with right-aligned figures and light row rules. That's what makes the sample-size problem visible at all, so keep it next to the chart.
- The definition and source are right on the page rather than in a tooltip. Once they agree with each other (finding 2), they're the reason a reader can trust these numbers.
