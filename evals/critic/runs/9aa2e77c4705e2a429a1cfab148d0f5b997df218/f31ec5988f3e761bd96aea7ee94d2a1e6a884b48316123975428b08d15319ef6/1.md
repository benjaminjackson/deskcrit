**First read.** My eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" and then hits three paragraphs that run nearly the full width of the window. It reads as a plain information page for people who use a river ferry. It feels calm and orderly, but the long lines make it tiring to read and the page gives no handholds. Nothing stands out below the heading. The facts a passenger came for (when the boat leaves, whether a bike can come, what changes in winter) are folded into the prose.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells current and would-be ferry passengers how the service runs so they can plan a crossing. No claim is at stake: it states facts and asks the reader to believe nothing.

**Verdict.** It does its job only partly. It is clean and readable at a glance, but a passenger still can't plan a trip from it, because it gives no times. The lines are also far too long to read comfortably.

**What would move it most**

1. **The page never gives a time a passenger can act on (defect).** The first paragraph says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening." The third says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk." Departure times are the one thing a reader acts on and later holds the operator to, and both lines can be read many ways. Does "early morning" mean 6:00 or 8:00? Does "before dusk" mean 15:30 or just before sunset on that day? A reader who guesses wrong misses the last boat. The page also sends readers to the printed timetables "in the shelter at each landing," so someone reading at home is told the answer is somewhere they aren't. The fix is to put the first and last departure times, for summer and for winter, in the text itself. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times in the regular timetable and in the winter timetable, and when the winter timetable starts and ends.

2. **The lines are about 125–130 characters long, nearly twice the comfortable measure (convention, Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* §2.1.2: 45–75 characters, 66 ideal).** All three paragraphs run in one column from the 80 px left margin almost to the right edge of a 1280 px window. At this length the eye loses its place on the way back to the start of the next line. You can feel it at "Each / crossing takes about…" and "lock them to the / rail…". The line spacing, about 1.5, would be fine at a normal measure, but it is too tight for lines this long (Bringhurst §2.2.1; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*: "the longer the line, the more linespacing it needs"). The direction is to cap the text column at about 65–70 characters, roughly 600–680 px at this size, and keep the left margin. That fixes both problems at once, and the empty space then gathers on the right where it belongs. Measure the rendered `max-width` and line height to confirm.

**Smaller things.**
- Taste: each paragraph covers one topic (the schedule, bicycles, winter). A short bold run-in at the start of each, or three small subheads using wording already on the page ("Bicycles", "In winter"), would let a skimmer jump straight to the bike rule without reading the schedule first.

**What's working.**
- The type is restrained and well chosen: one plain sans-serif at two clear levels. The heading is set bold and noticeably larger, and the body is a comfortable size in dark gray on white with strong contrast. Keep the two-level scale and don't add sizes when you add structure.
- The voice is plain and helpful, and it explains its rules. "So the deck stays clear for people walking to the stairs" tells the rider why bags stay on the bike, which makes them more likely to comply. Keep that tone.
- The margins are generous and the paragraphs are separated by space alone, with no rules or boxes, so the page reads as one calm document.
