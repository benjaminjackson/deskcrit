**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif heading, "Notes for the summer fete", then jumps down the bold phrases: "labelled with their ingredients", "collected by four", "on the field only". The two italic times, "arrive before ten" and "three o'clock sharp", get skipped on that first pass. It reads as a short web page of practical instructions for people helping at a village fete. It feels calm, plain and friendly, and it's well set.

**What it's for.** My assumption: notes for stallholders and helpers at a community fete, read on a screen before the day so they know when to arrive and what the rules are. No claim is at stake. The page only has to be understood and acted on.

**Verdict.** This is a clean, readable page that nearly does its job. Two things get in the way. The emphasis uses two styles that mean nothing different, so the first thing a helper must do (arrive before ten) is the easiest thing to miss. And one deadline can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The emphasis hides the most important instruction.** Five phrases in six lines are emphasized, in two styles. Bold goes on "labelled with their ingredients", "collected by four" and "on the field only". Italic goes on "arrive before ten" and "three o'clock sharp". In this face the bold is far louder than the italic, so a reader who skims the bold, as most will, picks up three rules and misses both times. The arrival time is the first thing a helper acts on. Times and deadlines are one kind of element, so they should look alike everywhere (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: repeated elements follow one program). Here one deadline is bold and the other two times are italic. With half the text emphasized, the emphasis also stops doing its job of pointing at what matters. Fix: give every must-do instruction one emphasis style, bold, and drop the italic, or use italic only for something plainly secondary. If the bold still crowds the paragraphs, set the instructions as a short list with the bold phrase leading each item. *Defect.*
2. **"All tickets must be collected by four" can be read two ways.** One reading: ticket holders must claim their prizes by four. The other: helpers must gather in all the tickets by four. The sentence names no one doing the collecting, and this is a deadline with a penalty ("the prizes go back in the box"), so a reader who picks the wrong reading loses a prize or misses a job. Fix: put back the subject, so the line says who collects and what they collect. Fact needed from the maker: who must collect the tickets by four, and whether "collected" means prizes being claimed or tickets being gathered in. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- The page never gives the date or place of the fete, and the gates' opening time is mentioned but not stated. A helper reading ahead has to find these somewhere else. Fact needed from the maker: the date, the venue and the time the gates open.
- The second paragraph puts raffle rules (for visitors) and parking (for everyone) next to setup rules (for stallholders), with no sign of who each line is for. Splitting by who it's for, or a list, would fix it. This is common sense, not a published rule.
- "Left on the trestle by the door": which door? Someone new to the venue won't know. It needs a landmark only the maker can give.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is simple: one heading size and one body size, with the heading set apart by weight and size together. There's no doubt where to start.
- The body is set well for a screen: comfortable size, dark text on white, flush left, lines at about 75 characters at most, and generous leading. It reads easily and should stay this way.
- The copy explains its reasons ("so the stalls can be set up by the time the gates open"). That makes the rules feel reasonable rather than bossy. Keep that voice when you tighten the wording.
