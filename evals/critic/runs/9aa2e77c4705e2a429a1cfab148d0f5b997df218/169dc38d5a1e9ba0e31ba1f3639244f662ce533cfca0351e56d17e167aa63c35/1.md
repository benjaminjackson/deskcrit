**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three gray paragraphs of equal weight and has nothing else to hold onto. It reads as a calm, tidy notice page for a local river ferry, written for passengers. It feels pleasant and quiet, more like an essay than something you check while catching a boat.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or info-screen notice that tells passengers when the ferry runs, how bicycles work, and where to find changes. They'll glance at it to answer one question: "when is the next boat," "can I bring my bike," or "is it running this week." It doesn't ask the reader to believe anything, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read. The design fails its job, though, because a passenger's questions get prose instead of answers. The schedule is buried mid-sentence and stated so vaguely ("early morning," "late evening," "before dusk") that nobody can plan a trip from it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on can be read two ways (defect).** The opening paragraph says the boat runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening." The third says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk." These are the lines people plan around and hold the operator to. Someone reading "late evening" could expect a boat at 9 pm, or at 11, and if they guess wrong they're stranded on the far bank. "The quay" probably means the market quay, but then the page never says when the last winter boat leaves the north landing. "In winter" doesn't say which months. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say what time the first boat leaves, so the 40-minute pattern can't be followed. The fix is to state actual first and last departure times from each side, in summer and winter, and name the side for each "last crossing." Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times from each landing in the regular and winter timetables, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends.
2. **Three topics are set as unlabeled prose, so every question means reading the whole page (defect).** The page covers three separate things: the regular schedule and crossing time, bicycles, and the winter timetable and where to check changes. Each is one paragraph of the same weight with no subhead. Passengers skim notices like this (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). A cyclist has to read the schedule paragraph before learning that bikes ride free. Someone wondering whether the boat runs today only finds the noticeboard in the last sentence. The useful facts ("every forty minutes," "about twelve minutes," "no extra charge," "one boat each hour") sit in the middle of sentences, where the eye won't catch them. Add a short subhead to each block, using words already in the text: the schedule, bicycles, winter and changes. Then pull the hard facts out of the sentences so they can be scanned, for example as a short list or a small timetable for the times. Keep the wording otherwise. The bicycle boarding steps already work well as a sequence.

**Smaller things.**
- The title "Notes for ferry passengers" could head any operator's page. Once there are subheads, a title that names this route, using "north landing" and "market quay" from the text, would tell a reader at a glance that they're on the right page.
- Body lines run about 70 to 75 characters, at the top of the comfortable range (Bringhurst). That's fine for now, but don't widen the column.

**What's working.**
- The text is set well: a plain sans serif at a comfortable size, dark gray on white, generous leading for the line length, flush left with a soft rag. Keep all of this when adding structure.
- The type scale is two clear levels, a bold title and regular body text, with no extra sizes. Any subheads should add only one more level, set in bold at or just above body size, not a new size.
- The copy is courteous and concrete where it describes the process ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). Keep that voice and change only the parts that hide facts.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I can't tell how this column reflows on a phone, where passengers are likely to read it. Check in a browser at phone width.
