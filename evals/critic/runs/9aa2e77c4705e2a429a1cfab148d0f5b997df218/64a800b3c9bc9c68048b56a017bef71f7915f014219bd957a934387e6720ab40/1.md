**First read.** The eye lands on "The Wexley Gazette", centered above a double rule. It then drops to the three bold headlines and moves across them left to right without stopping on any one. It reads as the front page of a small-town paper's website: calm, plain, trustworthy, and a little unfinished, because the bottom half of the screen is blank.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the web front page of a local paper, for Wexley residents who want to see in a few seconds what's happening in town and when. This is news, so it isn't trying to persuade anyone and there is no claim at stake.

**Verdict.** It's clean and easy to read, but it doesn't yet work as a front page. Three stories of exactly equal weight sit over half a screen of nothing, so the page gives no sense of what matters most today and looks like it stopped loading.

**What would move it most**

1. **There is no lead story.** The three columns have the same headline size, weight and length, start at the same height and have almost the same depth. Once past the masthead, the reader gets no signal that the bridge reopening matters more to the town than the school garden, or the other way round, and so scans all three as equals. A front page's main job is to rank the news, and this one leaves the ranking to the reader. Fix: make one story clearly first and set the others back by size. Usually that means a larger headline and a wider column or more text for the lead, with the other two narrower or lower (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance by differences of size). The type scale already has room for one more headline size. *Convention.*

2. **The bottom 45% of the screen is empty.** Everything ends by about y≈415, and from there to the bottom edge there's nothing: no rule, no next section, no footer. Because the empty area isn't shaped or framed, it doesn't look like breathing room. It looks like missing content, and with all the weight crowded into the top band the page feels top-heavy (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on weight). Readers will wonder whether this is all the paper has. Fix: either use the space so the page continues, for example by giving the lead story the extra depth from finding 1, or end the page clearly so the stopping point looks intended. If this was cropped from a longer page, ignore this finding. *Defect.*

3. **The relative dates point to no date.** The stories say "from next month", "in April" and "from March", but the masthead shows no issue date. "From next month" only means something if the reader knows which month it is now. Someone arriving from search or a shared link months later can't tell whether the market has already moved. These are the details people act on, and they'll go to the wrong place on the wrong Saturday. Fix: put the issue date under the masthead, and give the market move its actual month. Fact needed from the maker: the issue date, and the month the Saturday market moves into the corn exchange. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- At about 38–40 characters, the columns run a little short of Bringhurst's 40–50 for multi-column text. This will mostly sort itself out if the lead story gets a wider column, so no separate fix is needed.
- Nothing here looks like a link. If the headlines click through to full stories, they need some visible sign of that. I can't tell from a still; see below.

**What's working.**
- The type is good and should stay. A single serif family sits in three sizes with clear steps (masthead, bold headlines, regular body text), with comfortable spacing between lines and dark text on white. It reads as a newspaper without any fake nostalgia.
- The grid is tight. Three columns with equal gutters share one baseline, and the outer column edges line up exactly with the ends of the double rule (x≈100 to 1180). That gives the plain page its sense of order.
- The copy is specific and local: the Wex, Mill Lane Primary, the corn exchange, sixty stalls. Each headline states the news in a few words with a verb, which is exactly what front-page headlines should do.
