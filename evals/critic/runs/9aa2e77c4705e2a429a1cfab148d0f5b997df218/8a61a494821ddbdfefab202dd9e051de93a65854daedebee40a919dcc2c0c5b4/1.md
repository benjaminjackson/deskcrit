**First read.** The eye lands straight on the big serif quote. It fills the upper two-thirds and is the only thing at that scale, so the page reads as a warm, editorial lead for a local library newsletter. Next comes the bold attribution, and only after that the small gray paragraph, which is where the actual news is. The page feels calm, friendly, and a bit old-fashioned, which suits the subject. What jumps out at that size is the typewriter quotes and the hyphen.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the lead item of an email or web newsletter for regular library patrons, and that its job is to tell them the reading room reopens Monday and give them a reason to come back. It's an announcement, so the piece isn't asking the reader to believe a claim.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and has a clear focal point, but the focal point is the quote rather than the news. At this size, the straight quotes and the hyphen look amateur. Fix those two things and you have a good piece.

**What would move it most**
1. **The news is the quietest text on the page** (defect). What patrons need to act on is "reopens on Monday" and the new lamps, shelves, and puzzle table. That sits in the smallest, lightest text, gray on cream at the bottom, after a 90-px quote and a bold attribution. A reader who skims, as most newsletter readers do, takes away a nice line about what people call the room and never learns that it's opening again or when. Krug's point applies: the page should make clear within seconds what it is and what you can do. The quote also depends on context the page never gives. "The 'old' reading room" and "nobody calls it anything else" imply the room has a new name or a newer rival, and a stranger can't tell which. Keep the quote as the hook, but make the reopening the second thing the eye reaches: move it up so it takes over the bold attribution's weight, or put "reopens Monday" in the label or the attribution line. Fact needed from the maker: the date of the Monday, since a newsletter is read on different days and "Monday" alone could mean the wrong week.

2. **Straight quotes and a hyphen at display size** (convention; the curly-quote point has no checked source, so treat it as common sense). At around 90 px, the straight `"` at the start and end, the straight `'old'`, and the hyphen in "room - nobody" are each as big as a letter. They make a carefully set serif headline look like it was typed in a plain-text field. Bringhurst (5.2.1) calls for a true dash there: an em dash, or a spaced en dash. Use curly quotes (“ ” and ‘ ’) and the true dash. The catalog notes that this defect grows with type size, and here it's at its largest.

3. **The opening quote mark isn't hung** (convention, partly supported by Bringhurst; it gets worse at large sizes). The `"` sits on the shared left edge at x≈110, so the first word, "It's", starts about 35 px to the right of "LIBRARY NEWSLETTER" and the left edges of lines 2 and 3. At this scale the left edge looks ragged rather than crisp. Hang the mark in the margin so the letters of all three lines align with the label.

**Smaller things.**
- The comma inside the closing quote, "else,"", followed by a separate sentence starting with lowercase "said", reads as a single sentence split across two very different type styles. That's fine in print convention, but with this much difference in size the comma looks stranded. You could end the quote on a period and start the attribution as its own line, as an editorial edit.

**What's working.**
- There's one clear focal point: a large serif quote against small sans text, with nothing competing for attention. Keep that contrast in scale.
- The restrained palette (cream ground, near-black text, one rust accent on the label) fits a community library's warmth without decoration.
- The body paragraph has a comfortable measure (about 60 characters per line) and line spacing, and everything aligns to one left edge.
