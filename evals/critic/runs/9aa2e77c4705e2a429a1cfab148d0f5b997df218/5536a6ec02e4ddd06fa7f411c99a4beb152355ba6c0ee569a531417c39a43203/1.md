**First read.** The eye lands on "Harbour Lights festival" at top left, then drops into three dense, gray-ruled columns of the same weight and has no reason to pick one. It reads as the info section of a small-town event page, written as a newspaper item. It feels calm, plain, and a bit official, more like a council notice than a festival of lanterns and paper boats. The middle column sits slightly lower than the other two, and that catches the eye as an error within seconds.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling local families what happens at the festival, when and where, and asking for volunteers. It mostly states plain facts, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The writing is warm and specific and the type is well set, but as an event page it fails at the one thing people come for. The times and places are buried inside three unbroken paragraphs, so nobody skimming can find when the lantern walk starts or when pie entries are due without reading everything.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule is hidden in prose, and the columns have no headings.** Each column is one solid paragraph with no heading. Every fact a visitor needs is set mid-sentence at body weight: "ten each morning", "noon", "at dusk", "by one o'clock", "at four", "the town hall", "the long hall behind the church". Someone checking the page on the day has to read about 300 words to find one time. The three columns look identical, so nothing shows that the second is the lantern walk and the third is the pie contest. People skim pages for headings and short chunks, not unbroken blocks (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Headings should name what their section holds (WCAG 2.4.6). Fix direction: give each column a heading that names its event and day, using words already on the page, such as "Lantern walk, Saturday night" and "Pie contest, Sunday afternoon". Then pull the when and where out of each paragraph into a short, scannable line or list above the prose. Keep the prose for the color. *Defect.*
2. **The middle column starts about 14 px lower than the other two.** Its first line ("On Saturday night…") sits at about y=160, while columns 1 and 3 start at about y=146. So no line in it lines up with the lines beside it, all the way down. The rules make the three columns read as one set, so the offset looks like a bug, not a choice, and it is the first thing a careful eye catches after the title. Text in neighboring columns should share baselines (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Fix direction: remove the extra top space on the middle column so all three tops and baselines match. *Defect (craft).*
3. **The call for volunteers gives no way to answer it.** "Volunteers are still needed to hand out programmes, carry chairs, and guard the cake tent…" is the only thing the page asks readers to do. It is tucked into the last lines of column 1, with no name, place, link, or time to sign up. A willing reader has nowhere to go, so the ask yields nothing. Fix direction: lift it out of the paragraph into its own short block with one clear way to respond. Fact needed from the maker: how and where volunteers sign up. *Defect.*
4. **Nothing in the look says "festival".** The page is charcoal type and hairline gray rules on white, set like a broadsheet. The copy describes lanterns at dusk, paper boats on the river, and a brass band, but the page has no warmth, light, or color to match. Readers won't feel the event before they read about it. In my view, one warm accent or a single image of the lantern walk would carry the mood the copy already promises, and that matters for getting families to come. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- "seagulls." sits alone on the last line of column 1 (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Adjust the measure or rewrap so the last line carries at least two words.
- The date is given only as "the first weekend of September", with no day numbers or year. Since this is what people put in their diary, it should be exact. Fact needed from the maker: the dates of the festival.

**What's working.**
- The copy is specific and full of local life ("the pie that makes the judges laugh", "guard the cake tent against hungry seagulls"). It reads as this town's festival, not anyone's. Keep the voice when restructuring; only move the facts out.
- The text setting is sound. A readable serif at a comfortable size, near-black on white, lines of about 45 to 50 characters, generous leading, and a bold heading in the same family that clearly leads.
