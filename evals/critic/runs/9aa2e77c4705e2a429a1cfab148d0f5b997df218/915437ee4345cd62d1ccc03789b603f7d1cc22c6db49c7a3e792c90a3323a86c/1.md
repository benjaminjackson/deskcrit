**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers," and drops straight into three short paragraphs of dark gray text in a column on the left. It reads as a plain web info page for people taking a river ferry. It feels calm, sober, and a bit unfinished, like a document someone pasted onto a page. The right 45% of the frame and the bottom third are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long a crossing takes, and the rules for bikes and the winter timetable, so they can catch the boat. It states plain facts and asks the reader to believe nothing, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and readable, and the hierarchy is right. But the page doesn't do its job well. The things a passenger acts on (first boat, last boat, when winter hours start) are given as vague words inside running prose. The main craft flaw is a right edge that looks hand-broken.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger plans around can be read more than one way.** This is a defect. Paragraph 1 says the ferry runs "between early morning and late evening." Paragraph 3 says that in winter "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk." These are the lines someone reads to avoid getting stranded, and both are open to interpretation: "late evening" could mean 8 pm or 11 pm, and "before dusk" could mean any time in the hour or two before dark. Get it wrong and the passenger reaches the landing after the last boat has gone. "In winter" has the same problem, because it gives no dates. The direction is to state each as a clock time or a date range. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last sailing times in summer and in winter, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends.
2. **The facts a passenger is scanning for are buried mid-sentence.** This is a defect, and common sense rather than a checklist rule (Krug's "don't make me think" points the same way). The 40-minute frequency, the 12-minute crossing, free bikes and the one-boat-an-hour winter service are all real answers. But each one sits in the middle of a sentence, and paragraph 1 is a single run-on sentence that ends in sandbank and tide detail most riders don't need. A reader who comes to check "how often, how long, until when" has to read everything. The direction: put the timetable facts first and make them easy to scan, for example as short labeled lines or by restructuring paragraph 1 so frequency and hours lead and the tide note follows as its own sentence. Use the words already on the page.

**Smaller things.**
- **Rag (convention: Bringhurst 2.1.3; Hochuli, "The line").** The line breaks look forced rather than set. "market / quay" and "boarded, / and lock" break with room still left on the line. The last line of paragraph 1 ("sandbank … low tide.") runs about 40 px past every other line, so the right edge has a jutting tail instead of a gentle rag. Remove the hard breaks and let the text wrap to one column width.

**What's working.**
- **One clear level per job.** The heading is the only bold, large element, and the body is one comfortable size with generous leading (roughly 1.5). The eye knows where to start, and the paragraphs are easy to read. Keep this two-level scale and don't add more sizes when you restructure.
- **Line length and contrast.** Body lines run about 50 to 60 characters, in near-black on white. Keep that measure as you edit.
- **Restraint.** There are no boxes, icons, or decoration, which suits a plain public notice. Restructuring for scanning shouldn't bring them in.
