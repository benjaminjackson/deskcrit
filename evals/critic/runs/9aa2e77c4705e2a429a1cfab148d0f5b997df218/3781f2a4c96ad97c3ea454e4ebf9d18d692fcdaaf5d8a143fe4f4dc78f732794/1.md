**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", moves right to the white form card, and comes to rest on the blue "Send message" button. It reads at once as a studio's contact page: calm, orderly and easy to use. It also looks like it could belong to any company. Nothing on the screen says this was made by a design studio.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the inquiry page for people already thinking about hiring Fernwick Studio: they write a short brief and expect a reply. No claim is at stake. The reader came here by choice, and the only promise, a reply within two working days, is one they'll take on the studio's word.

**Verdict.** It works as a form, but it asks for the wrong information in the wrong place. The intro asks for a budget and a timeline, and the form has nowhere to put them. On a design studio's site, the page also looks like a default template, which is the wrong impression to give a prospective client.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form ignores the studio's own request.** The intro says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The only place for that is the Message box, which looks about five lines tall. Readers have to remember the request from the left column and fit it into free text. Many won't, so the studio's first reply becomes "what's your budget?", and that extra exchange uses up the two working days the page promises. *Defect.* It makes people remember something instead of showing it where they need it (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Putting needed input in front of the user is also common sense. Fix: give budget and timeline their own fields, marked optional to match "if you have them", using simple text fields so the page doesn't invent ranges. At minimum, move the line next to the Message box as helper text and make the box taller, since a project description is longer than five lines.

2. **The layout looks dropped in place, not designed, and on a studio's site that is part of the pitch.** The card's right edge (x≈1067) doesn't line up with anything: the header and nav run to 1224, so a 160 px strip on the right is just leftover space. The left column ends after three lines, leaving its lower half empty, and there's a wide empty band below the card. None of this space is shaped on purpose. Add the default-looking blue underlined links, the gray page and the white card, and the page could belong to any company. A prospect judging a design studio is judging this page too. *Convention* for the grid (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*: a block whose edges match no other was placed by eye; Ruder on gathering empty space into a few deliberate areas). *Taste* for the lack of identity. Fix: put the card's edges on the page's columns (for example, run it to the header's right edge), turn the open space into one deliberate area, and bring the studio's own type or color voice into at least the headline and button.

**Smaller things.**
- The nav doesn't mark "Contact" as the current page: all four links look the same. Mark it with one cue, such as a heavier weight or a bar (Laubheimer, NN/g menu-design checklist).
- No field is marked required or optional. Add an asterisk to required labels with a one-line legend, or "(optional)" after optional ones (Budiu, NN/g).
- The input borders look pale gray on white, probably under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure them and darken until they reach 3:1.
- The headline breaks as "Tell us about your / project", which splits "your project". Widen the column or break it as "Tell us about / your project".

**What's working.**
- Labels sit above the fields in bold and close to them, in one column with no placeholder-only labels. Keep that.
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a specific, believable promise. It's the only line that tells the reader what happens next, so keep it next to the form.
- There's exactly one primary action, and the "Send message" button names what it does. Keep it that way.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the fields and button, error messages, and what the visitor sees after sending. For an inquiry form, check that a clear confirmation appears and repeats the two-day promise.
