**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project," moves right into the white form card, and drops to the blue "Send message" button. This is the contact page of a small design studio. It feels calm, competent and plain, a little anonymous, which suits a page whose only job is to take a message.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is Fernwick Studio's contact page, where a prospective client sends a project inquiry, likely after looking at the Work page. Nothing here asks the reader to believe anything: they arrive already interested, and the page only promises a reply. So no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The form is clear, single-column, properly labeled and has one obvious action. The one thing holding it back is that the studio asks for a budget range and a timeline, but the place that asks is a long way from the box where people type, and that box is small. A good share of inquiries will probably arrive without the two things the studio most wants.

**What would move it most**
1. **The request for budget and timeline sits in the wrong place, and the box it applies to is too small.** "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them" is the last line of the intro paragraph in the left column, about 450 px from the Message field. By the time someone clicks into Message, they've already passed it, so they have to remember it instead of seeing it where they act. The Message box is also only about five lines tall. That signals "a short note" when the studio wants a brief with numbers in it. This is a defect: it costs the studio replies that lack what it needs to quote, plus a round of back-and-forth before work can start. It breaks two principles: help belongs next to the step it concerns (Nielsen, heuristic 10), and a control belongs next to the thing it governs (Norman, natural mapping). The fix:
   - Move that sentence to sit as helper text directly under the "Message" label, or split it into its own optional fields for budget range and timeline. Leave the intro to cover the reply promise.
   - Make the Message box tall enough to invite a paragraph. This part is common sense, not a cited rule.

**Smaller things.**
- **Field borders:** the input outlines look like pale gray on white and may be under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). The borders are the only thing marking the fields inside the white card. Measure them and darken them if they fall short.
- **Nav:** "Contact" looks the same as the other nav links, so nothing marks the current page. Give it one clear cue, such as a bolder weight or a bar (Nielsen, heuristic 1).
- **Required fields:** no field is marked as required or optional. Put an asterisk after each required label and explain it once, or write "optional" after the optional ones (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group).
- **Grid:** the card's right edge (around x=1068) lines up with nothing. The nav ends at about 1224, leaving an unexplained empty strip on the right. Either extend the card's right edge to the nav's right edge or set both columns on a shared grid (Müller-Brockmann).
- **Headline break:** "Tell us about your / project" leaves "project" alone on the second line and splits the phrase. Break after "Tell us" or widen the column so the second line carries more weight. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- **The reply promise:** "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a specific, checkable commitment. It tells the sender what happens after they click, which most contact pages never say. Keep it, and keep it in the intro.
- **The form:** labels sit above the fields, it's one column, and there's a single solid primary button with a verb-led label ("Send message"). It's conventional in the right way, with nothing to figure out.
- **Restraint:** one blue for both links and the action, with the links underlined so color isn't the only cue, on a quiet gray ground. That keeps the button as the one strong spot of color on the page.
