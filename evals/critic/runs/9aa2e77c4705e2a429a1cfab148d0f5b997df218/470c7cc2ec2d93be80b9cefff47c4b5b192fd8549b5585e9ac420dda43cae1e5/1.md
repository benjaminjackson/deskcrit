**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into a column of text that looks like one long block of evenly spaced stripes. The three paragraphs hardly separate from each other. It reads as a plain information page for people who ride a river ferry. It feels calm and clean, but loose: the lines seem to float apart instead of reading as paragraphs.

**What it's for.** You gave me no context, so I'm assuming this is a web page that tells passengers (locals and visitors) when the ferry runs and how to bring a bike, so they can plan a trip. It asks the reader to believe nothing; it's practical information, so no claim is at stake.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and the type is mostly well set, but it fails at its job in two ways. The line spacing is so loose that the three paragraphs blur into one striped block. And the schedule gives no times anyone can plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The line spacing is so loose that it hides the paragraphs.** In all three paragraphs, the gap between lines is about 45 px. That is roughly 2.4 times the text size, on lines of about 65 to 70 characters. The gap between paragraphs is only about 64 px, so the difference is too small to see at a glance. The page reads as eight evenly spaced stripes, not as three topics (schedule, bikes, winter). A rider scanning for the bike rule has to read from the top to find where it starts. Each line also floats free of the next, so the eye has to search a little for the start of every new line. Bringhurst (*Elements*, 2.2.1) and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 47) say leading should match the measure. At this measure that means something near 1.5, not 2.4. The rule that space should show groups comes from Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II) and Wertheimer's proximity principle. Fix: tighten the line spacing to about 1.4 to 1.6. Keep the paragraph gap, or make it larger, so it is clearly bigger than the line gap. The heading gap should stay close to the text it introduces. *Defect (convention).*

2. **The schedule gives no times you can act on.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "In winter" and "before dusk" are the lines a passenger plans around. Each one can be read several ways. "Early morning" could be 6:00 or 8:00. "In winter" doesn't say which months. "Before dusk" moves by an hour across the season. A passenger who guesses wrong misses the last boat home, and on a ferry that is the costly mistake. Fact needed from the maker: the first and last departure times, the months when the winter timetable runs, and the time of the last winter crossing. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- Nothing on the page says which quay a timed departure leaves from. "The last crossing leaves the quay" could mean either landing, because the first paragraph names two. Use the quay's name from paragraph one ("the market quay") if that is the one meant. Otherwise, fact needed from the maker: which landing the last winter crossing leaves from.
- The page has no subheads. With only three short paragraphs, fixing the spacing is enough to show the three topics. If more notes get added, short heads such as "Bicycles" or "Winter" would let riders jump to what they need.

**What's working.**
- The line length is right. At about 65 to 70 characters, it sits inside Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range, so keep the column width when you change the spacing.
- The type plan is restrained. One sans-serif family, a heading that differs from the body in both size and weight, and dark text on white with plenty of contrast. Nothing competes with the words.
- The copy is plain and written in full sentences. "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" tells you who does what, and in what order, without any filling in.
