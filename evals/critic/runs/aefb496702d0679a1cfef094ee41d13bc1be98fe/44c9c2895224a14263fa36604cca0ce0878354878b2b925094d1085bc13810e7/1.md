**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three evenly spaced serif paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry. It feels calm and quiet, closer to a printed notice in a shelter than a website. Nothing on the page pulls the eye after the heading, so you read it top to bottom as prose.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a web page telling ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and how bikes and the winter timetable work, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and gets nothing wrong. The page still falls short of its job, because the facts a passenger plans around are vague and buried in the middle of sentences. Someone who needs the last boat or the first boat cannot find it at a glance, and can't find it at all on this page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on aren't stated.** "Between early morning and late evening" (paragraph 1), "In winter" and "before dusk" (paragraph 3) are the lines someone will plan their day around. Each can be read more than one way. "Late evening" could mean 8 pm or 11 pm, and "before dusk" moves by an hour or more across a winter. "In winter" doesn't say which months. If a reader guesses wrong, they're standing at the quay after the last boat has gone. The page then sends them elsewhere: "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing." That means someone planning from home has to go to the landing to find out when the boat leaves. This is a **defect**, not a style point, because these are times people hold the operator to. Fix: replace each vague time with the real one: first and last departures from each landing, the months the winter timetable applies, and the winter last crossing as a clock time. Only the operator has those facts. If the times really do change with daylight, say so and say where the current times are published online.
2. **The facts are locked in prose, so nothing can be scanned.** "Every forty minutes," "about twelve minutes," "one boat each hour," and "no extra charge" sit mid-sentence in body text set the same way as everything else. Someone checking "how often in winter?" has to reread the whole page. The three paragraphs cover three different topics: the regular service, bicycles, and winter. Only the page title marks them. This is a **defect** for a reference page people come back to (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: pages should be self-evident and scannable). The checklist line on chunking passes because the page is short. The problem is findability, not length. Fix: give each topic a short heading that says what it's about. Pull the key numbers (how often, how long, the last boat) onto their own lines or into a small table near the top, so the answer sits where the eye already lands.

**Smaller things.** None. The checklist found nothing else wrong.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing does the hierarchy on its own. A bold sans heading over a regular serif body makes two clear levels with no rules, boxes, or color, and the two faces differ plainly enough not to clash. Keep that contrast when you add section headings. Use the heading face at a step between title and body, not a third family.
- The reading setting is right. The body looks about 20 px, with lines of roughly 70 characters and generous leading, near-black on white, ragged right with a soft rag. Paragraphs are separated by space, not indents. It's comfortable to read, and it should stay that way as the content grows.
- One shared left edge (x≈120) runs from the title to the last line, so the page holds together without any boxes.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let one number lead. "Every forty minutes" is the fact that defines this service. Setting it as the first thing under the title, larger and in the heading face, would make the page answer "how often?" before anything else. That is the question most passengers arrive with. (Taste. Refactoring UI argues for emphasizing the one piece of data the user came for.)
2. Use the empty right side for the timetable. The right ~40% of the frame is open, and the piece's own last line points to a printed timetable. A compact table of departures in that column, using the operator's real times, would put the shelter notice on the page. The prose would stay as the explanation beside it. (Taste: a reference page earns its space by putting the lookup next to the reading.)
