**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, then drops to the tall orange Friday and Saturday bars. It reads straight away as a small bakery's shop dashboard. It's calm and tidy, and it feels trustworthy until you try to read the numbers. Then it stalls: 3.8 what? 64.20 in which currency? 12.4 subscribers can't be right, so is it thousands? And over what period?

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: the bakery's owner or staff open this to see at a glance how the shop is doing and whether anything needs attention.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are clean and well made. But the three numbers at the top, the reason this page exists, can't be read with confidence. None has a unit or a time period, and none is compared with anything. Until that's fixed, the page looks informative but doesn't let anyone make a decision.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three headline numbers carry no unit, no period and no comparison** (top row of cards). *Defect.*
   - "3.8" is probably a percent. "64.20" is money in an unstated currency. "12.4" is the worst: "Active subscribers" at 12.4 only makes sense as thousands, or as some rate, so the viewer has to guess the scale of the business's most important figure.
   - None of them says what period it covers: today, September, or all time. The chart below says September 2026, so the viewer will probably assume the cards do too, and may be wrong.
   - None is compared with anything. Even with units, the owner can't tell whether 3.8% conversion is good news or a problem.
   - The fix (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"): put the unit, the time period, and a comparison, such as the change from last period or a target, next to each headline number. Write the units in full where they hold the meaning ("3.8%", the currency on 64.20, and "12.4k", or the actual count, on subscribers). Put the period once, where all three cards clearly share it. The comparison is a fact only you have, so pick the one you actually judge the shop by.

2. **The chart's title names the topic but not the finding** ("Orders by delivery day"). *Taste and common sense; the source in my reference isn't on my approved list.* The chart shows a clear pattern: orders climb through the week, peak on Saturday, then fall sharply on Sunday. The viewer has to work that out from seven bars. If the title or a one-line caption says what you want the owner to take from it, the chart becomes a reason to act, for example on staffing or baking volumes, rather than just a picture. You know which conclusion matters, so write that line yourself rather than let the reader infer it.

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honestly built.** Bars start at zero, the gridlines are light and evenly spaced, the days are in calendar order, there's one color for one series, and the source line names both the system and the month (Tufte, Few). Keep all of that when you revise.
- **One accent color, used with restraint.** The burnt orange appears only in the wordmark and the bars. That ties the data to the brand, and everything else stays neutral.
- **A clear, simple structure.** A page title, a row of three equal cards and one wide panel all sit on the same left edge and gutters, so the page reads in one sweep. Making the three cards equal is the right call for peer metrics.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let the comparison carry the color. Once each card has its change from last period, show only that change in the existing orange (or in a muted tone when it's flat), and keep the big number in near-black. The owner's eye would then go straight to what moved, which is what they open the overview for. *Taste.*
2. The Sunday bar is the one outlier on the page. A light annotation on that bar alone, rather than on every bar, would point to the one day that behaves differently without cluttering the chart (Tufte's case for putting words on the data). *Taste.*
