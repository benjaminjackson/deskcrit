**First read.** My eye goes straight to the three big numbers at the top, "3.8", "64.20" and "12.4", and then drops to the orange bars, where Saturday is the tallest. It reads as the admin dashboard for a small bakery's online shop. It feels calm and tidy, but the top row stops me: I can't tell what any of those numbers counts, and "12.4" active subscribers isn't a possible number of people.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's overview screen, for checking how the shop is doing at a glance and seeing which delivery days are busiest.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the chart is honest. But the three numbers that get the most weight on the screen can't be read, because they have no units, no time period and nothing to compare against. Until they do, the top half of the dashboard looks important and tells the owner nothing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three big numbers have no units, so the owner can't read them.** This is a defect. It's in the top row of cards. "Checkout conversion rate 3.8" is probably a percentage, but it doesn't say so. "Average order value 64.20" has no currency. "Active subscribers 12.4" is the worst: a count of people can't be 12.4, so it's either thousands with the "k" dropped or a different metric with the wrong label. The owner might read it as 12 people or as 12,400, and those lead to very different decisions. These are the largest, heaviest things on the page and the first place the eye lands, so the whole dashboard opens on a guess. Fix: put the unit in the value or right next to it (%, the currency, k), and make sure the subscriber label matches what is actually counted.
2. **None of the numbers says what period it covers or whether it's good.** This is a convention. A bare 3.8% (if that's what it is) can't be judged: is it up, down, on target? The cards also don't say which period they cover. The chart's source line says September 2026, but nothing ties the cards to that month. Few lists the bare headline number as a classic dashboard pitfall ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Fix: next to each number, give the period and one comparison, such as the change from last period or a target. Each card has plenty of empty space on the right for a small change figure.

**Smaller things.**
- Chart title: "Orders by delivery day" names the topic, not what the chart shows. The bars show a clear pattern: orders build through the week, peak on Saturday and drop sharply on Sunday. A title or caption that says this in words would save the owner from reading it off the bars. This is common sense; no approved source.

**What's working.**
- The chart is plain in a good way. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, there's one brand color, the days run in their natural order and a source line gives the month. Keep that restraint when you add things to the cards.
- The layout follows one grid. The three cards and the chart panel share the same left and right edges and the same gaps, and the current page ("Overview") is shown in bold in the top navigation. It reads as one finished screen.
- The type scale is clear: page title, card labels, big numbers, chart title, then small axis and source text. There are no near-duplicate sizes.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: once the cards have comparisons, let one number lead. If conversion is the measure the owner acts on, it should outrank the other two instead of all three sitting at the same weight. On a screen the owner checks every morning, an obvious lead number answers "how are we doing?" in one look. (Taste.)
2. The Saturday peak and the Sunday drop in the chart are the screen's most useful insight for planning bakes and staff. Marking those two bars, for example by graying the other days or adding a short note, would turn the chart from a report into a planning prompt. (Taste; Tufte's case for putting the ink on the data that matters.)
