**First read.** The eye lands first on the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4. Then it drops to the orange bars and catches the Saturday peak and the Sunday dip. This reads as an admin dashboard for a small online bakery. It feels calm, tidy and well built. Then you try to read the numbers and can't: 3.8 of what, 64.20 in what currency, and 12.4 subscribers can't be right.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an at-a-glance health check that the bakery owner opens to see how the shop is doing and when orders land.

**Verdict.** The layout is sound and the chart is honest, but the top row doesn't do its job. The three headline numbers have no unit, no time period and nothing to compare them with. The most prominent things on the screen are the ones the owner can't interpret.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers are missing their units, and 12.4 can be read wrongly.** This is in all three cards in the top row. "3.8" is presumably a percent, "64.20" is a price with no currency sign, and "12.4" next to "Active subscribers" reads as twelve and a bit people. It is almost certainly 12.4 thousand, or maybe something else. The viewer has to supply the missing unit, and for subscribers the likely guess is off by a factor of a thousand. The values are the biggest type on the page, so this is the first thing anyone takes away. *Defect.* Fix: put the unit back with each value (%, the currency symbol, "k" or the full count). It should read as part of the number, maybe a step lighter, but never left out (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").
2. **The KPIs have no time period and no comparison, so the owner can't tell good from bad.** This is in the same three cards. The chart's source line says September 2026, but the cards don't say whether they cover that month, today, or all time. None of them shows a change from the last period or a target either. A 3.8% conversion rate might be great or terrible, and the screen can't say which, so the owner either remembers last month's figure or learns nothing. *Defect / convention.* Fix: give each card its period and one comparison, such as the change from the previous period or a target (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Nielsen's heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Which comparison matters is the owner's call.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Orders by delivery day" names the topic, not the finding. Saturday is the busiest day and Sunday the quietest, and a title or short note could say so in words, so the owner doesn't have to find it in the bars. This is common sense, since no approved source states it.
- The y axis (0–1,200) has no unit. The title implies "orders", but the axis should say it, especially because it looks like a monthly total and not a daily average. That distinction is worth stating (Tufte, ch. 2, on clear labeling).
- There is no visible control for changing the period on the dashboard or the chart. An owner who wants to compare this month with last has nowhere to go (Nielsen's heuristic 7). If a picker exists off-screen, ignore this.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the weekdays run in their natural order rather than sorted by value, the gridlines are faint, and there is one brand color with no legend. Keep all of that.
- The grid is clean. The cards and the chart panel share the same left and right edges and the same gutters, so the page reads as one system at a glance.
- Color is restrained. The burnt orange of the wordmark is the only hue, and it is used only for the brand and the data, so the bars read as the bakery's own.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: once the cards have a comparison, let the change carry a direction (up or down, with its sign) in plain neutral type, without red and green. The owner should be able to scan the row in one pass and know whether this month was better or worse. Taste. Glanceability is the whole point of the top row of a dashboard (Few).
2. The Saturday bar is the story of the chart, and Sunday is the counterpoint. Keep the other days gray, or a muted tint of the orange, and give only Saturday (and maybe Sunday) the full orange. The chart would then say "this is your big day" before anyone reads the axis. Taste. It turns the chart from a record into an answer, which is what an owner planning baking and delivery would come for (Tufte's emphasis on data that speaks; Refactoring UI on using color for emphasis).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Contrast of the gray axis labels and source line. They look like they pass, but measure them against 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3).
- Focus states on the top navigation.
