**First read.** The eye goes to the dark serif headline first, then drops into the row of four illustrated cards and runs across them: window, bucket, T-shirt, plant. It reads as a local home-services company's landing page for homeowners who want to hand off chores. It feels calm, tidy and friendly. It also feels generic: swap the logo and it could be any cleaning startup's page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the home page of a home-care service. It needs to get a visitor to pick a service and book it, so the prices and the path to booking are what count.

**Verdict.** It's competently made and easy to read, but it stops short of what it's for. The subhead says "Pick a service, choose a day", yet nothing on the page lets you pick a service. The prices don't say what they buy. Those two gaps sit right at the point where a visitor decides.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cards are a dead end** (defect). The subhead tells the visitor to "Pick a service". The four cards are the services, but they show no sign that you can click them: no button, no link, no arrow, no "Book" label. The eye travels headline → card → "From $89" and stops there. The only way forward is "Book a visit" in the top-right corner, which is far from where the decision was just made and doesn't carry the chosen service with it. A visitor who has just settled on window cleaning has to guess whether the card is clickable or go back up to the header. Each card needs a visible way to act on that service, at the point where the eye stops, beside the price. Then the card itself becomes the "pick a service" step the subhead promises (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, on signifiers; Arnheim, on an eye path with no dead ends).
2. **The prices have no unit** (defect). "From $89", "From $189", "From $24" and "From $65" never say what they cover. Is $24 per load, per bag or per week? Is $189 for a deep clean of any home, or of a studio? Is $65 per visit or per month? These are the numbers a visitor will remember and later hold you to. Someone who reads $189 as the price for a whole house and gets a quote for more will feel misled. Add the unit or the basis to each price (per visit, per load, for a home of a stated size). Only you know those facts.
3. **The headline's promise clashes with the laundry card** (defect, in the copy). "On your schedule" is the page's main claim, but the laundry card says "We collect on Monday and bring it back folded on Wednesday". That is their schedule, not the visitor's. A careful reader catches the contradiction in the second row of text they read, and it undercuts the headline. Either make the headline claim only what is true of every service, or make the laundry line show what choice the customer actually has. The headline is also the kind of line that could sit on any competitor's page. It names no place, no particular strength and nothing a visitor couldn't get elsewhere. What's missing is the one fact that sets Fernway apart, such as where it works or what "vetted" means here.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks "on your / schedule" in the middle of its own phrase. It's held to a narrow width, so either widen it so it fits on one line, or break it after "house," (Bringhurst, on line breaks).

**What's working.**
- The four illustrations share one flat style, palette and level of detail, and each one reads in a glance. Keep them as a set. They do more to tell the services apart than the titles do.
- Each card has a steady inner layout: art, then a bold title, then two lines of description, then a teal price, with titles, text and prices on the same lines across all four. That makes comparing the services easy.
- The type plan is simple and clear: a serif for display (the wordmark and the headline) and a plain sans for everything else. Together with the navy, teal and coral on cream, it gives the page a warm, trustworthy mood that fits a service people let into their homes.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let the teal of the prices also carry the per-card action, so that within each card the price and the action read as one unit. The navy fill would stay for the single main "Book a visit" button. That keeps one primary action on the page while making every card something you can act on (taste).
2. "Vetted crew arrives with their own supplies" is the only line that speaks to a homeowner's real worry: who is coming into my house. It is currently the quietest text on the page. Give it more weight and back it with whatever proof you have about how crews are vetted. That fact is yours to supply (taste).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The empty cream band below the cards could be where the capture ends or real empty space. Check in a browser what comes next on the page.
- Whether the cards are links already, and what the hover and focus states look like.
