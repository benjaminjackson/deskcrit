**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", the only heavy, large thing on the page, then drops straight into three short gray-black paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for passengers of a small river ferry. It feels calm, orderly, and quiet, close to a well-set notice, and it has almost no personality.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells first-time ferry passengers what they need to know to catch the boat: when it runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bicycle.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and nearly faultless, but the page doesn't do its job. A passenger who reads it still can't plan a trip, because none of the times it gives are real times, and the one line that could help sends them to the landing to find out.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule has no times anyone can act on.** This is a defect. Paragraph 1 says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", and paragraph 3 says "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour." A stranger can't tell from this when the first boat leaves, when the last one leaves, which months count as "winter", or which minutes past the hour the boats go. Times are exactly what a viewer acts on and later holds the operator to, and a vague line here can mean someone misses the last boat. The page then says "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing," so the only route to the real schedule is to be standing at the landing already. That's the opposite of "before you board." To fix it, put the facts only the operator has into the existing sentences: first and last departure times, the dates or months the winter timetable runs, and ideally the departure minutes. Or link to the timetable from that last sentence.
2. **Three kinds of fact are buried in one stream of prose.** This is taste. Frequency, crossing time, bicycle rules, and winter changes all sit in the same paragraph style, so someone checking the page on their phone at the quay has to reread full sentences to find the one number they want. The writing is good and the paragraphs are short, so this ranks below finding 1. But once real times go in (finding 1), they should be easy to find at a glance: in a short list or small table, or at least with the numbers leading their sentences. Don't leave them mid-sentence.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines look about 80–85 characters long (e.g. "between early morning and late evening. Each crossing takes about twelve minutes in"). That's past Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Narrow the text column by roughly a tenth to land near 66 characters.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear: a small, letterspaced gray "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow, a big bold heading, then body text. That's three distinct levels with clear jumps between them, and weight and color do the fine distinctions. Keep this scale when you add content.
- The spacing is disciplined. There's one left edge for everything, the gap between paragraphs is clearly bigger than the line spacing, and the heading sits closer to the text it introduces than to the top of the page.
- The copy reads like a person talking: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail" says who does what, and when, in plain words. Keep that voice when the times go in.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Once the page has real departure times, let one of them lead, such as the frequency or the next departure, set larger than the body as a single standout fact under "Before you board." For a page whose whole job is getting people onto a boat, the time is the one thing to own. (Taste.)
2. The page is entirely neutral gray and black, so nothing marks it as this ferry's page and not a generic help page. If the service has its own color or mark, giving it to the "PASSENGER NOTES" eyebrow would be enough to place the reader without adding noise. That's a choice for the maker, who knows whether such a color exists. (Taste.)

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is where most passengers would read this at the landing. Check that the line length and the heading size hold up at narrow widths.
