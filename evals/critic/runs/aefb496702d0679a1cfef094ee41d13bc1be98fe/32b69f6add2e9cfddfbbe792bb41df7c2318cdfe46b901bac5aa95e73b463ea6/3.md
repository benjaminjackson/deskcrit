**First read.** The eye goes to the three big numbers along the top (184,220, 3,912, 2.12%), then drops into the large chart below them. The chart shows three colored lines rising gently, but nothing says which channel each line is, what the values are, or which weeks they cover. It reads as a tidy, generic SaaS analytics overview: calm and competent, until you try to get an answer out of the chart.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an internal growth/marketing dashboard. A team checks it to see traffic, sign-ups and conversion over the last 12 weeks, and which channels bring the visits.

**Verdict.** The top row works. The chart, which takes over half the screen, doesn't, because it can't be read. It has no channel names, no values and no dates, so the one question its title asks ("Visits by channel") goes unanswered.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart has no labels at all.** Defect. The "Visits by channel" card shows blue, orange and green lines with faint gridlines. There are no y-axis values, no x-axis dates or week numbers, and no legend or series names. A viewer can see that one channel is biggest and all three are rising, but not which channel that is, how many visits it brings, or when the dip in week 8 happened. Because the y axis has no numbers, they also can't tell whether it starts at zero, so "orange is about twice green" may or may not be true. Tufte and Few both require that a chart never leave the reader guessing what a mark measures. The empty strip below the baseline and the blank left gutter are where those labels belong, so the card looks unfinished rather than minimal.
   Fix: add y-axis values with a unit, week labels along the x axis, and a channel name at the right end of each line. Labels on the lines are better than a legend (Tufte; Wilke), and the space at the right end of the lines is free.
2. **The channels are told apart by color alone.** Defect, convention (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1). Even with a legend, matching blue/orange/green to names would rely only on hue. The orange and green lines look close in lightness, so a viewer with red-green color blindness could mix them up. The direct labels from finding 1 fix this too, because each line then carries its own name.
3. **The chart title describes instead of telling.** Taste, backed by convention (Tufte, Few on stating the takeaway). "Visits by channel" names a category, and the viewer has to work out what the lines mean for them. The KPI cards already say something specific ("+6.1% vs previous 12 weeks"), while the chart, the largest element on the page, says nothing. Once the lines are labeled, a title or subtitle that states what the chart shows would make the dashboard readable at a glance. Which channel grew, and by how much, is something only you know, so don't guess at it.

**Smaller things.**
- "Last 12 weeks" (top right): if this is the date-range control, it looks like a plain label, with no border, chevron or underline. Give it the look of a picker, or confirm it's only a caption.

**What's working.**
- The KPI row: label, then big number, then comparison delta. That's a clean three-level hierarchy, and every number comes with a comparison to the previous period, which is exactly what a headline metric needs (Few). Keep it.
- "+0.1 pts" on the conversion rate is the right unit for a change in a percentage. It avoids the "+0.1%" ambiguity, and since this is a number people will quote, that care matters.
- The layout is calm and holds together: one grid, cards that line up with each other, an even gutter, and light gridlines that stay behind the data.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the chart's lines carry the color system across the page. Taste. If each channel's color also appeared next to a breakdown of the Visits figure in the first card, the top row and the chart would read as one story rather than two separate widgets. The breakdown figures would have to come from your data.
2. The page is three equal cards over a chart, the template every analytics product ships, so nothing says what this team cares about most. Taste. If one metric is the goal, probably sign-ups or conversion, give that card more weight or size, so the eye starts where the decision is (Tschichold: show order of importance through difference of size).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the chart has hover tooltips with values. Even if it does, the static chart should still be readable without them.
- Focus states on the date-range control, if it is one.
