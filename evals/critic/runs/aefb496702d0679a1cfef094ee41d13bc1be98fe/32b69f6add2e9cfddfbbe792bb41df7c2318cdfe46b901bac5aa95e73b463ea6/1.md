**First read.** The eye goes to the three big numbers along the top (184,220 / 3,912 / 2.12%), reads them left to right, then drops into the large chart below. That's where it stalls. Three colored lines climb gently, but nothing on the chart says what any of them is, how big they are, or which weeks they cover. It reads as a clean, calm, fairly generic SaaS analytics overview whose top strip works and whose main panel says nothing.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal or customer-facing analytics dashboard. A marketer or founder opens it to see how traffic and sign-ups did over the last 12 weeks and which channels drove the change.

**Verdict.** The top strip works. The chart takes up about two-thirds of the screen and tells the viewer nothing they can use, because it has no series labels, no axis values and no dates. As shipped, "Visits by channel" is decoration. Either fix it or drop it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart is unlabeled, so it can't be read.** This is a defect. In the "Visits by channel" panel, the blue, orange and green lines have no legend and no direct labels, and the y-axis has gridlines but no values. The x-axis has 12 points but no week or date labels. The viewer can't tell which channel is which, whether blue is twice orange or 10% above it, or which week the dip at the 8th point (blue) falls in. The chart is the only place on the page that answers "why did visits go up 6.1%?", and it can't answer it. Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2) and Few both require labeled axes with units, so the viewer never has to guess what a number measures.

   There's an empty band about 70 px wide to the left of the plot and another below it, which looks like space reserved for labels that never rendered. If the labels are in the design and failed to load or render in this capture, check that in a live browser. If they aren't in the design, add them. Either way, this screenshot shows a chart with no information in it. Fix: put the values on the y-axis (visits per week), the week dates on the x-axis, and a channel name at the right end of each line.

2. **Channels are told apart by hue alone.** This is a convention (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1, *Use of Color*). Even once a legend exists, a viewer with a color-vision deficiency may struggle to match orange and green to their entries. Labeling each line directly at its right end, which Tufte and Wilke recommend over a legend, fixes this and finding 1 together. The viewer reads the name where the line is and never has to look back and forth.

3. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding.** This is taste; the source in the catalog is off the approved list. "Visits by channel" makes the viewer work out the story themselves. The cards above already state their change ("+6.1% vs previous 12 weeks"). The chart should do the same: say in the title or a short caption which channel drove that 6.1%, using the real figures only the maker has. This matters less than finding 1, but it turns the panel from a picture into an answer.

**Smaller things.**
- "Last 12 weeks" in the top right sits in the header's plain body style, with no border, chevron or underline. If it's the date-range control, it doesn't look clickable. Give it a button or dropdown shape. If it's only a label, it's fine as is.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards.** Each has one label, one large number and one comparison line, with a clear size step between the three. Every number comes with its previous-period context. Keep this structure.
- **"+0.1 pts" on the conversion rate.** Writing "pts" for percentage points instead of "%" is exactly the precision that stops a viewer misreading a rate change.
- **Restraint.** One sans family, a neutral gray-and-white base, hue kept for the data, and faint gridlines. Nothing competes with the numbers.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: tie the 6.1% visits change on the first card to the chart. The cards and the chart are the same story told twice, and nothing links them. A stronger version makes it obvious that the chart explains the first card, for example by putting the visits card and its chart in one panel or matching their styling. Then the viewer reads the number and the reason for it as one thing. This is taste.
2. The page is competent but could belong to any product. Nothing here says "Brightpath" beyond the wordmark. The blue line already gives the brand an obvious hue to own, for example on the active date range or the headline number's delta. That would make it a product someone recognizes rather than a template. This is taste.
