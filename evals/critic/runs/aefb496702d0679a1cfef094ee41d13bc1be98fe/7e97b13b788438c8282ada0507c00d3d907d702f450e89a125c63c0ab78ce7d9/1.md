**First read.** The eye goes straight to the large dark-blue 38% wedge, then up to the serif title, then across to the legend to find out what 38% is. It reads as a cost-breakdown page from a company's annual report: calm and nicely typeset. The upper-left of the pie breaks that calm. Nine thin slivers in nine colors, with tiny labels on leader lines, look busy and take work to read.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one page of a printed or PDF annual report. It should show shareholders and staff where the operating money went and which costs dominate.

**Verdict.** The page is competent and nearly there. The chart form is wrong for 14 categories, and the title only names the topic. Readers have to work out from the legend what the chart should tell them at a glance.

**What would move it most**

1. **The pie can't carry 14 slices, so the reader reads the legend instead of the chart.** *(Convention, with a real cost.)*
   - **Where:** Nine of the slices are 1–4% slivers, stacked in the upper-left from 10 to 12 o'clock.
   - **What the reader meets:** No slice carries its name, so every category means a trip to the legend and a color match. The colors don't hold up for that.
     - Travel red, Training brown and Office-supplies pink are close.
     - So are Insurance green and Bank-fees olive.
     - So are Rent teal and Equipment-repair light teal.
     - For a color-blind reader several of these pairs merge.
   - **The result:** The legend on the right is already the real chart, a sorted table of shares. The pie mostly duplicates it less precisely.
   - **Why:** Pies only work for about five or fewer slices (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1). Labels belong at the data, not in a legend (Tufte; Wilke). Fourteen categorical hues is far past what a reader can match by color. That last point is common sense: you can't reliably pair 14 swatches to 14 wedges.
   - **Fix:** Show the shares as one sorted horizontal bar chart, with names and values on the bars, in one color. Or keep the pie with the four or five largest slices and group everything at 4% and under into one "Other" slice, broken out in the table.

2. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** *(Taste / common sense.)*
   - **Where:** The title and subtitle at the top of the page.
   - **What the reader meets:** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" is a label. The chart's message is in the piece's own numbers: salaries and rent are 54% together, and the top four lines are 75%. The reader has to add that up themselves.
   - **Fix:** Have the title or subtitle state the point the maker wants taken from this page, built from the figures already shown. The current wording can move into the subtitle, which already does the describing well.

3. **No comparison, so no share can be judged.** *(Convention.)*
   - **Where:** Every percentage on the page.
   - **What the reader meets:** This is an annual report, but every figure stands alone. A shareholder can't tell whether 12% for cloud hosting is up, down or planned.
   - **Why:** A figure needs a comparison, such as the previous period or a target (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2).
   - **Fix:** If fiscal 2025 figures exist, put the prior-year share or the change next to each line in the table. That data is the maker's to supply.

**Smaller things.**
- **White labels inside the wedges:** the bold "12%" on orange, and to a lesser degree "16%" on teal, look close to or under 3:1. Measure them. If they fail, darken those two fills or set those labels in dark type.
- **The outside labels at the top of the pie:** "2% 2% 2% 2% 1%" are crowded and their leader lines are hard to trace to a slice. This goes away with finding 1. Until then, give them more room or drop them, since the table already has the values.

**What's working.**
- **The type pairing and the quiet page.** A strong serif title over a plain sans for the table and notes, on a warm off-white. The section label "4.2 COSTS" and "Section 4 of 9" place the page in the report without competing. Keep this voice.
- **The table.** It's sorted from largest to smallest, the bold percentages are right-aligned, and it has hairline rules. It's the most readable thing on the page, and it's the base for any revision.
- **The source line.** It gives the period and the rounding note, and the shares sum to exactly 100. That honesty matters in a financial report.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Let the 38% lead.** *Builds on finding 1.* When the slices become bars, color only Salaries and benefits in the dark blue and set the rest in one gray. The page is about where the money goes, and one color pointing at the dominant cost makes that point before anyone reads a number. *Taste.* Tufte argues for spending ink only where it carries data, and a single emphasis color does that.
2. **Use the unused right column for one sentence.** The table stops at about 1060 px, but the header runs to 1216 px, leaving an empty strip beside it. A short note there could tie the dominant costs to what the reader should conclude, in the maker's words. It would turn a reference page into an argued page, which suits an annual report. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this is printed or read on screen. If it ends up as an accessible PDF or web page, the chart needs a text alternative. The table could serve as that alternative if it's tagged as one.
