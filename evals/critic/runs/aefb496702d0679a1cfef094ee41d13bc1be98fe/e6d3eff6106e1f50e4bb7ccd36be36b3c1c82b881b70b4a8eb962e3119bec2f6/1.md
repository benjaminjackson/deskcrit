**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, drops through the gray byline, and settles into the body text without effort. It reads at once as a long-form feature on a small independent magazine site: calm, clean, a bit anonymous. The one thing that catches is the single word "line" sitting alone on the headline's second line. The other is that the credit "Photographs by the residents" promises pictures that aren't on the first screen.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the opening screen of a feature article on an editorial site. Its job is to pull a general reader into a long story and keep them reading comfortably.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the type is plain and readable, and the opening copy is genuinely good. What holds it back is craft at the two places a reader meets first: the headline breaks badly, and the body lines run a little long for sustained reading.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline breaks in the wrong place.** This is a convention issue. The break comes inside "bus / line", which leaves "line" stranded alone under a full first line. It splits the one phrase that gives the title its picture, and the headline looks lopsided: a heavy line, then a runt. This is the largest type on the page, so every reader sees it. Breaking at phrase boundaries is standard practice (Bringhurst, §2.4; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*), though the reference notes neither states it for headings specifically. Fix: control the break so the lines split at a phrase ("The allotment at the end / of the bus line"). Use a non-breaking space in "bus line", balanced wrapping (`text-wrap: balance`), or a slightly narrower headline box.
2. **The body lines are a bit too long for a long read.** This is a convention issue. Lines in the 720 px column run about 80 to 85 characters. The first line, "Ten years ago … Today forty", is about 83. Bringhurst's comfortable range is 45 to 75, with 66 as the ideal (§2.1.2). Over a few paragraphs it's fine. Over a full feature, the return sweep to the next line gets slightly harder, and readers tire sooner. Fix: narrow the text column to about 620 to 660 px at this size, or raise the body size a step. Measure the characters per line on the live page to confirm.

**Smaller things.**
- The nav at top right ("Features · Food · Places · Subscribe") doesn't mark where the reader is. This is a Features piece, but "Features" looks like its siblings. Give the current section one cue: bolder weight, an underline, or the kicker's green (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- The headline looks set with the same letter spacing as the text, so at this size Inter Bold reads slightly loose. Tighten the tracking a little (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 80; Bringhurst, §2.1.1). Lupton cautions against overdoing negative tracking.

**What's working.**
- **The opening copy.** "Ten years ago the plot behind the last stop was a car park nobody used" is concrete and specific. It ends on "We spent a season with the people who built it," which tells the reader exactly what they're about to get. The short third paragraph ("So the first beds were pallets and old bath tubs.") is a good rhythm break. Keep the paragraphs this short.
- **A clear, quiet hierarchy.** The small green "Feature" kicker, the dominant headline, the gray byline, and the body all share one left edge at the column. Each level differs in size, weight, or color, so nothing competes with the headline.
- **Restraint in color.** It's near-black, grays, and one green that quietly ties to the garden subject. Don't add more.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Open on one of the residents' photographs** (taste). The byline already credits "Photographs by the residents", which is the most distinctive thing about the piece. Right now the reader meets a text page that could be any magazine's. A strong lead image above or beside the headline would make the story's subject and its community authorship visible in the first second. The photograph is the maker's to choose.
2. **Give the headline a face with more character than the UI type** (taste). Everything here, from the nav to the headline to the body, is one neutral sans, so the page feels like a template rather than a magazine with a voice. A headline face with some warmth, paired in clear contrast with the body sans, would give "Groundwork" an identity (Bringhurst on type in concord or clear contrast). It would also suit a story about people building something by hand.
