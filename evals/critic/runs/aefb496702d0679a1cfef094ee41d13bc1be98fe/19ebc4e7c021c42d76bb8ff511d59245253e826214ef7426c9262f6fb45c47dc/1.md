**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. Six tiles of the same size sit in a 3×2 grid. The mustard Glasshouse tile and the brick-red Rose arch tile pull hardest, so the eye flicks between them and then wanders. It reads as a gallery index for a garden show, but every "photo" is a flat color field with a pale circle cut off at the bottom: a placeholder, not a picture. Under the grid, a line says the gallery has "a large feature tile" as its focal point, and it plainly doesn't. It feels tidy and calm, but empty, like a wireframe.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web gallery page that lets visitors to a garden show browse its areas and pick one to look at.

**Verdict.** As a gallery it doesn't work yet: there are no pictures, and nothing leads. The biggest problem is that all six tiles are placeholders, so the page can't show what a gallery exists to show. Next is that no tile dominates, which is exactly what the page's own closing line claims it does.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures are all placeholders (defect).** All six tiles are a flat color with a centered pale circle. A visitor can't see a rose arch, a pond edge or a glasshouse. The only real content is the captions, so the gallery is a list of names. The tiles also make up most of the page's color: six unrelated saturated hues covering about half the area. That noise comes from the stand-ins, not from any decision. The circles are cut flat by the bottom edge of each tile, which looks accidental (common sense; no approved source). Fix: put in the real photos, and judge the palette and the crops again once they're in. If this capture missed images that do exist, check the live page and treat this finding as being about the capture.
2. **No tile leads, and the page says one does (defect).** Six tiles of the same size, with captions of the same size, compete to be seen first. The eye gets no starting point and no path through them, so browsing means scanning all six equally. Tschichold asks that the order of importance be shown by differences of size (*The New Typography*). The closing line, "The large feature tile gives this gallery a clear focal point," makes it worse: it describes a layout that isn't there, so a reader looks for a feature tile, can't find it, and stops trusting the copy. Fix: make one area clearly the first thing seen, for example by giving it a double-width or double-height tile, and set the rest back by size. If the design is meant to stay an even grid, cut that line. Either way, the copy has to match what's on the page.

**Smaller things.**
- The Rose arch title and caption sit about 2 to 4 px higher than Herb bed and Pond edge in the same row, so the baselines miss each other. It reads as an error, not a choice (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on near-miss placement). Match its spacing under the image to the other five.
- The same tile breaks the pattern the other five set (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind share one style and spacing). The fix is the same alignment fix.

**What's working.**
- The grid is clean. The three columns have equal gutters, and the left edges of the heading, tiles, captions and footer line all line up, so the page feels ordered even though nothing leads.
- The caption pairs are well grouped: a bold name sits tight under its tile, with a plain gray line below it, and the rows are separated by much more space. Each tile and its words read as one unit. Keep that spacing.
- The descriptions are short and concrete ("Thyme, sage and chives.", "Tomatoes in late July.") and tell a visitor what they'll actually see. Keep that voice.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the "Garden show gallery" heading do more than label the page. It's currently the same weight as the tile names and only a little larger. A stronger version gives the page title a clear step up, so the page has a top before the tiles begin. This is taste, but it serves browsing: the visitor knows where they are before choosing where to go.
2. The descriptions do more work than the layout gives them credit for. "Tomatoes in late July" carries a time, so if the show has times or seasons for each area, those could become a quiet, consistent detail line on every tile. That content would have to come from you. This is taste: it would turn a list of names into a reason to visit each one.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the tiles are links, and what hover and focus look like on them. That matters, since picking an area is the page's job.
- Whether the grid drops to fewer columns on a narrow screen.
