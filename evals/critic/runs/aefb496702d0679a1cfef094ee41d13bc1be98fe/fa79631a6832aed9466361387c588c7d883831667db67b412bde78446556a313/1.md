**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the grouping is right and the copy is plain and usable. What holds it back is structure. The first step (where to put the bed) has no heading, so the subheads that a scanning reader relies on skip a step. Beyond that, the lines run a bit long.

**What would move it most**
1. **The first step has no heading, so the outline is missing a step.** The paragraph under the title ("Choose a spot… Mark the corners…") is a real step, the same kind as "Preparing the soil" and "Watering." But it is set as an intro, and nothing marks it. The blue subheads are what the eye follows on this page, and they read soil, planting, watering. Someone who comes back to check "how much sun did it need?" won't find it from the subheads. The order also matters here (site, then soil, then sowing), and nothing on the page says it's a sequence. Direction: give the site step its own heading at the same level as the other three, so the four subheads cover the whole job. *Defect* (mild): it slows the return visit, which is exactly how a how-to gets used.

**Smaller things.**
- Body line length: the paragraphs run about 88 to 92 characters per line (for example "Dig to the depth of a spade… Rake the surface level"), above Bringhurst's 45 to 75. With two-line paragraphs the cost is small, but narrow the column to roughly 65 characters. There's plenty of empty space to the right, so nothing else has to move.
- Copy, "six hours of sun": a reader will most likely read this as six hours a day of direct sun, but it says neither "a day" nor "direct". It's a number the reader acts on when picking a spot, so add the unit: "six hours of sun a day."

**What's working.**
- Spacing groups the content: each subhead sits about 8 px above its own text and about 30 px below the text before it, so every heading clearly belongs to what follows. Keep that ratio.
- The type scale is clear: three sizes (about 48 / 30 / 17 px by eye) with big steps between them, plus weight and color on the headings. Every level has an obvious job, and nothing is near another size.
- The copy is concrete and speaks to the reader. "Start with things that forgive mistakes: radishes, lettuce, and bush beans" names the cases instead of saying "easy crops."

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: number the steps. The sequence is the whole point of a bed-planning guide, and numbers turn the page from an article into a procedure the reader can follow with dirty hands and a phone. *Taste.*
2. The blue: the one hue on the page is the generic web-link blue, so the guide looks like any help page and nothing about it says garden. Keep a single accent for the headings, but choose one that suits the subject and still passes contrast. Itten's point is that a color's character should serve the message. *Taste.*
3. "Watering" is one line, while the other sections give a reason or a method. If the guide is for beginners, the missing content is how to tell the watering worked, for example how deep the water should reach. That's a fact only you can supply. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone. At narrow widths the line-length problem goes away, but check that the 48 px title doesn't break awkwardly.
