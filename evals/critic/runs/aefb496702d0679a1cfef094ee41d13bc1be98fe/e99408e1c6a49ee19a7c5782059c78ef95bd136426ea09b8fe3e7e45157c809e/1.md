**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", then gets caught in a heavy black cage. Every cell sits in its own 2 px near-black box, so the grid is as loud as the times. You have to work to read across a row. It reads as a plain, honest public timetable, but a stiff one, closer to a spreadsheet print-out than to something made for a rider standing at a landing.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a rider checks when the next ferry leaves north landing, and whether it runs on the day they're traveling. The page might be posted at the landing or put online.

**Verdict.** It works: the times are correct, legible and in order. Two things hold it back. The ruling is as dark as the data, which slows every lookup. And the "Weekdays" and "Weekends" exceptions, the one thing that can strand a rider, look exactly like "Daily".

**What would move it most**
1. **The grid outweighs the data.** *Convention.* Every cell is boxed in near-black rules about as heavy as the text strokes. So the table reads as 32 separate compartments, not 7 departures, and the eye has to step over a vertical bar to get from "Leaves north" to "Arrives quay" on the same row. The rules split each row, which is the unit a rider reads, into four boxes. The repeated "Morning / Morning" and "Midday / Midday" labels add to the noise: each label repeats in its own boxed cell, so the pairs never read as groups. Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 157: table rules "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell". Tufte (data-ink) and Wilke make the same case. Direction: remove the vertical rules. Make the horizontal dividers thin and pale gray, or replace them with space or a pale tone on alternate rows, so the times are the darkest marks on the page. Let each period label appear once for its group, or show the periods with space between the groups.

2. **The service exceptions are hidden in plain text.** *Defect.* In the far-right "Runs" column, "Daily", "Weekdays" and "Weekends" all have the same weight and color, and they sit about 600 px from the departure time they qualify. A Saturday rider scanning the times sees 12:40 and 17:10 with nothing to warn them. The evening rider has the reverse problem: 19:30 runs only at weekends. Those are the departures where a misread means a missed or nonexistent boat, which makes this the costliest ambiguity on the page. Direction: make the exceptions stand out from "Daily". Use weight or a mark, not color alone. Or bring the run-days next to the departure time. "Daily" is the default and can recede.

**Smaller things**
- Times in both time columns use proportional figures. "11:00" and "11:12" are visibly narrower than "06:40", so the colons don't line up down the column. Switch to tabular figures (Inter has them, `tnum`) so the times scan as a column (Bringhurst 4.4.3).

**What's working**
- The title and subtitle are a clean two-level pair: bold, large and near-black over a smaller gray line. The subtitle states the route and season, which is exactly the context a rider needs before reading a single time. Keep it.
- One sans face, one neutral palette, generous margins, rows in time order. Nothing competes with the content except the rules.
- The column headings "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are plain verbs in the rider's own words, not "Dep." and "Arr." codes.

**Where it could go further**
1. *Taste.* Let the departure time lead. "Leaves north" is the column a rider acts on. Setting it a step bolder or darker than "Arrives quay" would make the page answer "when do I need to be there?" at a glance. That suits a sheet read in a hurry at a landing.
2. *Taste.* Builds on finding 1. Once the cage is gone, size the columns to their content. Today the two time columns are each about 300 px wide for five-character values, which pushes "Runs" far from its time. A narrower table keeps each row together for the eye.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the table is real HTML table markup with header cells, so a screen reader can announce each column, and how it reflows on a phone if it's online.
