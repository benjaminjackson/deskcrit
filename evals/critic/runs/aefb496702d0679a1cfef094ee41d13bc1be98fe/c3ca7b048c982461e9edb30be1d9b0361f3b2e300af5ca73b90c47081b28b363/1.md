**First read.** Your eye goes straight to "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops to the paragraph, skims the three short lines and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. Within two seconds it reads as the hero of a small local ferry's website, for day-trippers and people crossing the harbor. It feels calm, tidy and quiet, and also anonymous: apart from the words, nothing on the screen is a ferry, a bay or a harbor.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing hero for a passenger ferry's website, meant to get locals and visitors to check sailing times and take the boat.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The order is clear, there's one action, and the headline is good. The biggest problem is in the copy: it promises a timetable but holds back the facts a traveler needs to decide, so the reader has to click before they can tell if the boat suits them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out what a traveler acts on.** The paragraph says boats run "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" and leave "the north landing". It never says where the boat goes, when the first and last sailings are, or what a ticket costs. Only bicycles get a price ("travel free"). Someone deciding whether they can get back tonight needs the last sailing, and "late evening" could mean 8 pm or 11 pm. If they guess wrong, they're stranded on the far side. "Cross the bay" also names no destination, so a stranger can't tell if this is their crossing. This is a defect, and common sense rather than a published rule. The fix is to put the facts the maker already has (the destination landing, the first and last sailing times, the fare) into the paragraph or the three short lines. Then "See the timetable" is for planning a trip rather than for finding out whether the boat is any use.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "twelve" ends line one and "minutes" starts line two, which splits the headline's one number from its unit. Break before "twelve" ("Cross the bay in / twelve calm minutes") so the promise reads as one piece. This is a convention (Bringhurst, 2.4.6).
- Type sizes: I count five sizes (brand about 14, list about 18, button about 18, paragraph about 20, headline about 56). The paragraph and the three short lines are only a couple of pixels apart, so they read as one size set slightly wrong rather than two levels. Put them at the same size, or separate them by weight or color, not by 2 px (Bringhurst, 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"; Refactoring UI).

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete, sensory promise ("twelve calm minutes") that sets this ferry apart and lands on the reader. Keep it as the dominant element.
- There's one primary action in one strong color, and the blue matches the "Coastal Ferry Co." wordmark. The palette is just ink, white and one blue, so the button is the only thing pulling at the eye after the headline.
- Centering works here because every block is short: a two-line headline, a three-line paragraph and three short phrases. It gives the screen the calm the headline promises. Keep it this short if it stays centered.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the "twelve minutes" number lead. It's the one figure on the page and the piece's best argument, so set it apart, for example in the wordmark's blue, so a reader skimming catches "twelve minutes" before anything else. Taste.
2. The page shows nothing of the crossing it sells: no water, no boat, no deck. A photo of the open deck or the harbor would turn the paragraph's "watch the harbour slide past" into something the reader can see, and that is the reason to choose a ferry over the road around the bay. The maker would need to supply the picture. Taste.
3. Treat the three short lines ("Bicycles travel free", "Seats on the open deck", "Hot drinks on board") as a real list with a visible marker or a row layout. As loose centered lines they read like captions that lost their image, and the free-bicycle offer is a concrete reason to travel that deserves to be seen. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover and focus states. The large empty band below the button could be the end of the hero or a section that hadn't loaded; scroll the live page to check.
