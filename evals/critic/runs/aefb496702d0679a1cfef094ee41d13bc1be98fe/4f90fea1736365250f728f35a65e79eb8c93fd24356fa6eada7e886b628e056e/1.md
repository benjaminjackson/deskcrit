**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes," then drops to the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads at once as the hero of a small ferry operator's website, aimed at locals and day-trippers. It feels calm, plain and competent. Then the eye hits the last line, "Design review: failed, everything on this page is centred," and the page stops feeling finished.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the desktop landing hero for a local ferry service, and its job is to get visitors to check departure times and take the boat.

**Verdict.** The core works: one clear headline, one clear action, and a clean left-aligned column. What's in the way is that it isn't ready to ship. A stray internal note sits on the page, and the right half of the screen is empty, so the layout looks like it's missing its picture.

**What would move it most**
1. **A leftover review note is printed on the page.** It's the line under the button. Defect. Any customer who reads it sees the word "failed" on the ferry company's own homepage, which undercuts the calm, trustworthy tone the headline sets up. The note is also wrong: every block on the page is set flush left, so nothing is centered. I'm treating the line as content, not as a judgment of the design. The fix is to remove it. This is common sense; no catalog entry covers it.
2. **The page tips to the left, and the right half reads as a missing image.** All the content sits in a column from about x=160 to x=790, and the roughly 490 px to its right is empty from top to bottom. Defect, judged against the balance principle (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). On a 1280 px screen this doesn't read as deliberate white space. It reads as a hero image that failed to load or was never placed. It also wastes the one thing that would sell "calm minutes": showing the crossing. If an image is meant to go there, check that it loads. If not, either give the right side something with weight, or narrow and re-center the frame so the empty space looks chosen.
3. **The timing copy is too vague to act on.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" is the line people will plan around, and "early morning" and "late evening" leave them guessing whether a 6:30 boat or a 22:00 return exists. Defect, because it's schedule information the viewer acts on. The button does send them to the timetable, but naming the actual first and last departures here would answer the most common question without a click. Only you have those times.

**Smaller things.**
- The three feature lines (Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board) look about 18 px against roughly 20 px body text, in the same face and weight. They read as a near-miss rather than a separate level (Bringhurst, 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). Set them at body size, or set them clearly apart, for example with markers or by weight.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete promise ("twelve calm minutes") at a size that clearly dominates, with tight two-line leading that holds it together as one unit. Keep both the number and the scale.
- There's one blue, used three ways: the small brand label, the button fill and nothing else. That gives the action an obvious color and keeps the button the only loud thing on the page.
- "See the timetable" names exactly what you get, and it is the logical next step for someone deciding whether to take the boat.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2. Let a picture of the actual crossing carry "calm" (the open deck, the harbour from the water) so that the twelve-minute promise is something visitors can see. Taste: this page sells a feeling, and right now only the words carry it. The photo has to come from you.
2. Let "twelve" lead. The number is the most distinctive claim on the page, and it's buried mid-headline at the same weight as everything around it. Taste: a viewer comparing the ferry with driving around the bay judges it by that number. The usual move is to make the number stand out, for example in the brand blue, while keeping it inside the sentence.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The button's hover and focus states.
- How the left column and the empty right half reflow at narrower widths.
