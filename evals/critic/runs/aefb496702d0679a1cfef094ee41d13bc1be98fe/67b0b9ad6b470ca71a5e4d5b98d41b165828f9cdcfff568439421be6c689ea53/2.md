**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, "Trial to paid conversion by plan," then drops to the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%, then wanders across the other three labels. It reads as a clean internal analytics page, probably for a growth or product team. It feels calm, careful and trustworthy. It also feels flat: four purple bars of about the same height, and nothing tells me whether that is good news, bad news or no news.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a report page for people inside the company who decide on pricing or onboarding. Its job is to show how each plan's trials convert to paid accounts and whether any plan stands out.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid and the chart is honest. As a report it stops short: it shows four rates between 3.1% and 4.0% and never says what they mean. There is no takeaway, no earlier period to compare against, and nothing to tell a real gap from noise. A reader can see the numbers but can't act on them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The smallest plan's rate is drawn as solidly as the largest, with no sign of how sure it is.** Look at the Enterprise bar next to the table. Its 3.4% rests on 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. Starter's 3.1% rests on 286 out of 9,210. From the table's own counts, a rough check puts the Enterprise rate at about ±1 point. Starter is closer to ±0.4. So the chart's order of Growth, then Team, then Enterprise, then Starter is partly noise, and the bars present it as a firm ranking. Someone deciding on pricing could act on a 0.2-point gap between Enterprise and Starter that the data can't support. *Defect* (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16: show uncertainty and sample size). Fix: put a range on each bar, or at least note the paid count under each plan label, so the thin sample is visible where the eye compares.
2. **There is nothing to judge the rates against.** Each bar is a lone number. The reader can't tell whether 3.1% for Starter is up, down, on target or worrying, so the page records the numbers without answering a question. *Convention* (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": put a comparison next to each key number, such as the last period or a target). Fix: add the previous quarter or a target for each plan, as a faint marker on each bar or as a column in the table. The maker has to supply that data.
3. **The title names the topic but not the finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" and its subtitle say what was measured, not what it showed. Every reader has to work out the point alone, and different readers will reach different points. *Taste*, because no approved source states this rule: a report page earns its place by saying what the reader should take away. Fix: once items 1 and 2 are settled, write the title or a one-line caption as the finding the data actually supports. Leave the subtitle as the method line it is now.

**Smaller things.**
- Layout balance: the right column ends at the Definition paragraph, about halfway down. The chart runs to the source line, so the lower right is an empty block and the page leans left (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Either end the chart and side column at the same depth, or move something with real weight into that space, such as the comparison from item 2.
- Table: it's titled "Trials by plan" but also holds Paid, and it has no rate column. A reader who checks a bar against the table has to do the division. A conversion column, or a title that names both counts, would tie the table to the chart.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and plain. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are pale, the value labels sit on the bars and the plans are in tier order, so nothing is distorted or decorated. Keep this restraint when you add ranges or comparisons.
- The method is stated in the open. The subtitle, the Definition block and the source line say exactly what counts as converted, the 30-day window and the date range. That is why the page reads as trustworthy.
- The table's raw counts make the chart checkable. Every rate matches its counts, and the counts are what expose the sample-size issue. Keep them next to the chart.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let the Paid column lead. The paid counts (286, 185, 118, 38) are this page's most revealing numbers, because they show how much evidence sits behind each bar. Setting them in the chart's purple, or bringing them under the plan names, would make the strength of the evidence part of the first read. *Taste.*
2. Use the empty lower right for the decision this page is meant to inform, such as a short note on what the team should look at next. The maker has to write it, and it should only say what the data shows. A report for decision-makers is stronger when it ends with a next step and not a blank. *Taste.*
