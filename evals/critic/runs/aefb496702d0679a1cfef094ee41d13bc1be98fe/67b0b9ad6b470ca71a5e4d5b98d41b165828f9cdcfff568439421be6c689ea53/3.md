**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Trial to paid conversion by plan", drops to the purple bars, and stops on the tallest one, Growth at 4.0%. Then it crosses to the small table on the right. It reads as a tidy internal analytics page for a growth or product team. It feels calm and competent, and also anonymous: nothing on the page says what the numbers mean or whether they are good.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an internal dashboard view that lets a growth or product team compare Q3 trial-to-paid conversion across the four plans and decide where to act.

**Verdict.** It's well built and honest, but it only reports numbers without interpreting them. The page shows four rates that sit within 0.9 points of each other, with no prior period or target and no sign of how much the gaps can be trusted. The viewer can read every number and still not know whether anything here calls for action.

**What would move it most**

1. **Nothing to compare the rates against.** In the chart and the subtitle, each bar is a bare rate for one quarter. 3.1%, 3.6%, 4.0%, and 3.4% could be a record quarter or a collapse; the page can't say. The only comparison it offers is plan against plan, and that spread is small. So the viewer's one possible conclusion ("Growth is best") is also the least useful one. *Defect* (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2: a metric needs a comparison such as a target or the previous period). Direction: put a reference next to each bar, such as last quarter's rate or the target. A pale tick or a ghost bar works, and so does a "vs Q2" delta in the value label. The page already shows the period and unit, so this is the missing third piece.

2. **The chart presents small gaps as if they were firm.** The table shows that the rates rest on few conversions: Enterprise's 3.4% is 38 paid accounts, Growth's 4.0% is 118. Worked roughly from the page's own counts, Enterprise could plausibly be anywhere from about 2.3% to 4.5%. It overlaps every other plan, and only the Growth vs. Starter gap clearly holds up. The chart shows four solid bars with one-decimal labels, which invites a ranking the data mostly can't support. Someone could reprice or re-target Enterprise based on noise. *Defect* (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16: show samples with their uncertainty). Direction: add error bars or interval whiskers to the bars, or at least a note that the Enterprise rate rests on 38 conversions. The counts are already in the table, but they sit in a separate column, so the viewer has to make the link alone.

3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" says what the chart is. The viewer still has to work out what it shows. A dashboard viewer who glances will take away "purple bars, about 3–4%" and nothing more. *Taste / common sense* (the source in the catalog isn't on the approved list): once findings 1 and 2 are settled, the title or subtitle should say in words what the chart shows. Use the maker's own conclusion, built only from what the data supports. For example, say which plan converts best and whether that changed from last quarter.

**Smaller things.**
- Table at right: it has Trials and Paid but no rate column, while the chart has rates but no counts. The viewer has to check them against each other by eye. Either add the rate to the table or put n under each bar label. Taste.
- Definition, "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted": this is clear only if the viewer works out that a trial starting 30 September hits its 30-day limit on 30 October. "Still open" can also be read as "still in trial" or "account not closed". A short edit that says what "open" means would remove the doubt.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are pale, values sit directly on the bars, and one hue is used with no legend. Keep this restraint when adding comparison marks; they should be quieter than the bars.
- The context is complete and well placed: the subtitle gives the population and window, the source line gives the dates, and the Definition block says what counts as conversion. Most dashboards skip this, and it's what makes the numbers trustworthy.
- The nav shows the current section clearly. "Conversion" is both bolder and colored, so it doesn't rely on color alone.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: make the change the main element, not the level. If the maker has the prior-quarter rates, the purple bars could become the "now" against a gray "then". The chart's question then becomes "what moved", which is what a growth team acts on. Taste.
2. Use the empty lower right, under Definition, to state the conclusion, in one or two sentences from the maker about what the team should take from this quarter. The column has room, and the page currently has no voice. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Keyboard focus on the nav tabs, and whether the bars or table rows have hover detail that already shows intervals or prior values.
