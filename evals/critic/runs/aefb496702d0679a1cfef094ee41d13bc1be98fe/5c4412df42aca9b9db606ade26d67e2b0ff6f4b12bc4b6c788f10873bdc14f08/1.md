**First read.** The eye lands on "Pricing," moves to the three big prices, and reads $9, $29, $79 across as a clean ladder. Then it hits the bottom of the cards and stops, because there's nothing to press. It reads as a calendar and booking tool for small teams. It feels calm, tidy and interchangeable with a hundred other SaaS pricing pages.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the pricing page of a booking and calendar SaaS, and its job is to get a small-team buyer to pick a plan and sign up.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The page explains the prices well, but nothing on it lets you choose one. The cards have no buttons, the only action is a generic "Start free" in the header that none of the plans mention, and the price unit is ambiguous. A buyer who has decided still has to guess what to click and what they'll be charged.

**What would move it most**

1. **No way to choose a plan from the cards.** All three cards end after their feature lists. The viewer decides "Team looks right" and then has nowhere to act, so the path dead-ends at the card bottoms. The only button is "Start free," far up in the top-right corner. Pricing tables normally put an action on every plan, and buyers expect it (Nielsen, Jakob's law: departures from a known pattern need a clear gain, and here there is none). **Defect.** Fix: give each card its own action at the same spot at the bottom of the card, phrased around that plan, and make one of them the primary style (see 2).

2. **Nothing tells the viewer which plan to start with.** The three cards are identical in size, border and weight, and "Pricing" competes with the three prices. The eye compares, but nothing helps it settle. Tschichold and Hofmann both ask for one element clearly first; here three equal things compete. **Convention, and it costs something here:** a buyer with no guidance either takes the cheapest plan or leaves to think about it. Fix: if one plan is the one you want most teams on, set it apart through its border, its button style or a label, and let the other two step back. Only you know which plan that is.

3. **The price and the free start can each be read two ways.** "$29 / month" could mean per team or per user. For a team plan that's a big gap, and the viewer will hold you to whichever reading they took. "Start free" names no plan, no length and no card requirement, and none of the three plans is free. So the viewer can't tell whether "free" means a trial of Team or a free tier that isn't listed. **Defect:** prices are what buyers remember and act on (Nielsen 2, the audience's own terms; Nielsen 6, everything needed to act is visible). Fix: state the unit outright ("per user" or "for the whole team," whichever is true), and say on this page what starting free gets the viewer. That fact only you have.

4. **The copy could sit on anyone's page.** "Simple plans for teams of every size" says nothing a competitor's page doesn't. The feature lists also don't show how the plans stack: does Team include the Booking page and Email reminders? Does Business include Reports? The viewer has to infer that each tier includes the one below, and may infer wrong. **Defect.** Fix: make inclusion explicit with something like "Everything in Solo, plus…," which is an edit of what's already there. Swap the generic subtitle for whatever makes Tidewell different; that's a claim you have to supply. Today nothing compares the plans with what the buyer uses now, so the viewer is asked to take the value on trust.

**Smaller things.**
- The bottom third of the screen (below about y 570) is empty leftover space, not a deliberate pause. It's where the actions, a plan comparison or a free-start note would go. Once those are added, the page stops looking top-heavy.
- The subtitle breaks to leave "you like." alone on its second line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rebalance it into two even lines, or let it fit on one.

**What's working.**
- The prices are the right size and weight against "/ month" and the plan names, so the $9, $29, $79 ladder reads in one glance. Keep that contrast.
- The three cards share one grid, equal gutters and matching baselines for name, price and first feature, so comparing across is effortless. Keep that alignment when you add buttons.
- The palette is restrained: one blue, used only for the brand, the active-nav underline and the single button. That leaves the blue free to mark whatever you want chosen.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: commit the existing blue to the decision. Today it sits on the logo and the header button. A stronger version would use that same blue only on the recommended plan's action, so the one saturated thing in the page body says "start here." *Taste*: restraint makes a single accent do the pointing (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*).
2. Let the plan names say who each plan is for. "Solo / Team / Business" are categories. A short line under each name, using whatever audience the plan really serves, would let the viewer recognize themselves instead of reading three feature lists. *Taste*: it speeds the choice, which is what this page is for. The audience facts are yours to supply.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether there's a monthly/annual toggle, more content below the fold, or hover states on the cards. If plan actions appear on hover, that's still a problem for touch and keyboard users. Check it in a live browser.
