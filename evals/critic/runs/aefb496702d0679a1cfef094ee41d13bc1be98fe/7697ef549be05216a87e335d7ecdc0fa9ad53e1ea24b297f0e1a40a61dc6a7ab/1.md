**What it's for.** My assumption: a short web article that walks a beginner through setting up a first vegetable bed, read on a laptop screen.

**Verdict.** It does the job. The page is quiet and well ordered, with clear levels and good spacing. Two things hold it back: an internal note was published as if it were article text, and the paragraphs run too wide for comfortable reading. The headings get by on size alone, which is why the page looks like a default stylesheet.

**What would move it most**
1. **An internal note is published as page content.** The line at the foot, "Design review: approved, a model of clear heading styles", is set in the same body style as the gardening advice, so it reads as part of the article. A reader who reaches it feels like they've seen something they weren't meant to, and they start to wonder what else on the page is unfinished. It also does nothing for someone planning a bed. This is a **defect** (common sense: copy that isn't finished, and an element with no job for the reader, per Nielsen's heuristic on aesthetic and minimalist design). Remove it before the page ships. I treated what the note says about the design as text on the page, not as evidence about the design.
2. **The paragraphs run too wide.** The text column runs from about x=160 to x=950, and the longest lines are about 90 characters ("Dig to the depth of a spade and mix in compost from last year's heap. Rake the surface level"). On two-line paragraphs the cost is small. Once a section grows past a few lines, the eye starts to lose its place going back to the start of the next line. This is a **convention** (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2: 45 to 75 characters, about 66 ideal). Narrow the text column to about 66 characters at this body size, or raise the body size and keep the column. Count characters per line on the live page to confirm.
3. **The headings differ from the body only in size.** "Preparing the soil", "Choosing what to plant", and "Watering" use the same face, regular weight, and near-black color as the body text, just larger (about 32 px against about 18 px). At this size the regular weight looks thinner than the body text. The levels can still be told apart, but the headings look pale, and the page reads as an unstyled default rather than as a designed article. This is a **convention** (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8–9). Pair a smaller step in size with a change of weight, such as a semibold, or with a change of color, so that size isn't doing all the work.

**Smaller things.**
- Straight apostrophe in "last year's" (second paragraph). Use the curly apostrophe. Common sense: the reference source doesn't confirm a rule on curly quotes, and at body size it's minor.

**What's working.**
- **The spacing groups each heading with its section.** Each heading sits about 5 px above its paragraph and about 30 px below the paragraph before it, so each section reads as one unit at a glance. Keep that ratio when you add weight to the headings.
- **The type scale is clear.** Three sizes (title, heading, body), each a big step from the next, and no two sizes close enough to look like a mistake.
- **The copy is good: concrete and plain.** "Six hours of sun", "the depth of a spade", "twice a week, rather than a little every day" each tell the reader exactly what to do and when, with nothing to puzzle out.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: once the headings have their own weight, consider tightening the spacing of the title "Planning a vegetable bed" a little, so it reads as a display line and not as body text blown up. This is taste. The page's whole job is to look trustworthy and easy, and a title set with care signals that the advice was put together with care too.
2. The advice is a sequence (choose a spot, prepare the soil, plant, water), but nothing on the page says so. Making that order visible, for example by numbering the sections, would tell a beginner they can follow it step by step. This is taste. It uses only the order the piece already has.
