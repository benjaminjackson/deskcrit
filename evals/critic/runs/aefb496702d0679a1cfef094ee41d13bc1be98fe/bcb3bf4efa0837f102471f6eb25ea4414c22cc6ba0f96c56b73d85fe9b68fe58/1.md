**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then drops through the paragraph and the three short lines to the blue button. Within a second this reads as a ferry company's landing page for local riders and day-trippers. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy. Then the eye reaches the last line, "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge." It stops the reader cold: it isn't about ferries, and it describes a page that is visibly centered.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a small ferry operator's home page. Its job is to make a casual rider want to take the boat and send them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The core works. It has one clear headline, one clear action, and the copy makes a promise you can picture. But a stray sentence about the design sits where the page should end, and it makes the whole thing look unfinished. Delete that line and this is a competent page that mostly needs more practical facts.

**What would move it most**
1. **The last line is leftover design commentary, and it isn't true** (defect). "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge" sits below the button. It's set at the same size and color as the intro paragraph, so it reads as part of the offer. A rider reads it as a mistake: it talks about the page instead of the ferry, and every block above it is centered, so it contradicts what they can see. It's also the last thing on the page, so the visit ends on a doubt and not on the button. It looks like a note or placeholder that shipped. Fix: delete it. If something has to sit under the button, it should be about the crossing.
2. **The page sells the trip but leaves out the facts a rider plans around** (defect, argued as common sense). "Every forty minutes" is concrete, but "from early morning until late evening" gives no first or last boat. A rider can't tell from it whether they'll make the 7 o'clock commute or get home after dinner. The page also doesn't say what a ticket costs or where you get one, and "the north landing" assumes you already know which landing that is. The button sends people to the timetable, which helps. But these are the lines a rider acts on, and as written they make everyone click through just to learn whether the boat fits their day. Fix: replace the vague span with the real first and last departure times, and add the kind of fact only you have (fare, where to buy, where the landing is) if it fits. Keep the button for the full schedule.
3. **The text below the headline has three sizes that are almost the same** (convention). The paragraph looks about 20 px, the three short lines about 18 px, and the stray last line about 20 px again. The step from paragraph to list is too small to read as a different level, so the list looks like a slightly smaller paragraph, not a set of perks. That weakens "Bicycles travel free", which may be the strongest selling point here. Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* 3.1.1: work from "a modest set of distinct and related intervals." Fix: either make the list the same size as the paragraph and set it apart with weight, a marker or spacing, or make it clearly smaller. Bolder or lighter, not a couple of pixels bigger or smaller (Refactoring UI).

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "twelve / calm minutes", which separates the number from what it counts. The promise reads in two pieces. Rebreak it so "twelve calm minutes" stays together on the second line, for example "Cross the bay in / twelve calm minutes".

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a specific promise: a number, a feeling and a place. "Twelve calm minutes" is something you can picture, and few competitors could say the same. Keep the number in the headline.
- There is exactly one action, and its label says exactly what you'll get. "See the timetable" sets the right expectation, and it's the only saturated thing on the page, so it's easy to find.
- The paragraph speaks to the rider in their own terms: "bring a bicycle, a pram or a picnic, and watch the harbour slide past." It covers who the boat is for and what the trip feels like in one breath.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: put "Bicycles travel free" first among the perks and give it the most weight. The page is for people deciding whether to take the boat, and free bikes is the perk that changes how they make the trip. Leading with it turns a list of extras into a reason to go (taste).
2. Let "twelve" lead the headline visually, with a color or weight change on the number using the brand blue that's already on the page. The page sells a short, easy crossing, and that number is the claim a rider will remember and compare against the drive around the bay (taste).
3. The page has no picture. A photo of the open deck or the harbour, which the copy already describes, would turn "calm" from a claim into something the rider can see. That's content only you have, so it's yours to supply (taste).
