**First read.** The eye goes straight to the dark band of cells in the middle of the grid, Monday to Friday, mid-morning. Then it jumps to the large "3,412" on the right and back to the title. Within two seconds it reads as a support-desk report: tickets pile up on weekday mornings and drop off at night and on weekends. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a support lead uses this Reports page to see when tickets arrive so they can plan staffing, with three headline numbers to check against last month.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made chart that does its job, and it is close to finished. The one real problem is that the chart and the "Busiest hour" figure seem to disagree. That is the number a staffing decision would rest on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart and the "Busiest hour" card disagree** (defect). The card on the right says "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00". In the grid, the darkest cells (30+) are on Monday and Friday in the 11:00 column, the one just left of the "12:00" tick. Tuesday to Thursday show 25–29 in both the 10:00 and 11:00 columns. So to the eye, 11:00 is the busiest weekday hour.
   - With values grouped into 5-ticket bands, the card could still be right if the 10:00 cells sit at the top of their band. But the viewer can't check that. What they see is a page that contradicts itself on the fact they came for.
   - The cost: a lead who trusts the card staffs for 10:00. A lead who trusts the chart staffs for 11:00. Either way, they stop trusting one of them.
   - "10:00" also doesn't say whether it means the hour starting at 10:00, or say how the column labels map to hours.
   - Fix: first check the data and the column alignment. If the card is right, make the chart show it, for example by marking the busiest column. Then write the card as a span, such as "10:00–11:00", so the label and the column read the same way.

**Smaller things.**
- **Date range doesn't match.** The subtitle says "1 to 28 September" but the first card says "New tickets, September". The reader can't tell whether 3,412 covers 28 days or the full month, or whether "Up 4% on August" compares equal periods. Make both say the same period (common sense).
- **Title names the topic, not the finding** (taste). "When new tickets arrive" tells you the subject. The right-hand card already says what the chart shows, so this costs little. Still, a title that states the weekday-morning peak would let the chart tell its story without the side column.

**What's working.**
- **The single-blue ramp, light to dark, with equal bands.** It's the right choice for counts. The busy hours jump out, the empty hours fall back to near-background, and the pattern reads the same in grayscale or for color-blind viewers. Keep it.
- **The subtitle and source line say exactly what is counted.** They give the time zone, that "hour" means when a ticket was opened rather than answered, all queues, and an average week. Those are the questions a careful reader would otherwise ask.
- **Each headline number has a comparison under it** ("Up 4% on August", "Down 6 min on August"). The figures mean something at a glance without a second chart.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 1.** Let the chart show the peak itself (taste). Pick out the busiest weekday column in the grid, for example by marking it under the hour axis. Then the "Busiest hour" card and the heatmap point at the same thing, and a lead planning staffing sees the peak where they are already looking.
2. **Use the weekend rows** (taste). Saturday and Sunday show a separate, much smaller late-morning bump. If this report is for staffing, a short note beside those rows saying what weekend cover the data supports would turn a pattern people notice into a decision. That note depends on the team's actual staffing, which only the maker knows.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether hovering a cell shows its exact count. The 5-ticket bands hide the difference that matters in finding 1, so an exact value on hover would settle it for the reader.
