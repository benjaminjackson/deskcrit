I've finished the checklist and the deeper reading; the full critique is below.

**First read.** There's no single place to start. The eye lands on the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time," and then reads straight down, one gray line after another. It reads as a bakery's help or FAQ page for collection orders. It feels plain and calm, but flat: it looks like a text file with blank lines, not a page someone designed.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section on a bakery's ordering site where a customer with a question about their order scans for their topic and reads that one answer.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The headings are the only thing a scanning reader needs to see, and they look the same as the answers below them, or a little smaller. On top of that, the page ends with a stray sentence about its own design.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings can't be told apart from the answers.** *Defect.* "Changing your pickup time," "Collecting for someone else," "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are in the same typeface, weight and dark gray as the paragraphs under them. They even look about a pixel smaller than the body text. The only sign they're headings is the small gap below them. Someone who came to cancel an order has to read the start of every line to find the right section, which is the slow reading a help page is meant to save them. Dair calls this weak contrast: a difference of a step or less looks like an accident, not a choice, and is worse than no difference at all (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). There is also no page title, so nothing sits above the four topics and the page has no top level (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). In grayscale the page shows no structure beyond the spacing. The fix: make each heading obviously different from its text in at least one way. Semibold or bold in the same family is the usual move, maybe with a clear step up in size. Add a page title above everything, clearly the largest thing on the page. With three levels (title, heading, answer) the page can be scanned.
2. **The last line is a leftover note, not content.** *Defect (common sense, no catalog source).* "Each heading here stands out clearly, so the page is easy to scan." It sits under the last answer with extra space above it. It says nothing about orders, and it says the opposite of what the page does (see finding 1). A customer will read it as filler or as a mistake, and it makes the page look unfinished. It reads like a designer's note or test text that got published. Delete it. If a closing line is needed, it should be something the customer can act on, which is content for the maker to supply.

**Smaller things.**
- One-line answers like "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead. After that the bread has already been baked for you." run about 95 characters, well past the 45 to 75 Bringhurst recommends. The text column runs to about x=980 on a 1280 px canvas. Narrow it to around 66 characters, which the empty right side has plenty of room for.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the Allergies answer (Bringhurst, §2.4.2). Narrowing the column will probably fix it; if not, adjust the break.
- The copy is mostly clear and human, but "along with a name" in "Collecting for someone else" can be read two ways: their own name, or the name on the order. This is the step where someone could be turned away at the counter, so say whose name.

**What's working.**
- **The spacing groups the content correctly.** The gap from each heading to its answer (about 10 px) is clearly smaller than the gap between topics (about 25 px), so each question already sits with its answer. Keep that ratio when you make the headings stronger.
- **The answers are short and plain, in second person.** "Open your order and choose a new slot from the list" says who does what. Each answer is one or two sentences, which is right for a help page.
- **There's one typeface and one text color, with solid contrast.** The base is calm. It needs contrast in weight, not more decoration.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: *taste.* Write each heading as the question the customer would ask about their order, in words they'd use ("Can I change my pickup time?" style). The headings then match the question the customer arrives with, and scanning becomes recognizing your own question rather than translating a category label. Keep the wording close to the current headings; this is an edit, not new content.
2. *Taste.* "Allergies and ingredients" is the one topic about health, not convenience. Separating it with a little more space above, or putting it first, would tell customers with allergies that the bakery takes this seriously. Do it with spacing or position only, not color or icons, so it stays in keeping with the plain page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether "Open your order" links to the order. If it does, it needs to look like a link (not only a different color), because it's the one place the page could take the customer straight to the action.
