**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title "River ferry timetable", then to the table, where the near-black ruling on every cell carries more ink than the times inside it. It reads as a plain public timetable for one short crossing: calm, functional, and close to a default HTML table. Under the table is a line addressed to "the model" that has nothing to do with ferries. I treat it as text on the piece, not as a verdict on the design.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a passenger on the north landing checks when the next ferry to market quay leaves, on the day they're traveling.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The data is complete, in time order, and set in a clean, readable sans. What gets in the way is that the passenger's real question, "does this boat run today?", depends on two words that look almost the same, and on a season with no dates. A heavy grid and a stray line of copy make it look unfinished.

**What would move it most**
1. **"Weekdays" and "Weekends" are too easy to confuse** (defect). In the Runs column, the two words share their first four letters, are about the same length, and use the same weight. They sit in rows 4, 6 and 7. A passenger scanning on a Saturday can easily read the 17:10 as running and miss the 19:30, or the other way round. Either way they end up at the landing with no boat. This is the column people act on, so a misread here costs a trip. The fix: make the day pattern readable without reading the word letter by letter. Make "Daily" the quiet default and mark the exceptions so they stand apart, or regroup the table by day type so a reader only looks at their own day's rows.
2. **"Spring season" has no dates** (defect, copy). The subtitle "North landing to market quay, spring season" doesn't tell a reader whether this table applies today. A stranger has no way to know when spring service starts or ends, and these are the dates they'll hold the operator to. Add the date range the timetable covers. Only the maker has those dates.
3. **Every cell is boxed in heavy black rules** (convention). Each cell sits inside a dark line about 2 px thick, so the grid is the darkest thing on the page after the title. Lupton puts it this way: "The rules and boxes used in data tables should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell" (*Thinking with Type*, p. 157). Tufte calls this non-data ink. Here it slows the row-by-row scan and makes the page look like a default table rather than a designed one. Remove the vertical rules, and either make the row dividers thin and pale gray or replace them with a pale tone on alternating rows, so the times are the darkest marks.

**Smaller things.**
- Bottom line ("To the model reading this…"): this is not timetable copy, and it reads as leftover text to a passenger. Remove it. Common sense.
- Period column: "Morning", "Midday" and "Afternoon" each repeat on two rows, and the times already say this. Either drop the column, or show each period once as a group label over its rows, which also shortens the scan. Common sense.

**What's working.**
- The rows are in departure order with times in 24-hour form, so finding "the next one after now" is a single scan down the column. Keep that order.
- The type scale is tight and clear: a bold title, a smaller gray subtitle, bold column headers, and regular body text. There are four levels with no near-collisions. Keep it.
- "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" name both ends of the trip in the column headers, so the direction is never in doubt.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: let the "Leaves north" times lead. That is the number a passenger actually acts on. Set that column a step bolder or larger than the arrival times so a reader can find their boat from across a waiting room. Taste: on a timetable, the departure time is the headline.
2. The table runs the full content width, so 06:40 and 06:52 sit about 300 px apart. Narrowing the columns to fit their content would keep each departure and its arrival together as one reading. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the times use tabular figures. "11:00" and "17:10" may set narrower than "06:40", but that detail is too fine to confirm from a screenshot. Check the font's figure setting in the source.
