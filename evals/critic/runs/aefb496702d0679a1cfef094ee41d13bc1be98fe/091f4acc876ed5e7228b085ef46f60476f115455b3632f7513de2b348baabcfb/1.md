**What would move it most**
1. **The photographs are credited but not shown.** The byline promises the residents' photographs. The first screen has none, and about 220 px of empty white sits under the third paragraph. A garden that used to be a car park is a story people want to see: forty households, beds made from pallets and bathtubs. Without a picture, the headline does all the work and the page could be any text post. *Defect, unless a picture failed to load in this capture (see below).* What to aim for: put one of the residents' photographs on the first screen, near the headline, so picture and headline make the case together. If the gap is a lazy-loaded image, make sure it holds its space and shows something at first paint.
2. **The headline leaves "line" alone on its second line.** The headline is the first thing anyone reads, and it breaks inside the phrase "bus line". The second line is one short word, so the headline looks unfinished and ragged on the most visible spot of the page. *Convention* (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.4.2, on a short word left alone on a last line). The fix: balance the break so it falls at a phrase boundary, for example by narrowing the headline's measure slightly or using balanced wrapping, so "the bus line" stays together.
3. **Body lines are too long.** The body paragraphs look like about 85 to 90 characters per line (for example, "the transport authority to ask whether the empty car park could be lent to residents"). That's past the 45 to 75 range, with about 66 the ideal (Bringhurst, 2.1.2). Over a long feature, the eye has more trouble finding the next line, and reading gets tiring. *Convention.* The fix: narrow the text column or raise the body size slightly until lines land around 65 to 70 characters. Measure the real count in a browser.

**What's working.**

- **The copy is specific and human.** The intro names what's grown there (beans, squash, herbs), and the body text gives names, a year, and "pallets and old bath tubs". The concrete details pull a reader in. Keep the plain, concrete voice.
- **Restraint with color.** Green appears only on the small "Feature" kicker, so it reads as a quiet label and nods to the subject without decorating it.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let one of the residents' photographs lead the page, full column width or wider, with a caption in the residents' own words. Their pictures are what makes this story different from a reporter's piece, so put them at the front. *Taste.*
2. The green of the "Feature" kicker is the page's only color, and it suits a garden story. A stronger version could use that one green, sparingly and consistently, for pull quotes or captions in the rest of the piece, so the story has a visual thread without adding a second color. *Taste.*
