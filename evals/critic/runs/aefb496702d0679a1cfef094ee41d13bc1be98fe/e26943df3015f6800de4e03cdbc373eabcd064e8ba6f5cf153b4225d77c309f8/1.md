**First read.** My eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Update the card that pays for your subscription", then down into the body text. Within a second it reads as a help-center article for a SaaS billing product. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. Nothing competes with the article, and nothing looks wrong at a glance.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help-center article for an account owner or billing admin who came here to change the card on file and wants to finish that in under a minute.

**Verdict.** The layout and typography are solid. The article doesn't do its job, though, because it never tells the reader how to update the card. It describes what happens around the change but gives no steps and no link to the place where the change is made. The one paragraph with real consequences, about a declined card, can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The article never gets to the task its title promises.** This is a defect. The headline says "Update the card…", and the first sentence says you can do it "from the billing page". That's the only direction anywhere on the page. "Billing page" isn't a link, and there are no numbered steps. The other four links go to related topics (billing history, roles, invoices, restoring a plan), not to the action itself. A reader who came here to change their card leaves knowing the rules but still has to find the billing page on their own. That's the exact hunt a help article exists to save them. This is common sense rather than a cited rule (the matching catalog entry, Tognazzini on anticipation, is not on the approved source list). The fix: make "billing page" a direct link, and add the steps as a short numbered list right under the headline, before the paragraphs about timing and permissions. The steps are content only you have, so I'm not writing them.
2. **The declined-card paragraph gives dates and a downgrade that can be read two ways.** This is a defect. "We try a declined card again after three days and after seven days" could mean day 3 and day 7 after the decline, or day 3 and then seven days after that (day 10). "If the card is still declined after the second attempt" could mean the second retry, or the second charge overall, which is the first retry. These two lines decide when someone's subscription drops to the free plan. A reader who picks the wrong reading will expect more time than they have and will hold you to it. "During this period" has the same problem, because the period is never defined. The fix: say every time from one fixed starting point, for example "3 days after the first decline", and name the attempt as "the second retry" or "the retry on day N". Then the downgrade date has only one possible reading.
3. **The navigation doesn't show where you are.** This is a convention (Nielsen's first heuristic, visibility of system status; Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist," NN/g). In the left sidebar, "Update a payment card" is drawn exactly like its siblings. In the top navigation, "Billing" looks the same as the other links. Someone who arrives from search can't see where this page sits or which neighbors are close by. That matters on a help site, where the next question ("Download invoices", "Refunds") is often the reason they stay. The fix: mark the current item in both menus with one clear cue, such as a bolder weight, a left bar or a light background.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length in the body column: the first line of the opening paragraph runs about 84 characters, and the others are similar. That's past the 45 to 75 range (Bringhurst, 2.1.2). Narrow the column a little so lines land near 66 characters.
- Naming across the page doesn't match. The breadcrumb says "Billing / Payment methods", but no sidebar group is called "Payment methods". The sidebar entry ("Update a payment card") is also worded differently from the headline. Use one name for each level so readers can build a correct map of the site (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2).
- There's no search in the header. On a help center, search is the main way people get in, so put a search field in the top bar (Nielsen's heuristic 7, flexibility and efficiency of use).

**What's working.**
- **The type hierarchy.** The large bold headline, the bold section heading at about 20 px and the regular body at about 17 px are three clearly separate levels in one family. The section heading sits closer to its own paragraph than to the one above, so the page scans by section. Keep this scale.
- **The links.** All four are blue, underlined and written as actions in plain sentences ("check your billing history", "restore your previous plan"). They are easy to spot, and each one says where it goes without the reader needing the surrounding text.
- **The alignment.** The logotype and the sidebar share a left edge, and the body column has one clean left edge. The page feels calm because nothing is fighting for attention.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the action lead. Put the "billing page" link right under the headline, as a clearly marked link or button, so the page answers "how do I do this?" before it answers "what happens when I do?". This is taste. The headline is already phrased as a task, so the page should open with the first move of that task.
2. Builds on finding 2: present the retry timeline as a short dated sequence, with the decline, each retry and the downgrade each on its own line from the same starting day, instead of one running sentence. This is taste. It's the part a worried reader rereads, and a sequence they can count down is easier to trust than a sentence they have to parse (Tufte's case for showing data plainly over describing it applies here in spirit).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the breadcrumb items are links. They look like plain gray text, so check this in a browser.
- Focus styles on the links and menu items.
