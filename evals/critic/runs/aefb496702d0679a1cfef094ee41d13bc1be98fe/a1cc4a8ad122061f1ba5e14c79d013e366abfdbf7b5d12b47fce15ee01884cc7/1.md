**First read.** The eye lands on the white serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops to the search field, then spreads across the 2×4 grid of topic cards. It reads as a help center home page for a software product called Harbor. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. It also looks like any SaaS help center: nothing on the screen tells you what Harbor is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the help center landing page for Harbor customers, and its job is to get someone with a problem to the right answer quickly, by search or by topic.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clean, the grid is tight, and there's one obvious action. What holds it back from being good is that the first screen only offers ways to look for answers and never puts a likely answer in front of you, and the copy is generic enough to belong to any product.

**What would move it most**
1. **The first screen sends people off to look but gives them nothing to read** (taste / common sense). Everything above the fold is a route: a search box and eight category doors. Someone arriving with "I can't sign in" or "where's my invoice" still has to type or guess a category, then pick again inside it. Help center visitors usually come in with a small set of repeat problems, and this page shows none of them. Under the search field or above the grid, add a short list of the most-asked questions or current known issues, as direct links. The maker has the data for which ones those are. The "Status" link in the nav hints that live issues matter to this audience, but it's a bare nav word that's easy to miss.
2. **"Orders and shipping" doesn't fit the product the other seven cards describe** (defect, unless Harbor really ships physical goods). Every other card describes a software workspace: "invite your team", "two-step sign-in", "Connect calendars, chat tools, and storage", "Fix sync problems". Then the top-right card says "Track a delivery or start a return". A first-time visitor has to stop and work out whether this is a software tool, a store, or both, and they can't form a clear picture of what Harbor is (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). If Harbor does sell hardware or physical plans, the card description should say what gets delivered so the link to the rest is visible. If it doesn't, the card is leftover template content and should go.

**Smaller things.**
- Six of the eight card descriptions end on one short word alone on the last line ("your team.", "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). Across a symmetric grid this ragged repetition is noticeable. Use a non-breaking space before the last word, or balanced wrapping, so the last lines carry two or more words (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- The search field's only label is its placeholder, and it has no magnifier icon or submit button. The headline above does give it context, but adding a search icon inside the field makes it read as search at a glance and keeps a cue after the placeholder clears (NN/g, placeholder-only labels).
- The words don't match: the subtitle and nav say "guides", the placeholder says "articles". Pick one word so people know search and "Guides" cover the same content (Nielsen heuristic 2). The subtitle also says "Browse a topic or search" while the page puts search first. Flip the order to match the layout.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear. The serif display headline on the navy band against Inter everywhere else gives one obvious place to start and one obvious action, the white search field. Keep that contrast between serif and sans, and between dark band and light page, when you add anything.
- The grid is strict. The logo, "Browse by topic", the card edges and the nav all hang off the same 64 px left and right margins, and the gutters are equal. That's why eight cards read as calm and not busy.
- The icon set is consistent: one stroke weight and one style, sitting at the same spot in every card. It helps scanning without competing with the titles.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Make the headline say something about Harbor (taste). "How can we help?" sits on thousands of support pages. The serif headline is the one place the page has a voice, and it could name the product or its customers' world so visitors know they're in the right place. The maker has to supply that angle. Done well, this makes the page feel like Harbor's and not a template.
2. Builds on finding 1: let the navy band carry the live state (taste). The band already has the most weight on the page. A single line in it showing current service status, or one known issue when there is one, would answer the most urgent visit ("is it broken or is it me?") before anyone searches.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether more content (popular articles, contact options) sits below the 800 px fold. If it does, finding 1 shrinks to "move some of it up".
- Hover and focus states on the cards and search field, and whether the whole card is the click target.
