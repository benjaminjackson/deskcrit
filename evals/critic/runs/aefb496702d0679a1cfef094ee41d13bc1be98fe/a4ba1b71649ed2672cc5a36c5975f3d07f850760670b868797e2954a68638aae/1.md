**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Shipments peak each third quarter", then drops to the row of chunky blue bars and goes straight to the tallest one, 705. It reads as a clean, competent board-deck slide from a mid-size outdoor-gear company. The type and layout feel current; the bars, with their bevelled tops and dark sides, feel like an older chart-wizard default.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one slide in a board sales review, meant to show the quarterly shipment pattern and where Q4 2026 is forecast to land, so directors can take in the point in seconds.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The headline states a finding, the chart backs it up, every bar is labeled, and the source is given. Two things get in the way: the 3D bars add noise and make the value edge ambiguous, and the forecast bar looks exactly like actual data, which matters most to a board.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast looks like a fact.** The rightmost bar (Q4 2026, 560) has the same solid blue, the same 3D treatment, and the same bold label as the seven actual quarters. The only sign that it's a projection is a parenthetical at the end of the subtitle and the source line in small gray type. A director scanning the chart, or someone seeing this slide quoted later, will read 560 as shipped units. If Q4 misses, the slide will look like it overstated. This is a **defect**: projections should look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Fix: give the Q4 2026 bar a different look, such as a lighter tint, an outline-only bar, or a hatch. Label it in place, for example by adding "forecast" to its value label or axis label. If the forecast has a range, show it as an error bar.
2. **The 3D bars are decoration that costs accuracy and coherence.** Each bar has a lighter top face and a darker side face that add depth with no data in it. The top face makes the value edge ambiguous: the 610 bar's front edge sits at about 610, but its back edge rises toward 630. The value labels float above the back edge, so the eye has to decide which edge to read. The extrusions also nearly double the blue ink. The bevelled look clashes with the flat, modern type everywhere else on the slide, so the chart looks pasted in from a different tool. This is a **convention** break that costs something here: no 3D without a third dimension of data (Wilke, ch. 26.1 "Avoid gratuitous 3D"; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2). The style mismatch is my **taste**. Fix (approved): "Draw the chart flat, with no depth, sides, or tilt, so that each value is read from one length." Flat bars will also match the rest of the slide.
3. **The headline names a pattern but not the news.** "Shipments peak each third quarter" rests on only two Q3s and tells the board something seasonal it probably already knows. The numbers already on the slide show something bigger: every 2026 quarter is above the same quarter of 2025 (455 vs 410, 590 vs 520, 705 vs 610, 560 vs 480 forecast). As written, the headline stops at describing the pattern and leaves directors to work out what it means for them. This is a **defect** in the copy. Direction: make the headline say what the board should take from the chart, whether that's the seasonality, the year-over-year rise, or both. The emphasis is the maker's call, but the line should land on the consequence rather than the shape. Also cut the repeated "Q4 2026" in the subtitle, for example "…eight quarters to Q4 2026, the last one forecast."

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- The headline states a finding instead of a topic ("Units shipped" would have been the lazy version), and the chart title and units line ("Units shipped by quarter / Thousands of units") clearly separate what is plotted from what it means.
- Labeling each bar with its value and fading the gridlines means nobody has to read values off the axis. Keep both when the bars go flat.
- The frame is disciplined: the brand mark at top left, the meeting context at top right, the source and page number on a hairline-ruled footer, all on one left margin. That makes the slide credible as one of a set of twelve.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: let the chart itself show the comparison the headline makes. If the point is year over year, pair each 2026 quarter with its 2025 counterpart, or tint the two Q3 bars so the peaks the headline mentions are marked on the chart. That way directors confirm the claim at a glance instead of hunting for it. (Taste.)
2. The plot area stops about 60 px short of the right margin that the header, rule, and footer all share. Taking the plot to the full width, and making use of the empty band below the footer, would give the bars more room and the slide a firmer edge. (Taste.)
