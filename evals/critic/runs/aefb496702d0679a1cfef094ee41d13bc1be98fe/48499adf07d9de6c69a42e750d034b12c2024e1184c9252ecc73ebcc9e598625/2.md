**First read.** My eye goes to the big blue headline, then gets pulled straight down to the four blue-outlined cards. They carry as much color as the button, so "Create your link" doesn't stand out the way it should. It reads as a tidy, generic SaaS landing page for a scheduling tool. The right half of the hero is empty, so it looks like the product shot never loaded. It feels competent and plain.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of a booking-link product, meant to get small appointment-based businesses to click "Create your link".

**Verdict.** It's clean and well set, but it doesn't sell. The page never shows the product, the one blue is on everything so the call to action doesn't stand out, and the copy talks about "meetings" and "calls" to customers who run clinics and lessons.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero never shows what a booking link is.** The headline and subhead sit in the left half above the fold, and the right half is empty white. A visitor has to imagine what their guests would see, or click "See a live example" to find out. That empty space also makes the hero lopsided: all the weight is on the left with nothing to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). If an image was meant to be there and didn't load in the capture, check that first. Otherwise, put the booking page itself in that space, the screen a guest picks a time on, so the picture and the headline make one point together: "this is the link, and this is what fills your calendar." *Defect* (the empty space and the missing product) and *convention* (balance).

2. **One blue is doing every job, so nothing is marked as the thing to click.** The same saturated blue is on the headline, all four nav links, both buttons, every card border, the four icons, the four card titles, the four badges, and the "Learn more" links. "Create your link" is one blue rectangle among dozens of blue marks, and the four cards with 2 px blue outlines are the biggest blue area on the screen. They pull the eye away from the button and box the page into compartments that white space could separate. Apple HIG and Material both keep the accent for the main action and interactive state. Keep the blue for the buttons and links. Set the headline and card titles in a dark neutral, the icons and badges in gray, and drop the card borders or turn them into a pale tint (Lupton, *Thinking with Type*). Then the button becomes the brightest thing in the hero. *Convention.*

3. **The copy is written for office meetings, but the customers named are clinics, tutors, and vets.** The strip lists Harbor Dental, Northfield Tutoring, Kestrel Physio, Lumen Coaching, and Orchard Vets. The words say "every meeting", "pool availability across a team", and "the first minutes of the call". A receptionist at a dental clinic or a vet's office has to translate that before it applies to them (Nielsen, heuristic 2: match the user's own words). The headline "Booking links that fill your calendar" could sit on any scheduling product. It also claims more bookings when everything below describes less back-and-forth. Nothing compares the product with what these businesses do today, such as phone calls or email. Decide who the page is for. If it's appointment businesses, use their words: appointments, patients, clients, sessions. Then add the kind of comparison only you can supply, the current way their customers book, so a visitor can judge the value on terms they already trust. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- First card, "Shared calendars": the "New" badge sits right-aligned on its own and squeezes the title onto two lines. That drops the card's body text and "Learn more" about 30 px below the other three, so the rows don't line up across the cards (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Put the badge inline after the title like the others, or shorten the title so it fits on one line.
- Badges: "New", "Auto", "Email", and "Beta" look identical, but two are release status and two describe the feature. A reader takes them as one set and has to work out what "Auto" means. Keep badges for status only (New, Beta) and put "automatic" and "by email" into the card text.
- Icons: the calendar is an outline, while the clock, envelope, and form icons are solid. Pick one style for all four (my own judgment; none of the approved sources covers this).
- Customer row at the bottom: five gray names with no label, so they read as stray text or footer links, not social proof. Give the row a short label that says what these businesses are to you.
- "Start free" (header) and "Create your link" (hero) look like the same action under two names. Use one label so visitors know it's one step.

**What's working.**
- The type is disciplined: one sans family, with clear steps from headline to card title to body to caption. The headline's tight leading holds its two lines together. Keep that scale when you neutralize the color.
- The card copy is concrete and talks to the reader ("so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows"). Each line ends on what the visitor gets. That's the voice to carry up into the headline.
- The grid is solid: the 64 px left edge holds from the logo through the headline, the cards, and the customer row, and the four cards share equal gutters.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: make the guest's view of a booking link the hero image instead of an admin dashboard. The page's whole promise is "let people pick a time that works", so showing what the guest sees proves that promise in a glance. *Taste.*
2. Builds on finding 3: let the customer strip do more than list names. Pair at least one of the five businesses that are already there with a real result they got, supplied by you, and move it up near the call to action. A peer's result persuades a small-practice owner more than a feature list does. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half of the hero is an image that didn't load in the capture. Check it in a live browser.
- Focus indicators on the nav, buttons, and links.
- How the four cards reflow at narrower widths.
