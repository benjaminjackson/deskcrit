**First read.** The eye goes straight to the red and orange block in the middle of the grid, then up to "When new tickets arrive", then across to the three big numbers on the right. It reads as a support-desk report showing when tickets come in during the week, made for whoever schedules the team. The page is clean and calm, except for the chart, which is shouting.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is for a support lead who uses the page to decide when to put more people on shift.

**Verdict.** The layout is solid, but the page fails at its main job. Two of its numbers don't match the grid, and the rainbow colors make the reader decode every cell through the legend instead of seeing the busy hours at a glance. Fix the numbers first. A staffing decision made from this page right now could put people on the wrong hour.

**What would move it most**
1. **The stat panel contradicts the chart. Defect.**
   - **The busiest hour.** "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" doesn't match the grid. The 09:00 label sits over the left edge of the first yellow column, so the solid orange column is 10:00. The two red (30+) cells on Monday and Friday are in the 11:00 column, and that column is orange on the other three weekdays. By the legend, 11:00 is the busiest weekday hour, not 10:00.
   - **The total.** "New tickets, September: 3,412" looks too small. Take the lowest value of each band for every cell: a weekday adds up to about 200 tickets, and a week to about 1,000. Over the four weeks of 1–28 September that is roughly 4,300 or more, which is already above 3,412.
   - **What it costs.** A lead who checks one number against the other stops trusting both, and a lead who doesn't check staffs the wrong hour.
   - **Fix.** Make the stats and the grid come from the same data, scope and date range. Recheck which column 10:00 is (see finding 3). If 3,412 really covers something else, say what in its label. The subtitle says "1 to 28 September" but the stat says "September", so state one range in both.
2. **The rainbow palette hides the order of the values. Convention (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 19.2 and 4.2), and a defect here.**
   - **The data is ordered.** Tickets per hour run from low to high, but the colors go blue, cyan, teal, green, yellow, orange, red. Their lightness doesn't follow the values: yellow (20–24) is the lightest cell in the chart, lighter than both the blue (0–4) and the red (30+).
   - **What it costs.** In grayscale the busy hours don't read as the darkest (Albers; *Refactoring UI*: value carries structure). A reader with color-vision deficiency loses the green/yellow/orange steps, which are exactly the middle of the day where the staffing decisions happen. Fully sighted readers still have to check the legend to know whether green is more than cyan.
   - **The mood is wrong too.** Red and orange signal an emergency (Apple HIG and Material status colors), so a normal Tuesday mid-morning looks like an alarm. Saturated color also fills almost all of a page that is otherwise quiet.
   - **Fix.** Use one hue that runs from light to dark, so darker always means more tickets. Keep the seven bands, and keep a strong accent for 30+ only if those cells really should alarm the reader.
3. **The hour axis is easy to misread. Defect.**
   - **The problem.** Labels appear only every three hours, and each one is left-aligned to the edge of its cell. That edge could just as well be read as a tick between hours. With 24 identical columns, the reader has to count cells to find 10:00 or 11:00, and finding 2 shows how easily that count goes wrong.
   - **Fix.** Center each label on its column, or label every hour in smaller type. Either way, a reader should be able to find the peak hour without counting (Tufte, ch. 2, on clear labels).
4. **The title names a topic, not what it found. Common sense; the reference source for this point isn't on the approved list.**
   - **The problem.** "When new tickets arrive" makes the reader find the pattern themselves.
   - **Fix.** Don't add new claims. Use the facts the page already shows, which are when the weekday peak falls and how quiet the weekend is, as the title, or as a one-line note on the grid. That only works once finding 1 is fixed.

**Smaller things.** None. Everything else on the checklist passed, or is covered by the findings above.

**What's working.**
- **The subtitle and source line.** The subtitle says exactly what each cell is ("per hour of the day, all queues, average week, 1 to 28 September"). The source line closes the obvious question ("opened, not answered"; "the support team's local time"). Keep both.
- **The stat panel.** Each figure has a label, one large number and a comparison with August. That is exactly how a headline metric should be framed.
- **The grid.** The white gaps between cells, the plain gray type and the restrained navigation leave the data as the only loud thing on the page. A calmer palette will make that work even better.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the 30+ band own the single warm color on the page. Set every other band in one cool ramp, so the handful of overloaded hours jump out and become the thing the lead staffs for. Taste.
2. The weekday/weekend split is the strongest pattern in the grid. Consider showing it through position, with a little more space between Friday and Saturday, so the two staffing regimes read as two blocks. Taste (Wilke, on grouping).
3. "Busiest hour" is the stat closest to the decision this page supports. Once it matches the grid, mark that column directly on the chart, so the number on the right and the place in the grid confirm each other. Taste.
