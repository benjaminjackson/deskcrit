**First read.** The eye goes to the headline "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks", drops to the orange "Start your free week" button, and then catches on the gray "Keep your streak going" box before it reaches the pale teal illustration. It reads as a clean, calm landing page for a speaking-first language app aimed at adult beginners. It is tidy and friendly, but generic: the layout could belong to almost any SaaS product.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the home page hero for a first-time visitor, and its job is to get them to start the free week.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The promise is clear, the button is unmissable, and the three steps explain the method. Two things stand in the way. A box written for people who already use the product sits in the middle of the pitch to people who don't. And the illustration adds nothing the headline hasn't already said.

**What would move it most**

1. **The streak card is aimed at the wrong person and sits in the wrong place.** It's the gray box at roughly x 56–456, y 390–498, directly under the button. "Keep your streak going" speaks to someone who already has a streak, but a first-time visitor has none. So the box reads like an app notification that leaked onto the marketing page. It sits 28 px below the button, the same gap as between the subhead and the button, so it reads as part of the pitch. It's also the last thing in the left column, so the eye ends there instead of on the button. The fact inside it, that learners who practice five days in a row finish twice as often, is the most persuasive line on the page, but it's framed as a nag. **Defect** (it confuses the intended viewer and pulls attention after the ask). Fix: either cut the box, or move the finding to where a visitor weighs the commitment (near the steps or the "ten weeks" promise) and rewrite it for someone who hasn't started. The claim also has nothing to compare against: "twice as often" as whom? Say who the comparison group is, a fact only you have.

2. **The illustration only repeats the headline, and it's off the grid.** The two speech bubbles in the teal panel (x 676–1076) just illustrate the word "conversation". They show nothing about how the product works, not the tutor that listens and not the feedback on each word, so the right half of the hero carries no information. The panel also ends about 150 px short of the right edge shared by the nav ("Log in") and step 3 (x 1224). Its left edge doesn't match any of the step columns below (461, 867) either. That leaves an empty strip on the right and makes the panel look placed by eye (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*). **Defect** for the grid; **taste** for the image, argued on its own terms: the panel is your best spot to show what's different about the product, and right now it's spent on a symbol. Fix: run the panel to the right margin, and fill it with something that shows the product doing what the subhead claims, such as a lesson screen with per-word feedback. That's content you'd need to supply.

3. **"A tutor that listens" can be read two ways.** A visitor can't tell whether the tutor is a person or software. That changes what they expect from the free week and what they think they'll pay for, so it's exactly the kind of line they'll hold you to. "Short daily lessons" and "ten weeks" have the same problem: the visitor can't work out the daily commitment behind the promise. **Defect** (copy on what the viewer commits to). Fix: say which kind of tutor it is, and give the lesson length in minutes. Both facts are yours to supply.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line break: "in" is left at the end of line 1. Break before it ("Hold a real conversation / in ten weeks") so each line is a phrase (Bringhurst on breaks in short text, as common sense).
- The bottom ~180 px of the screen is empty, after the steps run out at y 620. With finding 2's leftover strip, the space looks left over rather than placed. Tighten the vertical rhythm, or let the next section start in view.

**What's working.**
- The headline promises an outcome with a time frame ("real conversation in ten weeks") instead of a feature list. It's the strongest line on the page.
- The single orange button against an otherwise neutral and teal palette leaves no doubt about the one action. Keep the orange reserved for it.
- The three steps (Listen / Speak / Use it), each with a short verb heading, a thin rule above, and one line below, explain the method in a few seconds.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: commit the right half of the hero to proof of the speaking-first method rather than decoration. For a product whose difference is "start with speaking", showing a spoken exchange beats a symbol of one (taste).
2. Let "ten weeks" lead harder. It's the one number a visitor will judge the product by. Tie it visibly to the three steps (one daily loop, repeated for ten weeks) so the promise and the method read as one plan (taste).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the illustration panel's width comes from a breakpoint at this viewport. Check at other widths whether it fills to the right margin.
