**First read.** The eye lands on $4.82M in the first card, moves across to 112% and 1,246, then drops into the chart. The chart's heavy black frame and near-black gridlines pull the eye there at least as hard as the bars do. It reads straight away as a SaaS revenue dashboard for a founder or finance lead. It feels calm, orderly and competent, and also like a hundred other dashboards.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an at-a-glance yearly revenue summary for a company's leadership or finance team, who need the numbers to be right and to agree with each other.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job: the key figures come first, each has a comparison, and the chart is honest. But the numbers on the page don't add up to each other. For a finance audience that is the biggest problem, ahead of the heavy chart styling.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plan table's label says one thing and its total says another (defect).** "Revenue by plan, 2025" totals exactly $4.82M, which is the ARR figure from the first card ("Annual recurring revenue, Dec 2025"). The monthly bars beside it add up to about $4.49M of recognized 2025 revenue. A reader who checks will find two different "2025 revenue" figures a few inches apart and won't know which to trust. A reader who doesn't check will take the ARR breakdown for the year's revenue. Either way the dashboard loses the trust it depends on. The heading must name what the table actually shows (WCAG 2.4.6, Headings and labels): either relabel it as ARR by plan at Dec 2025, or make it total the same recognized revenue the chart shows. Only you know which one is true.
2. **The chart's frame and gridlines are as dark as the data (convention).** In the "Monthly revenue, 2025" chart, the black box around the plot and the near-black gridlines at every 100 compete with the blue bars. That is what drags the eye into the chart's scaffolding on first read, and it makes the small Apr and Jul dips harder to see against the lines crossing them. Remove the frame, make the gridlines thin and pale gray (or drop them), and let the bars be the darkest thing in the chart (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 23.2; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5).

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Monthly revenue, 2025" names a topic, not a finding. The chart shows steady growth with dips in April and July, and the reader has to work that out alone. A title or a short note that states the trend, and explains the dips if there's a reason, would save them the work. This is common sense; no approved source covers it.
- The two right-hand headings, "Monthly revenue, 2025" and "Revenue by plan, 2025", sit about 4 px apart vertically. That reads as a near-miss rather than a choice, so set them on one baseline (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- The "Jan to Dec 2025" chip in the top right has a faint fill, no visible edge and no chevron. If it's a date picker, it looks like a static tag, and its edge looks well under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Give it a border or chevron. If it's only a label, it's fine.
- Navigation: the page is titled "Revenue overview" under the nav item "Overview", and there's a separate "Revenue" item. A new user can't tell what the Revenue page holds that this one doesn't. Make the title and nav names match the structure (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2).

**What's working.**
- Every KPI card has a period and a comparison ("+18% vs Dec 2024", "+3 pts vs 2024"), so each big number can be judged at a glance. Keep that pattern.
- The chart is honest. The bars start at zero, the subtitle gives units and basis ("Thousands of US dollars, recognised revenue"), and there's a source line. That's exactly the right instinct for a finance audience.
- The type scale is tight and clear: about four sizes (title, KPI figure, section heading, body/label), with weight doing the finer work. Green appears only for positive deltas and always with a + sign.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Taste: let $4.82M lead. The three KPI cards are equal peers right now. If ARR is the number this page exists to report, giving that card more width or scale tells the reader in one second what matters most, and the other two become supporting evidence (Refactoring UI: emphasize by de-emphasizing the rest).
2. Taste, building on finding 2: once the chart is lighter, add the prior year as a pale gray context series behind the blue 2025 bars. The cards already compare against 2024, and the chart is the only element on the page without that comparison. This needs the 2024 monthly data, which only you have (Few; Tufte on comparison).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date chip opens a picker, and whether the chart has hover values.
