**First read.** The eye lands on "See where every dollar goes this month", drops to the subhead, and stops at "Try it free". Then it slides right into a large empty navy field, finds nothing there, and falls to three quiet feature columns and a blank white band. It reads as a budgeting app's landing page: calm, trustworthy, and very much like a standard SaaS template. It feels unfinished more than designed.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about it, so I'm assuming this is the top of a marketing landing page for a consumer budgeting app. Its job is to get a stranger to connect their accounts by starting the free trial.

**Verdict.** The words are good and the type is clean, but the hero promises a view ("See where every dollar goes") and then shows nothing. Half the main band is empty navy, so the page has no picture of the product and nothing that sets it apart. That empty half is the main thing holding it back.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half of the hero is empty, so the promise is never shown.** Roughly x 800 to 1216 of the navy band is blank. That leaves three problems:
   - **Defect: the headline has no proof.** It literally says "See", but the viewer is shown nothing to see. They have to take the product's main claim on trust.
   - **Convention: the page tips left.** All the weight sits in the left 60% of the band, with nothing on the right to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance").
   - **Defect: the eye path hits a dead end.** After the buttons, the eye drifts into the void instead of moving down to the features.

   If an image was supposed to be there and didn't load, check it in a live browser and this point goes away. If it is empty by design, put the product in that space: one real view of the app answering "where did my money go this month?" That lets visitors judge the value against their own spending, which is a stronger pitch than any adjective. Which screen to show, and its contents, is up to you.
2. **The trust message is buried, and the page asks for bank access.** "Connect your accounts" is the first thing the subhead asks. The answer to the obvious worry, "Read-only access … can never move money", sits as the third of three equal feature cards, below the fold for many visitors and no stronger than "Shared budgets". **Taste:** for a product that asks for bank logins, that line is the objection killer. Move it up next to the call to action, or give it more weight than the other two features, so a hesitant viewer meets it at the moment of deciding.
3. **"Try it free" is an ask that could sit on any page. (Taste/copy.)** It doesn't say what the viewer actually does or gets: connect an account? See their own month? The subhead already names the real first step, connecting accounts and seeing spending by category. The fix is an ask that names that concrete first action, ideally ending on their own numbers, rather than a generic "try". If the trial terms matter (is a card needed, how long does it last), those are facts only you have, and they belong under the button.

**Smaller things.**
- **Uneven white space.** The feature row sits only about 45 px below the hero, while a blank band of about 200 px of white trails below it. Give the features more room from the hero, and let the next section (or the end of the page) take up the slack.

**What's working.**
- **The headline and subhead copy.** Both are concrete and speak to the viewer ("before you go over" lands the benefit on them). The subhead names exactly three things the product does, each with a verb. Keep that plain voice.
- **The type.** It uses one family. The headline is set big, bold, and tight enough to hold together as a unit. Feature headings differ from body text by weight and color, not just size.
- **A real grid and a clear button hierarchy.** Left edges at 64 px, three equal columns, and a right edge that matches "Sign in" in the nav. The filled "Try it free" next to the outlined "Watch the tour" makes the primary action obvious.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 1:** let the headline's phrase "every dollar" drive the visual. Show one month broken down by category, so the hero image itself is the answer to the headline rather than decoration. **Taste:** this works because the product's value is a view, and a view is best sold by showing it.
2. **Make the navy mean "your money is safe".** The navy is already the brand's one strong color. Use it again on the trust signals (the read-only promise, the Security link), so the color itself comes to carry reassurance across the site. **Taste.**

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half of the hero and the blank band at the bottom are the design or a capture taken mid-load. Check in a live browser.
- The hover and focus states of the two buttons and the nav links.
