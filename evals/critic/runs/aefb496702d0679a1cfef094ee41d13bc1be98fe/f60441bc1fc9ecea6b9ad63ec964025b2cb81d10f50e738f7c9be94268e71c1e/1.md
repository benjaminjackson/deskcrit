**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big figures (3.8%, €64.20, 12.4k), takes in the small green deltas under them, then drops into the orange bar chart and lands on the Saturday peak and the Sunday dip. It reads as the owner's home screen for a small bakery that sells by order and subscription. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, with no noise.

**What it's for.** My assumption: it shows the bakery's owner or operator how the shop is doing this month at a glance, and when orders land, so they can plan baking and delivery.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made dashboard. The hierarchy is clear, the grid is clean, and every number comes with context. The one real problem is in the chart. It adds up raw orders per weekday across September 2026, a month with five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of every other day, so the gentle Monday-to-Thursday climb it shows is partly a calendar artifact. Nothing on the chart tells the viewer what to take from it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart compares unequal numbers of days.** September 2026 starts on a Tuesday, so the "Orders by delivery day" bars for Tuesday and Wednesday each total five delivery days, while every other bar totals four. Read per day, Tuesday comes out *below* Monday (about 142 vs about 160), not above it. The steady weekday rise the eye picks up is partly invented by the calendar. Anyone planning staff or bake quantities from this chart will over-prepare midweek. This is a defect: the chart misleads on exactly the question it exists to answer. The fix is to compare like with like: average orders per delivery day, or a fixed whole number of weeks. Then make the source line say which one it is. (Common sense, not a cited rule. The approved catalog entry for this has no approved source.)
2. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding.** "Orders by delivery day" tells the viewer what the axes are, but not that Friday and Saturday carry the week or that Sunday is about a third of Saturday. Of everything on the screen, the chart takes the most space and holds the most decision-relevant information, yet the viewer has to work out the point alone. Once finding 1 is fixed, let the title or a one-line caption say what the corrected chart shows. This is taste: the source for this entry is not on the approved list. I'm arguing it because a dashboard gets glanced at, not studied.

**Smaller things.**
- Comparison periods differ from card to card: two say "vs previous 30 days" and Active subscribers says "vs 31 August". Under it, "Last 30 days" sits next to "As of 30 September 2026", and the chart says "September 2026". If today is 30 September these all agree, but the viewer has to work that out. State the data date once near "Shop overview" so the windows read as one period. (Copy, common sense.)

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards.** The figure is large and heavy, the unit (%, €, k) is set lighter and smaller, the period is in gray, and the delta is in green with a ▲ and a sign. That gives four clear levels in a tiny space, and the change never depends on color alone. Keep this pattern for any card you add.
- **Restraint in the chart.** It is one series in the brand orange, with hairline gridlines, bars starting from zero, weekdays in their natural order, and a source line. Nearly all the ink is data (Tufte).
- **One grid.** The cards and the chart panel share the 48 px left and right edges and the same gutter, so the page reads as one object.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: the chart already uses the logotype's orange. A stronger version would keep that orange only for the days that matter (the peak, or the day the owner should act on) and set the rest in a quiet neutral. The emphasis then does the reading for an owner glancing between batches. Taste; this follows Tufte's case for spending ink only on the data that carries the point.
2. The three cards all say whether things went up, but not whether up is good enough. If the owner has targets for conversion, order value or subscribers, putting the target beside each delta would turn "+0.4 pts" into a judgment the owner can act on. That's the maker's content to supply, not mine. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the period is fixed or can be changed. For a planning chart, being able to see more than one month matters.
