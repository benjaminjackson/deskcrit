**First read.** The eye goes first to the bold headline, "Plan the week with your whole team", then jumps across to the blue "Create account" button, then back up to the card. Within two seconds it reads as a SaaS sign-up page for a team planning tool. It feels calm, competent and familiar. It's also anonymous: with the wordmark covered, it could be any product's sign-up page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a desktop sign-up page where a team lead lands from marketing and creates a free workspace.

**Verdict.** The layout and hierarchy do their job. The form itself is the weak part. Every field is labeled only by placeholder text that disappears once typing starts, and the page's one promise ("free… for up to five people") can be read two ways. The form is the whole point of the page, so those two problems matter more than everything else here put together.

**What would move it most**
1. **The fields have no labels, only placeholders (defect).** All five inputs in the card are labeled only by their gray placeholder text. The password field is the worst case. It never says "Password"; the only clue is "At least 10 characters", so a viewer works out what it is from the rule. The moment they type, the rule disappears, and they have to remember it while writing a password. The email field has the same problem: "name@company.com" is an example, not a label. If the browser autofills or the user tabs back to check a field, nothing on screen says what the field holds. Labels on fields that are already filled are also what people rely on to spot a mistake before they submit. This breaks the convention that every field has a visible label that stays on screen (Wroblewski, *Web Form Design*; NN/g, "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful"), and it costs recall (Nielsen heuristic 6). The fix: put a short label above every field (First name, Last name, Work email, Password, Company name). Move "At least 10 characters" into a line of helper text under the password field, where it stays visible while they type.
2. **The offer can be read two ways (defect).** "Start a free workspace for up to five people. You can invite the rest of the team later." One reading is that it's free for teams of five or fewer. The other is that it's free until the sixth person joins, and then you pay. The second sentence leans toward the second reading without saying so. A team lead deciding whether to sign up their 12-person team will remember this line and hold you to it. If they read it as "free" and get a bill at invite six, that feels like a bait-and-switch. The fix is an edit to the existing sentence: say what happens past five people (it costs money, it needs a plan, or it stays free with limits). Only you know which is true.
3. **The headline could be anyone's (taste, close to a defect).** "Plan the week with your whole team" describes what every team planner does. Nothing tells the viewer what Tallyboard does differently from what they use now, so the page relies entirely on "free" to win the click. The left column has about 400 px of empty space below the subhead and makes no case beyond that. The direction: say what changes for the viewer compared with how they plan the week today. That needs a fact only you have, such as the one thing the product does that a shared doc or their current tool doesn't.

**Smaller things.**
- **Required fields aren't marked.** Nothing says whether Company name is required. Either mark the optional fields "(optional)" or mark the required ones with an asterisk and explain it once (Budiu, NN/g).
- **"Sign in" doesn't look like a link.** In "Already have an account? Sign in" (under the button), the link is the same gray as the rest of the sentence, so returning users have to guess. Underline it or give it the brand blue (WCAG technique G183).
- **The input borders look pale.** The blue-gray outlines on white look under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for field edges. Measure them and darken if needed, so the fields read as fields at a glance.
- **The wordmark and card heading nearly align.** "Tallyboard" (about y151) and "Create your account" (about y148) sit a few pixels apart across the two columns. That reads as a near-miss rather than a choice. Put them on the same baseline or move them clearly apart.

**What's working.**
- **Clear hierarchy.** There's one dominant headline, one blue button and nothing competing with them. The page has exactly one primary action, so even a quick visitor can't miss the next step.
- **A disciplined palette.** One blue is used only for the wordmark and the button, on top of a gray range. That keeps the blue meaning "Tallyboard / act here", so keep it that strict.
- **A concrete subhead.** "Up to five people" is a real number, not filler. Keep that specificity and fix the ambiguity in finding 2.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: use the empty left column below the subhead to show the product's actual week view, so the viewer can judge the value against their own week before they commit. That needs a real screen from the product, which only you can supply (taste).
2. Let "five people" lead. The number is the most concrete thing on the page and the deciding fact for a team lead. Giving it more weight in the subhead would answer "is this for my team?" before the form does (taste).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The focus state of the fields and the button.
- Whether the fields use the right input types (email, password with a show/hide control).
- When the form shows validation errors.
