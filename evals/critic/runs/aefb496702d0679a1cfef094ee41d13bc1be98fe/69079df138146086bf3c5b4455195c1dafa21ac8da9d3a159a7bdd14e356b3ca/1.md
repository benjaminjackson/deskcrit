**First read.** The eye lands on the bold black headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then has to work to get any further. Everything below it is a pale gray that looks unfinished or disabled. It reads as a plain information page for people using a local river ferry. It feels calm and tidy but faint, as if the body text hasn't fully loaded.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page (1280 × 800) that tells ferry riders when the boat runs, how long a crossing takes, and how bicycles work, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound. The headline leads, there are three short paragraphs in a sensible order, and the reading column is well set. But all the text that holds the actual information is set in a gray too pale to read comfortably. The washed-out body text is the one thing stopping this page from doing its job.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body and the note are too faint to read (defect).** Both paragraphs and the winter note look like a light cool gray (roughly #9CA3AF) on white, which looks close to 2.5:1 contrast. WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 asks for 4.5:1 at this size. The body is about 20 px regular, which is under the 24 px "large text" threshold, so the 3:1 allowance doesn't apply. Riders with low vision, riders on a phone in daylight at the landing, and anyone skimming will struggle with exactly the lines they came for: the times, the crossing length, the bicycle rules. Gray this pale also reads as "secondary" or "disabled", so the page seems to say the headline is the content and the rest is filler. That's backwards. The fix: darken the text until it reaches at least 4.5:1, and measure the actual color to confirm. If you still want the heading to lead, keep the contrast between heading and body through weight and size, which the page already has, not through paleness.
2. **The line a rider most needs to act on is the smallest and faintest thing on the page (defect).** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." is the only instruction here, and it's the only line that can make someone miss a boat. It's set at about 16 px in the same pale gray, below the body, styled like a footnote. A rider skimming will read the forty-minute schedule above it as the timetable, not notice the caveat, and turn up in winter to a timetable that has changed. The fix: give it at least body size and full-strength color, and consider moving it up next to the schedule sentence it qualifies.
3. **The schedule doesn't say when the ferry runs (defect, copy).** "Between early morning and late evening" leaves the rider guessing at the first and last boats, and those are the departures people plan around and hold the operator to. One rider will read "early morning" as 6:00 and another as 8:00. The same goes for "change in winter": the page doesn't say which months or what changes. The fix needs facts only you have: the first and last departure times, and when the winter timetable starts. Add them; don't let the reader fill the gap.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing separately.

**What's working.**
- **The reading column.** The paragraphs run about 70–75 characters per line with comfortable line spacing, flush left, with a clear margin. That's at the top of Bringhurst's 45–75 range, so don't let it get any wider.
- **The headline.** It's set clearly heavier and darker than the text, as well as larger. The hierarchy comes from more than size, and the eye knows where to start.
- **The copy says who does what and when.** "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail along the side of the deck" is a full instruction in plain words. Keep that voice.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 3.** Once the first and last departure times are on the page, let them lead the first paragraph as the facts riders scan for. Then "every forty minutes" and "about twelve minutes" can support them instead of standing in for a timetable. This is taste: on a page riders check before they travel, the times are the point.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where people read this at the landing.
