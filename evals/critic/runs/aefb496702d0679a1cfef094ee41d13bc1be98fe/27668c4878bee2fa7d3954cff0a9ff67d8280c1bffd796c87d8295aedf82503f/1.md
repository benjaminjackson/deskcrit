**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three blocks of heavy brush script and slows to a crawl. It reads as a practical information page for people catching a river ferry, but the script makes it feel like a café chalkboard or a wedding invite. Pulling out the actual facts (how often, how long, the last boat) takes real effort.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a web page that ferry passengers scan quickly to learn the schedule, crossing time, bike rules and winter changes.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The information is clear and well organized, but all of it is set in a decorative script that's slow to read and wrong in tone. On top of that, the times a passenger needs most are vague or missing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in a brush script.** All three paragraphs, the whole reading content of the page, are in a heavy, connected script that looks like Pacifico. Every word has to be decoded rather than recognized, and that hits hardest on what people come for: "every forty minutes," "twelve minutes," "one boat each hour." Bringhurst's guidance on choosing text faces (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1) is that paragraphs belong in a face made for reading and decorative faces belong to a few words of display. The script also brings problems of its own. At this line spacing its tall loops nearly touch the line above (for example "quay" over "twelve" in the first paragraph), so the lines look cramped. And its playful tone fights the neutral sans heading and the practical content, so the page reads as two pieces stuck together. The tone mismatch is my judgment, not a published rule. *Defect.* Fix: set the paragraphs in a plain text face, either the heading's sans or a companion that clearly contrasts with it. If you want warmth, keep the script to a few words of display at most.
2. **The copy leaves out the times a passenger acts on.** "Between early morning and late evening," "a little longer when the tide turns" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" give no clock times. "Before dusk" is the one that can strand someone. A passenger reading in winter can't tell whether that means 3:30 or 5:00, and if they guess late they miss the last boat. The page then sends them to "the shelter at each landing" for the printed timetable, which is exactly where they aren't while reading this page. *Defect* (common sense: these are deadlines people hold you to). Fix: give the first and last departure times, the winter last-boat time (or a rule tied to a time), and a rough number for the tide delay. Only the maker has these facts.
3. **The key facts are buried in running prose.** Frequency, crossing time, bike rules and winter changes are each a separate thing someone looks up. Right now they sit inside sentences under one generic heading, so the reader has to read everything to find one fact. *Taste*, argued from scanning (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*): a short label per paragraph, such as schedule, bicycles and winter, or pulling the numbers forward, would let someone find their answer at a glance.

**Smaller things.** Lines run around 75–80 characters, at the top of Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Once the face changes, check the measure again and keep it near 66.

**What's working.**
- The heading is bold, dark, and clearly the start of the page.
- The paragraphs are short, one topic each, with the same space between them, so the structure is already right for adding labels.
- Everything sits on one flush-left edge with generous margins, so it reads as one calm column.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3. Let the numbers already in the copy lead: "forty minutes," "twelve minutes" and "one boat each hour" are the page's real content. Setting them in the heading's bold weight inside the text would let a hurried passenger catch them without reading every sentence. *Taste*, in line with Refactoring UI's "bolder, not bigger."

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page reflows on a phone, which is likely where passengers read it.
