**First read.** My eye goes to "Pricing," then moves across three cards that look the same, $9, $29 and $79, and stops at the feature lists. There's nowhere to go from there. The only button is the small blue "Start free" in the top-right corner of the nav, far from the prices. It reads as a clean, calm, standard SaaS pricing page for a scheduling tool. It's tidy, but nothing on it stands out.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the pricing page for Tidewell, a calendar and booking tool. Its job is to get a visitor from a small team to choose a plan and start.

**Verdict.** The page doesn't do its job yet. It shows prices but never asks the visitor to choose one. No card has an action, no plan is recommended, and the copy leaves out two things a buyer needs to compare plans: what "/ month" is charged per, and whether higher plans include the lower ones' features.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cards don't end in an action** (defect). Each card ends after three feature lines. A visitor who has picked a plan has to go back up to "Start free" in the nav, which doesn't say which plan it starts. Pricing cards normally end in their own button, so the gap is noticeable (Nielsen, Jakob's law). Give each card a button at its foot that names its plan. That is where the eye already stops. Keep one style for the primary button and use a lower one for the others; see finding 2.
2. **The three plans have equal weight, so the page doesn't help anyone choose** (taste, argued). The cards have the same size, border, weight and color. The visitor gets a three-way comparison with no signal about which plan fits "teams," the audience the subtitle names. Pick the plan you want most people to buy (probably Team) and make it stand out with one cue: a tinted or darker border, a label, or the only filled button. The other two then read as the edges of the range.
3. **The prices can be read two ways** (defect, because this is the line people act on). "$29 / month" for "Up to 10 calendars" could mean $29 for the whole team or $29 per person. The buyer remembers that line and will hold you to it, so if they guess wrong they get a surprise at checkout or leave assuming the price is too high. The feature lists cause a related problem. Team lists "Shared availability, Reports" and doesn't mention "Booking page" or "Email reminders," so it reads as if Team doesn't have them. Restore the unit after the price (per what, and when it's billed), and add a line to Team and Business that says it includes everything in the plan below. Only you can supply those facts.
4. **The copy could be on anyone's page** (defect). "Simple plans for teams of every size. Change or cancel whenever you like." fits every SaaS product. The cards then list category nouns ("Reports," "Shared availability") without saying what they do for the visitor. A stranger can't tell why Tidewell is worth $29 instead of the tool they already use. Bring the headline down to what this product does for a team's bookings, in the visitor's terms. Turn the feature lines into short statements of what each one lets the visitor do. The facts for both have to come from you.

**Smaller things.**
- The nav doesn't mark "Pricing" as the current page. It looks the same as "Product" and "Contact." Mark it with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist," NN/g).
- The subtitle wraps so that "you like." sits alone on its second line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrow or widen the text box so the two lines are closer in length.

**What's working.**
- The cards share one grid. The plan names, prices and feature lines sit on the same baselines in all three columns, so you can compare across without hunting.
- The type scale is clear. The price figures are big and bold, and "/ month" is small and light next to them. Weight and size do the separating, with no extra sizes. Keep this when you add the units.
- The palette is restrained: one blue, used only for the logo and the action. That leaves room for one plan or button to stand out as soon as you choose it.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the blue mean "act here." Right now it appears only on the logo and "Start free." Use it on the recommended card's button and nowhere else on the cards, so the one saturated color on the page points at the decision. This is taste: one accent with one job reads faster than a highlighted card plus a separately colored button.
2. Use the empty bottom quarter of the screen. About 230 px under the cards is empty. That's where a buyer's next questions come up: what counts as a calendar, how billing works, whether there's a trial. Put a short answer row there in your own words. This is taste: a pricing page that answers the doubts right under the prices keeps the visitor on the page instead of sending them to "Contact."

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav links and "Start free"; how the three cards stack on a narrow screen.
