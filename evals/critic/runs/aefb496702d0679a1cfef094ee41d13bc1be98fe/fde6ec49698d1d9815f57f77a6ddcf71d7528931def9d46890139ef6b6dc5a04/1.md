**First read.** The eye lands on "See where every dollar goes this month", big and white on electric blue. It drops to the subhead, then to "Try it free". Then it drifts right into an empty field of blue and finds nothing. It reads as a budgeting app's landing page for individuals and couples. It feels clean, confident and competent, but anonymous: you could swap in another logo and it would read the same.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a consumer budgeting app, meant to get first-time visitors to start a free trial or watch the tour.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid and the structure is right, but it doesn't make its case. It promises you'll *see* where your money goes and then shows nothing: no screen, no category, no limit, no alert. The blank right half of the hero is where the proof should be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero tells but never shows, and its right half is empty** (defect). The headline, subhead and buttons all sit in the left 60% of the blue band. The right side, from about x=800 to 1216, is bare saturated blue. So the band looks lopsided: all the weight is on the left with nothing to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Worse, the page promises something visual ("See where every dollar goes") and gives the viewer nothing to see. A visitor deciding whether to connect their bank accounts has to take the whole product on trust. Fix: use the right half to show the product doing what the subhead says, such as a category with its limit and a heads-up before going over. Then the headline and the picture make one point together instead of the headline working alone. If an image is meant to sit there and didn't load in this capture, check that first. That would fix the balance, but the page still needs a picture to make its case.
2. **"Try it free" can be read two ways, and it's the line people commit on** (defect, copy). It could mean a free plan or a free trial that ends and starts charging. Those are very different promises, and visitors who expected one and get the other feel misled. Fix: say which one it is, either on the button or in a short line right under the buttons, with the length and whether a card is needed. Only you know those facts. "Watch the tour" has a smaller version of the same gap: the viewer can't tell if it's 30 seconds or 10 minutes, so adding the length helps them say yes.
3. **The headline could be on any budgeting app's site** (defect, copy). "See where every dollar goes this month" is clear, but every competitor makes the same promise, so it gives the viewer no reason to pick this app. What actually sets it apart is lower down: shared household budgets, read-only access, and a heads-up *before* you go over. That last one comes before overspending, not after, and it's buried in the subhead. The ask also doesn't set the app against what the viewer uses now, like a spreadsheet, their bank's app or nothing. That comparison would let them judge the value on terms they already trust. Fix: bring whichever difference you think matters most into the headline or subhead, and name what the viewer is doing today that this replaces. You supply that comparison; I'm not writing it.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the findings.

**What's working.**
- **The grid is disciplined.** The logo, headline, buttons and first feature column share one left edge at about 64 px. The three feature columns are evenly spaced, and the last one ends at the same right edge as the nav. Keep this when you add the product image: line it up to the same right edge.
- **The type scale is clean.** There's one big bold headline, a regular-weight subhead, bold feature headings and gray body text. Each level differs in more than one way (size, weight, color), so nothing competes with the headline.
- **The subhead and the feature copy are good writing.** "Connect your accounts, set a limit for each category, and get a heads-up before you go over" says who does what, in order. "Can see balances and history but can never move money" answers the viewer's biggest fear in plain words. Keep that voice.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the product image carry the app's one real difference, the heads-up *before* you go over, rather than a generic dashboard (taste). A picture of the moment you're warned does more for "get people to start a trial" than a chart of past spending, because no other kind of budgeting app can show that moment.
2. Move "Read-only access" up next to the buttons (taste). Connecting bank accounts is the scariest step for this audience, and that line removes the fear at exactly the moment they click "Try it free". As the third of three equal columns below the fold, it reads like a minor feature. Next to the ask, it reassures the viewer.
