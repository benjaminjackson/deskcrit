**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", drops to the gray subtitle, then runs down the table. It reads at once as a plain, calm timetable for one route. Then the sentence under the table pulls the eye back: it says the table is "a mess" because of "heavy black lines" that aren't there.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a public timetable that lets a rider at the north landing find the next ferry to market quay and check whether it runs today.

**Verdict.** As a timetable it mostly works: it's clean, lightly ruled and easy to scan. The biggest problem isn't the table. It's the stray sentence under it, which tells every rider the timetable is broken. After that, the days each ferry runs need to stand out.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sentence under the table is a leftover note, and it isn't true.** "The heavy black lines around every cell make this timetable a mess." sits at the bottom of the page in body size. The table has pale hairline row rules and no cell borders, so a rider reads a complaint that doesn't match what they see. Either they stop trusting the page or they wonder whether they're looking at the right version. This is a defect, judged on common sense since the catalog has no entry for it: the copy isn't finished. Remove the line. If a note belongs there, it should be one that helps a rider, like the season's dates or where to get the return times.
2. **The days a ferry runs look the same as every other cell.** In the Runs column, "Weekdays" (12:40, 17:10) and "Weekends" (19:30) are set exactly like "Daily": same weight, same color, at the far right edge about 600 px from the time. A rider who looks up 19:30 on a Tuesday has to cross the whole row to find out it doesn't sail. Missing it costs them the trip. This is a defect (it misleads the viewer on what they act on), and the fix is my judgment, not a cited rule. Make the exceptions stand apart from "Daily", with weight or a quiet mark, so "Daily" reads as the default and the exceptions catch the eye. Moving Runs closer to the departure time would also shorten the trip across the row.

**Smaller things.**
- **Time columns:** the figures look proportional. "11:00" and "17:10" are narrower than "06:40", so the digits don't stack. Use tabular figures, which Inter has as a feature (Bringhurst, 4.4.3).
- **Subtitle:** "spring season" gives no dates, so a rider can't tell whether the table is valid today. Add the date range the maker has.

**What's working.**
- **Light row rules and lots of row space.** Each row is easy to follow across without boxed cells. This is the restraint Lupton asks for in data tables, so keep it as you revise.
- **A clean type hierarchy.** A bold title, a smaller gray subtitle, a bold header row and a regular body: four levels with no near-duplicate sizes.
- **Consistent margins.** The title, subtitle, table and note all start on one left edge at about 100 px, which keeps the page calm.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 2.** Let the departure time lead: make "Leaves north" the strongest column, by weight or size, since it's the one number a rider comes for. Period and Arrives quay can step back to support it. Taste.
2. **Use the Period column to group rows instead of repeating labels.** "Morning" and "Midday" each appear twice. Showing each period once, with a little extra space between periods, would let the eye jump to "afternoon" without reading the column. That fits a timetable people scan under time pressure. Taste.
