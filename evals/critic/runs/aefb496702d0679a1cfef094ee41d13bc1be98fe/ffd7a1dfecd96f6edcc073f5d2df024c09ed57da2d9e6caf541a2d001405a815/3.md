**First read.** My eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then down into three gray paragraphs of even weight. Nothing else on the page pulls at it. It reads as a plain information page about a local river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and quiet, almost like a note pinned to a board. The right 40% of the frame and the bottom third are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells people taking the river ferry when it runs, how long it takes and what to do with a bicycle, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and does its job. The page still fails passengers because it never gives them a time they can plan around. The schedule is described in words like "early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk" instead of being stated.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule has no times in it (defect).** This is the copy in paragraph 1 ("every forty minutes between early morning and late evening") and paragraph 3 ("one boat each hour, and the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk").
   - A passenger comes to a ferry page for the first and last boat. Here "early morning" and "late evening" could mean 6 or 8, 9 or 11, and the reader has to guess. A wrong guess means standing at a landing after the last boat has gone.
   - "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can be read two ways. It could mean the last boat from the market quay only, or the last boat in either direction. Read the wrong way, someone on the north landing waits for a boat that isn't coming.
   - "In winter" doesn't say from when to when.
   - The page then sends readers to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing". So the real information is only available once you've already made the trip.
   - Fix direction: put back the facts the prose is standing in for:
     - first and last departure from each landing
     - the dates of the winter timetable
     - the last crossing given as a time, in each direction

   Only you have these. Keep the sentences, but swap each vague phrase for the number.

2. **Every fact has the same weight, so nothing can be scanned (common sense, not a cited rule).** The three paragraphs are short and well set. They also cover three separate jobs: the regular schedule, bicycles and winter changes. Each one is set as running prose under one general headline.
   - Someone checking the page on a phone at the landing wants one fact, such as "when's the next boat?" or "can I bring my bike?" To get it they have to read from the top.
   - Fix direction: give each topic a short label that names what it covers. Once the times exist (finding 1), set the schedule so the times stand out from the sentences around them, for example as a small two-column list for summer and winter. Leave the bicycle and winter notes as prose; they read well.

**Smaller things.** None. The setting has no other failures.

**What's working.**
- **Body text.** It is set at a comfortable reading size in a plain sans. Lines run about 65–70 characters, which is right in the 45–75 band (Bringhurst). The line spacing is generous, so the three paragraphs read without effort. Keep this measure and leading if you add structure.
- **The headline.** It does all the ranking on its own: it is clearly larger and bold, while the body is regular. That is contrast from more than size, so there is no doubt where to start.
- **Restraint.** The page is near-black on white, with one typeface and nothing decorative. For a utility notice this is right, and it makes the page feel trustworthy.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Builds on finding 2.* Let the last-boat time lead (taste). Once it exists, it is the single fact that strands people when they get it wrong. Pull it up under the headline as the one thing set larger or bolder than the body, so a passenger who reads nothing else still leaves knowing it. That is Refactoring UI's point: emphasize the one thing that matters with weight, not with more elements.
2. Make the headline name the crossing (taste). "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any ferry's page. The body already names the north landing and the market quay. Using those places in the headline would tell a reader from a search or a shared link, in one glance, that they're on the right page for their crossing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page reflows on a phone, which is likely where people read it at the landing. Check that the column and the line spacing hold up at narrow widths.
