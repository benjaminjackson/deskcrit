**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound and I found no layout or type defects. The page falls short on content. The facts a passenger acts on are vague or arrive in the wrong order, so a reader can come away with a schedule that is wrong for the day they travel.

**What would move it most**
1. **The paragraph that gives the schedule doesn't say it only applies part of the year** (defect). Paragraph 1 says flatly that the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". The winter exception only comes in paragraph 3: "one boat each hour", with the last one "before dusk". A reader who stops after the first paragraph, which is what people skimming for times do, will plan a winter trip around a 40-minute service and a late-evening last boat. Both are wrong, and the second could leave someone stranded. The fix is to bring the season into the schedule sentence: say which part of the year the 40-minute service covers, or put the summer and winter schedules side by side so the reader meets the exception where they read the rule.
2. **The times passengers will hold you to don't name a time** (defect). "Early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk" are what someone uses to decide whether they can make the last boat, and each can be read more than one way. "Before dusk" moves by more than an hour over the winter months. A reader may take "late evening" as 9 pm or 11 pm, and the wrong guess means a missed crossing. Either give the first and last departure times, or say plainly that the printed timetable is the authority and where to find it. Only you have the actual times, so I'm leaving those to you. As it stands, the closing line ("Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing") sends the reader to the landing for the one fact they came to this page for.

Lines run about 65 to 70 characters, with generous but not loose spacing between them. The ragged right edge is gentle and no paragraph ends on a lone word. - **Restraint.** No boxes, rules or decoration. A single near-black on white gives full contrast, and every element is flush to one left edge at the same margin. For a notice, that plainness reads as trustworthy.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what the page tells you. A stronger version would let the heading or a first line lead with the one thing most readers came for, when the boat runs, so that people planning a trip find the answer without reading three paragraphs. This is taste, and it rests on Krug's *Don't Make Me Think* point that pages get scanned, not read.
2. Keep the three-paragraph prose voice, but give each paragraph a short run-in label in the bold weight the page already uses: the crossing, bicycles, winter. A reader could then jump to their own case without the page turning into a form. This is taste. It uses only the weight already on the page and doesn't add another size.
