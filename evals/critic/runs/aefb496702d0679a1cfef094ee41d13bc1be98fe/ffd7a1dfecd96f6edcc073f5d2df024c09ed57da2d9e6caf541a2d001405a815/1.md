**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading, then straight down into three even paragraphs of gray-black text in a column on the left. The right half of the frame is empty. It reads as a plain information page from a ferry operator or a town, and it feels calm, tidy, and a bit like a printed notice. Nothing in the body pulls the eye, so you read it top to bottom or not at all.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page telling riders when the river ferry runs, how long the trip takes, and how bicycles travel, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound, but the page fails its main job: someone planning a crossing can't find a single time they can act on. Every fact is in prose and vague ("early morning", "before dusk"), so the reader still has to go to the landing to read the printed timetable.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no first or last departure time anywhere** (defect, in the copy). The first paragraph says boats run "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". The third says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger needs to know whether they can make the 7-something boat or the last one home, and the page doesn't say. "Before dusk" changes every day, and the reader can't hold the page to it. The second paragraph then sends them to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing", so to plan a trip they have to already be standing at the river. The fix is to replace the vague phrases with the real first and last departure times, for summer and for winter. Only the operator has those times. If they change, say where the current ones are published online.
2. **The facts a rider will act on are buried in sentences, so the page can't be scanned** (defect for this job). The page answers five questions: how often, how long, bicycles, winter changes, where to check for changes. All five sit inside three paragraphs of equal weight, and the reader has to read every line to find "every forty minutes" or "one boat each hour". Each paragraph is short, so this isn't a wall of text. The problem is that a timetable page gets scanned, not read (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). The fix is to pull the frequency, crossing time, and seasonal hours out where the eye can catch them: a short heading per paragraph (schedule, bicycles, winter) or a small table of times. The plain prose can stay for the bicycle rules.
3. **The bicycle paragraph mixes up who it's speaking to** (defect, minor in scope but it's a rule riders follow). "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" describes what happens instead of telling the reader what to do. "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" puts the rule at a distance. A cyclist reading it should come away knowing the steps: wait until foot passengers have boarded, wheel the bike on, lock it to the side rail, leave bags on it. Edit toward the reader with the words already there, for example "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bicycle on…". "At no extra charge" is useful, but it also leaves the base fare unstated. If fares are part of what riders need, that information is missing too.

**What's working.**
- The type is set well for reading. The body looks to be about 17–18 px, lines run about 65–75 characters, and the line spacing is generous without the lines drifting apart. That's comfortable for a short read, and it stays comfortable however much text gets added.
- The hierarchy is clear and calm. One bold heading in the same family as the body, a clear step down to the text, and paragraph spacing that separates the topics without boxes or rules. Keep the restraint when you add structure.
- The voice is plain and specific where it is specific: "about twelve minutes in calm weather, and a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" sets the reader's expectation honestly.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the crossing pattern lead the page. "Every forty minutes, about twelve minutes across" is the most useful line here and already in the copy. Set it as the first thing under the heading, larger or bolder, so a rider gets the answer at a glance and reads the rest only if they need it. This is taste: one number that leads beats several facts given the same weight (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*: emphasize by de-emphasizing the rest).
2. Use the empty right half for the timetable once it has real times. The column already sits on the left, so a compact table of departures beside the prose would give the page a second, scannable entry point without crowding the text. This is taste. It needs times that only the operator has.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column behaves on a phone. That's where most riders would check this page, standing at the landing.
