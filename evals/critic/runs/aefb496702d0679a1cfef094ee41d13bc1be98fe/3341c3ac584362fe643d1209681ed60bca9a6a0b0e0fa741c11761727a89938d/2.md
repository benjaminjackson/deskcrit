**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops to the paragraph, slides past three plain lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the hero of a small local ferry's website. It feels calm, clean and competent, but also bare: a column of type pushed into the upper left of a big white screen, with nothing in it that looks like water, a boat, or a place.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing screen for a local ferry service, aimed at residents and visitors deciding whether and when to take the boat. The main goal is to get them to the timetable or onto a sailing.

**Verdict.** It does the basic job. The headline promises something concrete, and there is one obvious next step. But it holds back the facts a rider came for, and the layout leaves most of the screen empty, so it feels unfinished rather than calm.

**What would move it most**
1. **It describes the service but doesn't give a rider what they need to go.** This is a defect. The paragraph says boats leave "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". A rider can't plan with that: they don't know the first boat, the last boat, or the next one. The headline says "cross the bay" but never says where to. The north landing is named, but the other end isn't. There's no fare, and "Bicycles travel free" makes you wonder what everything else costs. All of it sits behind "See the timetable", so anyone who only wanted to know "can I make the last boat home?" has to click to find out. Put the real anchors in the copy you already have: actual times in place of "early morning" and "late evening", the landing at the other end, and the fare if there is one. Those facts have to come from you. The headline already shows how well a concrete number works ("twelve calm minutes"), and the paragraph should match it. This is common sense, not a cited rule.
2. **The layout tips into the upper left, and the empty space reads as missing rather than chosen.** This is a defect. Every element sits in a column that ends around x≈790 and y≈570. The right 40% of the screen and the bottom quarter are empty, and nothing on the light side balances the heavy side (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). On a hero this bare, the empty right half looks like an image that never loaded. Either fill that side with something that carries weight, such as a picture of the crossing or a block with the next departures, or commit to the type: make it big enough and place it so the white space looks deliberate.
3. **The paragraph and the feature list are almost the same level, so the list reads as stray lines.** This is a convention. The body text looks like about 20 px and the three lines "Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board" look like about 18 px. They have the same weight, the same color, and no bullets or marks. Two sizes that close look like a mistake rather than two levels (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"), and lines that look alike get read as one group. So the list looks like the paragraph broke into fragments rather than three separate selling points. Make the list clearly its own level: set it at the body size with a mark, an icon, or a weight change, or make it plainly smaller and quieter. Don't leave it two pixels away from the body text.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "Cross the bay in twelve / calm minutes", which puts the number on one line and its unit on the next. Break before "twelve" or before "in" so "twelve calm minutes" stays on one line. This is a convention (Bringhurst, 2.4.6 on keeping a number with its unit). The heading-specific part is my judgment.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes one concrete promise, with a number and a feeling ("twelve calm minutes"), in a heavy weight set tight. It owns the page without trying. Keep that specificity.
- There's one blue button with a plain label that says exactly what it opens. It's the only saturated element besides the small brand line, so it's impossible to miss.
- Everything sits on one clean left edge at x≈160, with sensible spacing between the groups. The structure is sound, even though the screen around it is empty.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let "twelve" lead. The crossing time is the most memorable fact on the page. Giving that number more visual weight than the rest of the headline would make the page feel like it belongs to this ferry and not to any service. This is taste.
2. Builds on finding 2: make the right side the place a rider looks to plan, a glanceable block of upcoming departures from the north landing. The page would then answer "when can I go?" before anyone clicks, and the blue button becomes "see more" instead of "see anything". The times would have to come from you. This is taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The button's hover and focus states, and how the column reflows on a phone, where most riders standing at a landing will see it.
