**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes," drops down the paragraph, skims three plain lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the hero of a small ferry operator's website, meant for locals and day-trippers. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy. It also feels unfinished, because all the content sits in the left 60% of the frame and the right half is blank white.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the landing view of a ferry company's website, meant to get a casual traveler to decide to take the boat and open the timetable.

**Verdict.** The words and the order of reading work. The headline is good and the call to action is clear. What's in the way is that the page doesn't answer what a ferry rider came to find out: where the boat goes, when the next one leaves and what it costs. The empty right half makes it look like a template with something missing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts a rider acts on are missing or vague** (defect, common sense). "Cross the bay" never names the other side. "Boats leave the north landing" names a departure point that a stranger can't place. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives a frequency but no first boat, last boat or next departure. There's no fare anywhere, and "Bicycles travel free" suggests people pay. A visitor who wants to know "can I get there, and when?" has to click through to find out. The button is a fallback for that answer, and this is the only page that tells them anything. Direction: name the destination in the headline or the line below it. Replace "early morning until late evening" with the actual first and last sailing times, and add the fare if there is one. These facts belong to you; the layout already has room for them.
2. **The right half of the frame is empty, so the page tips left** (convention: Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Every element sits between x≈160 and x≈790, and nothing on the right half carries any weight. The empty space doesn't read as deliberate rest. It reads as a gap where an image or panel was supposed to go, and it makes the page look like an unfilled template. Direction: put something in that half that does a job. A picture of the boat or the crossing would sell the "calm" in the headline. A next-departures panel would also solve finding 1. Or, if it really should be empty, widen and enlarge the column so the space looks chosen. (If an image was meant to be there and didn't load, see the last section.)

**Smaller things.**
- Feature list (y≈397–464): it's set a size or so smaller than the paragraph above it, about 18 px against about 20 px by eye. The two near-identical sizes look like a slip, not a separate level (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1). Set the list at body size, or make it clearly different with weight or color. With no bullets or icons, the three lines also read like stray sentences. Give them a marker or a little more structure so they scan as a list.

**What's working.**
- The headline. "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes" gives a number with a unit and a feeling, in the viewer's own terms. Keep it as the dominant element; at this size and weight it clearly leads.
- The call to action. "See the timetable" names exactly what you get, it's the only button and it's the only strong use of blue apart from the "Coastal Ferry Co." label above the headline. Keep that discipline with color.
- A clean single column with one left edge, a comfortable line length (about 60–68 characters) and generous spacing between lines. The reading order is never in doubt.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let "twelve calm minutes" set the art direction. Whatever fills the right half should show calm water and an open deck, not a generic boat stock photo, so the picture backs up the promise the headline makes (taste).
2. Let the number lead. "Twelve" is the piece's strongest fact. Pulling it out as a large figure, or tying it visibly to "every forty minutes," would turn two numbers lost in the prose into something you get at a glance (taste; Tufte's case for letting the data speak applies loosely).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half is a hero image that didn't load. Check it in a live browser before you treat finding 2 as a design choice.
- The button's hover and focus states, and how the column reflows on a phone.
