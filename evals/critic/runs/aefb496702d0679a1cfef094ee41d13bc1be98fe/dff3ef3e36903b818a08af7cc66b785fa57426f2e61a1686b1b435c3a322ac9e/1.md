**First read.** My eye goes straight to "Changing your pickup time" and then down the four bold headings, the way you scan an FAQ. It reads as the help section for a bakery's order-and-collect service, for customers who already have an order. It feels plain, calm and useful. Then the last line, "Design review: failed, the headings are impossible to pick out", catches me out. It's set exactly like the answers, so at first it reads as part of the page.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a customer help page where people with a bakery order can quickly find out how to change, hand off, check or cancel it.

**Verdict.** The page structure works. The headings are about the easiest thing on the page to pick out, so the "failed" line's own claim is wrong. Two things get in the way: that stray review note sits on the page as if it were customer copy, and the cancellation answer, the one line customers will hold the bakery to, can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **A stray review note is set as page content** (defect, common sense; no catalog entry). The line under "Cancelling an order" uses the same face, size and color as the answers. A customer reads it as part of the page and wonders what failed and whether the bakery is broken. It also makes the whole page look unfinished. If this is an annotation on the capture and not on the page, ignore this point. If it's in the page, take it out.
2. **The cancellation deadline is ambiguous, and the answer doesn't say how to cancel** (defect). "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" can mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time on the day before. A customer who picks the second reading and cancels at 9 pm for a 9 am pickup will expect a refund the bakery may not give. Say exactly what the deadline is measured from. The answer also has no verb for the customer. "Changing your pickup time" opens with "Open your order and…", but this one never says where or how to cancel. Name the method; that fact has to come from the bakery. The second sentence, "the bread has already been baked for you", is good: it gives the reason in the customer's terms.
3. **"Along with a name" doesn't say whose name** (defect). In "Collecting for someone else", the person collecting could think they should give their own name when the counter expects the name on the order, or the other way round. Then the handoff fails at the counter, which is the exact moment this answer exists for. Name whose name it is.

**Smaller things.**
- The single-line answers ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run to about 95 characters, past Bringhurst's 45–75. Because they're only one or two lines, this barely hurts here. If any answer grows longer, narrow the text column to about 66 characters.
- In "Allergies and ingredients", "anything." sits alone on the last line, a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). A slightly narrower measure or a small copy edit will pull a second word down with it.

**What's working.**
- **The heading/answer contrast.** Bold headings at roughly twice the answer size, in the same near-black, make a clean two-level scale. Each section's heading sits closer to its own answer than to the section above, so the four answers scan as four clear blocks. Keep exactly this when you revise.
- **Headings written in the customer's words.** "Collecting for someone else" and "Cancelling an order" name the situation someone is in. They don't name a category, so people find their question without reading the answers.
- **"Changing your pickup time".** It starts with what to do, then says what happens next (orders are held for two hours). That's the model the other answers should follow.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: make every answer open with what the customer does, the way "Open your order and choose a new slot" does, then the rule, then the reason. That gives the page one voice and makes it faster to act on, since someone scanning the first words of each answer gets the action (taste; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*, on scannable pages).
2. The page shows no title or bakery name, so out of context nobody can tell whose help page this is. If the screenshot is cut off above the first heading, ignore this. If not, a page title above "Changing your pickup time" would tell customers they're in the right place (taste).
