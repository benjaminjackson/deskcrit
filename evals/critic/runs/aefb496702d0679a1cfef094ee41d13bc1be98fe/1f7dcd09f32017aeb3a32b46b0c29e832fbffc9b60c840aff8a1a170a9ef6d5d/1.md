**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month," then drops straight to the June bar. That bar towers over the other five. Next it goes to "$4.82M" at the top right. This reads as a calm, credible member update from a small credit union. It feels trustworthy and a bit plain, and the first thing it says without words is that June was huge.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public page in the member report that shows credit union members how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026, read once and quickly, not studied.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made page that mostly does its job. The layout is clean, the type has a clear order, and the chart is honest and directly labeled. The one real problem is the June bar. It is the loudest thing on the page, and the reason for it, a one-off bonus payroll, appears only in the gray footnote. So most readers will take away a story the page doesn't actually tell.

**What would move it most**

1. **June's jump has no explanation where the eye lands.** The June bar ($1150k) is almost double January's ($612k), and it is the first thing anyone sees in the chart. The headline says only "rose every month," and the reason June is so high is the last clause of the source line, in small gray type below the panel. A member reading quickly comes away thinking savings nearly doubled in six months. The total card says something much smaller: "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025." The page doesn't lie, but its biggest visual statement contradicts its own numbers until you read the fine print. The headline and the chart also tell different stories: the headline is about steady growth, and the chart is about one spike. The fix is to mark June on the chart itself, right next to the bar, with a short note that it includes the mid-year bonus payroll. A lighter shade or outline for that bar would also work, as long as it comes with the note. Keep the footnote as the source line. *Defect:* it leads the intended reader to the wrong conclusion. The principle that key points are annotated on the chart is standard practice, but its sources are not on my approved list, so I'm arguing it as common sense here.

2. **"Average deposit" means something other than what it says.** In the middle card, $214 is exactly June's $1150k divided by the 5,374 accounts that made a deposit. So it is the average *per depositing account* in June, not the average size of one deposit. A member will read "average deposit" as the typical single payment. If accounts made more than one deposit, that is a different, smaller number. Members compare figures like this with their own habits, so the label needs to be exact. Restore the missing words so the label says what was averaged over what, for example "Average deposited per account, Jun 2026." If $214 really is the average per deposit transaction, then the label is right and the numbers only match by coincidence; check which one it is. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- Chart labels and the y-axis use "$1150k" and "$1200k", while the cards use "$4.82M". Readers have to convert between units in their heads, and four-digit "k" values with no comma read awkwardly. Pick one style ($1.15M, or keep "k" but stay under 1,000). (Tufte, ch. 2, on clear labeling.)
- "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 6.1% on Jan to Jun 2025" use "on" to mean "compared with." That is British usage, and a US reader can stumble on it and briefly read "Jun 2025" as a date the change happened. "Up $11 from June 2025" says the same thing with no gap to fill.

**What's working.**
- The headline states a finding, not a topic, and the subtitle defines exactly what is counted (new money into savings, by month, Jan–Jun 2026). Keep that pairing of a claim with a definition.
- The chart is clean. Bars start at zero, there is one color, the gridlines are faint, and each bar has its value directly on it, so nothing needs a legend or guesswork.
- Every card shows its big number with a comparison to the year before, labeled with its period. That turns three bare figures into three judgments a member can make.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the "Up 6.1%" comparison lead instead of the raw total. The year-on-year rise is the honest headline number on this page, and members care more about it than $4.82M on its own. Giving it more weight in the first card would make the page's real news the first number people see. *Taste.*
2. The bottom of the card column stops about 75 px above the bottom of the chart panel, so the right side looks unfinished. Ending the cards level with the chart, or using that space on purpose (for example, for the June note), would make the two columns read as one unit. *Taste.*
