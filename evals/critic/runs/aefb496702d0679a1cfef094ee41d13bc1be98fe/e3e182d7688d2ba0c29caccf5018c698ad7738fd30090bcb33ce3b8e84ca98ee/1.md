**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline at top left, drops to the blue "Create your link" button, then runs across the four cards. It reads as a scheduling tool's landing page: clean and competent, but you've seen it a hundred times. The empty right half of the hero is what I noticed most. It looks like a picture failed to load.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the homepage of a booking-link product, meant to get small service businesses to sign up or try a live example.

**Verdict.** It works at a basic level: it's clear, readable and well spaced. But it could be anyone's page. The hero has no visual and no point of view, so half of the first screen says nothing. The audience the product is clearly built for shows up only in an unlabeled row of names at the bottom.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero's right half is empty.** From x≈660 to 1216 and from the nav down to the cards, nothing is there. All the weight sits on the left, so the page tips (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). The viewer is also never shown a booking link, a booking page or a filled calendar, which is the thing being sold. If an image or product shot belongs there and didn't load in this capture, check it in a live browser. Even then, that image has to make the headline's point, not decorate it. If nothing belongs there, either fill the space with what a guest actually sees, or give the space back to the headline and the asks so the empty area looks chosen. *Defect* if a visual is missing; otherwise *convention* (balance).

2. **The page never says who it's for. The customer row does, too late and without a label.** The headline, "Booking links that fill your calendar", could sit on any competitor's page. Then the row at the bottom ("Harbor Dental, Northfield Tutoring, Kestrel Physio, Lumen Coaching, Orchard Vets") shows the real audience: small service practices. That row has no label, is set in gray at nav size, and reads like footer links or a second menu rather than proof. A dentist skimming the hero never learns this was made for people like them. To fix it, label the row so it reads as customers, and let the hero copy name the kind of business the product serves. That fact is the maker's to supply. *Defect* (the viewer can't tell what's different here).

3. **The four cards don't follow one pattern.** In card 1, the "New" chip is filled blue and pinned to the right edge. That squeezes "Shared calendars" onto two lines, so its body text and "Learn more" sit about 30 px lower than in the other three cards. The row stops lining up across the cards (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*; Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*). The chips also mix two kinds of thing in one shape. "New" and "Beta" are status. "Auto" and "Email" describe the feature, and the body text below already says that, so those two chips cost a glance and add nothing. Give every chip one style and one position right after the title, and keep chips only for status. *Convention.*

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "fill your / calendar", splitting "your calendar". Widen the text block slightly or break after "that" so "calendar" doesn't sit alone. *Taste*: the reference entry's sources for this rule aren't confirmed.
- The four links all say "Learn more", so out of context, for example to a screen reader user tabbing through, they can't be told apart (WCAG 2.4.4). Name each card's feature in its link text, or attach a hidden label to each link.

**What's working.**
- **One primary ask, with a way to judge for yourself.** "Create your link" is the only filled button in the hero. Next to it, "See a live example" lets a skeptic see the result before committing, which is a stronger ask than a bare "Try it". Keep that pair.
- **The card copy talks about what the viewer gets.** "so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful": each line ends on what changes for the viewer, not on a feature spec. Keep writing them that way.
- **Clean type scale.** I count about four sizes with clear steps between them: headline, subhead/card title, body, and chip. Card titles differ from body text by weight as well as size.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the hero speak to the practices in the customer row. A page that admits it's for appointment-based small businesses will beat one that tries to fill everyone's calendar. *Taste.*
2. The "See a live example" link is the most persuasive thing on the page. Show a piece of that live example in the hero instead of just linking to it, so the viewer can judge the value before clicking. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the empty right half of the hero is a design choice or an asset that didn't load; check it in a live browser.
