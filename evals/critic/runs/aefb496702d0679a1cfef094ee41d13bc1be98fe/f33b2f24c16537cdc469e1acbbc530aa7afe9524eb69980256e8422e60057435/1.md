**First read.** The eye lands on "Garden show gallery," drops onto the big red tile, and then moves across a grid of flat color tiles. Each tile has a pale circle cut off at its bottom edge. It reads as a tidy gallery layout that has no pictures in it yet. It feels calm and orderly, and anonymous.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page where visitors to a garden show browse its displays, with one display (Rose arch) featured.

**Verdict.** The layout works, but as a gallery it doesn't do its job yet: there is nothing to see. Every "picture" is the same flat block with a circle, so a visitor learns what each display is called but never what it looks like. Nothing else matters as much as that.

**What would move it most**
1. **The images are placeholders, so the gallery shows nothing** (defect). All six tiles are a solid color with one pale, cropped circle: red for Rose arch, green for Herb bed, blue for Pond edge, brown for Bean rows, yellow for Glasshouse, purple for Orchard path. The viewer has to read each caption to know what a tile is, and even then can't see the climbing roses over the east gate or the reeds and water lilies. The headline promises a gallery, and the page gives a viewer no reason to linger or to go to the show. Right now the color is doing the pictures' job: the hues roughly match their subjects, but that's a mood, not content. If these are stand-ins for photos that didn't load or aren't supplied yet, this finding goes away once real photos are in. Check that in a live browser. If they are meant as final art, they have to show the subject, either as photos or as illustrations that show each display. The direction: put the real image of each display in its tile, and make sure the Rose arch tile, the largest on the page, holds the strongest image, because the layout already tells the eye to start there.

**Smaller things.**
- Right column spacing: the gap below each caption ("Thyme, sage and chives.", about 16 px) is less than twice the gap above each title (about 9 px). With 14 px gutters everywhere else, the right column reads as one stack more than as separate cards. Open up the space between cards, or tighten the space between image and title, so each card reads as one unit (Arnheim, on proximity).

**What's working.**
- **The hierarchy is clear.** The title is the largest type on the page. The Rose arch tile spans two of the three columns and gets a much larger heading and caption, so the featured display is obvious and the eye has a clear order: title, feature, then the rest.
- **One grid runs the whole page.** Three columns with even gutters, a 2/3 + 1/3 split at the top, and the hero caption ending level with the "Pond edge" caption. Keep this structure when the real images go in.
- **The type is calm and uses one family.** Each card has a bold title over a gray caption, so the two differ by weight and color, not just size, and the large title is set tight. It stays out of the pictures' way, which is right for a gallery.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Once there are real images, crop them to one rule, for example every tile framed on its subject the same way. The set will then read as one show rather than six separate pictures. Taste.
2. Give the featured tile more weight than size alone. Rose arch is the one display with a sentence-long caption. A stronger version would give it a fuller line about why it's featured, with the content to come from you, so the visitor knows why it's the lead. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the tiles are links to each display, and whether they look clickable when hovered or focused. A gallery visitor will expect to open a tile.
- Whether the flat color blocks are images that failed to load. Check in a live browser.
