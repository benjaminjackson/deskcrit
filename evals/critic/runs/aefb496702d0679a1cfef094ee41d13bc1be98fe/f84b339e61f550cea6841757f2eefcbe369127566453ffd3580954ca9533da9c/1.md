**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "Quayside ferry crossings by daylight…", and reads it top to bottom. It's a clean, confident, very large heading. Next comes a short gray-black paragraph with useful facts. Then the eye hits a last line saying the page is hard to read because of a cramped, overlapping heading. That stops you cold, because nothing on the page overlaps. It reads as a ferry operator's spring service page in a plain, competent sans serif, until that sentence makes it look unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page telling riverside residents and visitors when and how often the spring daylight ferry runs, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The type and layout are sound, and the heading is fine: it isn't cramped and nothing overlaps. But the page doesn't do its job. It's labeled a timetable and gives no times, and it ends with a sentence that runs down its own design. Those two copy problems are what's in the way; the typography isn't.

**What would move it most**
1. **The last line is stray copy that undermines the page (defect).** "The cramped, overlapping heading makes this page hard to read." sits at the bottom, set exactly like the body text, so a reader takes it as part of the message. It's also false, since the heading lines clear each other with room to spare. Readers will be confused, or decide the operator published a draft, and that costs trust in the facts above it. It looks like a review note or test string that got into the content. Common sense, and Nielsen's heuristic 8 (aesthetic and minimalist design) says every element should support the reader's task. Fix: delete it.
2. **It's called a timetable but holds no timetable (defect).** The "Spring timetable" eyebrow and the headline promise a schedule. The only usable facts are "every forty minutes" and "about twelve minutes". A rider can't tell when the first or last crossing leaves, what "by daylight" means in clock times, or which dates "spring through early summer" covers. They'd have to guess or look elsewhere, which fails Krug's test that a page should explain itself at a glance. The heading also promises a timetable the page doesn't deliver (WCAG 2.4.6, headings describe their content). Fix: the operator needs to supply the actual departure times or first and last sailings, plus the season's start and end dates, and set them as the content the headline introduces. If that can't go on this page, rename the eyebrow so it doesn't promise a timetable.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline, lines 3–4: "early / summer" splits one phrase, and "summer" sits alone on the last line. Rebreak it so "early summer" stays together, for example by narrowing the measure or rebalancing the lines (Bringhurst, §2.4.2; the phrase-break rule here is common sense).
- Body paragraph: "weather." is a one-word last line. Widen the measure slightly or rebreak it so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst, §2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The scale contrast is decisive. The very large, heavy headline over a small, regular-weight body gives you an obvious place to start, and the small gray eyebrow reads clearly as a third level. Keep three levels with big steps between them.
- Everything hangs on one left edge at x=120, with generous gathered white space on the right. It's calm and easy to scan, which suits a utility page.
- The body measure looks about 70 characters, with comfortable leading, so it's easy to read.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the times lead. On a timetable page, the departure times are what riders come for. Taste: give them the visual weight the headline now holds alone, set as a clear column of times in the same typeface with tabular figures, so a rider finds their crossing in one glance.
2. Taste: put "every forty minutes" up front. It's the most useful fact on the page and currently sits halfway through a paragraph. Leading with it, as a short line or bolder phrase, answers "how long will I wait?" before the reader starts reading.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the very large headline reflows on a phone width. At this size it could break into many short lines or dominate the first screen, so check it in a narrow browser.
