**First read.** The eye lands on the title, then on the tall Growth bar in the middle. Then it compares the bars and sees Starter at about a third of Growth. It reads as a SaaS analytics report page comparing trial conversion across four plans. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, so readers will believe the picture the bars draw, and that picture is wrong.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an internal analytics page that lets product and growth people compare how well each plan's trials turn into paid accounts, and decide where to act.

**Verdict.** It's well made, but the main chart misleads. The bar axis starts at 2.5%, so a gap of under a point looks like a two-to-one difference, and anyone who takes the shape at face value will over-react. Fix the scale first. Everything after that is about making the page say what the numbers mean.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bar chart's value axis starts at 2.5%, not zero.** *(Defect; breaks a convention.)* Bar length is the message. Here Growth's bar is about 2.5 times as tall as Starter's, but the rates are 4.0% and 3.1%, so Growth is only about 29% higher. Enterprise looks about half of Growth when it's 85% of it. Someone scanning the page, or seeing it pasted into a slide, will take Starter and Enterprise as much worse than they are. Tufte's lie factor here is several times over 1. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 17.1) and Few both say bar axes start at zero. Fix: start the axis at zero so bar length matches the value. If the small differences are the point, switch to dots on a labeled non-zero scale, which Wilke allows for that purpose. The value labels above the bars are already there and will carry the precision either way.
2. **Nothing tells the reader whether these rates are good, bad or moving.** *(Defect.)* Each rate stands alone. There is no previous quarter, no target and no overall rate across all plans, so a reader can rank the plans but can't tell whether 3.1% is a problem. The table makes it worse. The gaps between plans are under a point, and Enterprise's rate rests on 38 paid accounts, so the reader can't tell a real difference from noise. Fix: put a comparison next to the rates, such as last quarter's rate per plan, the target, or the all-plans rate as a reference line (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). You'd supply which one. Also consider showing which differences are within normal variation at these sample sizes.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** *(Taste, which I'd argue hard for.)* "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells the reader what the chart is, and the subtitle already explains the measure carefully. Neither says what the data shows, so each reader works it out alone and may reach different conclusions. Once the axis is honest, the shape will say something different from what it says now. The title should state that finding in words, so the page and the chart agree. You know which finding matters, so you'd write it.

**Smaller things.**
- "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" on the right sits a few pixels off the baseline of the main title on the left, a near-miss across the two columns (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Put both on one baseline, or move one clearly off it.

**What's working.**
- **The supporting text is careful.** The subtitle defines the metric ("within 30 days"), the Definition block says how open trials are counted, and the source line gives the exact date range. Keep all of it. It's why the page can be trusted, and it's what makes the axis problem worth fixing.
- **The table and the chart agree.** 286/9,210, 185/5,140, 118/2,950 and 38/1,120 work out to the plotted 3.1%, 3.6%, 4.0% and 3.4%. The table holds the raw counts behind the rates instead of repeating them. That's the right split between a chart for comparing and a table for exact values.
- **The chart is pared down.** There's one hue, labels sit directly on the bars, the gridlines are faint, and the plans keep their tier order rather than being sorted by value. Keep the restraint when you redo the axis.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: put a rate column in the "Trials by plan" table next to Trials and Paid. Then the table and the chart line up row for row, and a reader checking one plan doesn't have to divide in their head. *(Taste.)*
2. The right column ends at the Definition block, leaving the lower half empty, while the chart runs the full height on the left. If you add a comparison, that empty space is the natural home for it, so the page answers "is this good?" without scrolling. *(Taste: it uses space the page already has.)*
