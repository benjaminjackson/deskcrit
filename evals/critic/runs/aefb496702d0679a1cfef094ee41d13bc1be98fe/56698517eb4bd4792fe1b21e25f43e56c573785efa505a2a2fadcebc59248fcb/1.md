**First read.** The eye goes straight to the color blocks. It lands on the red and the mustard yellow because they're loudest, then runs across six identical pale circles. It reads as a garden show's gallery with none of the photos loaded. The page heading "Garden show gallery" is small enough that you notice it last. The page feels like a wireframe, not a finished page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page where people visiting or planning a visit to a garden show browse its areas by picture.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. A gallery is its pictures, and there are none here: six flat color tiles with a circle in each tell you nothing about a rose arch or a herb bed. Every other problem comes second to that one.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures are placeholders** (defect). All six tiles are a solid color with a pale circle centered in it and cut flat by the tile's bottom edge. The viewer reads "Climbing roses over the east gate" and sees a red rectangle, so the caption makes a promise the image doesn't keep and there's no reason to look closer. If real photos are meant to load here, this is a capture problem and it's fine once they show. If this is the intended art, it fails on several counts: the circle does nothing (taking it out would lose nothing), its pale tint barely separates from its field, and it's cut at the tile edge as if by accident. The fix is to put a real photo of each area in each tile, cropped to show its subject. Any illustration that stands in has to show the specific place.
2. **Nothing leads, and the type levels are upside down** (defect, convention). The page heading looks about 20 px. The card titles ("Rose arch", "Herb bed"…) look about 22 px and just as bold, so the label for the whole page is smaller than the label for one tile. With six equal tiles below it, nothing tells the eye where to start, and it starts on whichever color is loudest. Tschichold asks that the order of importance show through differences of size. Make the page heading clearly the largest text, well above the card titles and not a few pixels off. If one area is the highlight of the show, let it take a larger tile.
3. **Six unrelated saturated colors fill half the page** (convention: Itten, *The Art of Color*; Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*). Brick red, sage green, steel blue, olive brown, mustard and purple each fill a big block at full strength. None of them means anything, so the page looks random rather than designed, and the purple and mustard sit oddly with a garden. Real photos would replace most of this. Whatever color stays as frame or fallback should come from one small, muted set.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Rose arch" title and description sit about 2–4 px higher and closer to the image than the other five captions, so row one's baselines don't line up across the columns. Space all six captions the same way.

**What's working.**
- The captions are plain and specific, with no marketing filler: "Thyme, sage and chives." and "Tomatoes in late July." tell you exactly what you'll find. Keep that voice.
- Each caption sits tight under its image, and there's a clear gap before the next row, so every tile reads as one unit.
- The three-column grid with equal gutters and a clean left margin is a sound frame for a photo gallery.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: let the descriptions shape the photos. "Climbing roses over the east gate" and "Plum and pear trees" each name a specific view, so shoot or choose each picture to show that view, not a generic plant close-up. Then picture and caption make one idea instead of the caption explaining the picture (taste).
2. The descriptions already hold the details a visitor plans around: a place ("east gate") and a time ("late July"). Where the maker has those facts for every area, giving each card one consistent detail line, such as where it is or when it's at its best, would turn the gallery into something a visitor can use to plan a route. Which details to show is for the maker to decide (taste).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the tiles are placeholders by design or photos that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check the live page.
- Whether the tiles are links, and what their hover and focus states look like.
