**First read.** The eye goes first to the tall blue Organic search bar, then to the title, and then hops along the bars by color. Orange Referral and red Paid social pull almost as hard as the blue, so no single bar is the story. The real point is in the note on the right, "What changed this quarter", and you find it last. It reads as a calm, competent internal growth report. It also looks generic.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal Growth page that tells a marketing or growth team where Q3 sign-ups came from and what changes because of it in Q4.

**Verdict.** It's clean, honest, and easy to read, but it doesn't tell its own story. The chart gives every channel equal, meaningless color, and the finding sits in a side column. A viewer sees "six channels, sorted" rather than "organic search grew, and events lose their budget."

**What would move it most**
1. **Six hues that mean nothing hide the two bars the page is about.** Every bar is a different saturated color: blue, orange, red, teal, green, purple. The colors don't encode anything, because the bars are already labeled on the axis. Orange and red are the warmest, loudest colors, so they grab the eye on Referral and Paid social, which the note never mentions. Meanwhile Organic search (the headline claim) and Events (the budget being cut) get no more emphasis than anything else. Red and green also carry status meaning, so the red Paid social bar reads as "bad" and the green Podcast ads bar as "good," which the data doesn't say. This is a **convention** break that costs the viewer here: Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 19.1) both say a single series should use one color, and status colors shouldn't be used as decoration (Apple HIG: Color; Material Design). Direction: draw all six bars in one neutral color. Then save one accent for the bar the reader must notice, which is Organic search, and possibly a second, quieter mark for Events, since the note's Q4 decision is about it.
2. **The title names a topic, and the finding is set like an afterthought.** "New sign-ups by channel" tells you what the chart is, not what happened. The sentence that matters, "Organic search brought a third of all new accounts, up from a quarter in Q2…", is in small body text in the far right column. It starts at a height that lines up with nothing in the chart, under a small bold label. Anyone who only skims the title and the bars leaves with a ranking, not the change. The Q2 comparison, the one thing that turns "biggest" into "growing," doesn't appear in the chart at all. This is **taste**, and common sense: a report page earns its place by saying what changed, and here the reader has to assemble that from two places. Direction: let the title or subtitle carry the finding using the note's own words, and annotate the Organic search bar with its share and the Q2 share the note already gives. Keep the side note for the Q4 decision.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the two findings.

**What's working.**
- **Honest, sorted chart.** The bars are sorted by value, start at zero, and carry direct value labels. The gridlines are faint, so the data carries the ink.
- **The source line does real work.** It gives the period (1 July to 30 September) and the total (5,400), so the reader can check the note's "a third" (1,840) and "fewer than 700" (410 + 260 = 670) for themselves. Keep it.
- **The note's copy is specific and complete.** Each sentence says what happened, by how much, why, and what follows, with no gaps a stranger would have to fill.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: commit to the blue that's already on Organic search as the page's only data color. Every other bar goes gray, and the same blue marks "Organic search" in the side note. Then color alone links the claim to its bar. **Taste**: for a growth team skimming several pages, one color repeated is the fastest way to link the claim and the evidence.
2. Drop the y-axis and its gridlines. Every bar already carries its exact value, so the scale repeats what the labels say. Tufte's data-ink argument (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*) supports this, and it gives the accent bar even more room to lead. **Taste.**
