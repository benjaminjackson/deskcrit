**Verdict.** It's readable and calm, but it isn't doing its job well. The centered body text fights the left-set headline and slows reading, and the copy leaves the times a passenger plans around vague. **What would move it most**
1. **The times you plan around aren't stated.** This is a defect, in the copy. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the exact lines a passenger acts on, and neither gives a time. One reader takes "late evening" as 9 pm and another as 11 pm. "Before dusk" moves by hours across the winter, so whoever guesses late misses the last boat. The page then sends the reader to printed timetables "in the shelter at each landing", which means the information is only there once they're already at the quay. Direction: put the first and last departure times, and the winter last-crossing time or the rule that sets it, in the text. Those are facts only you have. If they really do change week to week, say where to check them before leaving home.
2. **The body is centered under a left-aligned headline.** This is a convention break, and here it costs something. All three paragraphs are four lines each and set centered. Every line starts at a different point (x≈124 to 158), so after each line the eye has to hunt for the start of the next one. A notice like this is meant to be skimmed, so that slows exactly the reading it exists for (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.3). It also breaks the page's only structure. The headline hangs from x≈120 and the body from a center axis around x≈440, and the paragraph starts land a few pixels off the headline's edge. That reads as a near-miss rather than a choice (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"; Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Direction: set the paragraphs flush left on the headline's left edge, so the page has one edge and the text reads straight down.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Let the crossing frequency and the last-crossing time lead each paragraph, ahead of the prose, so a passenger checking their phone at the landing gets the answer in the first words. *Taste:* on a schedule page the number is the content, and the sentences around it are context.

A short bold label at the start of each, in the headline's weight, would let a cyclist go straight to their paragraph. *Taste:* it adds a third level of hierarchy without adding a new size (Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*: bolder, not bigger).
