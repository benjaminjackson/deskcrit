**First read.** My eye lands on "Tell us about your project", then drops right to the white card and goes down three empty fields to the blue "Send message" button. In about two seconds it reads as a contact page for a small agency. It feels calm, tidy and anonymous. Swap the wordmark and it could belong to any studio, accountant or SaaS company.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the contact page of a design studio's website, where potential clients describe a project and send it.

**Verdict.** It does the basic job: you can tell what the page is, the form is easy to see, and there is one clear action. The biggest problem is that the page asks for information it gives no place to put. The intro asks for "a budget range and a rough timeline", but the form only has Name, Email and Message. So briefs arrive incomplete, and the studio's own page shows none of its craft.

**What would move it most**
1. **The intro asks for a budget and timeline, but the form has no fields for them.** The intro says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them". The visitor has to remember that while typing in a blank Message box on the other side of the page, and many won't. The studio then gets vague messages and has to write back asking, which uses up part of the "two working days" it just promised. This is the problem Nielsen's "recognition rather than recall" heuristic describes: what the user needs should be visible where they act, not held in memory. **Defect.** Fix: either give budget and timeline their own optional fields in the form, or put the hint inside the form next to the Message box. Fields are better, because they also tell the visitor what kind of answer the studio expects.
2. **The page doesn't look like a design studio made it.** The gray background, centered white card, stock-blue button and underlined blue links are the default contact-page pattern. For a studio, the contact page is often the last thing a prospect sees before deciding to write, and nothing here suggests a point of view. That costs the most on this site in particular. **Taste**, argued from the page's job: a prospect reads the studio's own pages as a sample of its work.
3. **The required fields aren't marked.** None of the three labels says whether it's required or optional. Users find out by submitting and getting an error. With only three fields that costs less than it would on a long form, but if you add budget and timeline fields (finding 1), the visitor has to know those are optional or they'll hesitate. **Convention** (Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group, "Marking Required Fields in Forms"). Fix: put an asterisk after each required label and explain it once, or write "optional" after the optional ones.

**Smaller things.**
- Navigation: "Contact" looks exactly like Work, Services and About, so nothing shows you're on that page. Mark the current item with one cue, such as bold or a bar (Nielsen 1; Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Input borders: the pale gray outlines on white look like they're below 3:1 against the white. Measure them, and darken them if they fail (WCAG 1.4.11).
- Grid: the form card stops at about x=1068, but the nav ends at x=1224. That leaves a strip about 210 px wide on the right that lines up with nothing. Either run the card to the right margin or line the card up with something else on the page.
- Proportion and space: the left column (about 400 px), the card (about 560 px) and the leftover right strip don't follow any single ratio. Below the card, about 180 px of empty band then leads to a footer that just floats. The space looks left over rather than placed.
- Headline break: "Tell us about your / project" leaves one word alone on the second line, splitting "your project". Adjust the column width or the break so the line splits at a phrase.

**What's working.**
- **The headline is clearly dominant.** It's the only large, heavy type on the page, so the eye knows where to start and the page needs no further explanation. Keep that single dominant element.
- **The intro states a real promise:** "We read every message and reply within two working days." It gives the visitor a specific promise to rely on instead of "we'll be in touch". Keep it near the form.
- **The form is set up correctly:** one column, labels above the fields in bold, a Message box big enough for real text, and one primary button. Nothing in the form fights the action.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: commit harder to the one typeface already on the page. The headline and wordmark already use a heavy grotesque. Make the headline much bigger and let it carry the left column, so the page has some presence without adding anything new. Studio pages are judged on type, so let the type show the craft. (Taste)
2. Make "reply within two working days" the most visible promise on the page, placed directly above or beside the button. A prospect's real worry when writing to a studio is being ignored, and that line answers it at the moment they decide to send. (Taste)
3. Use the empty space in the left column for the kind of proof only the studio has, such as a recent project, a client name or a person who answers these messages. That shows the visitor who they're writing to. The content has to come from you; I'm only naming the kind. (Taste)

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The focus ring on the fields and button. What happens when a field fails validation, and the error wording. Whether the Email field uses an email input type. How the two columns stack on a phone.
