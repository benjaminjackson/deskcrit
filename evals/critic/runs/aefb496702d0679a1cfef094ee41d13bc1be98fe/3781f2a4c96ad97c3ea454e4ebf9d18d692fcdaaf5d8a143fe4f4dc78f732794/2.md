**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project," moves right to the white card, runs down the three fields, and stops on the blue "Send message" button. It reads at once as a small studio's contact page. It feels calm, tidy and competent, but anonymous: the page could belong to any studio.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a contact page where a prospective client sends Fernwick Studio a project inquiry, viewed on a desktop browser.

**Verdict.** It does its job. A visitor knows what this is and what to do within a second, and nothing blocks them from sending. The main weakness is the layout: the content stops well short of the right margin the header sets, so the page looks shifted left and unfinished. The rest are small fixes.

**What would move it most**
1. **The content doesn't use the page's width.** The header runs from 56 px on the left to the "Contact" link ending at about 1224 px. The form card stops at about 1068 px, leaving an empty strip of about 156 px down the right side that nothing above or below explains. The intro column is short and light, and the heavy white card sits left of center. The whole block reads as pushed left, as if placed by eye rather than on the grid the header sets. The visitor won't name it, but it makes the page feel unfinished. **Convention** (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*: one grid sets every block's edges; Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Fix: give the content the same left and right edges as the header. Either widen the two columns so the card's right edge meets 1224, or narrow and center the whole content block and the header together. Don't leave the card on an edge of its own.

**Smaller things.**
- Top nav: "Contact" looks exactly like "Work," "Services" and "About," so nothing marks it as the current page. Give it one cue, such as bolder weight, a bar, or dark text instead of a link (Nielsen 1; NN/g, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Form: no field is marked required or optional. If all three are required, add an asterisk with a one-line legend. If not, put "(optional)" after the optional labels (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- Field borders: the input outlines look like pale gray on white, perhaps under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 requires for a field's edge. Measure the border color against white and darken it if it falls short.
- Headline: "Tell us about your / project" leaves "project" alone on the second line and splits "your project." Re-wrap so the break falls at a phrase boundary or the line fits. This is common sense, since the source for the rule is unconfirmed.

**What's working.**
- The intro copy works hard. "We read every message and reply within two working days" makes a concrete promise the visitor can hold the studio to. "Include a budget range and a rough timeline" tells them exactly what a useful message contains. Keep both lines word for word.
- The form is right for the job: one column, three fields, labels above the fields in bold, a roomy message box, and a single clear primary button whose label says what happens.
- The restraint holds together: one sans-serif family, a clear scale (headline, body, labels, small footer), and one blue that means "clickable" everywhere, with every link underlined.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1, and on the intro's "Include a budget range and a rough timeline." Right now the visitor has to remember those two asks while typing into one blank box. A stronger version would make the form itself ask for them, for example as two short optional fields or as prompts inside the message area. Then inquiries arrive complete without the visitor having to do the work. Taste.
2. The headline "Tell us about your project" is the line every studio uses. The intro's two-working-day promise is the one specific, believable thing on the page, and it is set as gray body text. Giving that promise more weight, beside or under the headline, would tell a hesitant client why writing here is safe, and serve the page's purpose better than a generic invitation. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus rings on the fields and button; what an error or a successful send looks like; how the two columns stack on a narrow screen.
