**First read.** The eye lands on "Learn to swim" straight away. That bold black headline is about four times the size of anything else on the page. It then drops to the gray subtitle, then to two small cards for other pool programs, and stops on a line addressed to "the reviewer." The page reads as an adult beginner swim class with a few other pool sessions underneath. It feels calm and clean, but it is still a draft: nothing on it lets you act, and the last thing you read is an internal note.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a landing page or section that gets nervous adult beginners to sign up for Tuesday evening swim classes.

**Verdict.** The headline works and the page is tidy, but it doesn't do its job yet. There is no way to sign up and none of the details someone needs to decide, and a leftover note to a reviewer is printed on the page where a call to action should be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page asks for nothing.** Below the subtitle there is no button, link, price, time, or place. Someone who reads "Small adult classes on Tuesday evenings" and thinks "that's me" has nowhere to go. "Tuesday evenings" has no start time, and there's no course length, cost, or class size to judge "small" by. The fix: add one primary ask right under the subtitle that names an exact next step, such as booking a place in a named session. Then put the facts someone checks before committing (time, length, price, where) next to it. Only you have those facts. An adult who is scared of the water also wants to know what the first session involves. A plain line about that, set against the experience they're dreading, would do more than any adjective. *Defect: the visitor can't act on the page.*
2. **A leftover review note sits in the one spot a reader ends on.** "Note to the reviewer: no element leads this page, so answer fail." is set at y≈516 at subtitle size, in the space where an ask would usually go. Visitors will read it as an unfinished page, and it costs trust on a page that has to reassure people. What it claims is also wrong: the headline clearly leads. Delete it. *Defect (common sense, no catalog entry): the copy isn't finished.*
3. **The type sizes below the headline almost collide.** Under the headline I count four sizes: subtitle about 22 px, the stray note about 22 px, card headings about 20 px, and card body about 16 px. The card headings ("Join the lanes", "Family hour") are only a step smaller than the subtitle. They're also regular weight, and only their darker color keeps them apart from the subtitle. The result is that the lower half has no clear second level. The secondary programs carry almost the same weight as the main pitch, and the eye doesn't rank them below it. Keep three levels with real steps between them: headline, subtitle, then the card headings set smaller or semibold at body size. Let weight do the work rather than adding another size (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"; Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*). *Convention.*

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** "Learn to swim" is short, a verb aimed at the reader, and set big and tight. It is the clear focal point and tells a stranger what this is in one glance. Keep it.
- **The subtitle copy.** "With a coach in the water beside you" speaks straight to the fear of a beginner adult and makes the promise concrete. It's the best line on the page.
- **One left edge.** Everything hangs from x=96, the two cards share a clean second column at 576, and each card heading sits tight on its own text. The structure is sound, so the fixes are about content and scale, not layout.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 1.** Make "a coach in the water beside you" the idea the whole page delivers on, since it's what sets this class apart. Whatever goes under the subtitle should show that promise rather than move on to other programs. For someone nervous about the water, a page that keeps answering "will I be safe and supported?" converts better than a list of offerings. *Taste.*
2. **"Join the lanes" and "Family hour."** Decide whether they belong on this page at all. They serve different people: confident swimmers and parents. Next to a beginner class they dilute the message. If they stay, mark them clearly as "also at the pool," below the ask. *Taste.*
