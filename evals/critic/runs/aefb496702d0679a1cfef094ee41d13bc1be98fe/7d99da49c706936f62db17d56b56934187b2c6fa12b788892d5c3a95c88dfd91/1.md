**First read.** The eye lands top left on "Changing your pickup time" only because it comes first. From there it reads straight down like one long paragraph, because the four headings look like more body text. It reads as the help section of a small bakery's pre-order service. It feels calm and plain, but unfinished: nothing on the page tells you what it is or where to look.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help or FAQ section for customers who have pre-ordered bread for pickup. They scan it to find the one question they have.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. A scanner can't find their question, because the headings look like the body text. The copy also leaves the two things a customer will act on, the cancel deadline and what the collector must bring, open to two readings.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings don't read as headings.** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are set in the same face, weight and dark gray as the answers below them. They also look a pixel or two *smaller* than the body. So the page reads as eight equal paragraphs, and a customer looking for "cancel" has to read from the top. That defeats the point of an FAQ. *Defect.* Dair's point is that a weak contrast is worse than none, and a heading one step off its text looks like an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). **Fix:** make each heading obviously different at a glance. Here that most likely means a clearly heavier weight, since the sizes can stay close (Dair, ch. 8a–8g and 9). The heading must never be smaller than its answer.
2. **The cancel deadline can be read two ways.** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time on the day before. A customer who guesses wrong either loses the order they meant to cancel or argues at the counter. That is the line they'll hold the bakery to. *Defect (copy).* **Fix:** say ahead of what, and give the exact cutoff. Only the bakery knows the rule. The same applies to "two hours after the chosen time": say what happens to the order after that.
3. **"Along with a name" doesn't say whose name.** The collector won't know whether to give their own name or yours. If the counter needs the orderer's name and the friend gives theirs, the bread doesn't leave the shop. *Defect (copy).* **Fix:** say whose name, e.g. "…along with your name", if that's the rule.
4. **The allergy answer points somewhere a customer ordering online can't reach.** "Every loaf has a card by the till" only helps someone already standing in the shop. Someone ordering online with an allergy learns they can't check before they pay. *Defect (copy/content).* **Fix:** say where an online customer can see ingredients before ordering, or how to ask the baker without coming in. That information has to come from the bakery.

**Smaller things.**
- Two answers ("Give the person…" and "Orders can be cancelled…") run as single lines of about 95 characters, past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the text column by roughly a fifth.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergy answer. A narrower column or a small edit to the line should fix it.
- The section has no title, so a viewer landing here can't tell whose help page this is or what it covers. This is fine if a title sits just above the capture. Otherwise add one that names the bakery's ordering help.
- "Open your order" doesn't say where: in the confirmation email, or in an account on the site? Name the place.

**What's working.**
- The spacing already groups the content. Each heading sits about 10 px above its answer and about 25 px below the previous one, so the pairs hold together. Keep that ratio when the headings get heavier.
- The voice is plain and human. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule in the customer's terms instead of quoting policy. Keep that tone when tightening the deadlines.
- One typeface, one dark gray on white, and one left edge make it quiet and easy to read.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Taste.* Builds on finding 1: let the four questions do more of the scanning work. Phrase each heading as the question the customer would actually ask ("Changing your pickup time" → "Can I change my pickup time?"). A scanner then matches their own question to the heading without reading the answers. Krug's point is that a page should be self-evident at a glance.
2. *Taste.* "Cancelling an order" holds the one rule that costs the customer money. Put it first, or give its cutoff a little extra weight in the answer. The deadline is the line people most need to find quickly.
