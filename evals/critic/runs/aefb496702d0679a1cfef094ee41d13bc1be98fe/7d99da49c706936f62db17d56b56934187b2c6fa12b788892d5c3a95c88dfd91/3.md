**First read.** I can't tell where to start. There are eight lines of near-black text at almost the same size and weight, sitting top-left with a lot of white below and to the right. The four headings don't stand out, so the page reads as one block of plain text. You only learn that it's help for a bakery's order pickup by reading the sentences. It feels plain and calm, but unfinished, like a draft before anyone styled it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an FAQ or help panel for customers of a bakery that takes orders online, who come here to check one specific thing (a pickup change, cancelling, allergies) and leave.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The copy is mostly clear, but the page can't be scanned. The headings look the same as the answers and nothing names the page, so a customer has to read all of it to find their one question. The cancellation line, which is the one customers will hold the bakery to, can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **Make the headings look different from the answers** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are set in the same regular weight and the same dark gray as the answers. They're about the same size, or slightly smaller. On an FAQ, the questions are what people scan. Here they don't register as questions, so a customer looking for "cancel" has to read the page line by line. Dair, *Design with Type* (chapters 8 and 8a–8g): when two things are meant to differ, the difference has to be obvious at a glance, and a one-step change looks like an accident. Fix: set the headings apart clearly, usually with a heavier weight (semibold or bold) at the body size or one clear step larger, so all four questions can be read top to bottom on their own. The spacing already works: each heading sits about 10 px above its answer and about 35 px below the previous answer, so the groups will read correctly once the headings stand out.
2. **Make the cancellation deadline exact** (defect, copy). "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say a day ahead of what, or when that day ends. It could mean 24 hours before the chosen pickup time, or any time on the day before pickup. A customer who reads it the generous way and tries to cancel the evening before a morning pickup will be refused, and they'll blame the bakery. Fix: state the cutoff against the pickup slot, with the exact time if the rule has one. Only the bakery knows which reading is correct. "Along with a name" in the pickup paragraph has a smaller version of the same problem: it doesn't say whether that's the buyer's name or the collector's.
3. **Give the page a title** (defect). Nothing above "Changing your pickup time" says what this page is or whose it is. Someone landing here from a link or a search has to read a full answer before they know it's pickup help for this bakery. Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: a page should be self-evident within seconds. Fix: add one title above the list that names the topic, and make it the element that clearly dominates the page (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). That also gives the eye a place to start.
4. **Shorten the lines** (convention). The answers run across about 820 px, roughly 95–100 characters per line ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…"). Bringhurst puts the comfortable range at 45–75. With one-line answers the cost is small, but the eye has a long return trip, and longer answers will feel tiring. Fix: narrow the text column to about 600–650 px at this size. The page has plenty of empty space, so nothing else has to give.

**Smaller things.**
- The allergies answer leaves "anything." alone on its last line. Narrowing the column will probably fix this, or rewrap it by hand (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The spacing within and between groups: each heading sits clearly closer to its own answer than to the one above. Keep those ratios when you restyle the headings.
- The answers are written as instructions to the reader ("Open your order and choose a new slot…", "Give the person your order number"). Each one says who does what, which is right for help copy.
- "After that the bread has already been baked for you" explains a rule in the customer's terms rather than just stating it. It turns a refusal into a reason.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Taste: make the four questions the visible structure of the page. Set them so they read as a short index on their own, phrased the way a customer would ask them. That fits a page people visit for one answer each. Builds on finding 1 only in that it goes past styling to the wording of the headings.
2. Taste: let the two numbers that commit the bakery, "two hours" in the pickup answer and the cancellation cutoff, carry their own weight (bold or a separate short line) so a customer skimming for the rule finds the number first. This applies only once finding 2 has made the cutoff exact.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this is the whole page or a panel inside a larger one, which would change how much finding 3 matters. Also whether the headings expand or collapse their answers. If they're interactive, they need to look clickable.
