**First read.** The eye lands on the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time", and then just reads down. Nothing stops it along the way. There's no title, and every line is the same dark gray at about the same size and the same regular weight. So the page reads as one long paragraph of eight lines rather than four questions with answers. It feels calm and plain. It also feels unfinished, like a text dump.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help or FAQ section for a bakery's order-ahead service. A customer with one specific question (move my pickup, send a friend, check allergens, cancel) should find their question and get the answer in seconds.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The copy is mostly clear and human. But the questions don't look different from the answers, so a customer can't scan for their question and has to read everything. The cancellation deadline can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings look the same as the answers.** This is a defect. "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" use the same face, weight and color as the text under them, and they look at most a pixel or two smaller. Only the extra space above each one marks it as a heading. Someone looking for "can I cancel?" can't skip down the left edge to "Cancelling"; they read from the top. Dair's point applies directly: a contrast that isn't obvious at a glance looks like an accident (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). The page also has nothing that sits above the rest (Tschichold, *The New Typography*), and there's no title saying what the list is. Fix: make each heading clearly different from its answer in a way you see at a glance. Usually that's a heavier weight and a modest step up in size, not size alone (Dair, ch. 8a–8g and 9). If this isn't already sitting under a page title, add one that names the section so the eye has somewhere to start.

2. **"Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" can be read two ways.** This is a defect. It's a deadline, the one line here a customer will act on and later hold the bakery to. "A day ahead" could mean 24 hours before the chosen pickup time, or any time on the day before. Someone who reads it the second way and tries to cancel at 9 pm the night before an 8 am pickup will be refused and will feel misled. Fix: state the exact cutoff, either the number of hours before pickup or a clock time on the day before. Only the bakery knows which one is true. The same goes for "along with a name" under "Collecting for someone else". Whose name: the person who ordered, or the person collecting? Restoring that one word stops a friend from being turned away at the counter.

3. **The lines are too long.** This is a convention, and the cost here is small but real. The text column runs to about x≈980, and full lines like "Give the person your order number. They will need to show it at the counter along with a name." are around 95–100 characters. Bringhurst puts the comfortable range at 45 to 75 (*Elements*, §2.1.2). With answers only one or two lines long, the cost is lower than it would be in a long article. But the long lines also leave a wide empty strip on the right, which makes the block look like it's drifting. Fix: narrow the measure to about 65–70 characters.

**Smaller things.**
- "Allergies and ingredients": "anything." is left alone on the second line (a runt, Bringhurst §2.4.2). Narrowing the measure will likely re-break it. If not, adjust that line by hand.
- "Ask the baker if you are unsure about anything" is a soft line to give on allergies. The answer doesn't say whether the bakery handles common allergens or can guarantee anything. That's a fact only the maker has, so name it if it applies.

**What's working.**
- The spacing groups things correctly. The gap between a heading and its answer (about 8 px) is clearly smaller than the gap between items (about 35 px), so each pair stays together. Keep that ratio when you make the headings stronger.
- The copy talks to the customer in plain words and ends on what matters to them: "After that the bread has already been baked for you" gives the reason behind the rule instead of just stating policy. Keep that voice.
- The text is dark gray on white in a plain, readable sans serif at a comfortable size. Contrast looks fine.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1. Phrase the headings the way a customer would ask them: "Changing your pickup time" works already, but "Allergies and ingredients" names a topic rather than a need. Customers come with a question in mind, so headings that match their wording are found faster. This is taste.
