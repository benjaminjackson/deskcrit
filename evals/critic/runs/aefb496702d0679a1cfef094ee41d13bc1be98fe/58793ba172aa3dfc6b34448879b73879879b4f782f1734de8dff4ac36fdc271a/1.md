**First read.** The eye lands on "Low-light houseplants" and drops to the row of four cards. Then the four "Add to cart" buttons pull it sideways: teal, purple, orange, blue. They are the loudest thing on the page. It reads as a calm, tidy plant-shop category page, except that the buttons look like they came from four different sites, and the four plant drawings are so alike that the cards blur together.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a category page in an online plant shop, where someone with a dim room compares four plants and adds one to the cart.

**Verdict.** The structure is solid: a clear heading, an even grid, and tidy cards. But two things undercut the job. The same button appears in four unrelated colors, and the pictures don't show the plants being sold. Fix the button color first: it's the cheapest change and the most visible flaw.

**What would move it most**
1. **The same button in four colors** (defect). The four "Add to cart" buttons do exactly the same thing but are teal, purple, orange, and blue. Shoppers will read the difference as meaning something: a sale, a category, a different kind of purchase. There is no such meaning, so they spend a beat looking for it. The buttons are also the most saturated things on the page, so they beat the plants and prices for attention, and none of the four hues belongs to the earthy green, terracotta, and gray palette the rest of the page sets up. The row looks assembled, not designed. Elements that do the same job should share one color, and a different color should be kept for a different role or state (Material Design 3, color roles; Apple HIG, "Color"; Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind follow one program). Fix: give all four buttons one shared color. The plant green or the pot terracotta would tie the buttons to the brand instead of fighting it.
2. **The pictures don't show what's for sale** (defect). All four drawings are the same template: a terracotta pot with two or three generic leaves. The snake plant has no upright sword leaves. The pothos, whose own spec line says "trails to 6 ft", is drawn as two upright leaves. On a shop page, the picture is how a shopper checks they're buying the plant they mean and decides which one they want in their room. Here the pictures can't do either, and they read as placeholders. Fix: show each actual plant, either as photos or as drawings that capture each plant's real shape, so the four cards look different from across the room.

**Smaller things**
- Top navigation: "Houseplants" looks exactly like the other links, so nothing says this page belongs to it. Mark the current section with one cue, such as bolder weight, an underline, or the accent color (Nielsen heuristic 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Orange "Cast iron plant" button: white bold 16 px text on that orange looks close to 4.3:1, just under WCAG 1.4.3's 4.5:1 for text this size. Measure it. This goes away if finding 1 moves all buttons to one darker color.
- Card spec lines: the page sells on low light, but each card gives only pot size and growth. A shopper choosing between the four can't see how they differ on light or care, so they have to open each one to find out. Add whichever light or care detail sets each plant apart; the maker has to supply that fact. This is common sense, not a cited rule.

**What's working.**
- The headline and subhead say exactly who this page is for. "More than six feet from a window" is a concrete test a shopper can check against their own room, which is far better than "perfect for low light." Keep that specificity.
- The cards follow one clean structure: picture, name, spec, price, button, with even gutters and matching baselines across all four. The eye can compare prices straight across the row. Keep that structure when fixing the color and pictures.
- Type is one sans-serif family in a few well-separated levels, with bold carrying the name and price. That's quiet and correct.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 1: commit to the plant green as the one accent color for the buttons, the cart, and the current nav item. On a page selling plants, owning the leaf color makes the whole shop feel like one brand instead of a template. (Taste.)
2. The pale gray-green picture panels are a nice, soft stage. A stronger version would put the plant pictures in charge of the card by giving them more height relative to the text. People buy plants on looks, and the picture is what earns the click. (Taste.)

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The hover, pressed, and focus states of the "Add to cart" buttons and nav links. On a buying page, check that keyboard focus is clearly visible on the buttons.
