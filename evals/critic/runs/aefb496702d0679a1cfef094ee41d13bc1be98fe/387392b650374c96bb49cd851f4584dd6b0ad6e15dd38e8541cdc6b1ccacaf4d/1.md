**First read.** The eye lands on "New sign-ups by channel," drops to the tall Organic search bar, and walks down the staircase of bars from left to right. Then it jumps to the short note on the right. This reads as a quarterly growth report in a SaaS admin tool, for someone deciding where marketing money goes. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal growth page that tells a marketing or leadership reader which channels brought Q3 sign-ups and what changes next quarter.

**Verdict.** This is a good, clean piece that does its main job: the ranking of channels is clear in about two seconds. What holds it back is that its story is about change ("up from a quarter in Q2," "the events budget moves"), but the chart shows only Q3. The reader has to take the main claim on trust instead of seeing it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart can't show the change the note is about.** The right-hand panel is headed "What changed this quarter," and its first claim is that Organic search rose from a quarter of sign-ups to a third. The chart has no Q2 values at all, so that rise can't be seen anywhere on the page. The same goes for the budget decision in the second paragraph: the reader sees that events are small this quarter, but not whether they've always been small or just fell. A headline number needs a comparison, such as the previous period or a target, before a reader can tell good from bad (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Here the comparison exists, but only as a phrase in the side note. The usual fix is to put the previous quarter on the chart itself, for example as a quiet gray mark or bar next to each Q3 bar, or as a change figure under each value label. Then the note points at something you can check. *Defect*: the page's main claim, and the budget decision that rests on it, can't be checked from the page.

**Smaller things.**
- The right-panel heading "What changed this quarter" sits about 20 px above the chart's top gridline (the "2,000" line) and lines up with nothing on the left. It looks like a near-miss, not a choice (Arnheim on ambiguous placement). Line it up with the chart's top edge, or with the subtitle.

**What's working.**
- **Direct value labels on every bar** (1,840 … 260), combined with bars sorted by size. You read exact numbers and the rank order at once, with no legend and no squinting at the axis. Keep this.
- **One muted blue, very pale gridlines, and a gray source line** that gives the date range and the total (5,400, which matches the sum of the bars). Almost all of the ink is data, and the source line answers "of what, when?" before anyone asks.
- **The note's copy is concrete and checkable.** "A third," "fewer than 700 accounts" (410 + 260 = 670), and a named consequence ("the events budget moves to referrals in Q4"). That kind of sentence turns a chart into a decision.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Taste: the title "New sign-ups by channel" names the topic, but the finding is in the side note. On a page that exists to support a decision, a title that states the finding gets the reader to the point before they reach the bars. Use the note's own first claim, edited, rather than new content.
2. Taste: the note talks about two groups: Organic search (up), and Podcast ads plus Events (being cut). Right now all six bars are the same blue, so the chart doesn't point at either group. Keep the blue for the bars the note mentions and set the rest in a lighter tint or gray. Then the chart and the note make the same argument, and the eye goes from claim to evidence without searching.
