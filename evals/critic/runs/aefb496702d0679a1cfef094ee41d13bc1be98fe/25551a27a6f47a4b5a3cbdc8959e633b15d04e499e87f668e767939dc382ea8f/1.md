I critiqued the ferry page. The one real fix is the all-caps body text; the second is vague service times in the copy.

**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board", the only bold, large thing on the page. Then it drops into three paragraphs set entirely in capitals and slows right down. It reads as a passenger notice for a small river ferry. It's calm and plain, but the body sounds like a sign being shouted, which works against the friendly headline.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or panel that tells ferry passengers the schedule and the bicycle rules before they travel.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound: clear headline, a sensible line length, short paragraphs. But setting every paragraph in capitals makes the information that matters slow to read, and the copy is vague about the times people actually plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is all capitals** (defect). All three paragraphs, from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" down to "…EACH LANDING.", are set in caps. Capitals have no ascenders or descenders, so every word is a same-height rectangle and the reader has to spell their way through. Seven lines of it is a wall. Someone scanning for "how often" or "can I bring my bike" can't pick out "FORTY MINUTES" or "BICYCLES" from the words around them, because nothing differs in shape. Caps also add a shouting tone that clashes with the gentle "Before you board". Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") keeps capitals for headings shorter than one line and says not to capitalize whole paragraphs; Hochuli calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read." **Fix:** set the paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for the "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already does it well.
2. **The service times are vague** (defect, copy). These are the lines a passenger acts on, and two of them can be read more than one way:
   - "Between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing. One reader hears 6 am to 10 pm, another 8 am to 8 pm, and the one who guesses wrong misses the last boat home.
   - "In winter" gives no months. Someone traveling in late November can't tell which timetable applies.
   - "Every forty minutes" has no starting point, so you can't work out a departure from it.

   The page sends people to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which is useless to someone reading this before leaving home. **Fix:** the maker should put in the actual first and last sailing times, the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, and the minute past the hour the forty-minute cycle starts from. Better still, add a link to the timetable itself.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the two above.

**What's working.**
- **Line length.** Lines run about 65–70 characters, inside the comfortable 45–75 range (Bringhurst). The block also stays a narrow column instead of spreading across the full width. Keep that width when the text goes to lower case. Lower-case text sets narrower, so check the measure again afterwards.
- **Three clear type levels.** A small letterspaced gray label, a large bold headline, and plain body text. Each size has an obvious job and the steps between them are large. Don't add a fourth size.
- **Paragraphs by topic.** Each paragraph covers one subject: route and timing, bicycles, winter. A reader can skip straight to the one they need.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 1.** Once the body is in mixed case, the short paragraphs could carry run-in bold lead words taken from the copy itself, such as "Bicycles" and "In winter". A passenger who came for one answer could then find it in a single glance. This is taste; Refactoring UI argues for creating levels with weight rather than size, which keeps the type scale at three steps.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone. At that width, a mixed-case rewrite matters even more, because the caps block would get even taller.
