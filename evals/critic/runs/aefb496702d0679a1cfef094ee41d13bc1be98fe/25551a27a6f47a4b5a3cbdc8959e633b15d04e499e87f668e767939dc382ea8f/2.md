**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", the only bold, dark thing on the page. It skips past the small gray "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it and hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals. The capitals merge into one even gray slab, and the reader slows to near word-by-word. It reads as a tidy, calm information page for a river ferry, but the body text feels like it's being shouted.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that gives ferry passengers the practical facts before they travel: how often the boat runs, what to do with a bike, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The structure is clean, but all of the content is set in capitals, and that makes the facts a passenger came for slow and tiring to pull out. The second problem is that the times a passenger would plan around are left vague.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set entirely in capitals (defect).** All three paragraphs, eight lines in total, are uppercase at body size. Capitals have no ascenders or descenders, so every word has the same rectangular outline. The reader loses word shapes and has to read letter by letter. Skimming for one fact, like "can I bring my bike?", is much harder because nothing in the block stands out. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says don't capitalize whole paragraphs, and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read." Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for short labels like the "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already handles them well.
2. **The times a passenger acts on are vague (defect, copy).** "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone whether they can catch a 6:30 boat or a 22:00 one. "Every forty minutes" has no starting point, so the reader can't work out departure times. "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour" doesn't say which months count as winter. The last line then sends the reader to a printed timetable at the landing, which is exactly where they don't want to find out they've missed the boat. These are the lines people will plan around and hold the operator to. Fix: replace the vague phrases with the actual first and last departure times, the minutes past the hour the boats leave, and the dates the winter timetable applies, or link to the timetable on this page. Only the operator has those facts.

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- The three-step hierarchy is clear: a small, tracked, gray, bold label; a large bold heading; then body text. Each level changes size and also weight or color, so you can tell them apart at a glance.
- One shared left edge, comfortable line lengths (about 70 characters), and paragraph gaps clearly bigger than the line spacing make the page orderly and easy to scan. Keep all of this when you switch to mixed case.
- The copy is short and splits into three topics: frequency, bikes, winter. That chunking is right.

**Where it could go further.**
1. **Builds on finding 1:** give each of the three paragraphs a short lead-in that names its topic (frequency, bikes, winter), set in the same bold as the heading at body size. A passenger with one question could then jump straight to their answer. On a page meant to be checked in a hurry at the landing, that matters more than reading top to bottom. Taste; Krug (*Don't Make Me Think*) argues for exactly this kind of scannable page.
2. **The bike paragraph is the only real "before you board" instruction.** It's the only paragraph telling the reader what to do (wait for foot passengers, lock to the rail), while the others are about timetables. Either make the heading fit everything on the page or lead with the boarding instructions, so the heading's promise is kept first. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the text reflows on a phone, which is where people standing at a landing will likely read it.
