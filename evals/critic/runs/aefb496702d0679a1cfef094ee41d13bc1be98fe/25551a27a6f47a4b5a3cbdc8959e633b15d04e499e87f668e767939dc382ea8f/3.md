**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board", a bold dark heading with a small gray "PASSENGER NOTES" label above it. Then it hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, and reading slows to a crawl. The page reads as passenger information for a river ferry. It's quiet and orderly, but the caps make the body look like a notice someone is shouting.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells ferry passengers how the service runs (times, bicycles, winter changes) before they travel.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but the body text, which is the whole point of the page, is set in all capitals, so every passenger has to work to read it. Fixing that one setting would fix the page. After that, the vague service hours are the biggest gap.

**What would move it most**
1. **Body text in all capitals** (defect). All three paragraphs, from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING.", are set in caps at full text size, six lines in total. Capitals have no ascenders or descenders, so the words lose their outline shapes. The reader has to decode letter by letter, and the eye loses its place at each line break. That's the opposite of what someone checking times at a landing needs. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") keeps capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions, and says not to capitalize whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) says text in capitals is particularly hard to read. The fix is to set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for the "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already uses them correctly: short, tracked out, and set apart by gray. As a bonus, mixed case will shorten each line a little, so the lines will sit comfortably inside the 45 to 75 character range.
2. **The service hours a passenger would act on are vague** (defect, copy). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives an interval but no first or last sailing. The fact that decides whether you make the last boat is exactly what's missing. A reader might take "late evening" as 9 pm or as 11 pm, and the wrong guess leaves them stranded on the far bank. Likewise, "one boat each hour" in winter doesn't say when winter starts. The fix is to give the actual first and last departure times and the dates of the winter timetable. Only the operator has these facts. If they change too often to print, say where the current times are posted. The page already does this for the printed timetables in the shelters, so the same could be done for the online ones.

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- The heading structure is clear: a small, tracked, gray all-caps label, then a bold heading clearly larger than the text, then the body. That's three distinct levels using only size, weight, and color. Keep it.
- The text is cut into three short paragraphs, each on one topic (route and timing, bicycles, winter), with even spacing between them. Someone scanning can find the part about bicycles without reading the rest.
- The column is a sensible width, and everything hangs off one crisp left edge.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the times lead. Pull the facts people act on, the 40-minute interval, the 12-minute crossing, and the first and last sailings once the maker supplies them, out of the running text into a short list or a small set of labeled figures under the heading. A passenger at the landing could then find the answer in a second instead of reading a paragraph (taste). Tufte's case for showing the data plainly supports this.
2. "Before you board" promises a checklist, but the paragraphs are written as description ("Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded"). Speaking to the reader ("Wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock it to the rail") would make the page match its heading and turn facts into instructions people can follow (taste).
