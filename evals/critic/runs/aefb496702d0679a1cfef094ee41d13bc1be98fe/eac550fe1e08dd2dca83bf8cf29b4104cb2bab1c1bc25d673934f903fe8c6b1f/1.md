**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then reads straight down. It reads as a plain information page from a local transit service or council. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, though a little bare. It takes a moment to notice that the last line is smaller than the rest, and that line is the one that matters most.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells someone planning a river-ferry trip when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read, but it doesn't do its main job. Someone planning a trip finds no time they can plan around. The one warning that could leave them stranded, the winter timetable, is set in the smallest type on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The winter warning is the smallest text on the page.** *Defect.* "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." looks about 16 px, against about 19 px for the two paragraphs above it. It is the only line that tells the reader to do something, and the only one that can make the rest of the page wrong. Shrinking it makes it look like a footnote, so the readers who skim, which is most of them, will miss it. The size step is also too small to read as a separate level. It looks like a mismatch rather than a choice. Bringhurst, section 3.1.1, says to work from "a modest set of distinct and related intervals", and Dair, ch. 8, says a weak contrast is worse than none. Fix: set this line at body size at least. Then make it stand out from the body through weight, a label, or space, not by making it smaller.
2. **The copy gives no times anyone can plan with.** *Defect.* "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" has no first sailing, no last sailing, and no minute past the hour. The reader can't tell whether 7:10 or 7:30 makes the boat, or whether "late evening" means 8 pm or 11 pm. Missing the last boat home is the worst outcome, and the page can't prevent it. Then the winter line says timetables change but points to a noticeboard at the landing. That only works for someone who is already standing there. Fix: add the first and last departure times. If a timetable exists anywhere online, link to it. Only the maker has these facts. As it stands, the page describes the service without letting anyone use it.

**Smaller things.**
- The second paragraph is written in the third person: "Riders wheel them on… and lock them to the rail". The winter line speaks to the reader as "you". Addressing cyclists directly would make the order of boarding read as an instruction rather than a description, for example: "Wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock it to the rail along the side of the deck."
- "At no extra charge" suggests there is a fare, but the page never gives it. If fares belong on this page, say what a foot passenger pays.

**What's working.**
- The column width suits the text. Lines run up to about 74 characters with roughly 1.5 line spacing, in a plain, dark sans serif on white. Keep that column width and spacing when you add content.
- The headline is one clear step up from the body in both size and weight, so the page has an obvious place to start. Keep it that way, and don't add a third or fourth nearby size.
- Each paragraph covers one subject: the route and timing, then bicycles. That structure will scale if you give each subject a short subheading.

**Where it could go further.**
1. *Builds on finding 2.* Once the departure times exist, make them the most prominent thing under the headline, as a short list or table set apart from the prose. *Taste:* people come to a ferry page to answer "when is the next boat?", and the page should answer that before anything else.
2. The page has two topics, timing and bicycles, plus a warning. A short subheading for each, sized between the headline and the body, would let a cyclist jump straight to their part. *Taste:* Krug's point is that people scan rather than read, and labeled chunks give them something to scan.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column behaves on a phone. Also whether the empty right side is intentional or a slot for something, such as a map or photo of the landing, that didn't load. Check this in a live browser.
