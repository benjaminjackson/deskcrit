**First read.** The eye lands on "Changing your pickup time" at top left, then runs down four bold headings of the same size. It reads as a help or FAQ section for collecting bread orders. It feels calm, clean and plain, like a default docs template. Nothing on it says whose bakery this is or what page you're on.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help page for customers of one bakery who order online and collect in person. They arrive with one question and need the answer fast, before they act on it.

**Verdict.** The layout is tidy and easy to scan, so the structure is not the problem. The weak point is the words. The two answers that commit the customer to something, cancelling and collecting for someone else, can each be read two ways. The page also has no title to say what it is.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cancellation and pickup rules are ambiguous.** In "Cancelling an order", "up to a day ahead" could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot or any time on the day before. Someone who reads it the second way and cancels at 9 pm for a 7 am pickup gets refused, or charged, for bread they thought they had cancelled. In "Collecting for someone else", "along with a name" doesn't say whose name: the person who ordered or the person collecting. If the collector gives the wrong one, they may leave without the order. In "Changing your pickup time", the page says the bakery holds orders for two hours but stops there. It doesn't tell the customer what happens to their order and their money if they miss that window. *Defect.* These are the lines customers act on and will later hold the bakery to. Fix: state the deadline as a clock time relative to the slot, name whose name is needed, and finish the two-hour line with what happens next. Only the bakery has those facts.
2. **There is no title or top-level heading, so nothing leads.** All four headings are the same size and weight, about 32 px bold. The first sentence of the first answer is the only place that says this is a bakery. A customer who lands here from a search or a link has to read a paragraph to learn where they are. *Defect* (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: a page should be self-evident within seconds; Tschichold, *The New Typography*: one element seen first). Fix: add a title above the questions that names the bakery and the topic, clearly larger than the section headings. The four equal headings below it are then correct, because they are peer questions.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run too long. "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead…" and "Give the person your order number…" are about 95 characters, past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. Narrow the text column to about 65 characters. The headings can stay as they are.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line under "Allergies and ingredients", a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). A slightly narrower column or a small copy edit will pull it back.

**What's working.**
- Grouping by space. Each heading sits about 10 px above its answer, with roughly four times that gap before the next question, so every question-and-answer pair reads as one unit without rules or boxes. Keep that ratio.
- The headings name the situation the customer is in ("Collecting for someone else") rather than a category ("Collection policy"), so a scanner finds their case from the heading alone.
- The answers are short and in plain speech ("the bread has already been baked for you"). That line explains the rule from the bakery's side in a way a customer will accept.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: let the bakery's own voice carry the title. The body already sounds like a person ("Ask the baker"). A title in that same voice, rather than a generic "FAQ", would make this read as this bakery's page and not a template. *Taste.*
2. "Open your order" and "choose a new slot from the list" refer to places in the product but don't take you there. Turning them into links to the order would let a customer act straight from the answer instead of going looking. *Taste.*

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "Open your order" or any other phrase is a link. Nothing is styled as one here.
- How the column reflows on a phone, where most pickup questions are likely asked.

What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (one element dominates)
