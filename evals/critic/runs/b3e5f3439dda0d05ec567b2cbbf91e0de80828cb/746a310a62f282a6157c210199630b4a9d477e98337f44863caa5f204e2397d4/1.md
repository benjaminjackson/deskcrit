(Assumption, since nothing was given: a public release-notes page that existing users skim to find a version and see what changed.)

**Ranking**
1. Tile 1: Each version heading stands out from its notes, so a reader can jump straight to "4.1" without reading the paragraphs.
2. Tile 2: The version headings look almost the same as the notes, so the page reads as one block of text and finding a version means reading line by line.

**What decides it.**
- **Version headings against the notes.** In tile 1, "Version 4.2, 20 October" and the other two headings are bold serif. They look about 24 px against notes of about 19 px, and they're darker than the notes. In tile 2 the same headings are regular weight, in the same gray as the notes, and look only a pixel or two larger (about 20 px against 19 px). That's the case Dair warns about in *Design with Type*, chapter 8: a heading one step off from its text "looks like an accident, not a choice." Small differences leave the eye unsure whether two things are equal (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, chapter I). Here the cost is real, because the headings are how readers find their way on a release-notes page. In tile 2 nothing marks where one version ends and the next begins, apart from the gaps. This is a defect (typography.md "Weak contrast is worse than none"; art-direction.md "Visual hierarchy has distinct levels"). To fix tile 2, make the headings obviously different from the notes in weight, size, or color. Tile 1 already does that.
- **Type levels.** Tile 1 has three clear levels: the page title, the version headings, and the notes. Tile 2 effectively has two, because its headings and notes merge into one level. This comes from the same difference, not a separate one.
- **Spacing.** The spacing is about the same in both, and in both each heading sits close to its own notes. It doesn't change the ranking.

Best: tile 1
