**First read.** Both tiles are the same help article, "Change the card you pay with", with the same copy, column and spacing. The eye lands on the title in both. In tile 1 it then drops to three bold subheads that work as a table of contents for the task. In tile 2 the subheads are the same weight as the body and almost the same size, so the page below the title reads as six paragraphs of equal weight.

**Ranking**
1. Tile 1: the subheads clearly stand apart from the body, so a reader can jump straight to the step they need, such as "If the card is declined".
2. Tile 2: the subheads look like body text, so the reader has to read top to bottom to find their case.

**What decides it.**
- **Subhead contrast (defect).** There is one difference between the tiles: how the three subheads are set ("Add a new card", "Make it the default", "If the card is declined").
  - **Tile 1:** they are bold, near-black and a little larger than the body (about 22 px against 18 px at full size).
  - **Tile 2:** they are regular weight, the same color as the body and about the same size or slightly smaller (about 17 px against 18 px). Each one reads as a short first line of its paragraph, not a heading.
  - **Why it matters:** people come to a help page with one problem, and often it is the declined card. They scan for their case instead of reading the page, and in tile 2 nothing stops the eye at the third section. This is the case in Dair's "Weak contrast is worse than none" (*Design with Type*, ch. 8): "headings set in the size, weight, and color of the text, or within a pixel or two of its size." It is also the failure of "Visual hierarchy has distinct levels" (Müller-Brockmann), because tile 2 has only two levels, title and everything else, where tile 1 has three.
- **No other differences.** The breadcrumb, title, intro, measure, spacing and header are the same in both tiles. The space above each subhead is larger than the space below it in both, so each heading sits with its own text either way.

I assumed this is a self-serve help article for a customer who wants to swap their payment card or fix a declined one.

Best: tile 1
