**First read.** This is a museum exhibition page: a title, a one-line pitch, what's in the show, and the opening times, with a large image beside them. The two versions are identical except for the headings. In tile 1 they are regular-weight serif, the same face as the body. In tile 2 they are bold sans, the same face as the "Calder Museum" wordmark. In tile 1 the subheads "What you will see" and "Opening times" almost merge into the paragraphs below them. In tile 2 they stand out as signposts.

**What it's for (assumed).** A visitor deciding whether to go needs to find what's on show and when the gallery is open by scanning the page, without reading every line.

**Ranking**
1. Tile 2: Its headings differ from the body in weight and in typeface, not just size. A scanning visitor finds "Opening times" in one glance, and the page reads as three clear chunks under a title that clearly comes first.
2. Tile 1: Its headings match the body in face, weight and color and are only a few pixels larger. The subheads read as slightly bigger body lines, so the page looks like one long run of text with weak landmarks.

**What decides it**
- **How the subheads stand apart from the body.** The sizes are the same in both versions: subheads are about 26 px tall against body text of about 19 px, at x 96, y 265 and y 384.
  - In tile 1 the size is the only difference. That's a small step, so "Opening times" looks like a paragraph that happens to be larger, and a visitor scanning for the hours has to read their way to it.
  - In tile 2, bold sans against regular serif adds both a weight contrast and a typeface contrast, so the same small size step becomes obvious at a glance.
  - This is a convention, with a real cost for a page people scan for facts: Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8a–8g and 9 ("Contrast from more than size"; "Weak contrast is worse than none").
- **How strongly the title leads.** "Maps of the inland sea" is about 44 px in both. Tile 1's light serif title is elegant but sits gray on the page. Tile 2's bold title is the clear first stop and matches the bold sans wordmark, so both versions still use just two typefaces that clearly differ, with no clash. The stronger lead is a convention ("One element dominates"). Preferring the bookish serif voice of tile 1 is a matter of taste, and it doesn't outweigh how much easier tile 2 is to scan.
- **Not a difference:** both versions share the empty tan gradient where the picture should be (x 800–1220, y 106–666) and the large blank area under the text. Neither affects the choice, but both pages need a real image before they work.

Best: tile 2
