**First read.** This is the "Recent work" section of an architecture practice's site: a wordmark, a three-item nav, and three projects, each a picture with a title and a line of details. Tile 1 gives the page a starting point: a large "Recent work" heading, then a library image twice as wide as the other two. Tile 2 is three equal squares under a heading no bigger than the project titles under it, so nothing leads and the page reads as a flat row of thumbnails.

**Ranking**
1. Tile 1: it has a clear order. The heading opens the page, the library is the lead project, and the two smaller projects follow it. The page has tension and reads as edited.
2. Tile 2: the three images are the same size, and the heading is the same size as the titles below it. The eye has nowhere to land first, and the section heading doesn't sit above its contents.

**What decides it**

- **One lead project against two smaller ones (convention).** In tile 1 the library image is about 544 px wide and the other two are about 272 px each, on one grid with equal 24 px gutters. In tile 2 all three are about 363 px squares. That parity in tile 2 is exactly what "Contrast of scale gives the page tension" and "One element dominates" warn against (Hofmann, *Graphic Design Manual*; Tschichold, *The New Typography*). The viewer gets no hint of which project the practice is proudest of, and the row reads as a template gallery rather than a portfolio someone curated. Tile 1's 2:1:1 split gives the eye a start and a path. One fact only the maker has could change this: whether the library really should lead. If all three projects matter equally, the hierarchy in tile 1 points the wrong way. The layout still wins, but the lead slot should go to whichever project they want seen first.
- **The heading's place in the type scale (convention).** In tile 1, "Recent work" is about 56 px, against project titles of about 22 px and details of about 14 px. That gives three clear levels. In tile 2 the heading is about 22 px light, the same size as the project titles. Two levels collide, and the heading reads as one more caption, not the name of the section ("Visual hierarchy has distinct levels"; Bringhurst on working from a scale).
- **One cost of tile 1 (minor, common sense).** In the narrow column, "Two houses on Weir Road" runs almost to the gutter: the text ends around x=910 and the column ends at 912. The title is whole, but a slightly longer one would wrap or crowd the next column. This needs checking with real titles, but it isn't enough to flip the ranking.

Both tiles use flat color blocks where the photographs should be. The pick is judged on structure alone, and real images could shift how much weight the lead project carries.

Best: tile 1
