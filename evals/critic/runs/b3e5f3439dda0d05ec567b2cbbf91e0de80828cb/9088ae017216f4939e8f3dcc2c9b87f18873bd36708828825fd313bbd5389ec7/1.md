**First read.** This is a bus departures board for one stop (Stand C, Corn Exchange). It's for someone standing there who wants to find their bus and see if it's running. Both versions have the same title, columns, rows, type, and red status flags. The one difference is the table rules. Tile 1 boxes every cell in a near-black grid. Tile 2 keeps only pale horizontal lines between rows, with open space between columns.

**Ranking**
1. Tile 2: The rows read cleanly from left to right. The two red statuses ("4 min late", "Cancelled") stand out at once because no dark lines compete with them.
2. Tile 1: The same content sits inside a dark cage of cells. The grid is the darkest thing on the page after the bold times, so scanning a row is harder and the red flags lose some of their pull.

**What decides it.**
- **Rules versus content (convention).** In tile 1, every cell has a dark 1 px outline, vertical lines included, and those lines look about as dark as the body text. The eye gets stopped at each column edge, and the table reads as a set of boxes rather than as departures. In tile 2, the vertical lines are gone. Only thin light gray lines remain between rows, and that is all a viewer reading along one row needs. The text is now the darkest thing on the page, and the eye goes straight to the bold times and then to the red statuses, which is what a waiting passenger is looking for. Lupton (*Thinking with Type*, "Data tables," p. 157) says the rules in a table "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell." Tufte (*Visual Display*, ch. 4–5) makes the same case against non-data ink (layout.md "No rules, boxes, or ornament where space would do"). Tile 1 is the textbook case of this entry. Tile 2 is close to the fix the entry recommends.
- **Nothing else differs.** The type scale, column order, status color, and title block are the same on both tiles, so no other factor moves the ranking.

Best: tile 2
