**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and three plain lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top section of a local ferry company's home page. It feels calm, clean and orderly, but also anonymous. It's all type in the top-left of a white screen, and nothing shows a boat, the bay or the harbour.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: this is the landing screen for a passenger ferry. Its job is to get locals and visitors to plan a crossing, so they need to know when the boat goes, where it goes and what it costs.

**Verdict.** The words are mostly good and the hierarchy is clear. But the page holds back the facts a ferry passenger came for and hides them behind a button, and it uses less than two-thirds of the screen. It's competent, but it underperforms: it sells the trip and then makes you click to find out if you can take it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts a traveller needs aren't on the page.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" (paragraph, y≈280–345) has no first or last sailing time. Nothing on the page says where the boat lands on the other side, what a ticket costs, or when the next boat leaves. Someone deciding whether to go now can't decide, so every visitor has to click "See the timetable" just to find out whether the ferry suits them. This is a defect, argued as common sense: for a transport service, departure times and fares are the information people came for. Fix: put the essentials right here in plain terms, such as "First boat 6:40, last boat 22:20", the destination landing, the adult fare, and ideally "Next boat 14:20". Then the button becomes a way to see the full timetable, not the only way to learn anything. The copy is also vague exactly where readers will hold the operator to it. "Early morning" and "late evening" can mean 6 or 8, and 9 or 11, and a passenger who guesses wrong is left on the jetty.
2. **The page tips into the top-left corner and doesn't show the trip.** All the content sits in a column from x=160 to about x=790 and stops at y≈570. The right ~480 px and the bottom ~230 px are blank. The whole weight of the page is on the left with nothing to balance it, so it looks unfinished, as if a picture failed to load, rather than calm (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). This is a convention, plus taste. A ferry across a bay is about as visual a product as there is: "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck" is a promise a photo could keep in one glance. Fix: put a picture of the boat or the view on the right half (or full-bleed behind the text with a calm area for the type), or a simple route map from the north landing to the far side. Either one balances the page and makes the "calm" claim visible. If the empty right side is a picture that didn't load, check it in a live browser. It still counts as a finding as captured.

**Smaller things.**
- The paragraph (~20 px) and the three feature lines (~18 px) are almost the same size, so the difference looks like a slip rather than a level. Set them the same size, or make the list clearly different (bulleted or icon-led, or a step smaller and in a muted colour) (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale").
- The headline breaks "twelve / calm minutes", which splits the number from what it counts. Rewrap so the number stays with "minutes", for example "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" (Bringhurst, section 2.4.6, on short numerical expressions).

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete, specific promise (twelve minutes, calm) instead of a slogan. Keep the number and the feeling in it.
- One clear order: brand label, headline, paragraph, list, then one primary button. Everything sits on a single left edge at x=160, so the eye moves down without any effort.
- The colour is restrained: near-black type, one deep blue for the brand label and the button. That blue suits the sea and makes the button the only coloured mass on the page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The button's hover and focus states, and how the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where most people check a ferry.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 8 yes
What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (distinct levels of text)
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 12 yes, 1 no (balance)
Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (heading line breaks)
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: skipped, none
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 31 yes, 1 no (anticipation of what the user needs next), 1 unsure (focus indicator)
