**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then down three even paragraphs in one left-hand column. It reads as a quiet, plain information page for a small local river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and a little bare, with the right half and the bottom third left empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or notice for people planning a ferry trip. It needs to tell them when the boat runs, how to bring a bicycle, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read, so the type and layout don't fail it. The words do. The page never gives the facts a passenger plans around, so someone can read every line and still not know when to get to the landing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable has no times.** This is in paragraphs one and three. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "one boat each hour" and "before dusk" don't tell you which minute past the hour, the first or last departure, or which months count as winter. The page then points you to printed timetables "in the shelter at each landing", so you only find the real schedule once you're standing on the quay. Departure times are what passengers act on and later hold the operator to, so vagueness here is a **defect**, not a matter of style. Readers will fill the gaps differently: one turns up for a boat at 7:00, another at 7:20, and in winter someone misses "the last crossing … before dusk" by guessing when dusk is. Fix: state the first and last departures from each side, the minutes past the hour, and the winter dates. Set them where the eye can pick them out, such as a short table or list placed before the prose. Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web", explains why: people scan pages like this for one fact.
2. **Lines that can be read two ways.** "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" could mean the market quay only or both landings. A passenger on the north side needs to know which before deciding whether they can get back. "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes … a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" leaves out how much longer, and that matters to anyone with a connection to catch. This is a **defect**: name the landing for every "last boat" line and give a figure for the delay, for example "up to 20 minutes".
3. **The heading describes the page rather than telling the reader something.** "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any operator's page, and the eye passes it before reaching anything useful. This is **taste**. The heading is the strongest spot on the page, so give it the fact people came for, such as which two places the ferry links and how often it sails.

**What's working.**
- The body type is a plain sans at a generous size with comfortable line spacing, in lines of about 65 to 72 characters. That's within Bringhurst's range, so the prose is effortless to read. Keep these settings when you add the timetable.
- Each paragraph covers one topic (schedule, bicycles, winter), and the gaps between them are clear and consistent. The order is right and each topic would take a subheading easily.
- The bicycle paragraph is the best copy on the page. It says who does what, when and where: wheel them on after foot passengers, lock them to the rail, leave the bags on the bike. The other paragraphs should be written to that standard.
