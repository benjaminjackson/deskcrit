**First read.** The eye goes to the bold serif headline, then straight to the underlined italic phrases, which jump out of the paragraphs like five links. On a skim you get "arrive before ten / labelled with their ingredients / three o'clock sharp / collected by four / on the field only" with nothing joining them. It reads as a calm, well-set note from an organiser to helpers. Quiet and competent, but the emphasis makes it fidgety.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a short instruction note (web page or email) telling stallholders and volunteers the rules and times for a summer fete. They should be able to skim it and remember what to do and when.

**Verdict.** The type is good, but the note doesn't do its job reliably. The emphasis is doubled up and looks like links, and the most important instruction, the raffle deadline, can be read two ways. Fix the deadline wording first, then strip the emphasis back to one signal.

**What would move it most**
1. **The raffle deadline can be read two ways (defect).** "All tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box" doesn't say who collects what. Reading A: winners must come and claim their prizes by four. Reading B: sellers must hand in all the ticket stubs by four. If a winner reads B, they may skip the four o'clock deadline and lose their prize. If a seller reads A, they may hang on to the stubs. This is the line people will hold the organiser to, so it has to say it outright, e.g. "If you win, collect your prize by four o'clock, or it goes back in the box."
2. **The emphasis is stacked and looks like links (convention).** All five phrases are italic *and* underlined. That breaks the one-signal rule: Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, p. 94 says emphasis "usually requires only one signal", and Bringhurst §3.5.1 says to change one parameter at a time. On a screen, underlined text also reads as a link (common sense, and Nielsen's long-standing advice), so readers will try to click "arrive before ten". With five phrases marked across about six lines, nothing stands out because everything does. Pick one signal, either italic or bold with no underline, and save it for the real deadlines (ten, three, four).
3. **Rules and times are buried in prose (taste).** Five instructions with three different times are packed into two paragraphs. Someone checking "when do I need to be there?" on the day has to read the whole thing again. I'd set the times as a short list (10:00 arrive, 15:00 draw, 16:00 prizes claimed), with the cake and parking rules as plain lines below. The prose voice is friendly, but the list is what people will scan.

**Smaller things.**
- "By the time the gates open": no time is given. Add the opening time so "before ten" has a point of reference.
- There's no date or place for the fete anywhere on the page. Add both under the headline, unless the surrounding context already carries them.
- The body lines look about 80–85 characters long, a bit over Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the column slightly.
- "Cakes must be labelled… and left on the trestle by the door": which door? Name it if the venue has more than one.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: two sizes, a bold headline over a regular body, with a big step between them. Keep it.
- The serif body is generously leaded with a comfortable paragraph gap. It reads easily and feels right for a village fete.
- The tone is plain and human ("Please arrive before ten so the stalls can be set up"). Instructions that give their reason get followed, so keep that.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the underlines are actually links. If they are, they need real destinations; if not, the underline should go either way.
