**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then drops into three centred grey-black paragraphs. Each line starts in a different place, so after the heading the eye has to hunt for every line start. The block sits in the left half of the frame and the right half is empty. It reads as a plain, calm info page for a local ferry. It also feels unfinished: the right side and the bottom third look as if something failed to load.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or printed notice that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and what to do with bikes, so they can plan a trip and board correctly.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The information a passenger needs (times, frequency, the winter change) is buried in centred running prose with no actual times in it. The single biggest problem is that you can't scan the page for the one fact you came for, and when you find it, it's vague.

**What would move it most**

1. **The schedule facts are hidden in prose and given no figures.** *Defect.* A passenger comes to this page with one question: when is the next boat, or the last one? The answer is in the middle of paragraph 1 ("every forty minutes between early morning and late evening") and the middle of paragraph 3 ("one boat each hour… before dusk"). None of these are times. "Early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk" each leave the reader guessing, and the reader who guesses "late evening" means 10 pm misses the last boat. "In winter" has no dates either, so it isn't clear which timetable applies in, say, early November. These are the lines people act on and later hold the operator to, so a vague reading here is a real failure, not a style issue. The fix: give the first and last departure times for each season, and the dates each season starts. Pull these out of the prose into a short list or a small two-row table (summer / winter: first boat, last boat, how often), placed straight under the heading. Leave the prose for the bike rules and the timetable notes.

2. **Multi-line paragraphs are centred, on a different axis from the left-aligned heading.** *Convention, and here it costs the reader.* All three paragraphs run four lines and are centred, so every line starts at a different point and the eye has to search for the start of the next one. Tschichold rejects central-axis setting for running text, and Bringhurst's ragged setting assumes a steady left edge. The heading is also flush left at about x=120 while the body centres around x=440, so the page has two alignment systems that don't relate to each other. The block's own left edge wobbles from 125 to 160 px. The fix: set the paragraphs flush left on the heading's edge (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, 2.1.3). This alone makes the page read as one thing.

3. **The text block is stranded in the left half of the frame.** *Taste, argued.* The column sits at roughly x=120–760 in a 1280-wide frame. There's nothing on the right and nothing below y≈520. The empty space isn't shaped or given a purpose, so it reads as missing content rather than breathing room, and the page looks broken. Either centre the column in the frame (keeping the text flush left inside it), or use the right side for the schedule table or a simple route line from the north landing to the market quay. That would put the most-needed facts where the eye has nothing else to compete with.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 1: "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" gives no figure. Give a rough number ("up to 20 minutes").
- Paragraph 2: the bike rules are written as description ("Riders wheel them on after…"). Address the reader instead ("Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on…") so it reads as an instruction they follow.
- Paragraph 3: "the noticeboard there lists any changes" is the only way to learn about changes. If there's a web page or phone line, name it here, because people reading this page aren't standing at the shelter.
- The heading says "Notes", which describes a category, not what's inside. Something like "Ferry times and boarding: north landing to market quay" tells the reader at once whether they're on the right page.

**What's working.**
- The type is sound. There are two sizes with a clear step between them plus bold for the heading, so the hierarchy is unambiguous (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger). Keep it that simple when you add a table.
- The measure looks about 65–70 characters and the leading is generous, so the prose is comfortable to read once it's flush left (Bringhurst, 45–75 characters).
- The voice is plain and calm, right for a public notice. Keep that tone when you add the hard numbers.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the empty right and bottom areas are deliberate or content that didn't load. Check in a live browser and at narrow widths.
