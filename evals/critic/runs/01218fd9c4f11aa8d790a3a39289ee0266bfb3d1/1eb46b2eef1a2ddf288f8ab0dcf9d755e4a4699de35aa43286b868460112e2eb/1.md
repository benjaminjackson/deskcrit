**First read.** The eye goes straight to "About the trust" in heavy black, then drops into one twelve-line grey-black block and has nowhere else to go. It reads as the About section of a small charity's website: calm, competent and plain. About halfway down, the text changes from history to an open invitation, but nothing on the page shows that change. To the eye it's all one paragraph.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an About section on the website of a local conservation charity, written for nearby walkers and residents. It needs to tell them who the trust is and, ideally, turn some of them into volunteers or members.

**Verdict.** The type is clean and easy to read, but the page hides its only call to action. The invitation to the work days sits in the middle of an unbroken history paragraph. A reader who skims, which is most readers, leaves knowing the trust was set up in 1987 and not knowing they could turn up next Saturday.

**What would move it most**

1. **The volunteer invitation is buried in the history paragraph** (defect). The block holds two different jobs. Lines 1–6 cover who the trust is: footpath, woods, quarry, 1987, staff, members. From "Work days run every second Saturday…" to the end, it asks the reader to come and help. Both run on as one block with the same size, weight and colour, so the ask gets no more attention than the founding date. Web readers scan the start of each block and skip the rest (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Here the first line of the only block is about miles of footpath, so the invitation is never scanned. Fix: split the text into two paragraphs at "Work days". Put the second under its own small heading that says what the reader can do, e.g. "Come to a work day". Give it one clear next step (see 2).

2. **The invitation doesn't tell the reader how to take part** (defect, copy). The text says anyone is welcome "to join for the morning", but not where to meet, what time, how to sign up, or whether to sign up at all. Someone who wants to come has to go looking or give up. "Every second Saturday" is also ambiguous for a date people will act on. It can mean every other Saturday (fortnightly) or the second Saturday of each month. Anyone who reads it the wrong way shows up on a day with no one there. Fix: say which one it is ("the second Saturday of every month"), and add a meeting time and place plus one exact action, such as a link to the dates or "Email Sarah to say you're coming". The reassurance already in the text is good: tools, gloves, a hot drink, no experience needed. Keep it right next to the ask.

**Smaller things**
- Type scale: two sizes, a bold heading and regular body text. That's enough here. The step between them is clear.
- Right side of the frame: the column stops at roughly the middle and the right half is empty. That works as a reading column. If the full page also shows nothing there, it's a good spot for a photo of a work party or the dates box. That's taste, not a defect.
- "most of whom live within a few miles of the moor" ends the sentence on a fact. As a lead-in to the ask, it could speak to the reader directly ("you're probably one of them"). That's taste.
- The last line, "across the path.", is short but not a single-word widow. It's fine.

**What's working**
- The measure looks about 60–65 characters with generous leading, which is in Bringhurst's comfortable range. Keep this column width when you split the text.
- The copy is concrete and local: eleven miles, two small woods, the old quarry, drains, stiles, gorse. It sounds like a real place, not a template. Keep that voice when you add the ask.
- The heading sits close to its text and is clearly heavier. The hierarchy is plain and there's nothing to strip back.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the full page has a sign-up link, dates or contact details somewhere else. If it does, the ask still needs to sit right after the work-day paragraph.
