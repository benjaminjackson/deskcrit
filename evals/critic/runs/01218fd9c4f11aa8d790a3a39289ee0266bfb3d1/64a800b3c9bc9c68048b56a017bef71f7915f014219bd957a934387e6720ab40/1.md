**First read.** The eye goes to "The Wexley Gazette", then drops to the three bold headlines and moves across them left to right, giving each one the same weight. It reads as the front page of a small-town paper, or a mockup of one: calm, plain and trustworthy. It also feels flat. Nothing on the page looks like the main story, and the bottom half is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: the front page of a local news site or paper, where readers come to find out quickly what's happening in town.

**Verdict.** It's clean and easy to read, but it doesn't work as a front page yet. All three stories carry the same weight, so the reader isn't told what matters today, and the page gives no date to anchor the relative dates in the stories.

**What would move it most**
1. **No lead story (defect).** The three columns share the same headline size, weight, width and length. The masthead is only a step above them: it looks about 34 px against about 24 px headlines. Readers get three items in a row, and the page doesn't show which one the editors think matters most. That ranking is the main job of a front page. The checklist rules "One element dominates" and "Contrast of scale gives the page tension" both fail here, and Hofmann and Ruder build composition on that contrast. The fix is to choose a lead story (the bridge looks like the obvious one) and make it clearly bigger. Give it two columns or the full width, with a headline well above the others, and set the other two as secondary items below or beside it. One big step does more than several small ones.
2. **The page has no date, so the stories' dates can't be pinned down (defect).** The market moves in "from next month", the bridge opens to cars "in April" and to cyclists "from March". The masthead has no issue date and the stories have no datelines. Anyone reading this later, or reading it online, can't tell which month "next month" means. Readers act on dates and plan trips around them, so a date that can be read two ways is a defect, not a style point. Put the issue date in the masthead, and in the market story give the actual month or the first Saturday it opens.
3. **The bottom half is empty (taste, but it adds to #1).** The content stops about halfway down the frame, and the rest is plain white. That space isn't working as a pause or a contrast. It makes the page look unfinished, and it makes three short stories look like all the news there is. If this is the full page, use the height: a deeper lead story, a picture, or a second tier of stories. If it's a cropped mockup, ignore this point.

**Smaller things.**
- Market headline: "Market moves indoors" doesn't say where. "Market moves to the corn exchange" tells a local reader the news in the headline itself.
- Bridge story: the headline says "spring", but the body says cyclists can cross in March, before it ends. Either lead with the earliest date or make the headline about cars.
- The headline sizes are a clear step from the body text, but every level relies on size plus bold. A small date or section line in a lighter grey would give a fourth level without adding another size.

**What's working.**
- **The grid is strict.** It has three equal columns, one gutter width, and the double rule spans the whole width of the type, so the page holds together. Keep it when you add a lead story, and make the lead span whole columns.
- **The writing is concrete.** Sixty stalls, two of five arches, two days a week, Friday afternoons. Every story gives the reader something specific, and the copy reads like a person talking. Make the headlines that specific too.
- **The text is easy to read.** It's a serif made for reading, set dark on white with comfortable line spacing, and the columns look to be roughly 40 to 45 characters wide, which is right for news columns. Headlines sit close to their own stories, so each story reads as one unit.
