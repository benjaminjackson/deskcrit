**First read.** The eye goes to "The Wexley Gazette", drops onto the double rule, then stalls: three headlines of the same size and weight sit side by side, so it picks the left one by default and reads across. It reads as a quiet, traditional local paper, calm and trustworthy. Up close, the text columns look full of holes, and the bottom 45% of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the front page or homepage of a small local paper. Readers should see the town's news at a glance and know what matters most today.

**Verdict.** The page has a sound, plain base, but it doesn't yet do a front page's job. Nothing is marked as the lead story, the page carries no date to anchor its "next month" and "in April", and the justified narrow columns are pocked with gaps that undermine the craft the page is aiming for.

**What would move it most**
1. **Justified text in narrow columns without hyphenation (defect, convention).** All three body columns are about 45 characters wide and set flush both sides, so the spacing is stretched badly. Examples: "months  of  repairs  to  its  arches." and "Engineers  found  that  two  of  the  five" (left), "the  old  corn  exchange  from  next" and "many  shoppers  away.  The  hall  has" (middle), "a  regional  prize  for  the  vegetable" (right). The gaps break the even grey of the column and in places line up into rivers, so the eye trips along the line. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 34) says that under about 50 characters even word spacing "becomes difficult, if not impossible", and Bringhurst (2.1.1–2.1.2) warns that short justified lines produce "white acne". **Fix:** set the body ragged-right at this measure, or turn hyphenation on if you must justify. Ragged-right is the safer choice in a web column.

2. **No lead story (defect for a front page).** The three headlines are identical in size, weight and position, and the columns are equal width. The reader gets no signal about which story matters most, and the page reads as a list rather than an edited front. It also has no single focal point after the masthead. **Fix:** pick one story and make it dominant, with a bigger headline and a wider column (for example two thirds plus one third) or a lead image. The other two become secondary. The bridge story is the obvious candidate because it affects the most people.

3. **No date on the page, but the copy relies on relative dates (defect).** "From next month", "in April", "from March" and "the summer term" only mean something if the reader knows when this was published, and nothing on the page says so. People act on dates like these: a reader who arrives in March can't tell whether "next month" means April or has already happened. **Fix:** add a dateline or issue line under the masthead, and in the market story give the month outright ("from June") instead of "next month".

**Smaller things.**
- The masthead (about 34px) and the headlines (about 24px) are the same bold serif, only one modest size step apart, so the title doesn't clearly outrank the news. Make the masthead much larger or give it its own form (caps, letterspacing, different weight).
- The three columns end at different depths and leave a long blank field below. Once one story leads, balance the bottom with a fold line, more stories, or a footer.

**What's working.**
- The double rule under the masthead is a restrained, period-correct signal that this is a newspaper. Keep it.
- The headlines are specific and built on verbs ("Bridge to reopen in spring", "Market moves indoors"), and each lede answers who, what and when in its first sentence. That is good news writing.
- The reading serif, generous line spacing, dark-on-white contrast and even gutters give the page a calm, credible base. The fixes above build on that rather than replace it.
