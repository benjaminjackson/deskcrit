**First read.** My eye lands on the bold headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes". It drops through a three-line centred paragraph, past three floating lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a landing page for a small local ferry. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, and also generic: the layout is a stock centred template with no picture of a boat, a bay or a harbour.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a ferry operator's website. Its job is to get a local or visitor to decide to take the crossing and go to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The order of reading is right and the button is the right next step. But the page leaves out what someone needs to decide to travel: where the boat goes, what it costs and when it actually leaves. And it looks like any service's page rather than a ferry crossing a bay. It's competent and forgettable.

**What would move it most**

1. **The copy leaves out where, how much and when (defect).** "Cross the bay" never names the other side. The paragraph names the "north landing" but not where you arrive. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives a gap between boats but no first or last sailing, so a reader planning a 7 am crossing or a 10 pm return still can't tell whether a boat exists. Nothing gives a fare. "Bicycles travel free" quietly tells the reader that people don't, without saying what they pay. These are the facts people hold an operator to, and here they have to guess or click through.
   - **Fix:** name both ends ("North Landing to [town]"), give real times ("first boat 6:40, last 22:00") and put an adult fare beside the button or in the list.

2. **Nothing on the page shows a ferry, a bay or a harbour (taste, but it costs the piece).** The strongest thing this service sells is the experience the paragraph describes: "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck." The page shows none of it. It's black type on white with one blue button, so it could be a SaaS sign-up, and the word "calm" has to do all the emotional work on its own. Also, roughly the bottom third of the frame is empty and does nothing for the piece.
   - **Fix:** put one strong image of the crossing (deck, water, the far shore) into the page, sized to carry it. Let the headline sit with it rather than alone on white.

3. **The three feature lines don't read as a list, and two of them repeat the paragraph (defect, minor-to-moderate).** "Bicycles travel free / Seats on the open deck / Hot drinks on board" are centred with no markers. They are set only a little smaller than the paragraph, so they look like a stray second paragraph rather than a set of facts. "Open deck" and "bicycle" were already said one line above, so the list adds little.
   - **Fix:** make the list carry the hard facts from point 1 (fare, first and last boat, crossing time, bikes free). Give it a visible list form: markers or icons, or a row of short items. That turns it into the part a skimmer actually uses.

**Smaller things**
- The paragraph is centred over three lines. That's borderline, but a ragged left edge on both sides slows the return sweep. Set it flush left, or cut it to two lines (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst 2.1.3).
- Type scale: the paragraph (about 20 px) and the list (about 18 px) are close enough to read as an accident rather than two levels. Set them at one size, or separate them by weight or colour instead (Refactoring UI).
- The brand line "Coastal Ferry Co." is very small and uses the same blue as the button, so the accent colour is doing two jobs. Give the brand its own treatment (a proper logo or wordmark at a readable size) and keep the blue for the action.
- The button label is specific and good. It would be stronger still if it promised the answer, e.g. "See today's departures".

**What's working**
- **The headline makes a concrete, viewer-centred promise.** "Twelve calm minutes" gives a number and a feeling. Keep that construction.
- **The order is clear: headline, detail, one action.** The single filled button has no competitors. Keep one primary action when you revise.
- **The paragraph's second sentence speaks to real cases** ("a bicycle, a pram or a picnic"), so a reader can see themselves on board.
