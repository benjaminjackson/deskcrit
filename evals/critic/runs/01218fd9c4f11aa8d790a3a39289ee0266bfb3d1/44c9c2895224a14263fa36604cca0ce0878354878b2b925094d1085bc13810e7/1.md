**First read.** The eye goes to the bold sans headline at top left, then down into three short serif paragraphs. It reads as an information page for passengers on a small river ferry, probably on a local council or community site. It feels calm, plain and well mannered. No single fact jumps out, though. To find out when the boat leaves you have to read the prose from start to finish.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that lets a passenger plan a crossing, so they know when boats run, how long the trip takes, what to do with a bike, and when the last boat goes.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound and the page is easy to read. It doesn't yet do its job well, because the facts a passenger plans around are vague or ambiguous and are buried in running prose. The biggest problem is the copy, not the layout.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times a passenger acts on can be read more than one way.** This is a defect. It's in the first and third paragraphs. "Between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "in winter" are all things a passenger plans around and will hold the operator to, but none of them gives a time or a date.
   - "The quay" can be read two ways. It could mean the market quay only, or it could mean both ends. A passenger at the north landing who reads it the second way may arrive after the last boat and be stranded on the wrong side of the river.
   - "Winter" doesn't say which months, so a reader in late October can't tell which timetable applies.
   - "Every forty minutes" gives no starting time, so the reader can't work out when the next boat is due.

   Fix: give clock times and dates ("First boat 06:20, last 21:40 from the north landing"; "November to February: hourly, last boat 16:00 from each side"). If the times change with the season, say where the reader can find today's times. Right now that's only "printed timetables in the shelter", which is no help to someone planning at home.

2. **The facts are written as narrative, so you can't scan them.** This is a convention (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; Nielsen Norman Group on scannable text). All three paragraphs are the same weight and the same form. Someone checking "when's the last boat?" has to read every line to find "before dusk" near the bottom. Fix: pull the timetable facts (frequency, first and last boat, crossing time, winter change) into a short list or a small table above the prose, or give each paragraph a short subhead ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter"). The prose can stay as the friendly explanation underneath.

**Smaller things**
- Headline: "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what the page tells you. Something like "River ferry: times, bikes and winter service" would tell the reader what's on the page before they start.
- Second paragraph: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" describes the procedure but never tells the cyclist what to do ("wait until foot passengers are aboard, then…"). Address the reader as "you".
- First paragraph: "a little longer when the tide turns against the boat" gives the passenger nothing to plan with. Give a rough figure ("up to 20 minutes").
- Layout: the column sits left with the right half of the frame empty. That's fine for an article page, but if this is the whole page, nothing tells the reader where the landings are. A small map or the two landing addresses would earn that space.

**What's working**
- The type works. A bold sans headline over a serif text face is a clear contrast rather than a clash (Dair, *Design with Type*). The scale has just two sizes with a big step between them, and each has an obvious job.
- Body text reads comfortably. Lines run about 65–70 characters, line spacing is generous for that length, paragraphs are separated by space alone, and the headline sits tight to the text it introduces (Bringhurst). Keep all of this when you add structure.
- The plain, unadorned page suits a public-service notice. Don't add decoration when you fix the content.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- How the column reflows on a phone, which is where most people will check ferry times. Check it at 320 px wide.
