**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, then down into three even paragraphs of plain grey-black sans. It reads as a ferry operator's information page or a notice for passengers. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also like a page nobody will read closely: everything below the heading carries the same weight, so nothing tells you where the answer to your question is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or info-screen page that tells a passenger when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike, read mostly by someone checking quickly before setting out.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is competent, but the page doesn't do its job. The facts a passenger acts on, the times, are vague and buried mid-sentence. The biggest problem is the copy, not the layout.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times a passenger relies on are never given.** *Defect.* Paragraph 1 says "between early morning and late evening", and paragraph 3 says "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "in winter". You can't plan a trip from any of these. Someone reading "before dusk" in November doesn't know whether that means 15:40 or 16:30, and if they guess wrong they're stranded on the wrong bank. "The quay" can be read two ways: the market quay, or whichever quay you happen to be at. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say from which side, or from what time. These are the lines a reader will later hold the operator to, so each one needs a real value: first and last departure from each landing, the months that count as "winter", and the last winter boat as a clock time. If the times change too often to print, say so directly ("times change weekly; check the noticeboard at either landing") so the reader knows the gap is deliberate.

2. **The page has to be read in full to find any single fact.** *Convention* (text people skim should be broken up with headings, short paragraphs and lists; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). There are three paragraphs of the same size and weight with no labels. A cyclist has to read the timetable paragraph before finding out that bikes are free. A winter traveller has to reach the last paragraph to learn the timetable changes. Pull the facts out where the eye can catch them: a short "When it runs" block with frequency, duration and summer/winter times as a small table or list, then "Bicycles", then "Where to check for changes". Keep the prose for the conditions (tide, boarding order). Right now the hierarchy has two levels, heading and everything else. The content needs a third.

**Smaller things**
- The empty right side and empty bottom look left over rather than planned. That's harmless for a reading column, but if this is a fixed screen or a printed notice, use the space for the timetable block from point 2. (Taste.)
- "Bicycles ride on the lower deck" makes the bikes the ones doing the riding. "Bring your bicycle at no extra charge; it travels on the lower deck" speaks to the reader and puts the benefit first.
- "Notes for ferry passengers" is a category label, not a promise. Something like "Taking the river ferry" or "Ferry times and bicycles" tells the reader what they'll find.
- Line length on the longest lines (paragraph 2, line 2) looks close to 70 characters, which is the upper edge of comfortable (Bringhurst, 45–75). Don't let the column get any wider.

**What's working**
- The type scale is clean: two sizes with a clear step, and weight carrying the difference. Keep it. If you add subheads, set them at body size in the heading's bold, not at a new in-between size (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).
- The reading conditions are good: the body looks around 19–20 px, the leading is generous (about 1.45), the left edge is flush and ragged right with no bad breaks, and paragraphs are separated by space only. Dark text on white clears contrast easily.
- The heading sits close to the text it introduces, so the page reads as one unit.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether this is a responsive web page or a fixed screen or print notice. That changes how much the empty right side matters and whether a timetable table would fit.
