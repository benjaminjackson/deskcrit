**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three even grey-black paragraphs and reads them in order. It looks like a plain, calm information page for people taking a local river ferry, maybe a council or operator web page or a printed notice. It feels tidy and trustworthy, and a bit flat. Nothing on the page tells you which fact matters most.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page passengers check to find when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bicycle. They will scan it before travelling, not read it for pleasure.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is competent: one face, a clean two-level scale, a comfortable measure. But it fails the job a timetable notice exists for. The facts a passenger acts on are buried in prose and are too vague to act on, so after reading all three paragraphs you still can't say when the next or last boat leaves.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times can't be acted on (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening," "one boat each hour" in "winter," and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines a passenger plans around. None of them gives a time. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00. "Winter" has no dates. "Before dusk" moves by an hour across the season. "The quay" can be read two ways: the market quay named in paragraph 1, or whichever quay you happen to be standing at. If you read it the wrong way, you walk to the north landing expecting a last boat that has already left from the other side. Fix: give first and last departures from each landing as clock times, give the winter timetable dates, and name the landing in every sentence that sets a time. If the times change too often to print, say so in those words and point to where the current times are posted.

2. **The facts are buried in prose (defect; Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** The page holds three different kinds of information: when it runs, bicycles, and winter changes plus where to find timetables. All three are set as the same undifferentiated paragraph, with no subheads and no visual difference between them. Someone checking "can I bring my bike?" has to read the opening of every paragraph to find out. Fix: give each paragraph a short subhead in the language passengers use ("When the ferry runs", "Bringing a bicycle", "Winter and timetable changes"). Pull the times out into a small table or a short list of lines, so they are the first thing a skimming eye hits under the headline. Today the headline is the only point of emphasis, and it carries no information.

3. **The headline names a category, not what the page tells you (taste).** "Notes for ferry passengers" could head any ferry page anywhere. Naming the route ("North landing ⇄ Market quay ferry") tells passengers in one glance that they are on the right page, and gives the bold weight something worth saying.

**Smaller things.**
- The bicycle paragraph says riders lock bikes "to the rail," but never says whether a lock is provided or whether they need to bring one. Say which.
- "The noticeboard there lists any changes for the coming week": there is no online equivalent. If this is a web page, say where changes are posted on the web, or say that the landing noticeboard is the only source.
- The right ~40% of the frame is empty. That's fine for a reading column. But if this is a fixed screen or a printed notice, the empty space is leftover rather than chosen, and the column could move to the centre or run wider with a table beside it.

**What's working.**
- **Type scale and face:** one sans in two clear levels. A heavy headline over regular body text is a decisive step, not a near-miss (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger). Keep this restraint when you add subheads: do it with weight at body size, not with a third size.
- **Measure and leading:** lines look to run around 65–75 characters with generous line spacing, close to Bringhurst's range. The text is easy to read once you're in it.
- **Paragraph marking and spacing:** paragraphs are separated by space only, with no indents, and the headline sits closer to its text than to the top margin. The grouping is honest.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this is a responsive web page or a fixed notice. That decides whether the empty right side matters and how the text reflows on a phone, which is where most passengers would read it.
