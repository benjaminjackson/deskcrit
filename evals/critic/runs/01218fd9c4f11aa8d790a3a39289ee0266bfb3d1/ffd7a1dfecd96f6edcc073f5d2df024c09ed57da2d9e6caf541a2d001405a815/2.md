**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, then drops straight into three even grey blocks of prose. It reads as a plain information page for a small local ferry: calm, tidy and trustworthy, like a well-set notice. Nothing in the body stands out, so after the headline the eye just starts reading line one. No fact rises to meet it.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a web page for passengers who want to know when the ferry runs, how long the crossing takes, and whether they can bring a bike. Most of them will skim it on their way to the boat.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is competent. The page fails at its main job, because the facts a passenger acts on are buried in running prose, and the most important ones are too vague to plan around. The problem is the content and its structure. The styling is fine.

**What would move it most**

1. **The timetable facts are buried in paragraphs, so a passenger has to read everything to find one number.** Look at paragraphs 1 and 3. "Every forty minutes", "about twelve minutes", "one boat each hour" and "last crossing before dusk" are all set in the same weight and size as "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter". Someone standing on the landing asking "when's the next boat?" can't scan for the answer. They have to read through it and then work it out. This is a skim-reading task, so the text should be broken up with headings, short paragraphs and lists (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; Nielsen on scannable text). **Defect.** Fix: pull the schedule into a short block of label and value pairs, such as Route, Frequency (summer / winter), First and last boat, Crossing time. Keep the prose for the bike rules, where the order of steps matters.

2. **The service hours can't be planned around.** "Between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines a passenger is most likely to rely on. Neither gives a time. "Before dusk" changes by an hour or more across the winter. "The quay" could mean the market quay only, or the last boat from each side. If someone reads it the wrong way, they get stranded on the wrong bank. The paragraph then sends readers to a printed timetable in a shelter, which they only find once they're already at the landing. **Defect** (wording a reader will act on). Fix: give actual times, or a rule with a clock time ("last boat from market quay 16:30, Nov–Feb"). Name which landing each time applies to. If the times are published somewhere, link to them here instead of pointing to the shelter.

3. **Winter is in the last paragraph, after the bikes.** The seasonal change in paragraph 3 is the one most likely to catch someone out, and it comes after a paragraph only cyclists need. A reader in November gets the summer timetable first and may stop there. **Defect.** Fix: put the summer and winter frequencies side by side in the schedule block from finding 1, and let the bike section come after it.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "Notes for ferry passengers" is a category label and could sit on any page. A title that names the route ("North landing ↔ Market quay ferry") tells readers straight away that they're on the right page.
- Bike paragraph: "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" only states a rule. Adding the reason or the consequence (for example, that it keeps the gangway clear) makes people more likely to follow it. This is taste.
- Layout: at this width the column sits in the left half with the right half and the bottom empty. Reading-wise it works, but it looks unfinished. That space is a natural place for the schedule block from finding 1. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- The type is well set. The measure looks like about 70 characters, the line spacing is open, paragraphs are marked by space only, the text is flush left with a gentle rag, and near-black on white reads easily. Keep all of this when you restructure.
- There are only two type sizes, and the headline differs from the body in both size and weight. That gives a clear scale with no near-miss sizes, and it leaves room to add one small label style for the schedule without muddling it.
- The tone is plain and friendly. The bike instructions follow the order things actually happen (wait, wheel on, lock to the rail), which is the right way to write a procedure.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone. That's probably where most passengers will read this, and it's where buried timetable facts hurt most.
