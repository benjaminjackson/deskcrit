**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a section of a visitor web page meant to show recent rainfall at the reservoir, with practical travel information beside it.

**Verdict.** It fails at its main job. The chart has no labels, no scale, no units and no categories, so a viewer can see that one thing is bigger than two others but can't tell what any of it measures. The headline also promises rainfall while the only text on the page is about getting there.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart carries no information** *(defect)*. The three bars in the left panel have no category labels, no value axis, no values and no unit. The viewer can't tell whether the bars are months, years or sites, or whether the tallest is 40 mm or 400 mm, so "more rain here" is the only thing they can take away, and they can't say where "here" is. This breaks the basic rule that every chart labels its axes or data with units, and says its source and date where they matter (Tufte; Few). The fix: put a category under each bar (for example "Aug, Sep, Oct"), write the value with its unit at the top of each bar ("82 mm"), and add a source and date line. Direct labels at the bars are better than an axis here, because there are only three values.
2. **The title names a topic, not a finding, and nothing tells the viewer why rainfall matters to them** *(taste, which I'd argue strongly)*. "Rainfall at the reservoir" tells a visitor what the chart is about, not what it shows or what it means for their visit. Is the water high? Are paths flooded? Is it a good week to come? A visitor reads this page to decide something, so the chart should end on that: a line such as "September was the wettest month this year, so the lower path may be muddy" states the takeaway and lands it on the reader. Without it, even a fully labelled chart is trivia.
3. **The two blocks don't belong together, and the layout pretends they do** *(defect)*. The single H1 "Rainfall at the reservoir" sits above both columns, so "Getting there" reads as part of the rainfall story, and the side-by-side placement suggests the text explains the chart. It doesn't. The viewer looks for a link that isn't there. Either give each block its own heading at the same level under a page title that covers both (for example the reservoir's name), or separate them clearly with space or position so it's obvious they're two different topics.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart's frame is a heavy dark-grey border around a tinted fill. Both are ink that carries no data and pulls attention away from the bars (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 4). Drop the border, and mute or remove the tint.
- The bars sit about 30 px inside the frame, with a baseline that stops short of the frame on both sides, which leaves the plot floating and the empty right third of the panel unexplained. Size the plot to its content, or use the space for labels.
- The page's lower 40% is empty while the content is cramped into the top. If this is the whole section, that's fine. If it's a fixed panel, the space is wasted and the chart could be larger.
- "Getting there" is good, specific copy, but "parking is free after four in the afternoon" leaves the reader asking what it costs before four and where the car park is. Add the price or a link.

**What's working.**
- The travel copy is concrete and reads like a person talking: a named bus, a gate, a frequency, a landmark for the cycle racks. Keep that voice, and give the chart's caption the same specificity.
- The type scale is tight and clear: an H1, an H3 and body text, with steps big enough that each size has an obvious job. The body text is set flush left at a comfortable line length (about 45 characters).
- The single blue for the bars has good contrast against the tint. Keep one colour if all three bars are the same series.
