**First read.** The eye goes to the bold headline, then reads down two paragraphs of comfortable body text, then reaches a smaller last line that looks like a footnote. It reads as a plain information page for people taking a river ferry. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, and a bit bare.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a passenger is about to travel and wants to know when the ferry runs, how long it takes, what to do with a bike, and anything that could catch them out.

**Verdict.** The page is easy to read, but it doesn't fully do its job. The one line that can leave a passenger stranded is styled as the least important thing on the page, and the timetable is too vague to plan a trip around.

**What would move it most**

1. **The winter warning is demoted to fine print.** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." is the smallest text on the page, about 16 px against roughly 19 px body, and it sits last. Size tells the reader how much something matters, so this reads as a footnote to skim past. It's the only line that tells the passenger to do something, and the only one that can make them miss a boat. **Defect.** Fix: give it at least body size, and set it apart with weight, a rule or a light tint rather than a bigger size. Move it up next to the timetable paragraph, because it changes how that paragraph should be read. Also say when winter starts. "In winter" leaves a passenger in late October unsure whether this applies to them.

2. **The schedule gives no times you can act on.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" has no first boat, no last boat and no starting time for the forty-minute cycle. Someone arriving at the north landing can't tell whether they'll wait 2 minutes or 38, or whether there's a 22:00 boat home. Times are what readers hold the operator to, and here the reader has to fill in the gap. **Defect.** Fix: state first and last departures from each landing ("First boat 06:20 from the north landing, last 22:40 from the market quay"). If space allows, add a short list of departure times, since people scan times rather than read them in a sentence.

**Smaller things**
- The bicycle paragraph talks about "riders" in the third person, while the last line speaks to "you". Switch to "you" ("Wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded…") so the instructions read as instructions to the reader.
- "About twelve minutes in calm weather" sets up a question it doesn't answer: what happens in rough weather? Do crossings take longer, or get cancelled? Say so, or point readers to where they can check.
- The type scale has three sizes (headline, body, small). That's fine once the small size has a real job, but the warning shouldn't be that job.

**What's working**
- The text is well set: dark, high-contrast type, flush left, generous line spacing and a measure of about 70 characters. That sits within Bringhurst's 45–75 range, so the paragraphs are easy to read. Keep it.
- The headline is clear and says who the page is for. The two paragraphs each cover one topic (timing, then bikes), which keeps the page easy to scan.
- The wide empty area on the right is fine for a single-column notice. Don't fill it for the sake of it.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether this page appears on a phone, at the landing or on a website, which affects how big the timetable information should be.
