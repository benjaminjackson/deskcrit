**First read.** The serif headline lands first, then the eye drops to the tallest bar, Q3 2026 at 705, then moves along the row of numbers above the bars. It reads as a clean, calm board slide from an outdoor-gear company. It feels trustworthy and finished, and that is part of the problem below: everything on it looks equally certain.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one slide in a 12-page sales review. Board members read it, either live or in a pre-read, to understand the seasonal pattern in shipments and where Q4 is heading.

**Verdict.** This is a well-made chart that mostly does its job. The one thing that matters is that the forecast bar looks exactly like the seven actual ones, so the board will read 560 as a result rather than a projection.

**What would move it most**

1. **The Q4 2026 bar is drawn as if it were fact.** *Defect.* The last bar on the far right has the same solid blue fill and the same bold "560" as every quarter that has already happened. The slide says it's a forecast only in a parenthesis at the end of the subtitle and again in the grey source line, and those are the two places a board member skims past. So someone who glances at the chart, or who sees it pasted into another deck, will take 560 as shipped. A forecast is a number people later hold you to, so this mix-up costs the most of anything here. Projections should look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). The fix:
   - Give that bar a lighter tint, an outline, or hatching.
   - Label it at the bar itself, e.g. "560 forecast".
   - If the September forecast has a range, show it as a range or error bar rather than a single point.
   - Once the chart shows all this, cut the parenthesis from the subtitle.

2. **The headline states a pattern but not what it means for the board.** *Taste, argued.* "Shipments peak each third quarter" is true, but it's backed by only two cycles, and it leaves out what the chart shows more strongly: every quarter of 2026 beat the same quarter of 2025, by roughly 11–17%. Q3 alone went from 610 to 705. A board is asking whether the business is growing and what to plan for, and the slide leaves them to work that out from eight numbers. Make the headline land on the reader. For example: shipments still peak in Q3, but each quarter is running about 15% ahead of last year, and Q4 is forecast at 560. If you want the year-on-year comparison to read at a glance, a light divider or a small "2025 | 2026" label under the axis would help without breaking the time order.

**Smaller things**
- The y-axis numbers and gridlines repeat what the labels on the bars already say. Drop the axis numbers or lighten them further. As it stands, two scales compete.
- The value labels float about 20 px above the bars, so they read as a separate row of numbers. Tuck them closer to the bar tops.
- The subtitle and the chart's own title ("Units shipped by quarter / Thousands of units") say much the same thing at nearly the same size. Merge them: put the unit in the subtitle and drop one level. That leaves a cleaner scale of three sizes: headline, body, caption.
- About 110 px at the bottom of the slide is empty while the chart is squeezed. Either give the chart that height or move the footer down.

**What's working**
- The bars start at zero, are in time order, are one colour, and are labelled directly, with no legend and no chartjunk. Bar heights are honest, so the Q3 peaks are real and visible. Keep this restraint.
- The source line names the system and when the forecast was made. That's the right instinct; it just needs to show up in the chart as well as in the footnote.
- The serif headline over a sans-serif body gives the slide a clear entry point and a bit of character without decoration.
