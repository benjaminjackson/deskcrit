**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", moves right to the white card, and drops straight down to the blue "Send message" button. It reads as the contact page of a small design or dev studio, and it comes across as calm, tidy and a bit anonymous. You know what to do within two seconds.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a would-be client lands here to send a first project enquiry, and the studio wants enquiries detailed enough to act on.

**Verdict.** It does its basic job. The form is clear, well labelled and has one obvious action. The biggest problem is that the copy asks for a budget and a timeline, but the form gives people nowhere to put them, so most briefs will arrive without them.

**What would move it most**

1. **The form doesn't collect what the copy asks for** (defect). The left column says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form on the right has only name, email and one Message box about five lines tall. So the visitor has to remember an instruction from the other column while typing into a box too small for a brief. Most will skip it, and the studio loses the two facts it most needs to qualify a lead. The fix: give budget and timeline their own optional fields in the form, in the visitor's words (for example a budget range picker and a "When do you need this?" field). Make Message clearly taller, with a hint underneath about what to include. Then the left column can stop giving instructions and just reassure.

2. **The headline and button could be on any studio's site** (taste, but it costs something). "Tell us about your project" and "Send message" are what every contact page says. Nothing here tells the visitor what kind of work Fernwick does or why to write to them rather than someone else. The concrete line, "reply within two working days", is the strongest copy on the page, and it's buried in grey body text. Make the promise do more work: move the reply time up, close to the headline or next to the button ("We'll reply within two working days"). Consider a headline that names the work Fernwick takes on. Keep the button label as specific as the action it triggers.

**Smaller things**
- Grid: the card stops at about x=1068 while the nav runs to about 1224. That leaves an empty strip on the right, and the layout looks left-heavy with no clear reason. Either run the card out to the nav's right edge or centre the whole block on one width.
- Headline line break: "project" sits alone on the second line. Break as "Tell us about / your project", or narrow the column so the lines balance.
- Required and optional fields aren't marked. Once optional fields like budget and timeline are added, mark them "(optional)".
- Nav: "Contact" looks the same as the other links, so nothing shows you're already on this page. Give the current item a distinct state.
- Input borders look pale grey on white and may fall short of 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure the border against the white card.
- The empty band between the card and the footer (about y=580–740) looks like leftover space rather than a choice. That's fine for a short page, but the footer could sit closer, or the extra fields from finding 1 will fill it naturally.

**What's working**
- There's exactly one action. The filled blue button is the only solid colour block on the page, so nothing competes with it (primary vs secondary action).
- Labels sit above their fields in bold, closer to their own input than to the one above. The grouping is clear at a glance (proximity; labels never replaced by placeholder text).
- The type scale is short and clean: a large bold headline, 16 px body, smaller bold labels. Each size has an obvious job. Keep it as you add fields.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Focus states, validation and error messages, and what happens after "Send message". On a lead form, the confirmation screen matters: check that it repeats the two-working-day promise.
