**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", then moves right to the white card and down three fields to the blue "Send message" button. Within a second it reads as a small studio's contact page. It feels calm, tidy and competent, and also anonymous: with the logo covered, this could be any agency's page.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a desktop contact page for a design or creative studio, meant to get prospective clients to send a project enquiry with enough detail for the studio to reply usefully.

**Verdict.** It works. Anyone can fill it in and send it without thinking. What holds it back is that the page asks for a budget and a timeline in the left column, but the form has no place for them, so most enquiries will arrive without the details the studio says it wants.

**What would move it most**

1. **The form doesn't collect what the copy asks for.** The left column says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form on the right has only a name, an email and a short, blank Message box. The visitor has to remember an instruction from another column while typing into an empty box, and many won't. The studio then has to go back and forth by email to get answers it already asked for. This is a defect (Nielsen heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Fix: put the request where the typing happens. Either add two optional fields ("Budget range" as a short select, "When do you need it?") or put the prompt under the Message label as helper text ("What's the project, your budget range, and your timeline?"). Then make the Message box taller. At about five lines it looks too small for "tell us about your project".

2. **Input borders look too faint to work as edges.** The grey outlines on the three fields look like roughly 2:1 against the white card, and the card's own outline is fainter still. To anyone with low vision or on a washed-out screen, the fields fade into the card and the form looks like labels floating over white. This is a convention (WCAG 1.4.11 asks for 3:1 for component boundaries). Measure the border colour. If it's under 3:1, darken the field borders to a mid grey. The card outline can stay faint because it's decoration.

3. **The copy could sit on anyone's page.** "Tell us about your project" and "Send message" are the most common words on agency contact pages. Nothing tells the visitor what kind of project Fernwick takes on or what happens after they click. The subhead does the opposite: "reply within two working days" is concrete and a promise you can check, and it's the best line on the page. This is taste, but it costs something: the heading is the one line everyone reads. Fix: make the heading name the kind of work the studio wants, or say what happens next (for example, "Send a brief and get a reply within two days"). Make the button say what it commits the visitor to.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Contact" looks exactly like the other links even though this is the Contact page. Mark the current page (weight, colour or no underline) so visitors know where they are.
- Right edge: the card stops at about 1067 px while the nav runs to about 1224 px, so the content block's right edge doesn't match the header's. Either stretch the card to the nav's edge or narrow the header container to match.
- Lower third: about 150 px of grey sits between the card and the footer, and the footer links look stranded at the bottom. Pull the footer up or give the gap a top border so the page ends deliberately.
- Heading: "project" sits alone on line two. A slightly wider left column, or a two-line break that reads more evenly, would hold the heading together.
- No other way to reach the studio: there's no email address or phone number for people who'd rather not use a form. Add a plain email line under the subhead.

**What's working.**
- The subhead's two-working-day promise is specific and sets expectations. Keep it, and keep it near the form.
- The form itself is good practice. It's one column, every field has a bold label above it that stays visible, and there's one filled primary button with no competing secondary action, so there's only one way to go.
- The restraint: one blue for every interactive element (links and button) against neutral greys, so anything blue is clearly something you can click.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Validation and error states: whether a bad email gets a clear message next to the field, and what the visitor sees after clicking "Send message".
- Focus rings on the inputs and the button, and their contrast.
- How the two columns stack on a narrow screen.
