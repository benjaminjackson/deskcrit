**First read.** The eye goes to "Tell us about your project", moves right to the white card, and drops straight down three fields to the blue "Send message" button. It reads as a contact form for a small studio within a second. It feels tidy and calm, but anonymous: it looks like a default component library with the studio name typed in.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the contact page of a design or creative studio, and its job is to get prospective clients to send a useful enquiry.

**Verdict.** It works as a form. Nobody will be confused about what to do. It's weak as the moment a client decides to hire a studio. The page asks for a budget and a timeline but gives them nowhere to go, and nothing on it shows that this studio is any different from any other.

**What would move it most**

1. **The request for budget and timeline is disconnected from the form.** *Defect.* The left-column paragraph says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form on the right has only a free-text Message box. By the time someone is typing in Message, that line is about 300 px to the left and out of mind. Most enquiries will arrive without either detail, so the studio spends its first reply asking for them. That eats into the two-day promise. The fix is to make the ask part of the form: add optional "Budget range" and "Timeline" fields (a select with a few ranges is quickest to answer), or at least put the hint as helper text right under the Message label (Nielsen 6, recognition rather than recall).

2. **Nothing here sells the studio at the moment of commitment.** *Taste, argued.* The headline "Tell us about your project" could sit on any agency's page. The grey-and-blue, bordered-card styling is the default look of a hundred templates. A design studio's own contact page is the one place where the look itself is proof of skill. Here a prospective client sees no sign of craft and no reason to choose Fernwick over the next tab. The strongest line on the page is "We read every message and reply within two working days." It's specific and it makes a promise the viewer can hold the studio to. It's set as small grey body copy. Raise it, or move it next to the button where the decision happens. Then give the left column something only this studio could say: what kinds of projects it takes, a recent client, a named person who will read the message. That turns a generic form into a reason to send.

3. **The composition stops short and leaves a dead right side.** *Convention (Müller-Brockmann: one grid governs the page).* The nav and logo span 56–1224 px. The form card ends at about 1068 px, aligned to nothing above it. The result is a roughly 160 px empty strip on the right and a large empty band below the card, so the page looks like a fragment of a wider layout rather than a designed whole. Either take the card's right edge from the nav's right edge, or centre the two-column block as a unit so the empty space looks intentional.

**Smaller things**
- The input borders look pale grey on white, probably under 3:1. Measure them against WCAG 1.4.11 and darken them if they fall short.
- The nav shows no current-page state. "Contact" looks exactly like the other links. Mark it (bold, no underline, or similar).
- The headline breaks as "Tell us about your / project", leaving one word alone on the second line. Widen the column a little or rebreak it so the lines balance.
- There are no required or optional markers. If all three fields are required, say so once. If budget and timeline fields are added, mark those optional.
- The small sizes are close together: nav about 15 px, body about 16 px, labels and footer about 14 px. Collapse them toward two sizes and let weight do the rest.
- There's no direct email address as an alternative. Some clients won't use a form for a first contact. Put an address under the intro paragraph.

**What's working**
- Labels sit above each field, persistent and bold, with each label clearly closer to its own field than to the one above. Keep that spacing.
- There's one primary action, filled and high-contrast, and nothing competes with it.
- The reply-time promise is concrete and believable. Keep that kind of copy and give it more weight.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the fields and button, the error and validation messages, what the page shows after sending (it should repeat the two-day promise), and how the two columns reflow on a phone.
