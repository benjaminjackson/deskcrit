**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline, drops into the first column, and then reads three equal grey blocks left to right with nothing to choose between them. It reads as a calm, well-set information section for a local river ferry, maybe a web page or a printed notice. It feels orderly and quiet, but every block looks the same, so nothing shows where the answer to any one question is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an information page for people who ride or plan to ride the ferry, who come to it with one question: when does it run, can I bring my bike, what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** It's legible but doesn't do its job. Three separate topics are set as unlabelled prose under a headline that promises something else, so a rider has to read everything to find one answer. The details they would act on (times and dates) aren't there at all.

**What would move it most**

1. **Three topics, no signposts** (defect). The columns cover three different subjects: the regular crossing, bicycles, and the winter timetable. All three open with identical body text and no label. A rider asking "can I bring my bike?" has to start reading each column to find out which one answers it. Text people skim should be broken up by headings (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: make each page self-evident and scannable). The columns already split the content cleanly, so the fix is cheap. Give each column a short subhead that names the reader's question, such as "When it runs", "Bringing a bike" and "Winter crossings". Set the subheads in the headline's sans at body size or a little larger, so they add one clear level without adding a crowd of new sizes.

2. **The headline promises something the page doesn't deliver** (defect). "Through the seasons" suggests a story of how the ferry changes over the year, but only the third column mentions a season. The first two columns describe things that are true all year. A reader expecting a seasonal guide feels misled, and a reader who wants the timetable doesn't see it named. Make the headline say what the section actually is, for example "Riding the river ferry: times, bikes and winter service". Alternatively, change the content so it really does go season by season.

3. **The facts a rider acts on are vague** (defect, under "the words are part of the design"):
   - "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00, and the cost of guessing wrong is a missed boat.
   - "In winter" has no dates, so a reader in late October can't tell which timetable applies.
   - "The last crossing leaves before dusk" moves by about an hour across the season. Is that a fixed time, or does it change every day?
   - "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter" sends the reader somewhere else for the one fact this page should hold.

   Put in the actual first and last times, the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, and the last winter sailing time.

4. **The measure is too short for running prose** (convention). The columns look about 28 to 32 characters wide, well under Bringhurst's 45 to 75, so the text breaks every few words and reads choppily. Meanwhile about 270 px on the right is empty. Either widen the three columns into the full width, or keep the narrow measure and accept it only once subheads make each column a short, separate unit. The first option reads better. To decide, measure the characters per line.

**Smaller things.**
- Most of the empty space is in the bottom 40% of the frame and a strip down the right edge, so it looks left over rather than placed. Once the columns are widened, the block will sit as a deliberate shape.
- The first column runs one line longer than the others ("harbour wall."). The ragged bottom edge is barely noticeable, so leave it unless the columns change anyway.

**What's working.**
- The bold sans headline over a serif body is a clear contrast rather than a clash (Dair's contrast of structure). It gives two obvious levels with only two sizes. Keep the pairing and use the sans for the new subheads.
- The text is dark on white with comfortable leading, and the lines in neighbouring columns look as if they sit on the same baselines. The page reads as calm and trustworthy, which suits a public service.
- The columns are one topic each, so the structure for scanning is already there and only needs labels.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How these three columns reflow on a phone. Once stacked, unlabelled blocks become one long, undivided read, which makes finding 1 more urgent.
