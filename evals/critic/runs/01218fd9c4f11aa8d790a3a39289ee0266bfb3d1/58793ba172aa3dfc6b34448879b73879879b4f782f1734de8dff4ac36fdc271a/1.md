**First read.** My eye goes first to the row of four buttons, and only then to the headline. Teal, purple, amber and blue are the strongest colours on the page, so they win before "Low-light houseplants" does. After the buttons I read across the plant names and prices. It reads as a small, tidy plant shop's category page, calm and competent. Then the rainbow of buttons makes me wonder what the colours mean.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is an online shop's category page, meant to help someone with a dim room pick one of four plants and add it to the cart.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job: the structure is clean and the copy is concrete. Two things hold it back. Four different button colours suggest differences between the plants that don't exist. And the illustrations are so alike that they give the shopper nothing to choose by.

**What would move it most**

1. **Four colours on one action** (defect). The four "Add to cart" buttons are the same action, but each is a different saturated colour. A shopper reasonably expects colour to mean something: a sale, a category, stock level, pet safety. They look for that meaning and find none, so the page asks a question it never answers. The colours also make the buttons the loudest things on the page. They pull the eye away from the headline, the plants and the prices, and none of them reads as "the" action because no colour is held back for it. When elements do the same job, give them one shared colour, and keep a different colour only for a different role or state (Apple, Human Interface Guidelines, "Color"; Material Design 3, colour roles). Pick one brand colour for all four. A green that matches the leaves, or the teal, would tie in with the subject.

2. **The pictures don't help anyone choose** (defect). All four cards show the same terracotta pot with two or three generic green leaves. A snake plant has stiff upright blades and a pothos trails, but neither shows here. The snake-plant drawing even has round, floppy leaves. On a plant page, the picture is the main way people decide which plant they want in their room. Right now it's the largest area of every card and tells them nothing, and it reads as a placeholder. Use photos, or drawings that show each plant's real shape and habit.

**Smaller things.**
- Grey subtitle and spec lines ("6 in pot, grows to 2 ft") look a little light. Check that they reach 4.5:1 against white.
- The cards don't say anything about light or care, even though the page is about low light. If one plant copes better with real darkness, or needs less watering, one short line per card would answer the question the shopper came with. This one is taste.
- Everything ends about three-quarters of the way down, leaving a blank band. That's fine for four items, but a link to care guides or other categories would give the page somewhere to go next.

**What's working.**
- The copy is concrete and useful. "More than six feet from a window" gives the shopper a test they can apply in their own room, and "6 in pot, grows to 2 ft" gives a unit and a scale. Keep this voice.
- The hierarchy inside each card is clear: name, then spec, then price in bold, then the action. The price sits right above the button, where people look before they commit.
- The grid is clean, with equal cards, consistent spacing and an aligned left edge. The calm, light frame suits a plant shop.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the buttons and cards, and whether "Cart (0)" updates in a way people notice after they click "Add to cart".
