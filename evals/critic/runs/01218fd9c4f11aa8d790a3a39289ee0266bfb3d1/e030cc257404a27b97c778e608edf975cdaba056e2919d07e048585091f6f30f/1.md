**First read.** My eye goes straight to "See where every dollar goes this month". It's big, bold, white on navy, and it's clearly the main thing. From there I go down to the subhead and the white "Try it free" button. Then the eye drifts right into about 500 px of empty navy and finds nothing. I take it for a personal budgeting app aimed at individuals and households. It feels clean, calm and trustworthy, but bare, like a template with the hero image not yet added.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the first screen of a consumer budgeting app's landing page, and its job is to get a cautious visitor to sign up and connect their bank accounts.

**Verdict.** The copy is good, but the design gives the promise nothing to back it up. The headline says "see where every dollar goes" and the page never shows a single dollar going anywhere. The biggest problem is the empty right half of the hero. The second is that the reason to trust this app with your bank login sits third in a row of small text.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero promises something it never shows (defect).** The text block stops at about x=780, and the right 40% of the navy band is empty. That costs two things. First, the empty space doesn't read as chosen. It isn't shaped or set against anything, so it looks like leftover space or an image that failed to load (Ruder: white space should be gathered and in tension with the type, not left over). Second, the headline is a visual promise ("see where…"), and the viewer has to imagine what they'd see. The product's whole value is a picture of your month, and the page gives you no picture. Fix: put the product itself in that space. A cropped budget view would do it: a few categories with spent and limit bars, one of them close to its limit with the heads-up alert from the subhead. Then the headline, subhead and image make one case together, and the viewer can check the result against what they already know about their own spending.

2. **The trust message is buried (defect).** For a money app, the visitor's real hesitation is "do I hand over my bank login?" The answer, "The app can see balances and history but can never move money", is the strongest line on the page. It's set at about 15 px grey, third of three equal columns, at the same weight as "Shared budgets". The visitor reaching for "Try it free" never sees it. Fix: move a short version up next to the buttons, for example "Read-only. Penny Path can never move your money." That puts the answer where the decision happens. Then give the feature row a distinct job so it isn't three equal teasers.

3. **"Try it free" leaves the main question open (defect).** A price is something the viewer will hold you to, and this line can be read two ways: free forever, or a free trial that will want a card. A viewer who suspects the second hesitates. A viewer who assumes the first and later gets charged feels misled. The label also names a category ("try it") instead of the first thing that happens. Fix: say exactly what happens and what it costs, for example "Connect your bank, free for 30 days, no card" or "Start free, no card needed", whichever is true. Butterick's and Krug's advice that labels say what happens points the same way.

**Smaller things.**
- **The two hero buttons are close in strength.** On dark navy, a 2 px white outline with a bold white label is nearly as loud as the white filled button. Make "Watch the tour" a lighter outline or a text link with a play icon, so "Try it free" is plainly the one action (Material and Refactoring UI: one high-emphasis action per view).
- **The feature row is spaced badly.** It starts only about 45 px below the hero, then about 200 px of empty white follows it. The space is scattered, not gathered. Give the row more room above it, or a short section heading, so it reads as its own band.
- **The page looks templated.** A navy hero, a neutral grotesque face, three columns of features, and a bold text wordmark could belong to any fintech. The cost is that nothing tells the visitor how this differs from the budgeting app they already tried. The product image in point 1 is the cheapest fix for this too.
- **The "Penny Path" wordmark is plain bold text** at about the size of the feature headings, so the brand has no presence. That's taste, but even a small mark or colour accent would help.

**What's working.**
- **The headline copy.** It's concrete, it addresses the reader directly, and "this month" gives it a time frame. Keep it.
- **The subhead.** It uses three verbs in the order the user will do them: connect, set a limit, get a heads-up. It says who does what and when, with no gaps for the reader to fill.
- **The type is restrained.** I count about four sizes (roughly 56, 20, 18 and 15 px). The headline dominates clearly, and the feature headings use bold rather than extra size to stand out. Contrast is comfortably good throughout, and the feature text stays at a readable 40 to 50 characters per line.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I can't tell whether the empty right half of the hero and the blank band at the bottom are the design or an image or section that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check both in a live browser. If an image is meant to be there, finding 1 is about making it the product, not about adding one.
