**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Before you board", drops to the small grey "PASSENGER NOTES" above it, then reads down three even paragraphs. It reads as an information page for a small river ferry service, written for someone about to travel. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy. It also feels like a paragraph to read rather than something you can check quickly on the way to the landing.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page that tells a passenger what they need to know before catching the ferry: when it runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** The typography is good and gives the page a clear order. As information, though, it falls short. The facts a passenger plans around are vague words buried inside sentences, so a reader can't use this page to work out when to leave the house.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times can't be acted on.** This is in paragraphs 1 and 3. "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat goes. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say from when, so a passenger can't work out departure times. "In winter" doesn't say which months. "One boat each hour" doesn't say on the hour or at some other minute. These are the lines a passenger will hold the operator to, and each can be read more than one way. Someone who reads "late evening" as 10 pm when the last boat is at 8 pm is left on the quay. Then the last sentence sends them to printed timetables "in the shelter at each landing", which is exactly where they aren't while reading this page. **Defect.** Fix: give the first and last sailing times, the winter dates, and the minutes past the hour. Link to the timetable, or show it on this page.

2. **The facts are buried in prose.** The page holds about five facts a passenger needs: the route, how often it runs, how long a crossing takes, the bike rules, and winter changes. Someone skimming before boarding has to read every line to find "twelve minutes" or "no extra charge", because all the text is the same weight and the same kind of thing (*Text is broken into chunks*; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: make pages scannable). **Convention**, and breaking it costs something here, because this page is meant for checking quickly. Fix: put the key facts in a short labelled list or a two-column fact block (Runs / Crossing / Bikes / Winter). Keep the prose only for the bike boarding steps, which really are a sequence. The empty right third of the page could hold that block.

**Smaller things.**
- Body measure: the first two lines run about 83–88 characters, past the 45–75 range (Bringhurst). Narrow the column by roughly 10–15% or raise the size slightly.
- Paragraph 2: "Riders wheel them on…" talks about a third person. Address the reader as "you" ("Wheel your bike on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock it to the side rail") so it reads as an instruction.
- "Printed timetables are kept…" only states a fact. Say what it means for the reader ("Pick up a printed timetable at either landing"), or replace it with the link from finding 1.

**What's working.**
- The type scale: a small tracked-caps eyebrow, a bold heading and one body size. That's three clear levels with big steps between them, and the eyebrow gets its difference from weight, caps and grey rather than another near-size (Refactoring UI). Keep it.
- The spacing: the heading sits closer to its text than to the eyebrow above, the space between paragraphs is generous, and the leading suits the size. The page is easy on the eye.
- The bike paragraph gives a clear order (foot passengers first, then bikes, then lock to the rail). It's the one part a passenger could follow without guessing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column behaves on a phone, where most passengers would read this at the landing. Check that the measure and any fact block hold up at 320–390 px.
