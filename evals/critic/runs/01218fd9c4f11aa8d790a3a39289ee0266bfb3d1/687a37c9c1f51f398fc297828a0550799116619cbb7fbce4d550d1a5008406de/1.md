**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into an even column of short paragraphs. Each paragraph is one sentence with a full blank line under it. It reads as a plain, calm help page for people about to take a river ferry. It feels tidy and easy to trust, but every part of it carries the same weight. Nothing tells the eye where the facts a passenger needs are, so you end up reading it top to bottom like a letter.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page passengers check before or while travelling, to find out when the boat runs, how to board, and how to pay. That makes it something people scan for a few facts, not something they read through.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and readable, but it doesn't do its job yet. The facts a passenger acts on (times, prices, the last boat) are missing or vague. Also, the space between sections is the same as the space between paragraphs, so the headings barely split the page into parts.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts a passenger acts on aren't there.** This is a defect. The intro says the ferry runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". The tickets section says tickets are "sold on board and at the landing kiosk" but gives no price for a single, a return or the season pass. Winter service says the timetable "thins to one boat each hour, and the last…" and that's where the screenshot cuts off. A passenger checking this page wants to know when the first and last boats leave and what a crossing costs, and the page gives neither. Someone reading "late evening" could plan for 10 pm when the last boat is 8:40, and get stranded. Give the actual first and last departure times, the months "winter" covers, and each ticket price with what it covers. Put the times at the top, because that's the first thing people come for. Times and prices are what readers will later hold the operator to, so they need to be exact numbers and impossible to read two ways.

2. **Sections don't separate from each other.** This is a defect, backed by a published rule. By eye, the gap between two paragraphs is about the same as the gap above each heading (baseline to baseline it looks like roughly 52 px in both places, for example "…late evening." to "Each crossing…" against "…against the boat." to "Boarding"). The only thing marking a new section is the heading's bold weight, and the space tells the reader nothing. With every paragraph followed by a full blank line, the page breaks into nine or ten equal pieces instead of three or four groups, and a skimming reader can't see where "Boarding" ends and "Tickets" begins. Space should show the groups: gaps inside a group have to be clearly smaller than gaps between groups (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II, on grouping by proximity). Bringhurst (*Elements*, 2.2.2) allows subheads to have more space above than below, as one option. Two fixes, best done together: about double the space above each heading, and tighten the paragraph gap to around half a line. Some of these one-sentence paragraphs could also be merged, since they're steps in one sequence (the two boarding paragraphs, for example).

3. **The boarding rules are written as a story when they're really instructions.** This is taste. "Foot passengers board first, from the ramp on the upstream side" and "Riders with bicycles wait at the gate until the crew waves them on…" are steps people follow while standing at the dock. The three bullets below them are rules, but the steps before them are buried in paragraphs. Splitting this into "On foot" and "With a bicycle", each as a short list of steps, would let someone with a bike find their own instructions without reading about everyone else's. The voice is already clear, so keep it.

**Smaller things.**
- Most of the right half of the screen is empty, and the column sits off to the left. A ragged-left reading column is fine here, so this isn't a defect. A short "At a glance" box of times and prices could use that space and solve finding 1 at the same time.
- The title, "Notes for ferry passengers", could be on any page and tells a stranger nothing about this one. Naming the actual route (north landing to market quay) would confirm at a glance that they're on the right page.
- The bulleted list sits indented inside the text column, so the bullets break the clean left edge. Hang the bullets in the margin, or line them up with the text edge.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: three sizes (the title, section heads, and body text) with clear steps between them, and weight does the remaining work. Keep that.
- Line length looks to be about 65–70 characters, with comfortable line spacing and strong contrast between the dark grey text and the white background. The body reads easily.
- The tone is plain and specific where it does give detail ("the ramp on the upstream side", "the rail along the side of the deck"). Bring that same precision to the times and prices.
