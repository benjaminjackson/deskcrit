**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then down the paragraph to the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a ferry operator's landing page, aimed at day-trippers and locals. It feels calm, clean and competent, but also bare: everything sits in the left 60% of the frame, the right side and the bottom third are empty, and it looks a little unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top section of a web page for a small passenger ferry, and its job is to get a would-be passenger to decide to take the boat and find out when it sails.

**Verdict.** It's clean and easy to read, with one clear action, but it doesn't do its job well yet. A passenger's first two questions are "when's the boat?" and "what does it cost?", and the page answers neither. It sells a scenic, calm trip using words alone, next to a large blank area.

**What would move it most**

1. **The copy holds back the facts a passenger acts on (defect).** In the paragraph, "from early morning until late evening" isn't a time anyone can plan around. "The north landing" doesn't say north landing of what, and the page never says where the boat goes or what it costs. A reader can't decide whether to make the 7:40 home or whether this beats the bus, so they have to click through even to judge whether the ferry suits them. "Every forty minutes" is good: it's specific, and it's the kind of detail to use everywhere else. To fix it, give the first and last sailing ("06:20 to 22:40"), name both ends ("from North Landing to [town]"), and give the fare. The strongest version shows the next departure right next to the button ("Next boat 14:20"). The page would then answer the question itself, and the button would be for anyone who wants more.

2. **The right half of the frame does nothing, and the trip's appeal is carried only by words (taste, argued).** The text column stops at about x=790, and roughly 40% of the width and the bottom third are empty. That space isn't shaped around anything, so it reads as leftover. Ruder's tension between printed and unprinted areas needs something for the empty space to push against. More importantly, the selling point is how the trip feels: "calm", "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck". A photo of the deck and the water would show what the headline claims, and right now the empty space is where that picture belongs. Either fill that side with an image that shows the trip, or narrow the frame and move the content toward the centre so the empty space looks intended.

3. **The three-line list repeats the paragraph and has no visible structure (defect).** "Bicycles travel free" and "Seats on the open deck" repeat what the paragraph just said ("Bring a bicycle…", "from the open deck"). Only "Hot drinks on board" is new. The lines are set like body text, slightly smaller, with no markers, and their spacing is about the same as the paragraph's line spacing. They read as stray lines more than as a list of selling points, and the reader goes over the same ground twice. Use those slots for facts the paragraph doesn't carry (fare, crossing time, accessibility, how often the boats run), and give them a form of their own, such as icons or bold lead words, with a clearly larger gap from the paragraph than between the items.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "twelve / calm minutes" splits the phrase "twelve calm minutes". Break after "in" so it reads "Cross the bay in / twelve calm minutes" (phrase-boundary line breaks).
- Type scale: I count five sizes (label ~14, headline ~56, body ~20, list ~18, button ~18). Body and list are too close to read as separate levels. Set the list at body size and separate it with weight or a marker.
- The "Coastal Ferry Co." label uses the same blue as the button. Since blue means "clickable" on this page, it looks like a link. Keep the accent for the action, or make the label clearly a logo or wordmark.

**What's working.**
- The headline is concrete and makes a promise ("twelve calm minutes"). A number plus a feeling is much better than generic "Discover the bay" copy, so keep that approach when rewriting the body.
- There's a single primary action, it clearly looks like a button, and the label names exactly what happens next. Don't add a competing secondary button.
- The type is well set: the paragraph's line length looks about right (around 65–70 characters), the line spacing is generous, the headline is tightly spaced and dark, and the contrast looks comfortable throughout.
