**First read.** My eye goes to the headline, then slides across four big prices that all carry the same weight. Nothing marks one plan as the obvious pick, so I end up reading all four cards column by column. It looks clean, calm and competent. It also looks templated: I could swap the product name and it would still fit, and nothing tells me what the product is.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the pricing section of a SaaS site with editors and viewers (a design or document tool) for freelancers and small studios. Its job is to get each visitor to see which plan fits them and click it.

**Verdict.** The layout is tidy, but the plans don't add up. As you go up the tiers, Free offers more editors than the paid Solo plan. And all four cards look the same, so the page never helps anyone choose. A careful buyer will trip over the first problem. Most buyers will stall on the second.

**What would move it most**

1. **The tiers contradict each other on editors (defect).** In the second row of each card, Free says "3 editors" and Solo, at $12, says "1 editor". Anyone comparing the two learns that paying gets them fewer seats. They then either pick Free and never upgrade, or start to doubt every other number on the page. Free's "2 projects / 7 days" against Solo's "20 projects / 90 days" sets up a ladder, and the editors row breaks it. If it's intentional (Solo means one person, Free lets a small team try it), the card has to say so: "Just you" instead of "1 editor", or a note that Free is for trying it with your team. If it's not intentional, fix the number. Either way, no row should get worse as the price goes up unless a label explains why.

2. **No plan is recommended, so the visitor has to do all the comparing (defect).** The four cards have the same border, white fill, price size and filled blue button. That's four high-emphasis actions with equal pull. Material Design's button guidelines call for one high-emphasis action per view, with the others in lower-emphasis styles such as outlined buttons. Give the page one obvious place to land: the plan you most want sold (probably Team) should get a stronger frame or tint and a "Most teams pick this" style label. Make the other three buttons outlined. The headline "Plans for every size of team" could sit on anyone's page and does none of this work either. A headline that names who each plan is for, or what changes when you upgrade, would get people choosing sooner.

3. **The prices can each be read two ways (defect, because the viewer acts on these numbers).**
   - **Per team or per editor?** "$39 / month" could be a flat price for the team or the price per editor. Under the per-editor reading, Company comes to about $9,900 a month for 100 editors, so a buyer who assumes that walks away. A buyer who assumes flat when it's actually per seat feels misled at checkout. Add the unit: "$39 / month for the whole team" or "per editor".
   - **Fixed price or quote?** Company shows a fixed "$99" but its button says "Talk to us". The visitor can't tell whether $99 is the price or a starting point, or why a $99 plan needs a sales call. Either make it self-serve ("Choose Company") or show the price as "From $99" or "Custom".

4. **The feature rows have no labels, so comparing across means rebuilding each row in your head (defect, small cost per glance but felt on every glance).** The rows do line up across the columns, but they compare things of different kinds: "Community help / Email help / Priority help / Named contact" and "7 days / 90 days / 1 year / Full history". It's never said what "history" holds (version history? undo? backups?). "Full history" also sounds like a different kind of thing from "1 year", not just more of it. Name each row once, such as "Version history: 7 days", or make each line a full phrase ("Restore any version from the last 7 days"), so a stranger knows what they're buying.

**Smaller things**
- About 70 px of empty card sits under every button. The cards look like they were sized for content that isn't there. Shorten the cards to fit, or use the space for the recommended-plan treatment.
- There's a large empty band below the cards. On this screen nothing follows the offer, such as an FAQ, a trust line or a billing toggle. If this is the whole section, close it out with one line (for example, how billing works).
- "/ month" and the audience lines ("For freelancers") are small and light grey on white. They look borderline against WCAG 1.4.3's 4.5:1 for normal text, so measure them. "/ month" carries the price's meaning, so it shouldn't be the faintest text on the card.
- Type scale: I count about five sizes (headline, subhead, plan name, price, body and caption). They're well separated, and the audience line and "/ month" sit close enough to body size that weight and colour carry the difference, which is fine.

**What's working**
- **Strong price figures.** Large, heavy numbers next to small "/ month" text give each card a clear point to land on. Keep that size contrast when you add the unit.
- **One set structure for every card.** Name, audience, price, four rows, button, all in the same order and lined up across the columns, is what makes comparing across possible at all. Keep that rhythm when you add row labels.
- **The subhead does real work.** "Change or cancel at any time. Every plan includes unlimited viewers." answers two real objections in one line. That's the most useful copy on the page.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Hover and focus states on the four buttons, and how the four columns reflow on a phone. On a phone the cards would likely stack, and Free would then be the only plan seen without scrolling.
