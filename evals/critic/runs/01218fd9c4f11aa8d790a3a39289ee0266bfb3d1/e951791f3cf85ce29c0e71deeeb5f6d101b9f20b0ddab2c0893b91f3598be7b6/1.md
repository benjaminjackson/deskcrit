**First read.** The eye goes to the bold sans headline at top left, then drops into three narrow justified columns. Before you read a word, the texture stands out: the columns are full of gaps between words ("minutes    between    early", "In   winter   the   timetable"), so the text looks speckled rather than an even grey. It reads as a short information page about a local ferry. It feels plain and somewhat unfinished: the text block sits in the top-left of the frame, with an empty band down the right and the bottom third left blank.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short reference or feature page that tells passengers how the river ferry runs (schedule, bicycles, winter changes), meant to be read through in full on a screen.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The text is justified on a measure far too short for it, so every column is full of holes and reads slowly. That is the main problem, and fixing it fixes most of the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **Justified text on a very narrow measure. Defect.** All three columns hold only about 25–32 characters per line, and full justification spreads the leftover space into the word gaps. You can see it at "twelve   minutes   in   calm / weather,    and    a    little" in column 1, "bicycle        during        the" in column 2, and "In   winter   the   timetable" in column 3. The reader's eye jumps across holes on almost every line. Word gaps that line up also form rivers (column 1, lines 4–9). Bringhurst (*Elements*, 2.1.1–2.1.2) warns that short justified lines produce "white acne". Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, "The line") says even word spacing becomes "difficult, if not impossible" below about 50 characters per line. Fix: set the text flush-left, ragged-right. If the design must stay justified, widen the measure towards 45–75 characters and turn on hyphenation. Given the space on the page, the simplest fix is two wider columns, or one column at about 65 characters.
2. **The headline promises something the text doesn't deliver. Defect, in the copy.** "Through the seasons" sets up a year of change. The body covers the daily timetable, bicycles, and only one season (winter). A reader looking for summer hours, or for when the winter timetable starts, won't find them. That matters because timetables are exactly what people act on. Fix: either retitle to what the page actually covers (e.g. "Crossing on the river ferry"), or add the seasonal content the title promises. The winter column also needs dates: "in winter" with no start or end date leaves the reader guessing which timetable applies on the day they travel.
3. **Three columns with no labels, on three separate topics. Defect.** The columns are about the schedule, bicycles, and winter/notices. Nothing marks that, so someone who only wants to know "can I bring my bike?" has to read from the start. Fix: give each column a short subhead in the headline's sans, at a weight or size clearly below the headline, so the page can be scanned.
4. **The text block doesn't fill the frame. Taste.** The content sits in roughly the left 80% and top 65% of the canvas. That leaves an empty strip on the right and a large dead area at the bottom, so the page looks cropped or unfinished rather than deliberately spacious. Fix: widen the columns across the full width, which also helps finding 1, or centre the block in the frame.

**Smaller things.**
- Column 1 ends on "harbour wall." and column 3 on "high tide.", each a short last line. Once the measure is fixed, check again for runts.
- The columns end at three different depths, so the bottom edge looks ragged. Wider columns with subheads will make this matter less, but consider balancing them.
- "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives the reader no actual first or last departure time. Give them the times.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing is right: a bold sans headline over a warm, readable serif for the text gives two clear levels with no extra sizes in between. Keep it to these two sizes.
- The headline sits close to the text it introduces, and the gutters between columns are generous and even.
- The copy is plain and concrete ("lock them to the rail along the side of the deck"). It reads like someone who knows the boat explaining it, which is worth keeping.
