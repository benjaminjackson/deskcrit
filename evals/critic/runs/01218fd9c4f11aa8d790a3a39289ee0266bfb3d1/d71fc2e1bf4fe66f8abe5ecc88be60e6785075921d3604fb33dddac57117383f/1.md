**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Plan the week with your whole team", drops to the blue "Create account" button, then moves back up through the form. Within two seconds it reads as the sign-up page for a team planning SaaS called Tallyboard. It feels calm, tidy and safe, and also anonymous: nothing on the page is specific to this product.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the desktop sign-up page for a team planning tool, meant to turn a visitor who is already curious into a new free workspace.

**Verdict.** It works. The form is clean, correctly labelled and has one obvious action. What holds it back is the left half. It spends half the screen on three lines of copy that leave the offer unclear and give no evidence of the product, so the visitor signs up on trust instead of on something they have seen.

**What would move it most**

1. **The offer can be read two ways** (left column, subhead). *Defect.* "Start a free workspace for up to five people. You can invite the rest of the team later." One reading: free for good while you have five people or fewer. The other: you start free, and inviting the rest means paying. The second sentence makes the second reading likely, but the page never says what "later" costs or when it starts. Price is what a visitor holds the maker to. If someone reads "free" as permanent and then hits a paywall at person six, they will feel tricked. Fix: state the terms in one plain clause, e.g. "Free for teams of up to five. Add more people on [plan] at [price] per person." Then the visitor can judge the deal before giving an email address.

2. **The left half shows nothing and argues nothing** (everything below the subhead, roughly 140–600 px wide by 380–800 px tall). *Defect, and partly taste.* "Plan the week with your whole team" could sit on any planning or calendar tool, so the visitor learns nothing about what Tallyboard does differently. Meanwhile the biggest open area on the page is empty space that was left over, not planned. Use that space to let the visitor judge the product against what they use now. A real crop of a week planned in Tallyboard would work, as would one concrete line about what replaces their current spreadsheet, standup or calendar. Then rewrite the headline around that difference. The "Couldn't judge" section notes that this space may be meant for something that didn't load.

3. **The password rule lives only in the placeholder** (Password field). *Defect* (convention: never rely on placeholder-only instructions, WCAG 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions, NN/g). "At least 10 characters" disappears on the first keystroke, which is exactly when the user needs it. Ten characters is longer than many people expect, so this is the field most likely to fail on submit. Fix: move the rule to persistent helper text under the field and leave the placeholder empty. While you're there, drop "Enter first name" and the other "Enter…" placeholders. They repeat the labels and make the empty fields look already filled in.

**Smaller things**
- "Sign In" under the button is grey text with no underline, the same colour as the sentence around it, so it doesn't look clickable. Underline it or give it the link colour. Also fix the capital "I" so it matches the sentence case used everywhere else.
- Five fields is a lot to ask before someone has seen the product. "Company" can probably wait until after sign-up or be inferred from the email domain, and a single "Name" field would replace first/last (Nielsen 8, minimalist design).
- Type scale: I count about seven sizes (headline ~36, card heading ~22, wordmark ~20, subhead ~17, inputs ~15, labels ~14, sign-in line ~13). 20 and 22 collide, and 13/14/15 read as near-misses rather than levels. Keep four sizes and let weight separate the labels from the inputs.
- The button says "Create account" while the left column promises a "workspace". Make the button name what the user gets, e.g. "Create free workspace", so it carries the offer.
- The input borders look light against white. Measure them against the 3:1 minimum for component boundaries (WCAG 1.4.11).
- The wordmark uses the same saturated blue as the button. It's minor, but it dilutes the one accent that should mean "act here".

**What's working**
- The card heading's cap line aligns with the wordmark on the left, and the two columns start from a shared top edge. That alignment holds the split layout together, so keep it when you add content to the left.
- The form itself is right: labels above the fields and close to them, consistent gaps between groups, one full-width filled primary button, and the secondary action clearly weaker. Pairing first and last name on one row is the only side-by-side pair, and it's a legitimate one.
- The restraint suits the moment. One neutral ramp and one accent make the button the only loud thing in the card.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the empty lower-left area is intentional or a hero image that didn't load. Check it in a live browser.
- Error and validation states, focus rings, and how the two columns stack on narrow screens. On a sign-up form these matter: check that the password rule appears inline before submit, and that the form doesn't get pushed below the fold on mobile.
