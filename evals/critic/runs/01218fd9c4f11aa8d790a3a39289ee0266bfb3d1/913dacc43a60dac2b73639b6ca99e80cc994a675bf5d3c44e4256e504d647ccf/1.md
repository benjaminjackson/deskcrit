**First read.** The eye goes to the three blue bars, then up to "Rainfall at the reservoir", then right to a paragraph about buses and parking. Within two seconds I know the topic but not a single fact about it: there are no numbers, no dates, and no labels on the bars. The directions paragraph is pressed hard against the chart's frame, so at first glance it reads as the chart's caption, and it has nothing to do with rainfall. The bottom 40% of the page is empty. It feels unfinished, like a wireframe that has been filled in with the wrong copy.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public information page for a reservoir site, meant to show visitors recent rainfall and, separately, tell them how to get there.

**Verdict.** It fails both jobs. The chart has no labels, so it tells the viewer nothing. The directions are placed so tightly against the chart that they look like its caption, which makes the page read as one confused message instead of two clear ones.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart has no data a viewer can read.** *(Defect.)* The three bars have no category labels (months? years? sites?), no value axis, no units (mm?), no values, and no date range or source. The viewer can only see that the middle bar is tallest, without knowing what it is or by how much. Nobody can act on that, so the chart is decoration. Tufte (*Visual Display*, ch. 2) and Few both say every chart needs labelled axes or direct labels, with units and a source. Fix: label each bar under the baseline, put the value with its unit directly on or above each bar (with only three bars this can replace an axis), and add the period and source in small type below.

2. **The directions paragraph is attached to the wrong thing.** *(Defect.)* "Getting there" and its text start at the chart frame's right edge with almost no gap, and the heading's top lines up with the frame's top. Placed that close, the two read as one group (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II, on proximity), so the viewer looks for a link between bus times and rainfall and finds none. The page title also covers only half of the page's content. Fix: separate the two topics clearly. Either move "Getting there" below the chart with a large gap between them, or keep two columns and put a real gutter between them, at least as wide as the space under the title. If the directions matter as much as the rainfall, the page needs a title that covers both, or one subhead for each section.

3. **The chart's frame outweighs its data.** *(Convention.)* The box around the plot is a dark grey line about 3px thick on a pale blue fill. Its edges are as strong as the bars and heavier than the baseline. The frame makes the chart look like a placeholder and pulls attention to the box instead of the bars. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*) and Tufte (ch. 4–5) say to remove ink that shows no data. Fix: drop the frame and the tint, and make the bars the darkest marks in the chart, with a thin pale baseline.

4. **The title names a topic but gives no finding.** *(Taste.)* "Rainfall at the reservoir" tells the viewer what the chart is about, not what it shows. Once the data is labelled, a title that states the finding (for example, which month was wettest and by how much compared with normal) lands the point on the reader. None of my sources state this rule; I'm making the case on its own merits, because visitors skim and the title is what they will remember.

**Smaller things.**
- Composition: everything sits in the top-left 60% of the page, leaving a large empty block bottom and right that reads as left over rather than chosen. Once the content is fixed, set the column widths on a grid that covers the page.
- Type scale: about three sizes (title, subhead, body). That's a reasonable scale, but "Getting there" is only a little larger than the body text and relies mostly on weight. It's fine, but give it more space above so it heads its own section.
- Text alternative: the chart's message isn't stated anywhere in text. Add a one-line summary so people who can't see the chart get the finding (WCAG).
- Bar order: with no labels I can't tell whether the order is by time. If the bars are time periods, keep them in time order and say so on the labels.

**What's working.**
- The body copy is concrete and useful: route number, frequency, which gate, when parking is free, where the cycle racks are. Keep that plain, specific voice.
- The text is set well: a measure of roughly 45–50 characters, comfortable line spacing, flush left, and dark text on white with strong contrast.
- The bars start from a visible baseline and use one solid colour with no 3D or effects. That is the right base to label on top of.
