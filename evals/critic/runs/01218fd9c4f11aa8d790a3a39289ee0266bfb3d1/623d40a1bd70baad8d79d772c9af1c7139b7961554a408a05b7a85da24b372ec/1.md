**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. Headings and answers are the same size, weight and colour, so the page reads as eight lines of grey-black text, broken up only by spacing. It takes a second look to see it as an FAQ, a help page for a small bakery's pickup orders. It feels calm and plain, but flat. The last line says "Each heading here stands out clearly, so the page is easy to scan." That is untrue, and it is the first thing a careful reader will notice.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help or FAQ section for customers who order bread online and collect it, who scan for their one question and want a clear answer.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job of letting someone scan for their question. The headings are styled almost exactly like the body text, so they don't work as headings. Two answers that customers will act on (the cancellation cutoff and who shows what at the counter) can also be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings don't stand out from the answers (defect).** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else" and the other two headings are the same regular weight and the same colour as the answers, and about the same size. Someone looking for "cancel" has to read each line to find out whether it's a question or an answer. Only the spacing tells them apart: the gap between a heading and its answer looks slightly tighter than the gap between items. That helps, but it isn't enough to scan by. Refactoring UI's advice applies here: make it bolder, not bigger. Set the headings in semibold or bold and leave their size alone, or drop the answers to a softer grey. Also make the gap between items clearly larger than the gap between a heading and its answer, roughly double, so each pair reads as one unit.
2. **"Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" can be read two ways (defect, on a line people will hold you to).** It could mean 24 hours before the pickup time, or any time before the end of the day before. A customer who reads it the second way and tries to cancel at 9 pm the night before a 10 am pickup gets refused and feels cheated. Give the exact cutoff, for example "You can cancel until [time] the day before your pickup." The line after it ("the bread has already been baked for you") is good. It tells the customer why the rule exists, so keep it.
3. **"Along with a name" doesn't say whose name (defect).** The person collecting could think they should give their own name or yours. If the counter checks one and they give the other, they leave with no bread. Say which, for example: "They'll need your order number and your name at the counter."
4. **The last line is a claim about the design, not content (defect).** "Each heading here stands out clearly, so the page is easy to scan" means nothing to a customer, and it's false on this page. Delete it. If it's placeholder text, make sure it doesn't ship.

**Smaller things.**
- **Line length:** the answers run across about 820 px. The cancellation line looks to be around 95 characters, well over Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the text column to about 600–640 px.
- **Pickup time answer:** "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" doesn't say what happens next. Tell the customer, e.g. "After that we can't guarantee it's still here."
- **Allergies answer:** it only helps someone already standing at the till. Anyone ordering online can't check before they buy. If ingredients are listed on the product pages, say so here.
- **Lower half:** the bottom of the frame is empty. That's fine if this is a section of a longer page. On its own the block looks unfinished, because it has no page title like "Pickup and orders".

**What's working.**
- The copy talks directly to the customer and mostly says who does what ("Open your order and choose a new slot from the list"). Keep that voice.
- There's one typeface at a comfortable size with good contrast, and nothing decorative. That restraint suits a small bakery. The fix is a weight change, not more styling.
- The order of questions follows what customers worry about. Changing and cancelling, the two most common, sit at the top and bottom where they're easy to find once the headings stand out.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this list expands and collapses, and how it reflows on a phone, where most pickup customers will probably read it.
