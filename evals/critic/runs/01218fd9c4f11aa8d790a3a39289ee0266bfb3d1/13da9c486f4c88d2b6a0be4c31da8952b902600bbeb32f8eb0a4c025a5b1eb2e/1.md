**First read.** The eye goes straight to the heavy serif headline, "Notes from the boat shed", then drops to two short paragraphs side by side. It reads as the opening of a community or club page, perhaps a newsletter, for a volunteer boat-restoration group. It feels calm, warm and literate. Then the content stops a little over a third of the way down, and the lower 60% of the frame is blank.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or panel for a local boat shed. Its job is to make a passer-by want to come and help with the dinghies.

**Verdict.** The typography is well made, but the piece doesn't yet do its job. It sets a lovely mood and then gives an interested reader nothing to act on: no time, no place, no way in. The empty lower half makes it look unfinished rather than calm.

**What would move it most**

1. **The invitation has no practical details.** *Defect.* The right column is the only part asking the reader to do anything: "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings… anyone who wants to help." A stranger who is persuaded still can't turn up:
   - "Thursday evenings" has no time.
   - There's no address. "The end of the lane" only makes sense to people who already know the lane.
   - There's no contact and no line saying what to do first.

   These are exactly the facts a reader acts on and holds you to. Give them a separate short block below the two paragraphs, in a bolder weight or set apart, not buried in the prose. For example: "Thursdays, 6–9 pm · Slipway Lane, [town] · Just turn up, or email …". The prose can keep its voice. The ask needs to be one exact thing the reader can do.

2. **The headline names a genre, not the offer.** *Taste, plus a copy point.* "Notes from the boat shed" is a charming title, but it promises a diary. The real message is in the right-hand column: you can come and help, and you don't need experience. A reader who skims only the headline, which most will, never learns that volunteers are wanted. Keep the title if you love it, but let a line just under it or the first sentence on the left say the offer plainly. Or swap the columns so the invitation is read first.

3. **The white space is leftover, not shaped.** *Convention (Ruder: blank and printed areas should be in tension, and that tension comes from contrast).* All the content sits in the top 300 px. Below it is an unbroken white field with nothing to pull against. There's no image, no closing element, and no anchor at the bottom. At this size it reads as "the rest didn't load" rather than "deliberate quiet". Either give the space a job (a photo of the dinghies on their trestles would do more for this copy than any type change, plus the details block from point 1), or tighten the frame so the text sits in a composed area.

**Smaller things.**
- **Columns read as equals.** Two equal columns imply two parallel ideas. Here the second is a call to action, so it deserves a different treatment, not a twin. This becomes part of the fix for point 1.
- **Headline spacing.** At this size the word spaces in the headline look slightly open. A little negative tracking would make it hold together as one shape.

**What's working.**
- **Type pairing and scale.** A heavy display serif sits over a regular text serif from what looks like the same family. That gives clear contrast in both size and weight with only two levels, which is right for this little content.
- **Measure and leading.** Each column runs about 55–65 characters per line with generous line spacing. The baselines line up across the two columns. It's comfortable to read; keep it.
- **The writing.** "Tar and cut cedar", "a kettle on", "a spare apron on the hook by the door" are specific and sensory, and nobody else's page would say them. "Nobody needs experience" removes the main objection in three words. This is the strongest thing in the piece.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I can't tell whether this is the whole piece or the top of a longer scrolling page. If more content sits below the 800 px fold, point 3 weakens. Point 1 still stands, because the invitation is already above the fold and should carry its own details.
