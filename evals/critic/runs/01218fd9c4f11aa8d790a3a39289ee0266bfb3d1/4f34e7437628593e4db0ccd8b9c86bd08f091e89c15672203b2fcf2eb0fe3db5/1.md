**First read.** My eye goes to "$4.82M" in the top-left card, then across to the other two KPIs, then down to the blue bars climbing left to right. Within about two seconds it reads as a SaaS revenue dashboard for a finance lead or founder, and the message is "steady growth". It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. The one jarring note is the black box and dark gridlines around the chart.

**What it's for.** Assumption: an internal overview screen where an exec or finance person checks the year's revenue and growth at a glance, and trusts the numbers enough to quote them.

**Verdict.** This is a competent, well-ordered dashboard with a clear hierarchy. The biggest problem is that its revenue numbers don't agree with each other: the "Revenue by plan, 2025" table totals $4.82M, but the monthly bars add up to roughly $4.5M. A dashboard whose numbers can't be reconciled loses the trust it depends on. The heavy chart frame comes second.

**What would move it most**

1. **The revenue figures conflict, and the labels hide why.** Defect.
   - The table on the right is titled "Revenue by plan, 2025" and totals **$4.82M**. That is the same figure as the ARR card ("Annual recurring revenue, Dec 2025").
   - By eye, the twelve bars in "Monthly revenue, 2025" (recognised revenue) add up to about **$4.49M**.
   - December alone is about $447k. Multiplied by 12 that's about $5.4M, which doesn't match the $4.82M ARR either.
   - So a viewer can read the table two ways: as 2025 revenue (then it contradicts the chart by about $330k) or as ARR split by plan (then the title is wrong).
   - Either way, the first person who quotes "$4.82M revenue in 2025" in a board deck is wrong, and then they stop trusting the screen.
   - **Fix:** state what the table measures. If it's ARR by plan, title it "ARR by plan, Dec 2025". If it's 2025 recognised revenue, its total must match the chart. If ARR and recognised revenue legitimately differ (one-off fees, timing), add one line under the chart explaining the gap. People act on these numbers, so a line that can be read two ways is a defect, not a style note.

2. **The chart frame and gridlines are as dark as the data.** Convention (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*; Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 5).
   - The plot area has a near-black 1–2 px box, and the gridlines at 100–400 are dark grey, cutting straight across every bar.
   - This is the heaviest non-data ink on the page. It makes the chart look like a spreadsheet export next to the soft KPI cards, and the gridlines slice the bars into bands, so the growth line reads less cleanly.
   - **Fix:** remove the frame, and make the gridlines thin and pale grey (or keep only a light baseline at 0), so the bars are the darkest thing in the chart.

3. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding.** Taste, argued on common sense.
   - "Monthly revenue, 2025" makes the viewer work out the story from the bars: steady growth, small dips in April and July, December the highest month.
   - On an overview screen, one line that states the takeaway (for example, "Revenue grew about 43% from January to December") would save that work and give the dips a reason to be noticed.
   - Optionally, label the December bar with its value so there's an exact anchor.

**Smaller things**
- **Plan table:** gives absolute figures only. Adding each plan's share or its change from 2024 would tell the viewer which plan is driving growth, which is the question the table raises.
- **Date chip** ("Jan to Dec 2025", top right): it's unclear whether it's a filter or a label. If it can be clicked, give it a chevron or an obvious control style. If not, make it plain text.
- **Nav vs page title:** the nav highlights "Overview" but the page is titled "Revenue overview", and "Revenue" is a separate nav item. This blurs which page you're on. Title the page "Overview" or rename the nav item.
- **Green delta text** ("+18% vs Dec 2024" etc.): it's small and mid-green on a pale tint and looks borderline. Check it reaches 4.5:1. The "+" sign already gives a non-colour cue, which is good.
- **Mixed comparison bases in the KPI cards:** "vs Dec 2024", "+3 pts vs 2024" and "vs 2024" are three different comparison bases in one row. That's defensible, but a viewer has to read each one carefully.

**What's working**
- **KPI cards:** each one carries a metric name, a period, a single dominant number and a comparison. That's exactly what makes a headline number readable (Few). Keep that structure.
- **Type and hierarchy:** about four sizes doing distinct jobs (page title, KPI numbers, section heads, body and labels) with no near-duplicates. The order of reading is never in doubt.
- **Chart basics:** bars start at zero, units are stated in the subtitle ("Thousands of US dollars, recognised revenue"), and there's a source line with a scope ("closed invoices only"). That honesty is why the conflict in finding 1 stands out, and fixing it would make the whole screen trustworthy.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the date chip and the bars respond to interaction (filters, tooltips with exact values). The fixes for the chip and the chart title depend on that.
