**First read.** The headline grabs the eye straight away, as it should. It is big, heavy and near-black, and it is the only thing on the page with any real weight. From there the eye drops to the grey subhead and then to the blue button, then runs out of things to look at. The whole bottom third and the right half of the page are empty. It reads as a team-planning SaaS homepage: calm, competent and tidy, and nearly anonymous. Swap the logo and it could belong to any of a dozen tools.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero of a marketing homepage for a weekly team-planning tool. It's aimed at team leads or engineering managers, and its job is to get them to start a trial.

**Verdict.** The layout works: one dominant element, a clean eye path and one clear action. The weakness is the promise. The page promises "one honest picture" of the week and never shows it. A lead who already juggles a ticket tracker, a calendar and a leave sheet gets no proof that this one is different, so the button asks them to act on faith.

**What would move it most**

1. **The page describes the picture instead of showing it (defect).** The subhead's whole pitch is a visual result: tickets, meetings and leave merged into one view of five days. Yet the page has no image, no product view and no example. The space where that proof would go, the empty right half and the bottom third, is unused. So the viewer has to imagine the product, and most won't bother. The fix: put the actual week view in that space. Show a real-looking team with one person overloaded because of a meeting-heavy Wednesday and someone out on Friday. The viewer should spot the problem in the picture before they read a word about it. That one change also fixes the empty space and gives the composition a second weight to balance against the headline.

2. **The ask is generic and gives the viewer nothing to compare against (defect, copy).** "Start a free trial" could sit on any SaaS page. It invites the viewer to go and grade the product, but doesn't help them judge its value on terms they already trust. The strongest version sets Weeplan next to what the lead already has. For example, "See your team's real week: connect Jira and Google Calendar". Or a secondary line like "Import next week's tickets and see who's over capacity in two minutes". Either way the comparison is their own data against the plan they think they have. That's an argument they already accept.

3. **"One honest picture" stops at the fact and doesn't land on the viewer (defect, copy).** The concrete inputs (tickets, meetings, leave) are good. But "honest picture" leaves the viewer to work out what it means for them, and "honest" compared with what? Finish the line with what the viewer gains, said to them: "…so you see who's overbooked before Monday's standup, not after Thursday's missed deadline." The headline already has a point of view ("actually works"). The subhead should cash it in.

**Smaller things**
- The headline's letter and word spacing look loose for a bold sans at this size (about 90 px). The word gaps in "Plan the week your" read as holes. Tighten the tracking slightly (a small negative value) so the two lines hold together as one shape (Bringhurst: display type is set tighter than text).
- Nothing near the button answers the obvious trial questions: how long the trial is, whether a card is needed, which tools it connects to. Add one small line under the button.
- The nav's "Sign in" has the same weight and colour as "Product" and "Pricing". That's acceptable, but returning users would find it faster if it were set slightly apart, for example with extra space before it.

**What's working**
- **The hierarchy is clean.** There are three plain levels: headline, subhead and button. The blue fill is the only saturated colour on the page, so it's unmistakably the action. Keep that discipline when you add the product image: don't let the image bring in competing accent colours.
- **The headline has a stance.** "The week your team *actually* works" names a real gap between the planned week and the real one. That's more specific than most category headlines. Keep it.
- **The subhead's measure and contrast are right.** It runs about 60 characters a line in a mid-grey that still looks comfortably readable. It's subordinate to the headline without turning faint.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- The empty lower third may be a hero image or product shot that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check in a live browser. If a visual does load there, finding 1 becomes about whether that visual shows the merged week clearly enough to make the case.
