**First read.** The eye goes straight to the tall Growth bar in the middle, then to the short Starter bar on the left. It reads as an internal analytics dashboard page, clean and calm and a little templated. Within two seconds I've concluded that "Growth converts more than twice as well as Starter". That conclusion is wrong.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal analytics view that tells a product or growth team how trial-to-paid conversion differs by plan, so they can decide where to act.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The truncated value axis makes a 3.1%-vs-4.0% gap look like a 2.5× gap, so the first impression misleads anyone who doesn't read the labels. Everything else on the page is competent and secondary to that.

**What would move it most**

1. **The bar chart's value axis starts at 2.5%, not zero.** *Defect, and it breaks a convention.*
   - **What the viewer sees:** Starter's bar is about 0.6 points tall above the baseline and Growth's is about 1.5, so Growth looks about 2.5× Starter. The real ratio is 1.29×. Enterprise (3.4%) looks roughly half of Growth when it's 85% of it. A reader who scans the shapes, which is what bars invite, will overrate the differences between plans and may move budget or pricing on that basis.
   - **Rule:** bar length encodes value, so the bars have to start at zero (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 17.1, proportional ink; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 8).
   - **Fix:** start the axis at zero so the bar lengths keep the proportions of the values. If the small differences are the point, switch to dots on a scale that doesn't start at zero, labelled as such.

2. **The chart presents noise-sized differences as findings.** *Defect, my judgement; no approved source covers this.*
   - Enterprise is 38 conversions out of 1,120, and Growth is 118 out of 2,950. At those counts, 3.4% vs 3.6% (Enterprise vs Team) is very likely within noise. Growth vs Starter is probably real but less certain than it looks.
   - The labels are bolded to one decimal and nothing shows uncertainty, so every gap reads as equally solid. Combined with the cut axis, the page overstates its own evidence.
   - **Fix:** show an interval for each rate, or at least the n on the bar label ("3.4% · 38 of 1,120"). Then the viewer can see which gaps are worth acting on.

3. **The headline names the chart but doesn't say what it shows.** *Taste.*
   - "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells me the axes, not the answer. The viewer has to work out the takeaway from the bars, and right now the bars give them the wrong one.
   - Once the axis is honest, a title that states the result does the reader's work and stops the shape from overstating it. Something like "Growth trials convert best, at 4.0%; the other plans sit between 3.1% and 3.6%".

**Smaller things**
- **Table and chart are disconnected:** the right-hand table gives counts but no rate column, so the reader can't check the bars against it without doing the division. Add a "Rate" column, or fold the counts into the chart labels and drop the table.
- **Table title:** "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" also holds the Paid column. Call it "Trials and paid conversions by plan".
- **Definition paragraph:** "Trials still open on 30 October" quietly implies the 30-day window, but trials started on 1 July closed long before that date. Say it directly: "Each trial gets 30 days; trials started late in September are followed until 30 October."
- **Right column:** the lower half is empty while the chart is tall. If the table goes or shrinks, the chart can take the full width. If both stay, align the bottom of the definition text with something in the chart.
- **Gridlines:** the gridlines at 0.5% steps put more lines than data in the chart's height. With a zero baseline, two or three lines are enough because the bars are labelled directly.

**What's working**
- **Direct value labels on the bars:** they're the one thing that rescues the chart. Keep them when you fix the axis.
- **Subtitle and source line:** the subtitle states exactly what's being measured (share of Q3 trial starts paid within 30 days), and the source line gives the date range. That's the context that makes a metric trustworthy, so keep both.
- **Restraint:** one accent colour, plans in tier order, quiet navigation with the current section marked. The page has no clutter competing with the data.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bars have hover tooltips with counts or intervals, which would partly answer point 2.
