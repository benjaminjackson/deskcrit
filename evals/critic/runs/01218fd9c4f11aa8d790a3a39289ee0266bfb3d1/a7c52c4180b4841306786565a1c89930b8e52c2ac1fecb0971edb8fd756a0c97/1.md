**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Library opening hours" at top left, then drops down three bold branch names in a neat stack. It looks calm, plain and trustworthy, like a council or community library web page. Then you start reading the hours, and you slow down: "nine to eight", "ten to four". You have to turn each one into a time in your head, and one of them turns into the wrong time.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or panel where a library user checks when a branch is open, usually to answer "can I go now, or today?"

**Verdict.** The layout and type are clean and well grouped, but the page fails at its one job. The hours are written out in words, which is slow to read and in some cases says the wrong time, and the mobile library has no times at all. Fix the content first. The design is not what's holding this back.

**What would move it most**

1. **"Ten to four", "ten to six" and "ten to two" each read two ways** (Main branch Saturday, Harbour branch both lines). This is a defect. In everyday British English, "ten to four" means 3:50. So a reader can take "Saturday, ten to four" as "open 10am until 4pm" or as "something happens at 3:50". Opening hours are exactly what people act on and later hold you to. Someone who reads it the second way either turns up confused or doesn't come at all. "Nine to eight" has a smaller version of the same problem: it gives no am or pm, so the reader has to guess that it means evening. Fix: write times as numerals with a clear range, e.g. "9am – 8pm" or "09:00–20:00", using an en dash. The meaning is then fixed, and the reader takes in a time at a glance instead of decoding a phrase (Butterick, *Practical Typography*, on en dashes for ranges).

2. **The mobile library has places but no times** ("Wednesday at the market square", "Thursday at the school gates"). This is a defect. The reader knows where to go but not when, and doesn't know whether it's every week. The mobile library is the one service where turning up at the wrong time means missing it entirely. Fix: give each stop a time range and a frequency, e.g. "Every Wednesday, 10am – 12pm, Market Square". Put the time first so it lines up with the other branches.

3. **You can't scan the hours across branches or days** (all three blocks). This is taste, argued on the job. The page is laid out as prose, one sentence per line, so to answer "what's open on Monday?" or "what's open late?" the reader has to read every line. Days and times never sit in a shared column. Opening hours are lookup data. Fix: set days and times in two aligned columns inside each branch block (day on the left, time on the right, tabular figures), or use one small table of days by branches. Once the times are numerals, an aligned column makes "open until 8pm" stand out at a glance (Few and Tufte: tables suit looking up exact values).

**Smaller things**
- Closed days are only implied by leaving them out: Main branch says nothing about Sunday, Harbour branch says nothing about Monday or Sunday. Add "Closed Sunday" and "Closed Sunday and Monday" so nobody has to work it out from a missing line.
- The heading doesn't name the library or the town, and neither branch has an address. "Harbour branch" means nothing to someone new. Add a one-line address under each branch name.
- All the content sits in a narrow column at the left of a wide frame, with the right two-thirds empty. That's fine on a web page, but if this is a standalone panel or a sign, centre the column or give it a width that fits the frame.

**What's working**
- **Grouping by space.** Lines within a branch sit close together, and each branch is separated by a clearly bigger gap. You see three blocks before you read a word. Keep this ratio (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*: space shows the groups).
- **Hierarchy from weight, not size.** Branch names are the same size as the hours but bold, and the heading is the only larger size. That's three clear levels with no muddle. Keep this restraint when you add times and addresses (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).
- **Strong contrast and a single left edge.** Dark text on white and one shared alignment line make it easy and calm to read.
