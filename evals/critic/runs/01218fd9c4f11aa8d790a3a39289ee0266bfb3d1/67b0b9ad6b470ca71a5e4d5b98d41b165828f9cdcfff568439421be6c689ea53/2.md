**First read.** My eye lands on the tallest purple bar, Growth at 4.0%, then reads across the four labels on the bars, then goes to the table on the right. It reads as a clean, calm analytics page about which plan's trials turn into paying accounts. It feels trustworthy and finished. It also feels flat: the page shows four numbers and doesn't tell me what they mean.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal analytics dashboard page for product, growth or finance people who will use it to decide where to put effort across plans.

**Verdict.** The craft is sound and the numbers are honest. They match the table (286/9,210 = 3.1%, and so on), the bars start at zero, and the source and definition are stated. But the page invites a conclusion the data can't support. The four rates sit within one percentage point of each other, the samples behind them vary a lot in size, and nothing on the page shows that the bars carry different amounts of certainty.

**What would move it most**

1. **The bars look equally certain, but they aren't.** (Defect.) The Enterprise bar rests on 38 conversions out of 1,120 trials. Starter rests on 286 out of 9,210. By my rough calculation, Enterprise's 3.4% is give or take about 1.1 points, and Growth's 4.0% is give or take about 0.7. So the visible "Growth wins, Enterprise lags" story is mostly noise. Only Growth against Starter is a gap that might hold up. A reader will rank the plans by bar height and may move budget on a difference that isn't real. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization* (ch. 16), says to show the uncertainty of estimates. The fix is to add interval whiskers to each bar, or at least an n under each plan label ("n = 1,120"). Then the uncertainty sits where the eye already is, not in a table it has to go and find.

2. **The title names the topic but not the finding.** (Taste, argued on its own terms.) "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells me what the chart is about, so I have to work out the takeaway myself, and after point 1 I'll probably get it wrong. On an internal analytics page, the title is the one line everyone reads. Write the conclusion the data supports there, for example "Growth trials convert best; other plans are within noise of each other". Keep the current line as the subtitle.

3. **The table repeats the chart instead of adding to it, and the right column trails off.** (Taste.) The table gives Trials and Paid but no rate. To connect it to the chart, the reader has to do the division in their head. Below the Definition there's a large empty block, so the page looks heavy on the left and unfinished on the right. Making the table do real work would fix both: add a "Conversion" column (and an interval column if you don't put whiskers on the chart). Optionally, also add something the chart can't show, such as the change since Q2.

**Smaller things**
- The y-axis, five gridlines and the direct labels on the bars all show the same values. The labels are enough, so drop the axis or lighten it further (Tufte, data-ink).
- The 5.0% top gridline leaves an empty band above the bars. Ending the scale at about 4.5%, or dropping the axis, would tighten the chart.
- The table heading "Paid" is ambiguous: paid ever, or paid within 30 days? Use "Paid within 30 days" so it matches the subtitle.
- The source line says "1 July to 30 September 2025", but the definition counts payments up to 30 October. Say the date range covers trial starts, with payments tracked to 30 October.
- The definition says "first invoice is paid" and the subtitle says "became paid accounts". Pick one term.
- The source line and the table column headings are small and grey. They look readable, but check them against 4.5:1 contrast.

**What's working**
- The typography and spacing are disciplined. There are about four type sizes, each with a clear job, plus one small size for the source line. The single purple used for the brand, the active tab and the bars ties the page together without shouting. Keep it.
- Labelling each bar directly with its rate, and placing the plan names under the bars, means the reader never needs a legend.
- Stating the definition and the source on the page is exactly what an analytics page should do. It's why this page is fixable rather than misleading at its core.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether hovering over a bar shows n or an interval. If it does, the main finding gets smaller, but the static reading still misleads anyone who doesn't hover.
