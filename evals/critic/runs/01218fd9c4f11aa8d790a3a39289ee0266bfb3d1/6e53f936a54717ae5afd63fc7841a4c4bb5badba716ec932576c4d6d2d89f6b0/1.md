**First read.** The eye goes straight to the photo in the first card. It is the darkest, busiest thing on the page, and it is a dim wood-panelled room with leaded windows and red leather sofas. Then it jumps to the headline, then slides along the row of pastel illustrations. It reads as a friendly local home-services site: warm, tidy, trustworthy. That one photo makes it read like a mock-up someone forgot to finish.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the homepage hero of a local home-services company. A homeowner should see what Fernway offers and what it costs, then book.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the copy is mostly clear. But the odd photo in the first card pulls attention away from everything else, and the page shows prices without giving a clear way to book from them. Fix those two and it does its job.

**What would move it most**

1. **The window-cleaning photo breaks the set and grabs the eye** (defect). Three cards use flat illustrations on a pale mint tile. The first card uses a dark, high-detail photo of a stately interior, which looks more like a hotel lounge than a home. Because it has the strongest contrast on the page, it becomes the focal point. It also suggests the wrong customer: ornate, old, not the viewer's house. It doesn't show clean glass either, so it doesn't sell the service. Repeated elements need one rendering style. Mixing styles tells the viewer something differs about this item when nothing does. Fix: draw a window-cleaning illustration in the same style as the other three. Or, if you want photos, use them on all four cards with the same treatment (light, bright, real homes).

2. **The prices don't say what you get, and the page asks too little** (defect: prices are what people hold you to).
   - **Missing units.** "From $24" for laundry: per bag, per load, per pound? "From $89" for windows: per visit, per house, up to how many windows? A viewer can't compare these with what they pay now, so they can't judge the value. They may also expect $24 to cover a week's laundry and feel misled at checkout. Add the unit to every price: "From $24 a bag", "From $89 for up to 10 windows".
   - **No ask where the decision happens.** The only action is "Book a visit" in the top-right corner. The hero has no button. The cards show no sign they can be clicked: no arrow, no "Book", and the price isn't styled as a link. Someone who has just found the service and price they want has nowhere obvious to go. Fix: give each card a clear booking action ("Book window cleaning →"), or put one primary button under the subhead. Krug and Norman both apply here: an action should signal itself.

3. **Laundry's fixed days contradict the headline** (defect, copy). The headline promises "on your schedule". Then the laundry card says "We collect on Monday and bring it back folded on Wednesday", which is the opposite. A viewer will reasonably decide laundry only runs on one day a week. If it does, reword the headline so it doesn't overpromise. If there are more slots, say so: "Pick a collection day and we bring it back folded two days later."

**Smaller things**
- Headline break: "on your / schedule" leaves one word alone on line two because the text box is narrow. Widen it so the headline sets on one line, or break it as "Help around the house, / on your schedule."
- Headline copy could sit on any competitor's page. The subhead does the real work ("vetted crew arrives with their own supplies"). Consider pulling that difference into the headline.
- "Garden care … for small yards" tells anyone with a bigger yard they're excluded but doesn't say where "small" ends. Give a size, or drop the limit and price it per yard.
- Prices in teal on white look about right for contrast, but at this small bold size they're worth measuring to confirm they pass 4.5:1.
- There's a large empty band below the cards with nothing to close the section (no trust line, no service-area note). It's fine if more content follows when you scroll. If this is the whole page, it feels unfinished.

**What's working**
- The cards are disciplined: same width, equal gaps, image, title, two-line description, then the price pinned to the same baseline in every card. The prices line up across the row and are easy to compare. Keep that.
- The type pairing works. A slab-serif display face for the wordmark and headline sits against a plain sans for everything else, so it contrasts clearly with no clash. The scale is tight: headline, subhead, card title, body, price.
- The subhead is good copy. It says who does what and when ("Pick a service, choose a day, and a vetted crew arrives with their own supplies") and answers the two worries people actually have: who comes into my house, and do I need to supply anything.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the cards are clickable and what hover state they show.
- How the four-card row reflows on a phone, which is where most people will book home services.
