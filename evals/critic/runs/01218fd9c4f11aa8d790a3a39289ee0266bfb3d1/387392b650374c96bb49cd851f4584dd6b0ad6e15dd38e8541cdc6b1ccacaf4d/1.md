**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title, then to the tall Organic search bar on the left, then down the staircase of bars to Events. It reads straight away as a quarterly sign-up report for a growth or marketing team. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy. The point of the page sits in the right-hand column, and nothing in the chart sends you there, so you may never read it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal growth dashboard page. It has to tell the team which channels brought Q3 sign-ups, what changed, and what happens next.

**Verdict.** This is a sound, honest chart and it mostly does its job. The biggest thing in the way is that the chart shows the ranking but not the story. The two findings that matter (organic search grew, podcast ads and events are being cut) live only in a side column the eye reaches last, and the chart gives them no visual weight.

**What would move it most**

1. **The finding is in the sidebar, not on the chart or in the title.** "New sign-ups by channel" names a topic. The sentences under "What changed this quarter" hold the actual news: organic search went from a quarter to a third of sign-ups, and the events budget moves to referrals. A reader who skims the title and the bars learns "organic is biggest", which they probably knew already, and leaves without the decision. The six bars are all the same blue, so nothing points at the bars the text talks about. The direction: put the finding in the title (for example, "Organic search brought a third of Q3 sign-ups"). Then use one accent for the bar or bars that matter, with the rest in a muted tone, and add a short note on the chart beside Organic search and Podcast ads/Events. This follows the same logic as Few's advice to keep a second colour for the one point the reader must notice. *Taste. No approved source states the takeaway-title rule, but on a page whose job is to report a change, the change should be what you see first.*

2. **The main claim compares against data the page doesn't show.** "Up from a quarter in Q2" is the headline fact, but Q2 appears nowhere, so the viewer has to take the growth on trust. The chart can't show a change at all, only a single snapshot. The direction: show Q2 next to Q3, either as a pale paired bar or tick for each channel, or at least as a Q2 marker on Organic search. Then the reader can see the shift on terms they already accept: last quarter's numbers. *Defect for a "what changed" page. The comparison is the content.*

**Smaller things.**
- **Y-axis and gridlines:** every bar already carries its exact value, so the axis and gridlines repeat information. They're already pale. You could drop them for a cleaner chart, or keep them faintly for scale (Tufte's data-ink point, Wilke ch. 23.2). This is optional.
- **"The events budget moves to referrals in Q4":** podcast ads did nearly as badly as events (410 sign-ups) and are named in the same sentence. Readers will ask why only events is cut, so say whether podcast ads stay and why.
- **"What changed this quarter" heading:** this label could sit on any report. If the title takes the finding, this heading can name the decision instead ("Q4: events budget moves to referrals").
- **Empty space:** the bottom third of the page and the area under the sidebar are blank. That's fine for a single-chart view, but it's where a Q2 comparison or a short table could go without crowding anything.

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honest and easy to read.** The bars start at zero, are sorted by value, and have direct value labels and plain category names. Keep all of that.
- **The subtitle and source line are exact.** "Accounts created in Q3, by the channel of the first visit" defines the measure, and the source line gives the dates and the total (5,400). The numbers check out: the bars add up to 5,400, organic is 34%, and podcast ads plus events make 670. This is the kind of precision that makes a report believable.
- **The type scale is tight.** There are about four clear levels (title, subtitle/body, labels, axis/source), with weight doing the work at the small sizes rather than extra sizes. There's nothing to fix here.
