**First read.** My eye goes straight to the headline. The single word "line", left alone on the second line, catches it and pulls it back down. It then drops into the italic standfirst and reads on easily. This is a long-form feature in a small independent magazine. It feels calm, clean and readable, and fairly anonymous.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the opening screen of a magazine feature, which has to pull a general reader into a long story about a community garden.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The copy is strong and the page stays out of its way. The biggest thing holding it back is that the headline breaks badly, and that's the line every reader sees first. Second, the type looks like any default template, so the page doesn't sound like the story.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline breaks into a one-word runt.** "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" leaves "line" on its own. That splits the phrase "bus line", which is the image the headline is built on, and the second line looks like overflow rather than part of the title. (Convention: break headings at natural phrase boundaries, Bringhurst. Defect at this size, because it's the first thing anyone reads.) The fix is to control the break so the phrase stays whole, for example "The allotment at the end / of the bus line". Use a balanced-wrap setting or narrow the headline's measure slightly, and check it at every width this page will run at.

2. **The body measure looks too long.** The text column is about 720 px wide at roughly 18 px. Lines such as "It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to" run to about 85–90 characters. On a long read, the eye has further to travel back to the start of each line, and over thousands of words that tires the reader. (Convention: 45–75 characters, about 66 ideal, Bringhurst.) Either narrow the text column to about 600–640 px or raise the body size a step. Keep the headline at its current width if you want it to stay wide. Measure this in the live page.

3. **The page doesn't sound like the story (taste).** The words are specific and warm: bath tubs, the queue at the water tap, a letter that took four months to answer. The design is one neutral sans in three weights, with nothing that belongs to this publication or this piece. The cost is that the opening screen could belong to any blog. The byline promises "Photographs by the residents", but on this screen they're nowhere to be seen. If a photograph is meant to sit here, it's the obvious place for the page to show its character (see below). If it isn't, give the type the voice: a serif for the headline or body, or simply a more distinctive display face, would set the tone the copy already has.

**Smaller things**
- Standfirst vs body: they're the same size, and the gap before the first body paragraph looks no larger than the gaps between paragraphs, so italic alone marks the change. Four lines of italic also read more slowly than roman. Either set the standfirst a step larger in roman, or add more space after it so it reads as a separate unit (Dair: weak contrast is worse than none).
- "Subscribe" in the nav is set exactly like the section links, though it's the one action the business needs. Give it a different weight or style so it reads as the ask.
- Small sizes: the "Feature" kicker (~13 px bold green) and the byline (~14 px grey) sit about a point apart. They're told apart by colour and weight, which works, but they could share one size.

**What's working**
- **The copy.** The headline is concrete and makes you curious. The standfirst names a time, a place, forty households, and a reason to care ("the best place in the district to hear the news"), and ends with what the writer did. The first body paragraph opens on a person and a date. Keep all of it.
- **Restraint.** There's one accent colour (the green kicker), no boxes, no ornament, and generous space around the column. Nothing competes with the reading.
- **Clear order.** Kicker, headline, byline, standfirst, body: each level is distinct and in the right order, so the reader never has to look for where to start.
