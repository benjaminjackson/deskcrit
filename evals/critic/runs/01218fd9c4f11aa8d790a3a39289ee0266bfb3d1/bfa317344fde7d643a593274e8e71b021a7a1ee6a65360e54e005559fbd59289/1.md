**First read.** The eye goes to the big blue slice and its "38%", then to the grey "25%" at top left, the second-biggest thing on the page. To learn what that slice is, you have to go to the legend on the right, where it turns out to be "Other". It reads as a tidy, well-made annual-report page that feels calm and credible. It also feels like it says less than it should.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in an annual report that shows shareholders and staff how fiscal 2026 operating costs split up, so they can see where the money goes.

**Verdict.** It's well made, but it doesn't finish the job. A quarter of the costs are hidden in an unexplained "Other". The title names a topic but never states the finding. And no amount appears anywhere, so the reader learns proportions but not money.

**What would move it most**
1. **"Other" is the second-largest category and says nothing.** At 25% it's bigger than rent, hosting and marketing. On the pie it's the slice the eye reaches second, and in the legend it's tucked in last. A reader of an annual report will ask what a quarter of the spend was and get no answer. That looks evasive even if it isn't. **Defect.** Fix: break "Other" into its two or three largest parts until what's left is clearly the smallest slice. If you can't, add a one-line note saying what it contains. Keep it last in the order, but make it small enough to earn that place.
2. **The title states the topic, not the finding.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" tells the reader what the chart covers, then leaves them to work out what it means. Write the takeaway in words, e.g. "People were 38% of operating costs; rent and hosting together another 28%", or whatever point the report wants this page to make. Then the dek can carry the scope line it already has. **Taste,** argued from the fact that most annual-report readers skim titles and never decode the chart.
3. **The percentages have no base.** "38%" of how much? The subtitle and source line never give total operating costs, and the legend lists no amounts. In a financial report, the amount is what readers remember and compare with last year. Shares alone can't show whether salaries went up or other costs fell. **Defect** for this context. Fix: put the total in the dek ("…of $X.Xm total") and add an amount column to the legend next to the percentages. A year-on-year column would make the page actually useful.
4. **The legend is set apart from the slices.** The slices carry only numbers and the names live in a separate list, so every slice means a trip right and back. This is "Label directly instead of using a legend" (Wilke). With five slices you can label them directly ("Salaries and benefits 38%") and turn the right-hand list into the amounts table from point 3. **Convention,** and breaking it costs the reader something here.

**Smaller things.**
- The white "12%" on orange and "16%" on teal look below 4.5:1. White on that orange looks closer to 3:1. They're bold but not large enough to count as large text under WCAG 1.4.3. Measure both, then use dark labels on those two slices or darken the fills.
- The legend sits high on the right with a large empty block below it. The bottom-right quadrant is dead space rather than planned white space. Centre the table on the pie vertically or let it fill the column.
- The pie's left edge doesn't align with the left margin set by the title and source line. Hang it on the same edge.

**What's working.**
- The type is restrained: serif headline, sans data, small caps section kicker, and only about four sizes with clear jobs. Keep it.
- There's no chartjunk: a flat pie, thin white separators, and rules in the legend that barely show. Five slices is within what a pie can carry.
- The source line states the rounding, which heads off "it doesn't add to 100" questions. Keep that honesty when you add amounts.
