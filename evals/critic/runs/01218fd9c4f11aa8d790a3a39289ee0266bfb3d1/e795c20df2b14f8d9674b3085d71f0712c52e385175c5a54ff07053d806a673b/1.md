**First read.** My eye goes first to the dark blue block in the middle of the heatmap, then up to the title, then across to the three big numbers on the right. Within about two seconds it reads as a support-team report: tickets arrive on weekdays between roughly 9 and 5, and weekends are quiet. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, like a good internal report.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a report screen in a help-desk product. It shows support leads when tickets arrive so they can plan staffing, and pairs that with a few headline numbers for the month.

**Verdict.** The chart works and the page is close to good. The biggest problem is that the headline "Busiest hour" figure doesn't match the chart. That breaks trust in the whole report, and the hour axis is labelled too sparsely for a reader to notice the mismatch or check it.

**What would move it most**

1. **The busiest-hour figure doesn't match the heatmap.** *Defect.* The right column says "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00". The hour labels are centred on their cells: 09:00 sits over the 10th column and 12:00 over the 13th. That puts the darkest cells, Mon and Fri at 30+, in the 11:00 column. The 11:00 column is as dark as or darker than 10:00 on every weekday, since 10:00 is 25–29 all week. A lead who staffs up for 10:00 because of the big number will be an hour early for the actual peak. A careful reader who spots the mismatch will stop trusting both the chart and the numbers. There are two possible causes. One is that the figure or the chart is simply wrong. The other is an unstated convention, for example the figure means the hour ending at 11:00, or the cells are drawn shifted by an hour. Either way, fix the data, then make the convention explicit. Use one definition of "hour" in both places, and say "10:00–10:59" if that is what a cell means.

2. **The hour axis is too sparse to read a cell's hour.** *Convention (Tufte, Few: label axes so the reader never has to guess).* There are 24 columns but only 8 labels, one every 3 hours. To find the hour of any dark cell, the reader has to count columns from a label, and that is exactly how the mismatch in point 1 hides. For a staffing decision, the hour is the whole point. Label every column, or every other one with short labels like "9", "10", "11". Also mark the peak directly on the chart, for example an outline on the busiest cell with its hour, so the chart and the number visibly agree.

3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** *Taste (the matching checklist line draws on a source outside the approved list).* "When new tickets arrive" tells the reader what the chart is about and leaves them to work out the conclusion. The finding is strong and simple: almost all tickets come on weekdays between about 08:00 and 17:00, peaking late morning, and weekends see a fraction of that. A title like "Most tickets arrive on weekdays between 9 and 5, peaking at 11:00" gives a lead the answer before they read a single cell. The current line can stay as the subtitle.

**Smaller things**
- **Different time periods:** the chart covers 1–28 September but the first number says "New tickets, September". A reader can't tell whether 3,412 includes the 29th and 30th. Use the same period for both, or label each one.
- **"Down 6 min on August"** doesn't say whether that's good. For first-reply time, down is better, so say "6 min faster than August".
- **The colour ramp blurs at both ends:** the 0–4 and 5–9 steps are hard to tell apart from each other and from the grid, as are 10–14, 15–19 and 20–24 in the afternoon (e.g. Tue 13:00–15:00). Either widen the lightness steps or merge bins. Five steps would read more clearly than seven.
- **Too many type sizes too close together:** I count about six sizes. The axis labels, source line, stat labels and subtitle are all small and only a point or two apart. The big numbers (~26 px bold) are nearly the title's size and weight, so they compete with it. Use one small size for axis, legend and source text. Make the big numbers clearly smaller than the title, or the title clearly bigger.
- **"New tickets per hour" appears twice,** in the subtitle and again as the legend label. Shorten the legend label to "Tickets", or drop it.

**What's working**
- **The single blue ramp on a neutral grid with white gutters.** The weekday pattern reads at a glance and nothing competes with the data. Keep the restraint.
- **The subtitle and source line define the data well:** per hour, all queues, an average week, the date range, the team's local time, and "opened, not answered". That last note heads off the most likely misreading. Keep it.
- **Every big number has a comparison to August,** so none is shown bare. Just make the direction clear (see "Down 6 min" above).
