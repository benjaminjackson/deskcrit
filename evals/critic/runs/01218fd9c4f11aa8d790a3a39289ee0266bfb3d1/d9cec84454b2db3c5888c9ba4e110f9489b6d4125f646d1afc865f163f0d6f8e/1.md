**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading, then settles into three even paragraphs of comfortable grey-black text. It reads as a plain informational page for people taking a local river ferry: calm, orderly, a little bookish. Nothing stands out once you're past the heading, and every paragraph carries the same weight.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about it, so I'm assuming this is a web or printed notice that tells ferry passengers when boats run, how long the crossing takes, and what to do with a bicycle. The test is whether someone can quickly find when they can catch a boat.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is good, but the piece doesn't do its job. The facts a passenger acts on, such as times, the last boat and when winter starts, are vague, and they're buried in running prose. A reader looking for "when's the next boat, and when's the last one?" can't find an answer, because the page never gives one.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times can't be acted on (defect).** In paragraph 1, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last departure, and it doesn't say which landing the forty minutes counts from. In paragraph 3, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" makes a reader guess what dusk means on a given day. "In winter" gives no dates. "One boat each hour" doesn't say on the hour or at what minute. These are the lines people plan around and later hold the operator to. Someone who reads "before dusk" as 5 pm in November and arrives at 4:50 can be stranded if the real last boat was 4:15. Fix: give clock times and dates. For example: "First boat 6:20 from the north landing, then every 40 minutes until 21:40. 1 November to 28 February: hourly on the hour, last boat from the quay at 16:00." Then point to the noticeboard for changes, as the page already does.

2. **Prose hides what people skim for (convention, from the basics checklist: text people skim is broken up by headings, short paragraphs and lists).** The page has three things a passenger looks up: the timetable, the bicycle rules and the winter changes. All three are set as equal-weight paragraphs under one generic heading. Someone who only wants the bike rule has to read the opening of each paragraph to find it. Fix: give each block a short subhead ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter"). Pull the departure times out into a small list or table so they're the second thing the eye lands on after the title. Krug's "don't make me think" applies directly here.

3. **The heading doesn't tell the reader which ferry (taste, but it matters).** "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category. "North landing ⇄ Market quay ferry" would tell a stranger right away that they're on the right page, and it would give the most important fact the largest type.

**Smaller things.**
- Numbers are spelled out ("forty", "twelve"). In information people scan, numerals are faster to find and compare. Use 40 and 12.
- The text column sits on the left of a wide frame, leaving roughly the right 40% and the bottom third empty. That's fine for a reading column, but at this width it looks unfinished rather than deliberate. Either centre the column in a defined page width or use the space for the timetable block.
- "Bicycles ride on the lower deck at no extra charge" assumes the reader knows the base fare. No fare appears anywhere, and passengers will want it.

**What's working.**
- The measure and leading are right. Lines run about 60–70 characters with generous line spacing, so the text is easy to read. Keep this when you add subheads (Bringhurst's 45–75 range).
- The type scale is clean: two sizes, a bold heading and regular body, with an obvious step between them. Add any subheads by weight at body size or close to it, not as a third size a few points larger.
- The tone is clear and human. "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" says exactly who wants what. Keep that voice when you turn the vague times into exact ones.
