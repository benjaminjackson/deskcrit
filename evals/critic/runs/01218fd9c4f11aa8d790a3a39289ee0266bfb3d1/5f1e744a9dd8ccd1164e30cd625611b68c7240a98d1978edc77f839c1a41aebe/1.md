**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Help for ferry passengers", then splits between the two bold subheads, which carry the same weight. It reads in seconds as a plain help page from a small ferry operator, written for anyone travelling. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. The bottom third and the space under the right column are empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or printed help notice. It tells passengers how to travel smoothly and what to do when something goes wrong, and it is read before the trip or at the landing.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound and the page reads easily. What lets it down is the words at the two points where a passenger has to act: the last instruction sends people to an address the page doesn't give, and "If something goes wrong" only offers a written complaint. The layout is fine. The copy needs the work.

**What would move it most**

1. **The last step points somewhere else for the one fact it needs (defect).** Step 3 on the right says "send it to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter". Anyone reading this at home or on the boat can't finish the task. They have to go back to the shelter to find out where to send their note, so many won't send it. The page is the obvious place for that address, so print it here, and add an email if one exists. The same applies to "the quay office": say which landing it's at.

2. **"If something goes wrong" promises more help than it gives (defect).** The heading reads like help for someone in trouble right now: missed the last boat, lost a bag, hurt on the ramp. The three steps that follow are a process for writing a complaint later. A passenger in a real problem gets nothing to do in the moment and may feel brushed off. There are two fixes. Retitle the column for what it is, such as "To report a problem after your crossing". Or add a first line for urgent cases ("Tell a crew member straight away, or call …") before the written report.

3. **The rule that costs most if it's missed is the vaguest (defect).** "The last boat leaves before dusk" is about time, and passengers will act on it and hold you to it. "Before dusk" can be read two ways. It could mean a fixed winter time, or it could change from day to day. Either way the passenger can't plan around it. "Winter sailings run once an hour" also leaves summer unstated. Give the actual last-sailing time, or say plainly that it changes daily and is on the timetable board. This is also the one item on the page that deserves emphasis: set the time in bold and leave the rest at body weight.

**Smaller things**
- Both columns end high and leave about a third of the page empty at the bottom. The space looks left over rather than placed. Either size the page to its content or let the extra space gather in one deliberate place.
- The two subheads compete equally. That's fine for a reference page, but if the report process is the main reason people come here, put it first or on the left.
- Step 2 says "the number printed on the back of your ticket". If the ticket has more than one number, name it ("the ticket number", "the six-digit code").

**What's working**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (title, subhead, body) with clear steps between them and no near-duplicate sizes, so each level has an obvious job.
- The lists are the right kind. Bullets are used for independent tips and numbers for a sequence. Each item starts with a verb ("Check", "Bring", "Wheel", "Write"), so the page skims as a set of actions. The markers hang outside the text so the left edge stays clean, and the line length looks to be about 50–60 characters with comfortable spacing between lines. Keep all of this.
- Each tip gives its reason ("so that the gangway is kept clear"). That makes the rules feel fair rather than bossy, and it suits a public service.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I can't tell whether this is a web page or a printed notice. If it's posted at the landing, check that the body text can be read from a step or two back.
