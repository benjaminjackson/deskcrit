**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline and stops there, because the four lines run into each other. Descenders from one line sit on top of the ascenders of the next: the "y"s of "Quayside ferry" hit "crossings", and "g"/"y" in line 3 cut into "summer". For a second it looks like a rendering glitch, not a heading. Once you work it out, it's a calm, plain spring timetable page for a river ferry, but it doesn't feel finished. It feels broken.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the top of a timetable page that tells local riders and visitors when the ferry runs and how long a crossing takes.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The headline's line spacing is so tight that the lines overlap, so the most important text on the page is the hardest to read. Behind that, a page called "timetable" gives no times.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline lines collide.** *Defect.* In all four lines of "Quayside ferry crossings by daylight, spring through early summer", the line spacing looks well below the type size. Descenders cut into the letters of the line below: "y" in "Quayside" and "ferry" runs into "crossings", "g" and "y" in "through early" run into "summer", and the line-3 descenders cross line 4. You have to piece the words together, and the page looks broken, which costs trust in a timetable, where people need to believe the information. Bringhurst (*Elements*, p. 37) allows a title to be set with negative leading only "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide", and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 28) faults a heading for exactly this. Fix: open the heading's line spacing until no letter of one line touches a letter of the next. It can still be tighter than the body text, just not overlapping. Shortening the headline helps too (see 3), because fewer lines means fewer collisions.
2. **The page is called a timetable but has no times in it.** *Defect.* The eyebrow says "Spring timetable" and the headline promises crossings "by daylight", but the only schedule information is "every forty minutes". The rider can't tell when the first and last crossings are, what "daylight" means in hours, which dates "spring through early summer" covers, or whether the forty minutes applies in both directions. Those are the facts they'll act on and hold you to, so leaving them out is the real gap. Fix: put first crossing, last crossing, and the season's start and end dates right under the paragraph, in a form people can scan, if this is the whole page. If a table follows below this screen, at least put the first and last times in this section.
3. **The headline does the body copy's work.** *Taste.* At eleven words and four lines, the headline is trying to carry the route, the hours and the season. That's why it's so big and dense. A short title ("Quayside ferry") with "daylight crossings, spring to early summer" moved into the body copy or the dates line would give you one strong focal point and fewer lines to space.

**Smaller things.**
- "From the north landing to the market quay every forty minutes": say whether the return runs on the same interval, and from what time ("every forty minutes from 7:20").
- The "Spring timetable" eyebrow is small, bold grey and looks faint. Check it reaches 4.5:1 against the white.
- "In calm weather" leaves riders wondering what happens when it isn't calm. Add a line on what happens to crossings in bad weather, or where to check.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (eyebrow, body, headline) with big jumps between them, all in one family. Keep that.
- The body paragraph is well set. The line length looks about right for reading (roughly 65 to 70 characters), the spacing is comfortable, and "about twelve minutes in calm weather" is concrete and honest.
- The left alignment is strong and uncluttered, and the whitespace lets the page breathe. That suits a quiet public-service page.
