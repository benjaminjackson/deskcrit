**First read.** The eye lands on the big navy wedge and its "38%", then drops to the headline, then crosses to the legend on the right, because nothing else on the left tells you what anything is. It reads as a clean, calm annual-report page about costs. Then the upper-left fan of thin slivers with tiny "3% 3% 3% 2% 2%" labels turns it into a colour-matching job.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page in a printed or PDF annual report that shows shareholders and staff where the company's operating money went, and what dominates it.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and honest, but it uses the wrong chart. A 14-slice pie makes the reader do the work: match colours to a legend and compare thin wedges by angle. And it never says what the reader should take away. It needs to be rebuilt as a sorted bar chart or a grouped pie, not polished.

**What would move it most**

1. **Fourteen slices is far too many for a pie (convention, and a defect here).** Nine of the slices are 4% or smaller and sit packed into the upper-left quarter. Each is labelled with only a number, so to learn what "3%" is you have to find its colour in the legend. Software licences, Utilities and Salaries are all blues. Equipment repair and Rent are both teals. Four wedges show 3% and four show 2%, and nothing but colour tells them apart. Few's "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1) both limit pies to about five slices. Fix: either keep the four largest slices (salaries, rent, cloud, marketing) and group everything under 5% into one "Other, 21%" slice, or drop the pie and use a horizontal bar chart sorted by value. Your legend is already that list. Put a bar on each row and the legend becomes the chart.

2. **The chart sits apart from its labels (convention).** Category names live only in the legend, about 300 px to the right of the pie. Every reading takes a trip back and forth by colour, and that fails for anyone with a colour-vision deficiency. Travel (red) sits right next to Insurance (green), the classic red–green pair. Tufte and Wilke both say to label the data directly. The bar-chart fix above solves this for free. If you keep a pie, put names on the slices that remain.

3. **The headline names the topic, not the finding (taste, but it matters on a report page).** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" tells the reader what the chart is, and they still have to work out the point themselves. The obvious point is right there: people cost more than a third, and the top four lines make up three-quarters of the spend. Say that in the headline or the deck, e.g. "Salaries took 38% of operating costs, and four categories took three-quarters". If it's true, also say how this compares with last year. In an annual report that comparison is what readers act on, and one year's shares alone can't give it.

**Smaller things.**
- No absolute figure appears anywhere. A reader can't tell whether 38% is £2m or £20m. Add total operating costs to the subtitle or the source line, and ideally an amount column in the legend.
- The slice percentages for the small wedges look about 10 px and mid-grey on cream, so check their contrast against 4.5:1. They become unnecessary once the slivers are grouped.
- The pie and the legend are both pulled to their edges with a dead gap between them. Once the chart is redrawn, close that gap so the labels and data read as one unit.
- The "4.2 COSTS" eyebrow in brown tracked caps is the only warm accent on the page and pulls a little attention from the headline. It's fine if it's a report-wide system; otherwise mute it.

**What's working.**
- The legend is sorted by value, uses tabular-aligned bold percentages and thin row rules. It's essentially a finished bar-chart table, so keep its structure.
- The type system is right for a report: serif headline and deck, quiet sans for data, three clear size levels plus one for captions. There are no near-miss sizes.
- The source line states where the numbers come from and that they're rounded, and the shares add up to 100%. That's honest, so keep it.
