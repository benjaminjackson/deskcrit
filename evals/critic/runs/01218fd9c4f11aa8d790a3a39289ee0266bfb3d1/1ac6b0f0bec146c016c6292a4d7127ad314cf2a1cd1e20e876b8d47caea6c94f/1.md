**First read.** My eye goes straight to the headline. It's huge, near-black and tightly set, so it couldn't go anywhere else. Then it drops to the grey subhead, lands on the blue button, and stops. Below and to the right there is only white. In about three seconds it reads as a team-planning SaaS landing page that is calm, competent and generic. I know what it claims, but I've seen nothing of the product.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the hero of a marketing site. Its job is to get team leads or engineering managers who juggle tickets, meetings and time off to start a trial.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy is clean, but the page doesn't make its case. It promises "one honest picture" and never shows that picture, so the viewer is asked to start a trial on trust, with nothing they can check against their own week. Showing the product is the fix that would change the most.

**What would move it most**

1. **The promise has no proof (defect).** The subhead promises a specific artefact: tickets, meetings and leave combined into one view of five days. Nothing on the page shows it. The right half beside the copy and the whole bottom third (roughly y 540–800) are empty. A team lead can't tell whether this view is better than the Jira board plus calendar they already have, so "Start a free trial" means signing up just to find out what the product is. Put a real five-day view in that space. Its most convincing form is one where the viewer can see their own situation, for example a person whose Wednesday is half meetings and whose Friday is leave, with the tickets that won't fit flagged. That turns "honest picture" from a claim into something the viewer can judge for themselves.

2. **The empty space is leftover, not shaped (convention / taste).** The headline runs to about x 1030, but the subhead stops at about x 720 and the button at about x 290. Below the button the page simply ends. Ruder's point is that white space works when it is gathered and set against something dense. Here nothing is dense except the headline, so the space reads as "unfinished", not "confident". Fixing #1 fixes this too. If there truly is no visual, end the hero right after the button so the next section's content shows above the fold.

3. **The ask is generic and only invites the viewer to rate the product (defect, copy).** "Start a free trial" could sit on any SaaS page. It doesn't say what the viewer will get or how quickly, and it doesn't set Weekplan against anything they already trust. Point it at their own material, e.g. "See next week from your Jira and calendar". Add a line under the button about what the trial costs them, such as length, whether a card is needed, and setup time. Then the viewer can weigh it against their current setup, not just trust the claim.

4. **The copy leaves gaps a stranger has to fill (defect, copy).** In "Plan the week your team actually works", "actually" is doing all the work. It implies the current plan is fiction but never says so to the viewer. "Leave" next to "meetings" is ambiguous for a moment (time off, or leaving?). "Honest picture" is a category label, not an outcome. Make the subhead end on what it means for the viewer, e.g. "…so you can see who's actually free before you commit to the sprint." That says who does what, and why it matters to them.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav "Product" is a category, not a destination. "How it works" or "Tour" tells the viewer what they'll get.
- The text-only "Weekplan" wordmark is the same face as the nav, just bolder. It has no character of its own, which makes the templated feel worse. That's taste, but it's cheap to fix.
- Grey subhead: the contrast looks fine (well above 4.5:1), but it's lighter than it needs to be for the one sentence that explains the product. Darken it a step.

**What's working.**
- **Headline setting.** The size contrast is decisive, the line spacing is tight so the two lines read as one unit, and the letter spacing is pulled in properly for display size. The break after "team" falls at a natural phrase boundary. Keep all of it.
- **One action.** There's a single filled button and no competing secondary button, so the eye path from headline to subhead to button is unambiguous.
- **Type scale.** I count about four sizes (headline, subhead, nav/button, wordmark) with clear steps between them. Weight, not extra sizes, separates the button and wordmark. It's disciplined.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the blank right side and bottom band are the design or a hero image or scroll animation that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check the live page. If a visual does load there, findings 1 and 2 shrink to "make sure it shows the real five-day view, not decoration".
