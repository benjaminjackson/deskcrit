**First read.** Your eye goes straight to the big serif quote and reads all three lines. It then drops to the bold attribution and only reaches the grey paragraph last, if at all. It reads as a warm, quiet newsletter lead: calm, literary and well made. After the headline you still don't know what happened. "The 'old' reading room" suggests a story about a rename that the page never tells.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the lead item of a library newsletter, and its job is to tell members the reading room reopens and get them to come.

**Verdict.** It's handsome but it gets the order wrong. The news (reopening Monday, new lamps and shelves, a puzzle table) sits in the smallest, faintest text on the page. The largest, strongest elements carry a quote and an attribution that mean little to someone outside the story.

**What would move it most**

1. **The news is at the bottom of the hierarchy** (defect). The page has three levels: a huge quote, a bold black attribution, and a small grey paragraph. The only line a reader can act on (reopens Monday, what's new) is in the grey paragraph. Someone skimming the newsletter takes away a nice quote and misses that the room is open again. The quote can stay large. The reopening needs to be the second thing the eye hits, so swap the weights: the news line gets the bold, darker setting and the attribution gets the quiet grey one.

2. **The headline only makes sense if you already know the story** (defect, copy). The scare quotes around 'old' and "nobody calls it anything else" point to a renaming or a replacement that a stranger doesn't know about. Nothing on the page explains it, so the reader has to work out why this matters and may guess wrong, for example that something is being taken away. Either add the missing fact near the quote (what is it officially called now, or what's "new" that makes it "old"), or keep the quote and make the line underneath say what it means for the reader: the room you've always used is back.

3. **"Monday" has no date** (defect, copy). This is the one line a reader acts on, and the day is the part they'll hold you to. A newsletter is read days or weeks after it goes out, so "Monday" can mean this Monday or next, or one that has already passed. Give the date ("Monday 1 December") and, if it's true, the opening hours.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count four sizes: the caps kicker, the display quote, the attribution (looks about 20 px) and the body (looks about 18 px). The attribution and body are only a couple of pixels apart, so they look like a mistake rather than two levels. Set them at the same size and separate them by weight and colour only (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).
- Quote mark: the opening quote mark isn't hung. At this display size, "It's" starts visibly to the right of "room" and "anything", so the left edge wobbles right where reading starts. Pull the mark into the margin so the letters line up (Bringhurst §4.4.2 supports hanging only for lists, not quotes, so for quotes this is common sense: at this size you can see the offset).
- Attribution: "a volunteer" sounds generic next to a detail as specific as thirty years of stamping books. Naming the person would make it read as real reporting (taste).

**What's working.**
- The scale contrast. One very large serif block against small sans text gives the page a clear entry point and real calm. Keep it.
- Typographic craft is right: curly quotes and apostrophes, a true em dash, and leading tight enough that the three display lines hold together as one shape.
- The restrained palette (cream, ink, one rust accent on the kicker) feels like a library and not a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the display quote wraps at narrower widths. A different break could leave "else,”" alone on a line, so check this on mobile.
