**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then meets three wide grey blocks of text that look the same. It reads as a plain information page for a local river ferry. It feels calm and tidy, but it looks like an essay. Nothing on the page says "here's the time you need".

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells passengers how to use the ferry, meaning when it runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bicycle. The reader is someone checking quickly before they travel.

**Verdict.** It looks clean, but it doesn't do its job. A passenger comes for times, and the page gives them no actual times. The few facts it does give are buried in full-width paragraphs that are tiring to read.

**What would move it most**

1. **There are no actual times, and the vague ones can be read more than one way (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "one boat each hour" and "before dusk" all ask the reader to guess the first and last sailings. Forty minutes from when? When does "winter" start? Does "before dusk" mean 15:30 or 16:30 in December? A passenger who guesses wrong misses the last boat, and that is the one fact on this page they will hold the operator to. The page then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", so the reader has to be at the quay to plan the trip to the quay. Fix: give real times. That means the first and last departures for each season, the minutes past the hour for the 40-minute service, the months that count as winter, and a link to the current timetable and changes on the page itself, not only on the noticeboard.

2. **The facts people act on are buried in the paragraphs (defect).** Frequency, crossing time, bicycle rules, winter service and where to find changes are each half a sentence inside a paragraph, all in the same weight. To find "how often does it run in winter?" you have to read the whole third paragraph. With only one heading, the page gives the eye nothing to scan for. Fix: give each topic a short subhead (Times, Bicycles, Winter and changes) and put the numbers first, either in their own short lines or in a small table. Keep the prose for the rest, like the sandbank and keeping bags on the bike.

3. **The lines are far too long (convention: Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2).** The body text runs the full width of the content area, about 125–130 characters per line, which is nearly twice Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Readers lose their place going back to the start of each line, especially in the second and third paragraphs. The line spacing looks fine for a normal measure but too tight for this width (Bringhurst 2.2.1: "Longer measures need more lead"). Fix: limit the text column to about 65 characters. At this size that is about half the current width. Don't add line spacing to make up for the long lines.

**Smaller things**
- "When the tide turns against the boat" says the crossing takes "a little longer" but doesn't say how much. If a delay can affect connections, give a figure ("up to 20 minutes").
- "Bicycles ride… at no extra charge" suggests there is a fare, but the page never states one. If fares belong in these notes, add them. If not, say where to find them.
- Nothing tells a first-time reader which landing they are at or how to get to either one. If that matters for this audience, a one-line address or map link would cover it.

**What's working**
- **Two clear text levels.** A bold heading and regular body text, in one family, with an obvious size step. There is no scale muddle, so keep it that way when you add subheads by using bold at body size, not a new in-between size.
- **Contrast and paragraph spacing.** The dark text on white is easy to read, and paragraphs are separated by space, with no indents as well.
- **A plain, human tone.** Lines like "so the deck stays clear for people walking to the stairs" explain the reason behind each rule, so passengers are more likely to follow it. Keep that voice in the prose that's left.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- How the text wraps on a phone. Many passengers will read this at the landing, so check that the times and subheads come first on a narrow screen.
