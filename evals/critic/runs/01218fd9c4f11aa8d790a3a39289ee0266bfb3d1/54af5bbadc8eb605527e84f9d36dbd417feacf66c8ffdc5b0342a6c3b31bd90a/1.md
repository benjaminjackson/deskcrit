**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, then down into four even blocks of serif text. It reads as a calm, well-set notice page from a small local transport operator. It feels quiet and trustworthy, a bit like a parish newsletter. Nothing in the body stands out, so the reader has to read every word to find the one fact they came for.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that lets someone about to take the ferry find out when it runs, whether they can make the last boat and how bikes work, mostly on a quick scan, possibly at the landing on a phone.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is good. As a passenger notice it fails, because the facts a passenger acts on (times, last crossing, winter hours) are vague, scattered or ambiguous, and they're buried in prose of equal weight with nothing to scan by. This is mostly a content and structure problem, not a styling one.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts a passenger acts on are missing or can be read two ways** (defect). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last departure. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" doesn't say which quay (the market quay or the north landing?). "Before dusk" changes by about two hours across a winter, so nobody can plan around it. "In winter" has no dates. One reading of the last-boat line gets you home. The other leaves you on the wrong bank. The page then sends the reader to "printed timetables… in the shelter", which only helps someone already standing at the landing. Fix: give real times (first boat, last boat from each side, summer and winter) with the dates each season starts. Every one of these lines should say who leaves from where, and when.

2. **Prose where people want to scan** (convention: text that people skim is broken up by headings, short paragraphs and lists; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). There are four paragraphs of the same weight and no subheads. The timetable is split between paragraph 1 (summer frequency) and paragraph 3 (winter), with bicycles in between. A passenger looking for "can I still get back tonight?" has to read the whole thing. Fix: group by question with short subheads, for example *Times*, *Bicycles*, *At the landings*. Put both seasons' times together, ideally as a small table with the two landings as columns.

3. **The order follows the writer, not the reader** (taste). The closing paragraph about last spring's rebuild is background, but it gets the same space and weight as the deadline information. Lead with times. Keep the landing improvements, but show them as secondary: last, shorter, or folded into an "At the landings" note. The covered waiting area and wider ramps are useful for step-free access, so say that to the reader ("step-free ramps at both landings"), not as a history.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 2 ends on "crossing." alone on its own line. It's a runt, so rewrap or edit it.
- "Bicycles ride… at no extra charge" implies a fare that the page never states. Either state the fare or drop the implication.
- "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" is stated as a fact. Say it to the reader instead: "keep your bags on your bike".

**What's working.**
- The type is well matched: a bold sans heading over a readable serif body is clear contrast, not conflict. There are two sizes with one big step between them, so the scale is clean.
- The measure looks to be about 65–70 characters, the leading is generous, and paragraphs are marked by space only. It's comfortable to read, so keep these settings when you add structure.
- The plain, quiet tone suits a public notice. Keep it, and just make it specific.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where this page is most likely to be read at the landing.
