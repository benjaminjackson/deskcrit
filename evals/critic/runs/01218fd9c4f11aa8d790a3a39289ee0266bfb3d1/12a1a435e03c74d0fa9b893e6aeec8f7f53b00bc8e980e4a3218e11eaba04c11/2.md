**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers across the top: 3.8, 64.20, 12.4. It then drops to the orange bars and catches Saturday as the tallest and Sunday as the stub. This reads as the back-office overview for a small bakery that ships orders and sells subscriptions. It feels calm, tidy and competent. Then you try to read the numbers and stall: 3.8 what? 64.20 in which currency? 12.4 subscribers?

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing screen of an internal dashboard for the shop's owner or staff. Its job is to tell them at a glance how the business is doing and where to look next.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the chart is honest, but the three headline numbers, which are the whole point of an overview, can't be read for certain and can't be judged. None of them has a unit, a time period or anything to compare against. Until they do, the top of the screen shows figures without telling the owner anything.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headline numbers have no units, so at least one can be read two ways.** This is in all three cards in the top row. "3.8" is probably a percentage, but the screen never says so. "64.20" looks like money, but with no currency symbol a shop that sells in more than one currency, or a new hire, has to guess. "Active subscribers 12.4" can't be a head count. The reader has to supply "thousand" themselves, and someone glancing quickly could take it as 12 subscribers or 12,400. These are the figures the owner will quote to a partner or a lender, so an ambiguous one is a **defect**, not a style note. Fix: put the unit on the figure itself (3.8%, £64.20, 12.4k or 12,400), set in the same large size so it can't be overlooked.

2. **Each number stands alone, so the owner can't tell good from bad.** This is also the top row. There's no time period, no change from last period and no target. Is 3.8% up or down? Is 64.20 better than August? The cards answer "what is it" but not "should I care", and that second question is what an overview exists to answer. The chart's footnote says September 2026, but nothing ties the cards to that month, so the reader can't even assume the cards and the chart cover the same period. This breaks a published **convention** (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a bare headline number is a dashboard pitfall), and it costs the screen most of its value. Fix: put the period and a comparison next to each headline number, such as "Sep 2026" and "▲ 0.4 pts vs Aug" or a target, using a small size and a muted colour so the big figure stays dominant. If you colour the direction, also show an arrow or sign so the colour isn't the only cue.

3. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding.** This is "Orders by delivery day", in the lower panel. The pattern in the bars is real and useful to a bakery: orders climb through the week, peak on Friday and Saturday, and fall by about two-thirds on Sunday. That pattern affects baking schedules and staffing. As things stand, the owner has to work that out from the bars every time. This is **taste**, and here is why I'd argue for it: one line such as "Saturday is the busiest delivery day, and Sunday has a third of its orders" turns the chart from a picture into a decision. Keep the current title as a small label if you want to keep the topic too.

**Smaller things.**
- The y-axis has no label. "Orders" in the title covers it, but if this chart ever counts something else, label the axis "orders" or add direct values on top of the bars. Exact values like 1,130 vs 1,040 are what someone planning batches will want (Tufte, ch. 2, clear labelling).
- The chart says it covers September 2026, but that's only stated in the 11 px-looking source line at the bottom. Move the period up beside the title, where it's read first.
- The source line and axis labels look like small, light grey text. Check that they reach 4.5:1 contrast against white (WCAG 1.4.3).
- The lower third of the screen, below the chart card, is an empty grey band. Either it's room for the next section or the chart could take more height. As it stands it reads as leftover space.
- The orange bars are the same colour as the "Crumbwell Bakes" wordmark, so the brand colour is doing double duty as data colour. That's harmless with one series. If a second series or a highlighted bar is ever added, it will compete.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined. I count about four sizes: the large figures, the page title, the card and chart labels, and the small axis and source text. Each has a clear job, with big steps between them. Keep that when you add units and comparisons, and set the comparison in the existing small size, not a new one.
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the days run in their natural order rather than sorted by value, gridlines are faint, and there are no effects. The single colour suits a single series. Don't decorate it.
- The grid holds. The three cards and the chart panel share left and right edges and a single gap, so the page reads as one structured surface.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the navigation links and cards are clickable through to detail views, and how the cards behave at narrow widths.
