**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers (3.8, 64.20, 12.4), then drops to the orange bars and lands on the tall Saturday bar. Within a second it reads as a tidy small-business dashboard for a bakery's online shop. Then it stalls. I can't tell what any of the three big numbers means: 3.8 what, 64.20 in what currency, and 12.4 of what? The page looks calm and competent but says less than it seems to.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the overview screen a bakery owner opens to see at a glance how the shop is doing and where to act.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The cards are the strongest thing on the page, and none of them can be read on its own. Each number lacks a unit, a time period and a comparison, so the owner can't tell good from bad, or even what is being counted.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three headline numbers can't be read** (defect). In the top row, "3.8" has no %, "64.20" has no currency, and "12.4" for *Active subscribers* can't be a head count, so it must be thousands or a rate, and the viewer has to guess which. None of the cards says what period it covers (today, this month, all time?), and none has a target or an earlier value to compare against. The page gives these cards the largest, heaviest type, which tells the owner they matter most, and then they can't be acted on. Is 3.8% conversion up or down? Nobody can say. Few's "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (pitfall 2) names exactly this bare-number pattern. Fix: put the unit right on the number ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400" or "12.4k"), give the period on the label line ("September 2026", matching the chart), and add a comparison below each number, such as a change from last month with its direction ("▲ 0.4 pts vs August") or the target.
2. **The chart shows the pattern but doesn't point at the insight** (taste, building on Few's emphasis guidance). All seven bars are the same brand orange at the same weight. What an owner would act on is the Friday–Saturday peak, roughly 1,050–1,130 orders, against Sunday at about 380, which is plausibly a staffing or baking-schedule decision. Right now the viewer has to dig that out by reading bar heights against faint gridlines. Fix: grey the ordinary days and keep orange for the bars that carry the story, or put the value on top of each bar so exact counts don't need the axis. A one-line takeaway as the chart title would also help ("Saturday is your busiest delivery day; Sunday is a third of it").

**Smaller things.**
- The y-axis has no unit. "Orders" is implied by the title, but adding "orders" to the axis or the title ("Orders delivered, by day") removes the guess.
- The card labels ("Checkout conversion rate", regular weight) and the chart title ("Orders by delivery day", bold) do the same job in different styles. Pick one treatment for panel titles.
- The axis labels, source line and inactive nav items are small mid-grey text. They look borderline for 4.5:1 contrast, so measure the grey against white and against the #F5-ish page background.
- The active nav item is marked only by darker, bolder text. That's a weak cue next to the other three links; an underline or indicator bar would make "you are here" clearer.
- Roughly the bottom tenth of the screen is empty grey. That's fine if more panels follow; if not, the chart could take the height.

**What's working.**
- **The layout is clear:** a three-card KPI row over one full-width chart, on a consistent grid with even gutters. The owner knows where to look first and second. Keep that structure and fix only what the cards say.
- **The chart is honest and well made:** the bars start at zero, the gridlines are quiet, the days run in natural Monday-to-Sunday order, and the source line gives the system and the month. That source line is the model for the period the KPI cards are missing.
- **The colour is restrained:** one warm brand orange, used for the wordmark and the data, and everything else neutral. It feels like a bakery without decoration getting in the way.
