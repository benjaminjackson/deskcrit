**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, then drops to the orange bars, where Saturday stands out as the peak and Sunday as the dip. It reads as an admin dashboard for a small bakery's online shop. It feels calm, clean and competent. Then you try to read the numbers and they stop making sense.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the overview a bakery owner or manager checks to see how the shop is doing and to plan production and delivery staffing.

**Verdict.** The layout and chart are solid, but the three headline numbers can't be read. None of them has a unit, a time period or anything to compare against. So the most prominent part of the page tells the owner nothing they can act on.

**What would move it most**

1. **The KPI numbers have no units, so each one can be read more than one way (defect).** In the top row of three cards:
   - "3.8" could be a percentage, though a stranger can only assume it is.
   - "64.20" has no currency, so it could be pounds, dollars or euros.
   - "12.4" for *Active subscribers* is the worst. You can't have 12.4 subscribers, so the viewer has to guess whether it means 12.4k, 124, or a percentage.

   These are the figures an owner remembers and quotes to a partner or a bank. If one of them is misread, a real decision gets made on the wrong number. The fix is to print the unit with each number: "3.8%", "£64.20", "12,400" or "12.4k". Give the unit a lighter weight than the number so the number still dominates.

2. **The numbers have no time period and nothing to compare against (defect).** The chart's source line says September 2026, but the cards don't say which period they cover. It could be today, this month or all time. A number on its own also can't show whether things are going well. 3.8% means nothing to the owner until they know whether it was 3.1% or 4.5% last month. Add a period label to the cards, or one shared label above the row such as "September 2026". Under each number, add a small comparison line, for example "▲ 0.4 pts vs August", so the owner can judge each figure against their own history.

**Smaller things.**
- **Big empty band at the bottom:** about 80 px of grey sits below the chart card with nothing in it. If more content is meant to go there, this is fine. If not, the page looks cut short.
- **Nav active state:** "Overview" is marked only by being darker and bolder than the other links. That is readable at this size, but an underline or a pill would make it clear at a glance.
- **Chart has no number labels on the bars:** with only seven bars, putting the value at the top of each (for example "1,130" on Saturday) would remove the need to read across to the gridlines. It would also let you lighten or drop the y-axis. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honest and quiet.** The axis starts at zero, there is one colour for one series, the gridlines are faint, the day labels are written out, and there is a source line with a date. The Friday/Saturday peak and the Sunday drop come through instantly, which is exactly what someone planning production needs.
- **The type scale is clear.** I count five sizes, each with a distinct job: the KPI numbers, the page title, the card and chart titles, the small labels, and the source line.
- **Brand colour is used with restraint.** The burnt orange appears only on the logo and the bars, so it reads as "the data" and not as decoration.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards are clickable through to Orders or Subscribers, and what the tooltips or hover states on the bars show.
