**First read.** The eye goes to "Landing stage rebuild" at top left, then to the blue boat in the picture, then across to the blue "Sign up" button. It reads as a news item from a local ferry operator, with a sign-up box for timetable alerts beside it. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, but plain, close to a template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a ferry service page that tells passengers about the rebuilt landing and gets them to sign up for timetable-change alerts. The sign-up is the action that matters.

**Verdict.** The page reads well, but the form can't do its one job. It never asks for an email address or phone number, so you can't tell where "one short note" will go, and the service has nowhere to send it. Fix that first. Everything else is refinement.

**What would move it most**

1. **The form never asks how to reach you** (right card). *Defect.* The two fields are "Your name" and "Home landing", followed by "We send one short note when the timetable changes." A stranger reads that and asks: send it where? Either signing up does nothing, or the viewer has to guess that an email step comes later. People hesitate on a form that doesn't add up. Add the contact field (email, or a phone number for SMS) and say which it is in the helper line ("We email you once when…"). The name field doesn't help deliver the note, so consider cutting it. Fewer fields means more sign-ups (Nielsen 8, keep only what the task needs).

2. **"Home landing" is a blank text box** (right card, second field). *Defect / convention (Nielsen 5, error prevention; Norman, constraints).* The service knows its own landings. Passengers don't know the official names, and they will type "north", "North Landing", "the pier". The alerts then either fail to match or need cleaning up. Use a select or radio list of the real landings. That also shows the viewer what counts as a landing.

3. **The small text is set light and small** (field labels, helper line under the button, figure caption, footer). *Convention: weight and stroke thickness at small sizes; WCAG 1.4.3.* All of it looks like about 12–13 px in a light weight. Thin strokes at that size look grey even when the colour is dark. The field labels are what people read while filling in the form, so they are the costliest place for this. Use the regular weight for these labels and lines, and keep them at body size or one step below. Measure the contrast of the helper and caption text against white and check it reaches 4.5:1.

4. **The picture doesn't show what the copy claims** (illustration and the paragraph after it). *Taste.* The news is "the ramps now meet the deck at every tide, so prams and wheelchairs roll straight on." The figure shows blocks and a boat but no ramp. The caption names a tide state, but you can't see one. A placeholder graphic is fine for a fixture image. Whatever replaces it should show the ramp meeting the deck, because that is the claim a wheelchair user or a parent will act on.

**Smaller things**
- The boat in the illustration uses the exact blue and rounded shape of the "Sign up" button, so it reads as a second button and pulls the eye. Keep the accent blue for the action (accent colour restraint) and give the boat a neutral or muted tone.
- The headline "Landing stage rebuild" is a label with no verb and doesn't say which landing. Something like "The north landing has reopened with step-free ramps" tells passengers what changed for them.
- The button label "Sign up" names a category, not a result. "Send me timetable changes" says exactly what you get.
- The text runs about 30 px wider than the image (text column ends near x≈690, image at x≈656), so the left column has two right edges. Size the figure to the text measure.
- The helper line ("You can stop the notes at any time") sits under the button, after the decision. Move the reassurance above the button so it's read before clicking.

**What's working**
- The reading column has a comfortable measure (about 66 characters on the first line) and generous leading. Keep it.
- The form uses persistent labels above the fields, dark input borders that read clearly as fields, and one filled primary button with nothing competing. That is a correct form pattern.
- The article copy is concrete and human (benches face the water, tea and bread rolls, prams and wheelchairs). It tells passengers something real instead of generic "improvements". Keep that voice when rewriting the headline and button.
