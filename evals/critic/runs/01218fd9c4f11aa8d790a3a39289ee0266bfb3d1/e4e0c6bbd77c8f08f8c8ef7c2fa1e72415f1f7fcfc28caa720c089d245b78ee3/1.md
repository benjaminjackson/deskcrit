**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big serif headline, crosses the first line, then drops onto "valley" sitting alone. Next it reads the sans-serif paragraph, then reaches the bottom 40% of the screen, which is empty. It reads as the top of a local bakery's website: warm, calm and well set. It also feels unfinished, because it ends without a next step.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a small independent bakery's site. Its job is to get local people to come in, or order, and to give them a reason to pick this bakery over the supermarket.

**Verdict.** The typography is good, but the screen doesn't do its job yet. It tells you the bakery exists and then stops. There's no way to act, nothing to see, and the headline you see first is the line any bakery could use.

**What would move it most**

1. **There's no next step (defect).** The screen ends after "on Mill Street." There's no opening hours, address link, "order for collection" or even a scroll cue. A local visitor usually arrives with one question: when can I get a loaf, and where? Here they have to guess or scroll. The empty band from about y=520 to the bottom makes this worse, because it looks like the page has finished. The fix is to add one specific ask under the paragraph, such as "Open from 7am, 14 Mill Street" or "Order tomorrow's loaf by 6pm". Name the actual action and time rather than writing "Visit us". Use the empty space for it, so the paragraph leads into something.

2. **The headline could be on anyone's page (defect, through the copy).** "The finest bread in the valley" is a claim every bakery makes, and the viewer has to take it on trust. The specific, believable facts are all in the small paragraph: wood-fired, baked every morning, Mill Street, since 1962. So the line the eye lands on is the generic one, and the line that would persuade gets read second, if at all. Move the specifics up. Let the headline say what is different, for example a wood-fired oven, every morning, or what the visitor gets compared with a supermarket loaf they already buy. That gives them a comparison they can judge for themselves.

3. **"valley" sits alone on the second line (convention: line breaks in headings).** At this size the headline breaks as "The finest bread in the / valley". That leaves one word on its own line and a big empty area to its right, so the headline reads as one line plus a stray word. Break it at a phrase boundary ("The finest bread / in the valley"), narrow the measure so it breaks in a balanced way, or rewrite it under point 2 and check the break again. Check it at other viewport widths too, since this break comes from the width.

4. **Nothing on screen shows the bread (taste).** This is a food business whose whole appeal is sensory: a crust, a wood fire. Here it's all text on a cream background. Restraint can work, but this is too spare to make anyone hungry, and it adds to the templated feel. One strong photograph of the loaves or the oven, placed in the empty right side or lower area, would carry the mood the words only describe.

**Smaller things**
- Headline leading looks slightly open for two lines of display type. Tighten it a little so the lines hold together as one unit (Bringhurst).
- The eyebrow "HARTLEY'S BAKERY · SINCE 1962" is the only place the name appears, and it's in small capitals. If there's no logo or nav above this, the brand is easy to miss.

**What's working**
- **Type pairing and scale.** There are three clear levels with large steps between them: small tracked capitals, a large humanist serif and a readable sans for the paragraph. Each level has an obvious job, and the serif and sans clearly contrast rather than clash (Dair). Keep it.
- **The supporting paragraph.** "Sourdough, rye, and white tin loaves, baked every morning in a wood-fired oven on Mill Street" is concrete and believable. Its line length is about 65 characters, which is right. This is the best copy on the page, so give it more prominence.
- **Restrained palette.** Warm cream, near-black and one brown accent suit a long-established bakery. The brown eyebrow looks like it has enough contrast against the cream.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether a nav bar, logo or button sits above this crop or loads late. If one exists, point 1 is less severe, but the first screen still needs its own ask.
- How the headline breaks at other widths (see point 3).
