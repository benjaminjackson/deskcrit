**First read.** My eye goes to the bold headline, then down the first paragraph. The tinted box at the bottom catches it next. It reads as a short notice from a ferry operator, plain and calm. The first thing I noticed was that everything is jammed against the left edge of the frame, while the right third of the page sits empty. It looks like a page whose container lost its padding, not a decision.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web notice telling winter passengers when the ferry runs, what can delay it, and what they'll pay. They need to get the times and the rules at a glance.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and the copy is mostly clear. But the content sits flush on the left edge, and that makes the page look broken. The facts a passenger acts on (times, fares, the ice delay) are buried in prose or left vague. Fix the margin and pull the times out where people can see them.

**What would move it most**
1. **No left margin (defect).** The headline, both paragraphs and the edge of the tinted box all touch x=0. Letters like the "H" and "F" sit right on the frame. To the reader this looks like a layout bug, and it makes the whole page feel less trustworthy. Text needs a margin between it and the edge of the page. Give the content column a real margin. Since the right side is empty anyway, either centre the column or indent it by roughly the box's own padding or more.
2. **The times are hidden in a sentence (defect).** What a passenger needs most is the frequency, the first crossing and the last crossing. These sit mid-paragraph and are written out in words ("every forty minutes", "seven in the morning", "six in the evening"). Someone checking whether they can make the last boat has to read the whole line and turn the words into a clock time in their head. Put them in figures (07:00, 18:00, every 40 min). Give them their own visual weight: a short list, or bold numbers. The tinted box is the page's only emphasis device, and it would do more good on the timetable than on the fares.
3. **The lines people act on can be read two ways (defect, copy).** These are the lines readers will hold the operator to:
   - "Bicycles ride free on weekdays." Readers can't tell what bikes pay at weekends. Some will assume they pay something, others will assume they can't board.
   - "Cars pay the standard fare." The fare is never given, so a driver still has to look it up somewhere else.
   - "Vans over two metres high use the cargo deck." This leaves open whether vans pay more and whether they need to book.
   - "Check the board at the ticket hut." This only reaches people who are already at the landing, which is too late for someone deciding whether to make the trip. Tell readers where else they can check: this page, a phone number, a status line.
   
   For each line, add the missing number or consequence in the reader's own terms.
4. **The lines are too long (convention, Bringhurst).** The body lines run about 100 characters, well past the 45–75 range. Readers lose their place going back to the start of each line. Narrow the column to roughly 600–680 px at this type size. That also gives the empty right side a job, as margin.

**Smaller things.**
- Tinted box: there looks to be about twice as much padding below the text as above it, so it reads as unfinished. Make the padding even.
- Type scale: two sizes (headline and body) do the job here, so it's fine. If times get pulled out as in point 2, use weight to set them apart, not a third size.
- The headline promises "how it runs", but the box is about fares. If the box stays, a short label such as "Fares" would tell readers why it's set apart.

**What's working.**
- The headline names the subject and the season in plain words, so readers know immediately whether this page is for them.
- The ice paragraph gives the cause, the effect and an action in two sentences. Keep that pattern when rewriting the fare lines.
- The body text is a readable sans with good contrast and comfortable spacing between lines. Keep the type as it is.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the zero margin is how the page really looks or an artefact of the capture. Check in a live browser at a few widths.
