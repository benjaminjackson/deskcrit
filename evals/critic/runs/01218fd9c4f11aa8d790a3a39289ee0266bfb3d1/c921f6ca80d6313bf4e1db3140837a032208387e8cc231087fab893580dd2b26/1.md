**First read.** In both versions the eye goes straight to the big serif headline, then down to the standfirst about the last boat being for sale, then the byline. It reads as the opening of a long-read feature in an independent magazine. It's calm and confident, and the only colour is the small red "Long read" kicker. The two versions are identical except for the headline's line spacing.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the top of a long-form article page, meant to draw a reader into the story and set a quiet, literary tone.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. Version 2 tightens the headline's line spacing so far that the lines collide, and the biggest, most important element on the page loses its legibility and polish.

**What changed, and why it decides it**
1. **Headline leading in version 2 is too tight, so letters crash into each other (defect).** In version 2 the descenders of "people" and "keeping" (the p's and g's) run into the ascenders of "lighthouse" and "lit" on the next line. The descenders of "forgotten" and "lighthouse" (the g's) touch "Fair Skerry" below. The words "lighthouse" and "people" become tangled, and the reader has to sort the strokes apart on the one line that has to read at a glance. On a feature that sells itself on craft, it also looks like a rendering bug. Tight leading suits display type, but only up to the point where descenders and ascenders meet (Bringhurst on leading; Butterick, *Practical Typography*, on line spacing). Version 1 sets the headline at about 1.1× its size. That's still tight and still reads as one block, but every line is clear. Keep version 1's value. If you want a denser block, tighten in small steps and stop before the g's and p's reach the line below.

The other knock-on effect is small. Version 2 pulls the standfirst and byline up about 40 px, but nothing below gains from it. The page is still mostly empty underneath, so the saving buys nothing.

**Remaining findings for version 1**
1. **Nothing appears below the byline (couldn't judge).** The lower third of the frame is blank. It names a photographer, so a lead image almost certainly belongs there. It's either still loading or placed further down. If it really sits below the fold, the opening loses its strongest pull. See below.

**Smaller things.**
- The byline's grey sans looks light against white. Check that it meets 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3).
- The nav items are separated by middots with no visible "current page" or button treatment for Subscribe. If Subscribe is the business ask, it reads as just another section link.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: a big headline, a mid-size serif standfirst, then small sans for kicker, byline and nav. Each step has an obvious job.
- The standfirst is strong copy. "Now the last boat is for sale" gives the reader a reason to keep reading in nine words.
- The red kicker is the only colour on the page, so it labels the format without competing with the headline.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the lead photograph exists and loads in the blank lower area. Check it in a live browser.
