**First read.** My eye lands on the bold headline, top left. Then it goes straight into three paragraphs of grey-black sans set at one size. It reads as a short notice from a ferry operator, maybe a web page or a printed sheet. It feels calm, plain and tidy. It also feels like an essay. Nothing marks the times, the bike rule or the winter change, so you have to read every word to find the one fact you came for.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a notice on a web page or posted sign. It's for passengers who want to know when the ferry runs, whether they can bring a bike and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read, but it doesn't do its job. Someone planning a crossing can't get a departure time or a last boat out of it. That's partly because the facts are buried in prose, and mostly because the prose never states them.

**What would move it most**

1. **The times aren't there, and the one deadline can be read two ways (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first sailing, no last sailing and no departure minutes. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" makes it worse in three ways:
   - "The quay" could mean the market quay only, or both ends.
   - Dusk moves by an hour or more across a winter.
   - "Before" doesn't say how long before.

   A passenger who reads it as "around dusk from either side" and turns up at the north landing at 4:30 could be stranded. "Winter" doesn't say which months, so nobody can tell which timetable applies on a given day. Last-boat times are exactly what people hold an operator to, so this matters more than anything else on the page.

   The fix: give clock times. That means first and last departures from each landing, the departure minutes or a small timetable, and the winter dates with their own last sailing from each side, for example "last boat from market quay 16:20, Nov–Feb".

2. **The facts are written as a story when people come to look them up (convention).** People skim a notice like this for one answer: "when's the next boat?", "can I bring my bike?" Three paragraphs at the same size and weight give them nothing to jump to, so the bike rule sits in the middle of the page looking just like the timetable (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").

   The fix: split it into labelled chunks such as Timetable, Winter service and Bicycles. Put the times in a short table or list where they stand out, and keep the bike rule to one or two lines under its own label.

3. **The most urgent fact comes last and gets no emphasis (defect).** The winter cut-back and early last boat are the facts most likely to catch someone out, and they sit in the final paragraph at body weight.

   The fix: move the winter service up next to the main timetable, or set it apart (bold label, rule or tint) so a returning passenger sees it before planning a late trip.

**Smaller things.**
- "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded": the passive phrasing hides who gives the signal. Say it to the rider: "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on and lock it to the side rail."
- "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes in calm weather" leaves open what happens when it isn't calm (delays? cancellations?). Add one line on what to expect.
- Type scale: two sizes (headline and body). That's enough for this length. If you add labels, do it with weight, not a third size close to the body size.

**What's working.**
- The measure looks about 65–75 characters with comfortable leading, set flush left. It reads easily, so keep the column width when you restructure.
- The headline is clearly dominant on weight and size alone, with no ornament. That restraint suits a public notice.
- Dark text on white looks well above the contrast minimums.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this sits in a wider site layout (the empty right half may hold navigation or an image that didn't load), and how the column reflows on a phone. Many passengers will check it on a phone at the landing, so look at it at phone width in a live browser.
