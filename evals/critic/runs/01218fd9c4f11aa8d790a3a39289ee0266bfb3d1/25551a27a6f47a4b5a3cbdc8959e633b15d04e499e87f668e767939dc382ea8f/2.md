**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a short web info panel. It gives passengers the practical facts (frequency, crossing time, bikes, winter service) before they travel, and they should get those facts in one quick scan.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The structure is sound, but setting all the body text in capitals makes the very facts people came for slow and tiring to read. The copy also leaves out the times a passenger actually needs to plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **All three paragraphs are set in capitals** (defect, backed by convention). Every sentence from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING." is in caps, about eight lines in total. Capital letters all share the same rectangular outline, so the reader loses the word shapes they normally use to skim. They end up reading letter by letter, and nothing can stand out because everything is already "emphasised." It also reads as shouting, which fights the calm, helpful tone the heading sets. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says don't capitalise whole paragraphs and keep capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels and captions. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) notes that text in capitals is particularly hard to read. Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case. Keep capitals only for the "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which already uses them correctly.

2. **The service times are vague where passengers will act on them** (defect, in the copy). "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell anyone whether they can catch a 6:30 or a 22:00 boat. That is exactly what someone reading "Before you board" needs to know, and a wrong guess means a missed last boat. "In winter" has the same problem: from which date to which date? And "one boat each hour" doesn't say whether winter boats run on the hour or at some other time. Send people to the timetable in the shelter, but give the real first and last sailings and the winter dates here. A passenger shouldn't have to walk to the landing to learn whether the trip is worth making.

3. **The key facts are buried in running prose** (taste). Every 40 minutes, about 12 minutes per crossing, bikes free, hourly in winter: these are four numbers a passenger scans for. Right now they sit mid-sentence and are written out as words ("FORTY MINUTES", "TWELVE MINUTES"). Using numerals and pulling them forward helps: start each paragraph with its fact, or put a bold lead-in on each ("Every 40 minutes.", "Bikes ride free.", "Winter: hourly."). Then the eye can pick them out without reading every word. Once the caps are gone, bold is enough to do this, and you won't need another size.

**Smaller things.**
- "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" is a rule people need to follow, but it's written as a description. Say it to the reader: "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on and lock it to the rail."
- "PASSENGER NOTES" is small, grey and letterspaced. Check its contrast against white: it looks close to the 4.5:1 minimum for small text (WCAG 1.4.3).

**What's working.**
- The heading area: a small, spaced-out grey label over one bold heading is clear and confident. "Before you board" says exactly when this text is useful. Keep both.
- The type scale is tight: three sizes (label, heading, body) with clear jumps between them, so every size has an obvious job.
- The measure and paragraph spacing are good. Lines run about 65 to 70 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range, and there's a clear gap between paragraphs. Once the text is in lower case it should read comfortably as it stands.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I can't tell whether the capitals are typed in or applied with a CSS text transform. That affects screen readers and copy-paste. If it's a transform, removing it fixes everything above with one change.
