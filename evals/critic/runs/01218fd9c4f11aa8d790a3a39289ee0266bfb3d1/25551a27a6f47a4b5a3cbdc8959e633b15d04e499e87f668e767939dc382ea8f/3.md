**First read.** The eye goes to "Before you board", then hits three grey-black slabs of capital letters and slows right down. You read it word by word instead of sweeping across it. It reads as a short help or info page for passengers on a river ferry. The header feels calm and well made, but the body feels like it's shouting a notice at you.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a web page that gives a first-time ferry passenger the practical facts (route, frequency, bikes, winter timetable) before their trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The whole body is set in capitals, so the one part people came to read is the hardest part to read. After that, the copy is vague about the service times a passenger needs to plan around.

**What would move it most**

1. **The body text is all capitals** (all three paragraphs, 2–3 lines each). Capitals have no ascenders or descenders, so every word becomes the same rectangle. The reader loses the word shapes they skim by and has to spell their way through about 90 words. It also reads as raised-voice warning signage, which clashes with the calm sentence-case heading above it. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says caps suit headings shorter than one line and that you shouldn't capitalise whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) says text in capitals is especially hard to read. Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for the short "PASSENGER NOTES" label, where they already work. *Defect.*

2. **The copy is vague about the times people act on.** "Between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last sailing. A passenger can't tell whether a 6:30 or a 22:00 boat exists, and the last boat home is exactly what they'll hold you to. "In winter" has no months, so someone travelling in March or November can't tell which timetable applies. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say from when, so they can't work out a departure. And "printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends the reader to the dock to find out what this page should tell them. Fix: give the first and last sailing times, the winter date range, and either the departure times or a link to the timetable. "Twelve minutes in calm weather" is good, because it's concrete and says when it might not hold. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- The bike paragraph states rules ("riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded") without saying what that means for the reader. If you put "you" back in ("wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on and lock it to the side rail"), it reads as instructions to follow.
- For a page called "Before you board", it leaves out the first question most passengers have: fares and where to buy a ticket. If this page doesn't cover that, add a line or a link.
- The column ends about halfway down the frame, leaving a large empty area below and to the right. That's fine for a single article, but it's worth checking that this is the whole page and not a capture cut short.

**What's working.**
- The header has a clear, tight scale: a small, letterspaced grey label, then a heavy sentence-case heading, then body text. That's three distinct levels with real steps between them. Keep it exactly as it is when you fix the body.
- The text column is a good reading width (roughly 65–70 characters a line), with even paragraph spacing. The page is calm and uncluttered, and once the body is in sentence case that will carry it.
- "Before you board" speaks to the reader directly and tells them when the page is useful.
