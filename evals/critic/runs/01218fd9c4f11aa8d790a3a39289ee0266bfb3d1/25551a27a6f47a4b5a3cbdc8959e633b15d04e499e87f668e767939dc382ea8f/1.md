**First read.** The eye goes to "Before you board", which is the right place to land. Then it hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals and slows to a crawl. The text reads as a block to get through rather than notes to skim. It's a plain, quiet page for ferry passengers, and the capitals make it feel like a legal notice or someone shouting.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web or info page that tells ferry passengers how the service runs before they travel: when, how long, bikes, and winter changes.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The body text is in all capitals, which makes the only content on the page slow and tiring to read. The copy also leaves out the facts a passenger would actually plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **All three body paragraphs are set in capitals** (defect, and a broken convention). Each paragraph runs two or three lines. Capitals remove the word shapes readers rely on, so every line has to be read letter by letter, and the uniform rectangle of text hides the facts a passenger is scanning for ("forty minutes", "twelve minutes", "one boat each hour"). Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says caps suit headings shorter than one line and that you shouldn't capitalise whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) says text in capitals is particularly hard to read. Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for the short "PASSENGER NOTES" label, where they already work.
2. **The times are vague exactly where a passenger has to act** (defect, copy). "Between early morning and late evening" doesn't tell a reader whether they can catch a 6:30 boat or a 22:00 one. "Every forty minutes" with no starting time means they can't work out the next departure. "In winter" doesn't say which months, so a traveller in late October can't tell which timetable applies. The final line also sends them to a printed timetable at the landing, which is no help to someone reading this before they leave home. Fix: give the first and last sailing times, an anchor such as "on the hour and every 40 minutes after", and the dates the winter timetable runs. Better still, link to the timetable or include it here.
3. **The notes are three paragraphs that people will skim** (convention). Readers on a screen scan for the one fact they need: time, duration, bikes, or winter (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). In paragraphs, the bike rule and the winter change sit inside sentences. Fix: lead each note with its subject, for example a short bold run-in like "Bicycles", or make it a short list, so each fact can be found at a glance.

**Smaller things.**
- Bicycle paragraph: "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" describes the process but never tells the reader what to do. Address them directly, for example "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on and lock it to the side rail."
- Line length: the capitals make the lines look like about 75–80 characters, at the top of the comfortable range. Once the text is in lower case, check the measure stays around 66.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy at the top is clean. A small, spaced, grey caps label sits over one bold heading, so there are two clear levels with an obvious job each. Keep it.
- "Before you board" is a good heading. It speaks to the reader, names the moment the page is for, and has a verb.
- The single left-aligned column with generous paragraph spacing is calm and right for a short notes page. The empty space to the right and below doesn't hurt it.
