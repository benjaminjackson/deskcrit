**First read.** The eye goes to the big blue headline, then is pulled down to the four blue-outlined cards, which together are the heaviest thing on the page. The "Create your link" button is the same blue as the headline, nav, card titles, icons, borders and badges, so it doesn't stand out. It reads as a clean, generic SaaS scheduling homepage. It feels tidy and trustworthy, but also flat and anonymous. The right half of the hero is empty white.

**What it's for.** You gave no context, so I'm assuming this is the marketing homepage for a booking-link tool. Its job is to get a small service business (the names along the bottom are a dentist, a tutor, a physio, a coach and a vet) to create a link or see an example.

**Verdict.** It's competent and readable but doesn't sell. One blue is used for everything, so nothing ranks above anything else, and the page never shows the product. A visitor gets a claim ("fill your calendar") with nothing to judge it by.

**What would move it most**

1. **One blue does every job, so the main action doesn't stand out.** Defect. The same saturated blue covers the headline, nav links, card titles, icons, card borders, badges, both buttons and every link. Blue stops meaning "you can click this." The four bordered cards hold more blue than the button does, so the eye goes to them and the "Create your link" button has to be looked for. This breaks accent restraint: keep one accent for the main action and interactive states (Refactoring UI; Material Design). It also breaks saturation hierarchy: saturated colour should mark what matters. Fix: set the headline and card titles in near-black, drop the card borders to a light grey or remove them (spacing alone can group the cards), and make the icons neutral or tinted. That leaves full-strength blue on the buttons and links, so "Create your link" becomes the obvious next step.

2. **The page never shows a booking link.** Defect. The product is a page a client sees and picks a time on, yet the right half of the hero is empty. That space is leftover, not deliberate. The visitor has to accept "fill your calendar" on trust. "See a live example" is the strongest ask on the page because it lets them judge for themselves, but it's a small underlined text link. Fix: put the real booking page in that right-hand space, ideally one of the named customers' pages (e.g. Harbor Dental's) as a client would see it. The headline then has a picture to back it up, and the empty space gets used.

3. **The copy is written for office meetings, not the customers on the page.** Defect. The names along the bottom are a dental practice, a tutoring service, a physio, a coach and a vet clinic. Those businesses book appointments, sessions and visits, while the copy talks about "every meeting," "a team," "the call" and "the first minutes." Someone at the front desk of a vet clinic has to translate the page before it speaks to them, and "keep every meeting in the right place" says nothing specific to anyone. "Booking links that fill your calendar" could be any competitor's headline. Fix: pick the audience and use its words. For example, write the subhead around their result ("clients book themselves, get reminded, and turn up"), and say what the reminders do in their terms, such as fewer empty appointment slots.

4. **The customer names don't read as proof.** Defect. Five plain grey names sit under a thin blue line at the bottom edge with no label. They look like footer links, not "these businesses use us." Social proof that isn't recognised does no work. Fix: add a short label ("Booking for 2,000+ clinics, tutors and coaches" or whatever is true), use logos or a darker weight, and consider one specific result from one of them next to the hero.

**Smaller things**
- Badges: "New" and "Beta" are status labels, but "Auto" and "Email" describe features, and all four use the same pill style, so it suggests they mean the same kind of thing. "Beta" on intake forms also reads as a warning on the homepage. Keep pills for status only, or drop them.
- Card 1: the "New" badge is pushed to the right, which makes "Shared calendars" wrap to two lines. That card's text and "Learn more" then sit one line lower than in the other three, breaking the row. Put the badge inline as in the other cards, or shorten the title.
- The main action has two labels, "Start free" in the header and "Create your link" in the hero. Use one name for one action (Nielsen 4, consistency).
- Four identical "Learn more" links: name what each opens ("How reminders work"). That helps scanning and link purpose (WCAG 2.4.4).
- Type sizes: the subhead (~19 px) and card titles (~20 px) are nearly the same size, so they read as one level. Separate them with weight or colour, not another size step.
- Headline break: "calendar" sits alone on line two. Re-break it, or widen the headline so it sits on one line once the right column holds the product image.
- Cards are equal height but the text sits at the top, leaving empty space at the bottom of cards 2–4. This shows less once the borders are lighter or gone.

**What's working**
- The text is genuinely readable. The subhead runs about 60–65 characters per line, body text is a sensible size and dark grey, and everything aligns to one left edge and one grid of four equal columns. Keep that grid.
- The feature descriptions are good copy. Each says who does what and why it matters to you ("so nobody has to do the math," "to cut down on no-shows"). Apply that standard to the headline and subhead.
- Having a main button plus a "See a live example" link is the right setup. The example link just needs more visibility and a product visual behind it.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the page goes on below the customer names (a demo, pricing, testimonials). If proof comes further down, findings 2 and 4 are less severe, though not by much, because the hero is where people decide to keep reading.
