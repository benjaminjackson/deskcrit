**First read.** There's no single place for the eye to land. It goes to the top-left line and reads down a grey-black block of eight lines that all look about the same. Only on reading do you find that the lines alternate between a topic and an answer. It reads as a FAQ or help panel for a bakery's click-and-collect orders. It feels calm and plain, but flat, almost like an unstyled draft.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help section on a bakery's online ordering page or order screen. A customer with one specific question should be able to scan the topics, find theirs, and get a clear answer they can act on.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The topic headings are no heavier than the answers and look slightly smaller, so the page can't be scanned and every question takes reading from the top. Two answers also leave out what the customer needs to act: when the cancellation cutoff falls, and what to bring at the counter.

**What would move it most**

1. **The headings don't work as headings (defect).** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are set in the same regular weight and colour as the answers, and they look a pixel or two *smaller*. That turns the hierarchy upside down. Someone arriving with one question has to read every line to tell topic from answer, which is the one thing a FAQ exists to avoid. Only the spacing does any work here: each heading sits closer to its own answer than to the block above, which is right (Bringhurst §2.2.2), but spacing alone can't carry it. Fix: make the headings clearly the stronger level. Weight is the usual way (semibold, same or slightly larger size, per Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger), and you could drop the answers to a slightly softer grey. Right now there are two sizes that are almost equal, which reads like a mistake. Keep two levels and make the gap between them obvious.

2. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways (defect).** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say ahead of what, or when exactly. It could mean 24 hours before the pickup time or any time the day before. A customer with an 8 am pickup who tries to cancel at 9 pm the night before will get a different answer from each reading, and that becomes a lost refund or a complaint. Since the customer will hold the bakery to this line, it needs an exact cutoff: "You can cancel until 24 hours before your pickup time" or "until 6 pm the day before". The second sentence works well because it tells the customer *why*. Keep it.

3. **"Collecting for someone else" leaves the customer guessing what to send (defect).** "Along with a name": whose name? The person collecting or the person who ordered? A stranger will rebuild this either way, and the friend may turn up at the counter with the wrong one. Say it outright: "They'll need to show your order number and give your name at the counter." Also say whether a screenshot of the number is enough.

4. **The allergy answer only helps someone already in the shop (defect, given the assumed job).** "Every loaf has a card by the till" is no use to someone ordering online, which is exactly when they need to know. For an allergen question that's the costliest gap on the page. If ingredients are listed on the product pages, point there ("Each loaf's page lists its ingredients and allergens"), and keep "ask the baker" as the backup, not the only route.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the full-width lines ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") look like about 90–95 characters, over Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the column by about a fifth.
- "Changing your pickup time": "Open your order" assumes the customer knows where that is. Say where ("in your confirmation email" or "under My orders"). "Holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact. Finish it with what it means for them: what happens to the bread after that.
- Short last lines ("anything.", "after the chosen time.") leave ragged single words. Once the column width changes, adjust the wording or measure so they don't hang alone.
- The text block sits high and left in a mostly empty frame, with a large dead area below and to the right. That's fine if this is a crop of a longer page. As a standalone panel it looks unfinished.

**What's working.**
- The headings are written from the customer's side: they name the task ("Changing your pickup time"), not a policy category. Keep that wording when you restyle them.
- The tone is plain and human. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" explains a rule without sounding defensive. That's the voice to keep.
- Grouping by spacing is right: the gap between items is clearly larger than the gap between heading and answer, so the four pairs hold together once the headings have weight.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the headings are meant to be expandable (an accordion). If they are, they need a visible affordance, and a check that the collapsed state still lets people scan.
