**First read.** My eye goes to the headline, then moves across the four big prices from left to right. All four cards have the same weight, so nothing stops it. This is a standard SaaS pricing page for a collaboration tool. It feels clean, calm and competent. It also feels anonymous: it could be the pricing page of any product. Nothing tells me which plan is for me.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a pricing page that should get a small team or freelancer to pick a plan and click, ideally the paid plan the business most wants to sell.

**Verdict.** The layout is tidy, but the page doesn't help anyone choose. All four plans are presented as equal, and the feature lists contain a contradiction (Free gives more editors than Solo) that will make careful buyers distrust the whole table. Fix the numbers first, then give the page one plan to look at.

**What would move it most**

1. **The feature numbers contradict the price ladder (defect).** In the first two cards, Free includes "3 editors" and Solo, at $12, includes "1 editor". A reader comparing row by row sees that paying gets them fewer editors than not paying. They will either think it's a typo, and wonder what else is wrong, or think Free is the better deal and never upgrade. Either way the page loses trust at the exact moment it asks for money. If Solo really is single-seat by design, the row has to say so, e.g. "Just you (1 editor)", so the drop reads as a choice and not a mistake. Otherwise correct the numbers so each step up never gives less.

2. **No plan is marked as the one to choose (convention: one element dominates, one focal point; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*).** The four cards are identical in border, background, button colour and button weight, so all four buttons shout equally. A visitor has to read all four columns and decide alone, and comparing four columns is the work pricing pages are meant to spare them. Pick the plan you want most people on (probably Team) and make it dominant with a stronger border or tint, a short "Most teams pick this" label, and the only solid button. Make the other three buttons outlined or secondary. Right now four primary actions sit side by side, which breaks "Secondary actions are not styled as strongly as the primary one."

3. **"$99 / month" next to "Talk to us" reads two ways (defect: this is a price).** If Company costs a flat $99 a month, why do I have to talk to someone? If I have to talk to someone, is $99 the real price or a starting price? The same problem runs through every card: "/ month" doesn't say whether it's per team or per editor, and at 15 or 100 editors that difference decides whether someone buys. Say it outright: "$39 / month for the whole team" or "$12 per editor / month". Then either give Company a self-serve button, or change the price to "From $99" with a CTA that names what happens, e.g. "Book a 20-minute call".

4. **The copy could sit on anyone's page (defect).** "Plans for every size of team", "For small studios" and "Named contact" could belong to any SaaS product. The headline doesn't say what the product does. The plan names and taglines describe who the plan is for, not what changes for them. Give the headline a real claim and make the feature rows say what the viewer gets. "1 year of history" becomes "Restore any version from the past year". "Priority help" becomes something with a number in it, e.g. "Replies within 4 hours".

**Smaller things.**
- The gap between the Team and Company cards looks wider (about 37 px) than the other two gaps (about 25 px), which splits Company off from the set. Make all three gutters equal.
- About 70 px of empty card sits below each button, so the cards feel unfinished. Either end the card closer to the button or use the space for a line like "No card needed" under Start free.
- The subhead's best fact, "Every plan includes unlimited viewers", is buried in grey second-line text. It's a real reason to buy, so promote it.
- Feature rows are separated only by hairlines. Without row labels or a consistent order key, comparing rows across the four columns depends on everything lining up perfectly. They do line up now, so keep it that way, or add a simple comparison table below.
- The grey taglines ("For trying it out") look close to the 4.5:1 contrast minimum at that small size. Measure them.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: a headline, big prices, plan names, then body and button text, with clear steps between them. Prices are clearly the biggest thing in each card, which is correct for this page.
- Each card follows the same structure (name, tagline, price, four rows, button) and the rows line up across all four, so row-by-row comparison is possible. Keep that alignment when you add emphasis to one card.
- Restraint: one accent colour, no ornament, plenty of air. Highlighting a recommended plan will stand out precisely because nothing else competes.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether there's an annual/monthly toggle, or how the cards stack on mobile. With four columns, check the stacking order puts the recommended plan near the top.
