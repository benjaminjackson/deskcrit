**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading, then down a narrow column of grey-black sans text, and the first thing you notice is the space between the lines. Every line sits so far from the next that the column looks like a stack of separate one-liners rather than three paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for people using a river ferry. It feels calm and tidy, but airy to the point of looking unfinished.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short reference page, on the web or printed at the landing, that tells passengers when the ferry runs and how bicycles board.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and easy to read in its basic choices, but the line spacing breaks it apart. The lines are so far apart that the paragraphs stop holding together, so the eye can't tell where one note ends and the next begins. The copy also leaves out the facts a passenger needs to catch a boat.

**What would move it most**
1. **The line spacing is far too loose, so the paragraphs fall apart. (Convention.)** This affects all three paragraphs. The body text looks about 19 px tall, but the lines sit about 45 px apart, roughly 2.4 times the type size. The gap between paragraphs (about 64 px from one baseline to the next) is only a little bigger than the gap between lines. So the white space between lines is almost as strong as the white space between paragraphs, and the page reads as eight loose lines, not three notes. The reader has to work out from the sentences where a paragraph breaks, because the spacing doesn't show it. Bringhurst (2.2.1) and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 47) say line spacing should suit the typeface, the text and the line length. At about 70 characters a line, this column needs nothing like this much. Bring the line spacing down to about 1.4–1.6 times the type size. Then keep a clearly larger gap between paragraphs, roughly one blank line, so each note reads as one unit. The gap under the heading is about the same as the gaps between paragraphs, so the heading doesn't hold onto the text below it either. Once the line spacing tightens, check that gap too.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts passengers will act on. (Defect.)** "Between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes", "in winter", and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are all things a passenger would plan around. None of them gives a time or date they can use. Someone reading this can't tell when the first boat leaves, whether they'll make the last one, or which months count as winter. They might guess "before dusk" means 5 pm when the boat actually leaves at 3:30. "Runs from the north landing to the market quay" can be read two ways: one-way only, or both directions. A passenger who guesses wrong waits on the wrong bank. Give the first and last departure from each landing, the winter dates, and the winter last-boat time. If times change through the year, a small table of departures would do this job far better than running text.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" could head anyone's page. Something that names the route, such as "North landing ↔ Market quay ferry", tells the reader at once that they're on the right page.
- The bicycle paragraph is the only instruction on the page ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded"). It's buried in the middle of prose and would scan faster as a short labelled section.

**What's working.**
- The line length is right: about 65–72 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Keep the column this narrow and don't let it stretch across the empty right side.
- There is one heading, set clearly heavier and larger, over one body size. That's a two-level type scale with no sizes competing, which fits a short page like this.
- The text is flush left, near-black on white, with good contrast, in a plain sans built for reading. None of that needs to change.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where most passengers standing at a landing would read it.
