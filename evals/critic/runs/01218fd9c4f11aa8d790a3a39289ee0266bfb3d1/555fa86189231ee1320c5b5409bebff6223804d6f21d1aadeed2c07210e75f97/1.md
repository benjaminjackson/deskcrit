**First read.** In both versions the eye lands first on the big serif headline, "Small-batch ice cream, made on Mill Street", then drops to the three pink script subheads and on to the hours bar. It reads as the home page of a small local ice-cream shop: warm, homemade, a little twee. In version 1 the three paragraphs read as quickly as plain prose. In version 2 they turn into slow, loopy handwriting, and the page starts to feel like a greeting card.

**What it's for.** My assumption: to tell a local passer-by what the shop is, why its ice cream is worth a trip, and when they can come.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The only change between them is the body typeface, and in version 2 that change makes the paragraphs hard to read. Even in version 1 the page never shows what's in the freezer or where the shop is, so it makes the case for the ice cream but doesn't get anyone through the door.

**What changed, and why it decides this**
- **Version 2 sets the paragraphs in the same brush script as the logo and subheads.** That's about 60 words a column, set tight, in connected letters with heavy strokes. A script typeface is meant for a few words, like a name or a heading. Across whole paragraphs the shapes of the letters blur together, so readers have to work out each word, and many will skip the copy. The copy is the best thing on this page: one dairy farm, Saturday-market fruit, 50p off for bringing your tub back. In version 2 that's the part people won't read. The script also stops marking anything special. In version 1 it signals "brand voice" in three places: the logo and two levels of heading. In version 2 it's everywhere, so the subheads no longer stand out from the text under them. This is a defect: it slows every reader and loses the content. Bringhurst and Dair both treat script as display type, not text type. Version 1 keeps the script for the logo and headings and sets the text in a plain sans. That's the right split.

**Remaining findings for version 1**
1. **There's no next step and no address.** The page ends on opening hours and then about 250 px of blank cream. A visitor learns the street name, but not the number, a map or a way to get there. They also never see a single flavour on the board, even though "When a fruit is out of season, its flavour leaves the board" sets that up. Add the full address next to the hours, and a link such as "See what's on the board today" that points to the Flavours page. That gives the visitor something concrete they can act on. Defect.
2. **The hours bar looks like a footnote.** The one thing a visitor will act on and hold the shop to sits in a pale tinted box, in body-sized text, under three paragraphs. The January closure is the line that will save someone a wasted trip. Give the hours and address more weight than the paragraphs, or move them up near the headline. Taste, but with a real cost to people who don't find them.

**Smaller things**
- The bottom third of the screen is empty. Fill it with flavours or a photo rather than leaving the page to fade out.
- Each column ends on a fact and never turns it to the reader. "Eleven miles up the valley… churn it the same day" could finish with what that means for them, such as "so it tastes like fresh milk".

**What's working.** The type scale is clear: a big serif headline, script subheads, sans text and small nav, four levels with obvious jumps. The copy is specific and couldn't belong to any other shop. The three-column grid is aligned and calm.

Better: version 1
