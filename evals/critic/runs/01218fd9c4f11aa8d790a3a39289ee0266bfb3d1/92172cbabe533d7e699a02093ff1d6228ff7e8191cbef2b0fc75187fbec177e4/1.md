**First read.** The eye lands on the title, then drops to the tall blue bar on the left and walks down the staircase of six bars. Each bar is a different loud colour, so the eye stops at every one. The note on the right comes last, and you only find it if you go looking. It reads as a tidy internal growth dashboard: calm, competent and a bit generic.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a quarterly growth view for the team and leadership. It should show where Q3 sign-ups came from and back up the two takeaways in the note: organic search grew, and events money moves to referrals.

**Verdict.** It's accurate and readable. The numbers add up to the 5,400 in the source line, and organic search at 1,840 really is about a third. But the chart and the story are separate pieces. The chart gives every channel equal, shouting emphasis, while the note is about two specific findings that the chart neither highlights nor shows. The colour scheme and the split between chart and note are what's holding it back.

**What would move it most**

1. **Six colours that encode nothing are drowning out the story.** Every bar already has a name under it and a number above it, so the blue, orange, red, teal, green and purple add no information. Each one still pulls the eye, which makes six things equally important when the note says two matter. The red on Paid social also reads as "problem" to anyone used to status colours, which isn't what you mean. Saturated colour should mark what matters, with everything else neutral (Refactoring UI; Tufte, *Visual Display*, on cutting ink that carries no data). Make the bars one neutral grey and put a single accent on the bars the note talks about: Organic search, and Podcast ads plus Events. Then the chart makes the argument before anyone reads the note. *Defect: it hides the point.*

2. **The note's headline claim isn't in the chart.** "Up from a quarter in Q2" is the actual news, but the chart shows Q3 only, so the reader has to take the growth on trust. For a quarterly review, the change is the point. Add Q2 as a reference: a light ghost bar or a tick mark behind each Q3 bar, or at least on Organic search. That lets the reader see the jump for themselves. *Defect: the claim can't be checked on the page.*

3. **The decision is buried at the end of a sidebar paragraph.** "The events budget moves to referrals in Q4" is the one line people will act on and hold you to. Right now it's the last sentence of body text in a column the eye reaches last, cut off from the Events and Referral bars by about 70px of empty gutter. Move the annotations onto the chart next to the bars they describe, or give the decision its own clearly marked line. Also name the amount, because "the events budget" could mean all of it or some of it, and readers will remember whichever they assumed. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- The page has no year. "Q3" and "1 July to 30 September" will be ambiguous within a year, so add it to the subtitle.
- Type scale: I count about seven sizes. The title is about 26px, and the rest (subtitle, note body, bar labels, value labels, source, axis ticks) are bunched between about 12 and 15px, so they blur into one level. Keep title, body, and one small size, and separate value labels from axis labels with weight, not size.
- The y-axis and gridlines repeat the direct value labels. Drop the axis, or fade it further, since every bar is already labelled.
- The bottom third of the page is empty and the note column floats high and short. That's fine for a single view, but the note would sit better aligned to the chart's top edge at 2,000 instead of starting below it.

**What's working.**
- The bars are sorted by value, start at zero, and carry direct labels. Reading off and comparing values is instant, so keep all three.
- The subtitle defines the metric exactly ("by the channel of the first visit") and the source line gives the period and the total. That's the context a skeptical reader needs.
- The note's copy is concrete and quantified, says what caused the change ("after the guide pages were rewritten"), and ends with a decision. The words are right; they're just in the wrong place and missing the year and the amount.
