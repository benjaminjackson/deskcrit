I have enough to write the critique, so here it is.

**First read.** The eye goes to the purple "Sign in" button, then up to the email field because its red border is the only warm colour on the page. Within a second this reads as a sign-in form that has refused the email address. It feels calm, tidy and familiar, but the second look finds nothing that says what went wrong.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the sign-in screen for Quillpost, captured in the state where the email field is invalid. Its job is to get a returning user signed in, and when they make a mistake, to tell them what it is and how to fix it.

**Verdict.** As a normal sign-in form it works. As an error state, which is what this capture shows, it fails: the only signal is a red outline with no message, so the user knows something is wrong but not what. The biggest thing in the way is that missing error message.

**What would move it most**

1. **The error has no words.** The email field (`dana.ortiz@exmaple`) has a red border, and that is the only sign of trouble. There's no message, no icon, and the label "Email" hasn't changed. The user has to guess what's wrong. The real problem is a typo ("exmaple") and a missing ending like ".com", and both are easy to miss in your own email address because you read what you meant to type. People will press "Sign in" again, or assume their password is wrong and click "Forgot your password?". Both waste their time.
   *Defect.* It also breaks WCAG 1.4.1 (Use of Color), because the meaning rests on colour alone, which people with red-green colour blindness may not see. It breaks WCAG 3.3.1 (Error Identification) too, which requires a text description of the error.
   *Fix:* put a one-line message right under the field, in red text with an icon, that says what to do, for example "This email looks incomplete — check the part after @." If you know the domain is a likely typo, offering the fix ("Did you mean example.com?") is better still.

2. **The password field's border is very faint.** The pale grey outline on the white card looks well below 3:1 contrast. Users with low vision, or anyone on a glare-washed laptop screen, will have trouble finding where to click and type. It also makes the red email border look like the field's normal style rather than a warning.
   *Convention:* WCAG 1.4.11 asks for 3:1 on component edges. Measure the border against the white card. If it's below 3:1, darken the field border grey until it passes.

**Smaller things**
- "New here? Create an account" is all the same grey, so "Create an account" doesn't look clickable. Give it the purple link colour that "Forgot your password?" already uses.
- "Forgot your password?" is small, purple and sits right above the main button. When the email is wrong, it pulls attention to the wrong fix. It's fine where it is once item 1 gives the real reason.

**What's working**
- There's one clear action. The full-width purple "Sign in" button is the only heavy shape on the card, so nobody has to look for what to do next. Keep that.
- The type scale is tight. Wordmark, heading, bold labels, input text and small links each have one job, and there are no near-duplicate sizes.
- Plain, familiar layout: labels above the fields, the card centred, nothing extra. A sign-in page should be exactly this boring.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- When the error appears (on blur or on submit), whether it goes away as the user fixes the address, and whether screen readers announce it.
- Focus rings on the fields and links.
