**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, drops to the header row, then runs down a clean, quiet table of seven rows. It reads as a one-way ferry schedule for ordinary riders. It feels calm and orderly, and a little generic. Then the sentence at the bottom says "heavy black lines around every cell", and the table above it has none. The reader stops and wonders what they are missing.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a rider checks it to find the next boat from North landing to the quay on a given day, either at the landing or on their phone.

**Verdict.** The table works and is easy to read. There are two real problems. The caption at the bottom is false, and it sits on the piece as if it were part of it. And the one column that can leave a rider stranded, "Runs", is the quietest thing on the page.

**What would move it most**

1. **The caption contradicts the design (defect).** Below the table, the line "The heavy black lines around every cell make this timetable a mess" describes something that isn't there. The rules are pale grey hairlines under each row, with no vertical lines and no boxes. A reader who takes the line at face value now distrusts a correct table, or hunts for a different version. Delete it. If it was a note about an earlier version, keep it out of the published piece.

2. **The service exceptions are hidden in plain text (defect).** In the right-hand "Runs" column, "Weekdays" (12:40, 17:10) and "Weekends" (19:30) are set in the same weight and colour as "Daily". This is the column that decides whether a boat exists today. A Saturday rider scanning the time column will find 12:40 or 17:10 and turn up for a boat that doesn't run. Make the exceptions stand out and let "Daily" go quiet. Either mark only the exceptions (bolder text, or a symbol with a key), or split the table into "Every day" and "Weekdays only / Weekends only" groups. That is lightness doing the work, not another size (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).

3. **"Spring season" gives no dates (defect).** The subtitle is the only scope line, and a rider can't tell from it whether this timetable applies today. Dates are what people hold the operator to. State the exact range, for example "Valid 1 March – 31 May". While you're there, say that this is one direction only, because the title "River ferry timetable" promises more than the table holds.

4. **The "Period" column leads, and it adds nothing (taste).** The first and most prominent column repeats words the times already make obvious ("Morning, Morning, Midday, Midday…"). So the eye starts each row on the least useful cell, and the departure time, which is what a rider is looking for, sits in the second column. Drop the column. Or, if the periods help, turn them into small group labels between rows ("Morning" above 06:40 and 07:20) and start each row on "Leaves north".

**Smaller things.**
- The table is stretched across about 1,080 px, so there's a long empty gap between the departure and arrival times. A narrower table, or tighter columns, keeps each row together for the eye.
- The times look like they use proportional figures: "11:00" sets narrower than "06:40", so the colons don't line up down the column. Switch on tabular figures (Bringhurst; the checklist's "Figures in tables" line).
- The journey always takes 12 minutes. One line saying so ("Crossing takes 12 min") would let you cut or quieten the arrival column, if space ever gets tight.

**What's working.**
- Light horizontal rules only, with no vertical lines or boxes. Each row reads as one unit with no grid clutter (Tufte: keep the ink for the data). Keep this.
- The column headers "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are verbs with places, so a stranger knows exactly what each time means. That's better than "Dep."/"Arr."
- The type scale is clean: a title, a subtitle, bold headers and body text. Each level has an obvious job, and nothing collides.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the four columns reflow on a phone, where a rider at the landing is most likely to read this.
