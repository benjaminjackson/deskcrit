**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then down the paragraph to the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as a simple landing page for a local ferry: quiet, plain and trustworthy. Then the last line, "Design review: failed, everything on this page is centred", stops you. It looks like an internal note that got published by mistake.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a ferry operator's web page. Its job is to get a local or a visitor to decide to take the boat and find out when it leaves.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy is clean and the headline is strong, but the page doesn't yet do its job. It ends on a stray note that makes the operator look careless. It also holds back the one fact a rider needs, which is when the next boat leaves, and sends them through a button to find it.

**What would move it most**

1. **Remove the "Design review" line at the bottom.** *Defect.* It's set at body size, flush left in the main column and in full-strength text, so nothing marks it as separate from the page. It's the last thing a visitor reads, and it tells them the page "failed". It's also wrong: every block on the page is flush left on one edge, and nothing is centred. I'm treating it as text on the page, not as a verdict on the design. If it's a leftover review note, delete it. If something else belongs in that spot, such as the landing address or the fare, put that there instead.

2. **Put the times on the page.** *Defect, from the copy.* "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives a rider nothing to plan with. They can't tell whether "early" means 6:00 or 8:00, or whether "late" means 21:00 or 23:00. "The north landing" assumes they already know where that is, and the page never says what the far side is called. So the only way to answer "can I make it?" is to click "See the timetable", and the button exists only because the page left that answer out. Give the first and last sailings in exact times, and say where the boat goes from and to. If the page can be live, show the next departure. Then the button can name a specific thing to do: "See today's departures" or "Next boat 14:20 – full timetable". A rider who can see a boat at 14:20 can check it against their own afternoon, which persuades better than a button they have to trust.

3. **Use the empty right half, or close it up.** *Taste.* All the content sits in a column on the left 60% of the frame, and the right side is blank white with nothing balancing it. On a ferry page, that space is where the promise belongs. "Watch the harbour slide past from the open deck" is the most appealing line here, and the page never shows it. A photo of the deck or the bay on the right would make "calm" something the viewer can see, not just read. If you don't have a good photo, narrow and centre the frame so the blank side doesn't look like a missing asset. As it stands, the page could belong to any company.

**Smaller things**
- The three feature lines ("Bicycles travel free" and the two below) have no bullets, icons or other markers. They're also a size step smaller than the paragraph above, close enough that it looks accidental. Either give them a clear list treatment at body size, or set them noticeably smaller and lighter.
- "Bicycles travel free" repeats the "Bring a bicycle" in the paragraph. Cut it from one place, or use the list slot for something new, such as the fare.
- I count six text sizes: the eyebrow, the headline, the body, the list, the button and the stray line. Body and list are too close to read as two levels. Keep four sizes: eyebrow, headline, body and button label.
- The "Coastal Ferry Co." eyebrow uses the same blue as the button, so it may read as a link. If it isn't clickable, give the button the blue alone and set the brand name in dark text or as a logo.

**What's working**
- The headline. "Twelve calm minutes" gives a concrete number and a feeling, and it's specific to this crossing. Its tight two-line setting holds together as one unit. Keep both the wording and the setting.
- Spacing that shows the groups. The gaps between the headline, paragraph, list and button are larger than the gaps within each one, so the page reads as four clear steps down to one action.
- One clear primary action. A single filled button is the only coloured shape on the page, so there's no doubt where to click.
