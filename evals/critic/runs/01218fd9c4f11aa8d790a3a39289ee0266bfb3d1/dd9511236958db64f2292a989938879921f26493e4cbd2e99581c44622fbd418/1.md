**First read.** Your eye goes to the bold serif headline first, then drops left to the lighthouse, then back to the text. It reads as a short, friendly page about a small local landmark, maybe a heritage site or tourism page. It feels calm, warm and well made. The type is good, and the illustration's flat blue, red and yellow suits the coast.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or a section of one that introduces Gull Point lighthouse to people who might visit, and should leave them knowing they can go and how.

**Verdict.** It reads pleasantly, but it doesn't do the job of getting someone to visit. The visiting details are buried in the last two paragraphs and are too vague to act on. The picture also floats loose from the text block, which makes an otherwise tidy page look unfinished.

**What would move it most**

1. **The visit is buried and too vague to plan from** (defect). The useful facts sit at the ends of paragraphs 2 and 3, set exactly like the history: "open to visitors on summer weekends" and "Tickets are sold at the cottage by the car park." A reader who wants to go can't tell:
   - which months count as "summer"
   - what the opening hours are
   - what a ticket costs
   - where the car park is
   
   And they'll only find even that much if they read to the end. Nothing on the page names an action or stands out as one.
   
   Fix: pull the visiting details out of the story into their own short block, with the months, the hours, the price and where to go. Give it its own weight or a small subhead. Dates, times and prices are what people plan around and hold you to later, so they have to be exact.

2. **The picture lines up with nothing in the text column** (convention: Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "The photograph in the grid system"; Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I on placements that are nearly but not quite aligned).
   - The illustration's top is about 85 px below the top of the headline.
   - Its bottom is about 75 px below the last line of text.
   - So it isn't top-aligned, bottom-aligned or centred on the text block.
   
   It looks like it was dropped in rather than placed, and that small miss makes the whole spread look a little unfinished. Fix: choose one alignment and commit to it. The usual choice is to put the image's top on the headline's cap height. You could also size it so the top and bottom match the text block's first and last lines.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 1 ends with "room." alone on its last line, a one-word widow. Re-break the line or adjust the wording so at least two words carry over.
- The copy says "the white tower", but the illustration shows a strong red band. A reader may stop and wonder whether this is the right lighthouse. Change one of them so they agree.
- Paragraph 2's best line, "you can follow the ferry all the way across the bay", is the real reason to climb, and it's mid-paragraph. Consider leading the visiting block with it so the invitation lands before the logistics.
- The empty space is spread around the page (a deep band below, a wide margin on the right) instead of gathered in one place, so the pair floats high and to the left. This is taste: once the visit block is added, place the group deliberately within the frame.

**What's working.**
- The type is in good shape. The heading is a bold serif from the same family as the body, so it contrasts by weight as well as size. There are two clear levels and no in-between sizes. The body measure is about 65–70 characters, with comfortable leading and a good rag. Keep all of this when you add the visit block, and don't introduce a third or fourth size for it.
- The illustration's restrained flat palette (pale sky, two blues of sea, red and yellow accents) gives the page a clear seaside mood without clutter. Its red picks up the warmth of the dark headline.
- The writing is concrete and specific ("ninety steps", "brass plaques in the lamp room", "bakes scones every morning"), which is what makes this place feel like no other. Keep that voice in the practical copy too.
