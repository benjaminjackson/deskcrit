**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline, reads all three lines, drops to the subhead, and stops on the dark green button. Only then does it drift right to the small white invoice card, and after that down to the three feature cards. It reads as a calm, tidy invoicing tool for freelancers and small studios. It feels competent and friendly, and also like a lot of other SaaS landing pages.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a marketing page for an invoicing app. It has to persuade freelancers and small-business owners that sending invoices and chasing payment will be faster and less painful, and get them to start a trial.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy works and the headline is good. Two things let it down: the only picture of the product shows none of what the headline promises, and the button asks for a trial period rather than an action. Each makes the page less convincing than its copy.

**What would move it most**

1. **The invoice card doesn't show the promise.** (Taste, close to a defect for this job.) The right half of the hero has one small, finished invoice floating in empty cream space. The headline promises speed, and the subhead promises that Tallyfold fills in clients, rates and tax, then chases late payments. The card shows none of that. It's a finished invoice that any tool, or a spreadsheet, could produce, so the viewer has to take the claim on trust. It's also the quietest thing in the hero: thin border, small text, lots of air around it. The fix is to make the picture show the claim. For example, show the invoice being filled in, with the client, rate and tax fields marked as filled in for you. Or show the reminder email going out to Harbor Print Co. Then make it large enough to fill the right column and hold its own against the headline, so the hero reads as headline plus proof, not headline plus decoration.

2. **The button names a trial length, not a thing to do.** (Defect against the job.) "Start free for 14 days" tells the viewer how long they get but not what happens when they click. It also says nothing about whether a card is needed or what it costs afterwards, which is what people hesitate over at this point. And nothing sets the product against what they use now, whether that's a Word template, a spreadsheet or another tool, so they can't judge the time saved for themselves. Name the first concrete action, such as "Send your first invoice free". Put a short line under the button that answers the hesitation ("No card needed · Import clients from a spreadsheet or [current tool]"). That makes trying it feel small and the comparison obvious.

3. **The subhead and the first feature card disagree about reminders.** (Defect: this describes messages sent to the user's own clients in their name.) The subhead says Tallyfold "chases late payments for you". The first card says "Three gentle nudges before an invoice is overdue." One reading is that reminders go out before the due date, which isn't chasing late payments. The other is that it chases after the due date, which the card seems to deny. A user who wants their clients nudged after the due date, or who would be upset at reminders before it, will feel misled whichever one is true. Say plainly when they go out, for example "before it's due, and again if it's late", and use the same wording in both places.

**Smaller things**
- Headline line breaks: "the" is cut off from "time", and "coffee." sits alone on the third line. Break at phrase boundaries instead: "Send an invoice / in the time it takes / to pour coffee." Widen or narrow the measure as needed.
- The headline, subhead and button are packed tightly together, with roughly the same small gap between each, while there's a lot of empty space above and below the hero. Give the headline-to-subhead gap a little more room than the subhead-to-button gap so the block reads in two steps.
- The feature cards are a fixed height, so the second and third have a single line of text and a large empty lower half. Either let them shrink to fit the content, or drop the boxes and let space do the grouping (fine as long as the titles stay bold).
- "Fills in your clients, rates, and tax": a stranger will ask, filled in from where? Add the source, for example "from your last invoice" or "from your client list".
- "Export a clean year of income in one file": name the file type, or say who it's for ("ready for your accountant").
- The left edge of the invoice card looks a few pixels off the left edge of the third feature card below it. Check the measurement and line them up.
- The look (warm cream background, Inter-style sans, three bordered cards) is a familiar template. At the moment only the copy marks this out as Tallyfold. Fixing point 1 is the best way to give it a look of its own.

**What's working**
- The headline is specific and lands on the viewer: a moment everyone knows, not "Invoicing made simple". Keep the idea and its weight.
- The hierarchy is clear: one dominant headline, one filled button, quiet grey nav and "Sign in", and no second button competing with the first. The type scale is disciplined, with about four distinct levels and weight doing the finer work.
- The sample invoice adds up (640 + 45 + 8% tax = $739.80) and uses a believable client and line item. Keep that care when the mock-up is redone.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- How the hero holds up on mobile, where the invoice card will probably stack below the button or drop out, and whether the headline breaks get worse.
