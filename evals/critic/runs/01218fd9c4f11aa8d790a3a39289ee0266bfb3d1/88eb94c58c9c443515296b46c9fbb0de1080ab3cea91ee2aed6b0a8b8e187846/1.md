**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold sans headline, then drops into four even blocks of serif text. It reads as a short web info page for people using a local river ferry. It feels calm and orderly, but it looks like an essay more than a notice. The big first-line indents catch the eye before the words do.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a page people check before or during a trip to find out when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and what they can bring.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is mostly sound, but the page doesn't give people what they came for. The facts a passenger acts on are vague and buried in prose. A smaller typographic mistake, marking paragraphs with both an indent and a space, makes the column look clumsier than it should.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts are vague and hidden in sentences (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "one boat each hour" in winter, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines people plan around, and none of them gives a time. Someone trying to catch the last winter boat can't tell from this page whether that's 15:30 or 17:00. The page then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which only helps once they're already there. These lines are timetable commitments, so a line that can be read two ways is a defect. Give actual first and last departure times for summer and winter, say when winter starts, and name the direction: does "the last crossing leaves the quay" mean only the market quay, or both landings?
2. **Nothing can be skimmed (defect, for this job).** Four paragraphs that look the same hold four separate topics: schedule, bicycles, winter, and facilities. A rider looking for the bicycle rules has to read from the top. Add a short label to each paragraph ("Timetable", "Bicycles", "Winter", "At the landings"), or turn the schedule into a small table. Then the page answers "when?" and "can I bring my bike?" at a glance. The rest can stay as prose; it reads pleasantly.
3. **Paragraphs are marked twice, and the first one is indented (convention).** Each paragraph has a first-line indent and a blank line above it, so the indent does no work. The indent also looks about 2.5–3 ems deep, so the column's left edge looks chewed. Bringhurst (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.3.1) sets the first paragraph after a heading flush left, and the indent is pointless there anyway. Use one marker: keep the space and drop the indents, or keep a modest indent of about 1 em and close up the space. Labels from point 2 would favour keeping the space.

**Smaller things.**
- Line spacing in the body looks generous enough, but the space between paragraphs looks barely bigger than the space between lines. If you drop the indents, increase it so the breaks are clear.
- "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" doesn't say what happens to the passenger. Say it to them: "Keep your bags on your bike during the crossing."
- The column sits hard left with about 40% of the width empty on the right. That's fine for a narrow reading column, but on wide screens it looks unfinished. Centring the column or using the right side for the timetable would help (taste).

**What's working.**
- The bold sans headline over a readable serif body gives two clearly separate levels with no in-between sizes. That's a clean type scale; keep it.
- The measure looks like about 60–70 characters per line, which is comfortable for reading.
- The prose is plain and friendly ("when the tide turns against the boat"). Keep that voice in the labels and times you add.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is where most passengers will read this. Check that the indents (if kept) and the headline size scale down.
