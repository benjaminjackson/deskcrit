**First read.** My eye goes to the headline, then the short paragraph, then the blue button, in that order and without any effort. It reads as the landing page of a small local ferry service, and it feels calm and tidy, if a little plain. Then the last line ("The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge") stops me. It describes a page that is plainly centred, so it looks like a stray note left in the layout.

**What it's for.** Nothing was given, so I'm assuming this is the top section of a ferry operator's website. Its job is to get a local or a visitor to decide to take the boat and click through to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy works: one headline, one paragraph, one button, in a clear order. What holds the page back is the words. It never says where the boat goes, what a ticket costs, or when the first and last boats leave. Those are the facts a rider decides on, so the page sells a nice feeling but doesn't give them enough to plan a trip. There is also a stray sentence at the bottom that contradicts the page and undermines trust.

**What would move it most**
1. **The stray last line.** *Defect.* The sentence under the button claims a "strong left alignment" on a page where everything is centred. It's the same size and colour as the body text, so it reads as part of the page. Either the viewer takes it as a mistake, which makes the company look careless, or they don't understand what they're meant to take from it. It's design commentary, not content. Delete it. (I've treated it as text on the page, not as a verdict on the design.)
2. **The headline and paragraph leave out where the boat goes and what it costs.** *Defect.* "Cross the bay" doesn't say to where. "The north landing" assumes the reader already knows where that is. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" makes them guess the first and last boats, which is exactly what someone planning around a train or a dinner needs. The price is missing entirely, yet "Bicycles travel free" implies people do pay. A stranger has to fill in every one of these gaps.
   - **Direction:** name both ends of the route in the headline or the line under it ("North Landing to Harbour Town").
   - Give real times ("6:40 am to 10:20 pm, every 40 minutes").
   - Add the adult fare.
   - These are the lines people act on and later hold you to, so make them exact.
3. **The button doesn't connect to what the reader is trying to do.** *Taste, but it matters.* "See the timetable" is clear and specific, which is good. But the reader's real question is "when's the next boat?" If the page can show the next sailing or two right there ("Next boat 10:40, then 11:20"), the reader can decide on the spot, and the button becomes a way to see more rather than the only way to find out. If the page has to stay static, a label like "See today's sailings" at least points at what the reader is after.

**Smaller things.**
- **Centred paragraph.** The three-line paragraph is centred, so every line starts in a different place and it's harder to read than it needs to be. At three lines that's tolerable. If it grows, set it flush left, or cut it to two lines.
- **Feature list.** The three lines ("Bicycles travel free", etc.) are centred with no bullets, icons, or extra weight, so they look like loose captions rather than a list of perks. Give them a shared marker or set them on one line with separators so they read as a group.
- **Two size levels that barely differ.** I count four text sizes. The feature lines look only slightly smaller than the paragraph, so they don't read as a separate level. Make them clearly smaller or a different weight, or set them the same size as the paragraph.
- **Brand name.** "Coastal Ferry Co." is a small blue label that looks like a link. It works as a kicker, but it's the only brand presence on the page. If there's a logo, use it.
- **Empty lower half.** The lower third of the frame has nothing in it. A photo of the boat or the harbour would deliver the "calm" the headline promises far better than words alone.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** "Twelve calm minutes" is concrete and sounds like this particular ferry. It promises a duration and a feeling at the same time. Keep that tone while adding the route.
- **One clear action.** There's a single solid blue button, and the only other blue is the brand name, so nothing competes with it. Keep it that way.
- **Restraint.** One typeface, generous spacing, and a short page all fit the "calm" message.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the centred headline and paragraph break on a phone; and the button's hover and focus states.
