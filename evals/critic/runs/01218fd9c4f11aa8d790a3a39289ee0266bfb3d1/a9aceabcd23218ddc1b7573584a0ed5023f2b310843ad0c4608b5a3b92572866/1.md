**First read.** My eye goes to "Changing your pickup time" and then drops down a clean ladder of four bold questions, each followed by one or two lines of answer. It reads at once as the pickup help or FAQ section of a small bakery's ordering site. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. Nothing is fighting for attention.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a customer who has ordered bread online for collection lands here mid-task, needs one specific answer (change, delegate, check allergens, cancel) and needs to act on it correctly.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job. The scanning structure is sound, and a customer finds their topic in a second. What lets it down is the copy. Three of the four answers cover a deadline, a counter check or an allergy, and each leaves out the one detail the customer will act on. That will send people to the wrong place at the wrong time or with the wrong name.

**What would move it most**

1. **The cancellation deadline can be read two ways (defect).** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say ahead of what, or how a "day" is counted. It could mean 24 hours before the chosen pickup slot, or any time before the end of the previous day. A customer with an 8 am pickup who reads it the second way and cancels at 9 pm the night before is refused, after the bread is baked. They'll blame the bakery. The line also never says *how* to cancel. The fix is to give the exact cutoff in the customer's terms, for example "Cancel by [time] the day before your pickup," and to name where it's done, as the first answer does ("Open your order…").

2. **"Along with a name" doesn't say whose name (defect).** In "Collecting for someone else", the friend at the counter can't tell whether to give their own name or the name on the order. Get it wrong and they're turned away at the till, which is the exact moment this answer exists to prevent. The fix is to say it outright: "They'll need your order number and the name the order is under."

3. **The allergy answer only works once the customer is in the shop (defect).** "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps someone standing in the bakery. It doesn't help someone deciding what to order online, and that's who reads this page. It also covers only "loaf", so anyone buying pastries or cakes is left guessing. For an allergy question, that gap is about safety, not style. The fix is to point to where ingredients appear before ordering, and to make the answer cover everything sold, not just loaves. Keep "Ask the baker" as the fallback.

4. **The answer lines run too long (convention).** Answers run the full content width, at roughly 95 to 100 characters a line ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead…"). Bringhurst's comfortable range is 45 to 75 characters (*Elements of Typographic Style*, §2.1.2). With one or two lines per answer the cost is small, which is why this ranks last, but the text column is visibly wider than it needs to be. The fix is to cap the answer column at about 65 to 70 characters.

**Smaller things.**
- **No page title:** there's nothing above the four questions saying what this page is. Add a short page heading, e.g. "Collecting your order", so someone arriving from a link knows they're in the right place.
- **Missing consequence in "Changing your pickup time":** "holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact. Say what it means for the customer: what happens to their order if they're later than that.
- **"Open your order":** say where (the confirmation email link, their account), because a stranger won't know.
- **Headings are large for this little content:** at about 30 px against about 18 px body, the four questions shout slightly at one-line answers. One step smaller, or the same size with weight doing the work, would sit more quietly. This one is taste.

**What's working.**
- **Spacing does the grouping:** each heading sits tight to its answer, with clearly more space before the next question. Each Q&A reads as one unit without rules or boxes, so keep that spacing.
- **Two sizes, clearly apart:** bold heading and regular body are far enough apart in size to read as two distinct levels. That's the right scale for a page this simple.
- **The voice:** plain and human. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" gives the reason behind the rule, so the rule feels fair. Keep that tone while you add the missing details.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether these are expandable accordion items or static text, and whether the page has its navigation and order links (cut out of this capture).
