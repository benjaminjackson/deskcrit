**First read.** The eye goes straight to the illustration: red cap, red band and yellow lamp room against pale sky. It then crosses to the bold serif headline and settles into three calm paragraphs. It reads as a page or feature section about a heritage lighthouse, and it feels quiet, warm and well made, like a good guidebook.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page or section for a local lighthouse that should make a reader want to visit, and tell them how.

**Verdict.** As a piece of reading it works. Hierarchy, measure and image–text pairing are all sound. But as a page meant to bring visitors it stops short: everything someone needs in order to go is in the last two prose paragraphs, and it's vague. A reader who is sold has nothing to act on.

**What would move it most**

1. **The visit details are buried and incomplete (defect).** The opening hours, ticket point and the reason to climb are spread across paragraphs 2 and 3. They're written in the same voice and weight as the history, so someone scanning for "can I go, when, how much" has to read everything to find them (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: pages get scanned, not read). When they do find them, they can't act:
   - "Summer weekends": which months, and what hours?
   - "Tickets are sold at the cottage": at what price, and are there limits on who can climb 90 steps?
   - Where is the car park?

   Those are the lines a visitor plans a trip around and holds you to. "Summer weekends" can mean June to August or Easter to September, and the wrong guess is a wasted drive.

   **Fix:** keep the two story paragraphs as they are. Below them, add a short block set apart by space or a small label, with the hard facts as one line each: season and hours, price, where to buy, where to park. Then add one specific next step, such as "Plan your climb" or "See this summer's opening days", rather than leaving the page to end on scones.

2. **The best line isn't working as the hook (taste).** "Climb to the gallery on a clear morning and you can follow the ferry all the way across the bay to the harbour wall" is the one sentence that makes a reader want to go. It speaks to them ("you") about something they'll see for themselves. Right now it's the middle sentence of paragraph 2, after a timer and a schedule. Move it up, for example to open paragraph 2 or as a standfirst under the headline, so the reason to visit arrives before the logistics.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 1 ends on "room." alone on its last line. Rewrap or edit the sentence so the line carries at least two words.
- The tower in the illustration stands straight in the water with no rock or shore, and its base sits exactly on the waterline. That tangency makes it look pasted on. Give it ground, or overlap it into the wave.
- The lamp room is a flat yellow block with no gallery. Since the copy invites readers to "climb to the gallery", drawing a gallery rail would tie picture and text together.
- About 300 px of empty white sits below the content, and it reads as leftover, not as a choice. If this is the whole page rather than the top of a longer one, centre the block vertically or give it a footer to sit on.

**What's working.**
- **Image–text alignment.** The image top lines up with the headline and its bottom with the last line of text, so the two halves read as one unit. Keep that when you add the visit block: let the image keep that edge, or extend the grid on purpose.
- **Type.** Two sizes with one clear step, a bold serif headline over a readable text serif, lines of roughly 65 to 75 characters, and generous leading. It's easy, pleasant reading, and it needs no extra sizes. Do the visit block with weight or a small label, not a third size.
- **Voice and palette.** "The light at Gull Point" is evocative, not generic. The muted sea blues with a single warm red-and-yellow accent suit a calm heritage site, and they point the eye at the tower.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether this is the full page or the top of a scroll. That decides whether the empty space below and the missing visit info are real gaps, so check the live page.
