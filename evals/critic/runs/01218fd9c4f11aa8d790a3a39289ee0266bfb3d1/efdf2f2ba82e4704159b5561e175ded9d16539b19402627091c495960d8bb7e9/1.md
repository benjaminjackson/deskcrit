**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then hits a solid grey block of 16 lines with no break in it. It reads as a plain information page for a local river ferry: clean and calm, but heavy going. Nothing in the block stands out, so you can't jump to the one fact you came for, such as when the last boat leaves.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells ferry passengers how to use the service (times, boarding, bicycles, tickets). People will skim it for one answer, often while they're on their way to the landing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The type is tidy, but everything is in one undivided paragraph, and the facts people act on (times, the last crossing, tickets) are vague or buried. A passenger who needs one answer has to read the whole thing.

**What would move it most**

1. **The whole page is one block of text** (defect). Timetable, boarding order, bicycle rules, tickets and winter changes all run together in one 16-line paragraph. Someone who wants "when's the last boat?" has to read it top to bottom, and the most important line ("the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk") is near the bottom, mid-sentence. People skim text on screens, and a long unbroken block is where they give up (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). **Fix:** split it into short sections under subheads that match what people ask: *Timetable*, *Boarding*, *Bicycles*, *Tickets*, *Winter*. Turn the bicycle rules (wait at the gate, lower deck, lock to the rail, bags stay on, keep the stairs clear) into a list, because they are separate instructions.

2. **The facts people act on are vague or can be read two ways** (defect, in the copy).
   - "Between early morning and late evening" and "before dusk" give no clock times. A passenger can't plan a return trip from either. Dusk moves by hours across the winter.
   - "The last crossing leaves the quay": does that mean only the market quay, or the north landing too? If someone reads it the wrong way, they're stranded on the wrong bank.
   - "In winter" has no months.
   - "A season pass covers any number of crossings for a month": a calendar month, or 30 days from when you buy it? People will hold the operator to whichever reading they took.
   - There are no ticket prices at all.

   **Fix:** give real first and last departure times from each landing, name the winter months, and say exactly how long the pass lasts. Put the timetable first, because that's what most people came for. If the times change too often, say so and point to where the current times are, instead of leaving the times out.

**Smaller things.**
- The column hugs the left, and roughly half the canvas to the right is empty. That's fine for reading, but the page looks unfinished. Either centre the column or use the right side for a timetable table or a "last boat" box (taste).
- "Notes for ferry passengers" is generic. Naming the route ("North landing ⇄ Market quay: passenger guide") tells people they're on the right page.
- "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter" sends web readers to paper. If there's a timetable to give, put it on this page.

**What's working.**
- Body text is a comfortable size and dark enough to read easily. Lines run about 60–70 characters, which is in Bringhurst's 45–75 range. Keep the measure and leading when you split the text into sections.
- The type scale is two clear levels: a bold headline over regular body text. Subheads can go in as a third level (body size or a little larger, bold) without crowding it.
- The tone is plain and direct, and it already uses the imperative ("Lock bicycles…", "Leave the stairs clear…"), so it will turn into a list easily.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where people read this on the way to the landing.
