**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline, then drops to the larger left paragraph, a short, vivid piece about tide, varnish and cedar. The right column reads like a footnote because its type is smaller and lighter. Below the text, the bottom half of the frame is empty white. It feels calm, literate and warm, like a page from a club newsletter.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or newsletter section for a community boat shed, meant to set the scene and recruit volunteers. Correct me if recruiting isn't the point.

**Verdict.** The writing and the type do a lot of good work, but the layout puts its weight on the wrong column. The only part a reader can act on, the volunteer invitation, is set smallest and has no time, place or contact. So the piece charms people and then gives them nothing to do.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hierarchy is the wrong way round.** *Defect.* The left column (scene-setting) is set at roughly 22 px with generous leading. The right column ("Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings…") is set at roughly 18 px with tighter leading. A reader assumes the smaller text is secondary and may never read it, yet it is the one paragraph that turns a reader into a volunteer. Fix: if recruiting is the goal, give the invitation equal or greater weight than the description. Set both columns at one body size and pull the invitation out with weight, a short subhead ("Come and help") or its position, not by shrinking the other column.

2. **The invitation is missing the facts people need to show up.** *Defect, in the copy.* "Thursday evenings" has no time. "The end of the lane" doesn't say which lane, and nothing says how to get in touch or whether to just turn up. A stranger who wants to come can't, without finding out more somewhere else, and most won't bother. The paragraph is lovely ("a spare apron on the hook by the door" is exactly the right detail to lower the barrier). It just never says the one concrete thing to do. Fix: add one plain line that names the ask, for example "Come by any Thursday from 6 pm, [address], no need to book." Make that line the most findable thing in the right column.

3. **Two body sizes that are too close together.** *Convention.* I count three sizes: a headline at about 56 px and two body sizes at about 22 and 18 px. The two body sizes are close enough to look like an accident, not a choice (Bringhurst; Refactoring UI says to separate levels with weight or colour before adding another size). Their different leadings also stop the lines of the two columns from sitting on shared baselines. Each column's first line roughly matches the other, then they drift (typography.md, "Lines of text align across columns"). Fix: use one body size and one leading for both columns, so the lines run across on the same baselines.

4. **The empty lower half looks left over, not designed.** *Taste, tied to the job.* About half the frame below the text is blank, so the piece looks unfinished, or like a screenshot of a page that didn't load. White space works when it is shaped on purpose (Ruder: the printed and unprinted areas should be in tension). Here it is just the space after the content runs out. Fix: either crop the frame to the content, or give the space a job. A photograph of the shed or the dinghies would carry the "tar and cedar" mood better than any extra copy, and could hold the call to action.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "Notes from the boat shed" works as a column title but doesn't say where or whose shed. If this page stands alone, add the place, either in the headline or in a small line above it.

**What's working.**
- The left-column copy is concrete and sensory (clinker dinghies, trestles, tar and cut cedar). It makes the place real in four sentences. Keep that voice.
- The type choice is good: a sturdy text serif with a bold cut of the same family for the headline. It gives one coherent, readable voice with a clear headline-to-body jump. Line lengths in both columns look comfortably within about 45–75 characters.
- Margins are generous, and both columns hang cleanly off a shared top line under the headline. The grid is there; it just needs one body setting to carry it.
