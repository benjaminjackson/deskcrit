**First read.** My eye goes to "River ferry timetable", then straight into the heavy black grid. The grid is the loudest thing on the page, louder than the times. It reads as a plain, competent bus-stop timetable: calm and legible, but boxy. Then I hit the "Runs" column and have to go back up the list to work out which sailings actually run today.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a public timetable, on a web page or printed at the landing, for riders who want to know when the next ferry leaves today.

**Verdict.** It's legible and honest, but the table doesn't answer the one question riders bring to it: "what's the next boat *today*?" To answer it, they have to cross-check every row against a "Runs" column sitting far off to the right. Second, the near-black rule around every cell draws attention away from the times.

**What would move it most**

1. **Which days a sailing runs is buried in the last column** (defect). Seven sailings mix three patterns: Daily, Weekdays (12:40, 17:10) and Weekends (19:30). Someone on a Saturday has to read every row across to "Runs" and mentally strike out two of them. The answer they need sits about 600 px away from the time it qualifies. It's easy to miss that 17:10 doesn't run Saturday, or that 19:30 doesn't run Tuesday. Then they're stranded on the quay. Make the day pattern visible right beside the time. Two ways to do it: two departure columns, Mon–Fri and Sat–Sun, with a dash where there's no sailing, or two short tables. Either way the rider reads one column top to bottom and gets their day's service.

2. **Every cell is locked in a heavy black box** (convention: Lupton, *Thinking with Type*; Tufte, ch. 4). The 2 px near-black rules on every row and column carry no information, and they are darker than the content. The page reads as a grid of compartments rather than a list of times. Remove the vertical rules and either thin the horizontal ones to a pale grey or replace them with a light tint on alternate rows. Lupton: rules should "illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell."

3. **"Spring season" gives no dates** (defect, and it's on a line riders act on). Without start and end dates, nobody can tell whether this table applies on the day they travel, or when it stops applying. Put the actual date range in the subtitle, e.g. "Valid 1 March – 31 May".

**Smaller things**
- The "Period" column repeats each label twice and only says what the clock already says. Either drop it, or make it a group label above each pair of rows so it isn't repeated in every row.
- The columns are spread across about 1,080 px for content a few characters wide, so the departure and arrival times sit far apart. Size the columns to their content so each row reads as one line.
- The table is labelled only for one direction. If a return service exists, a rider coming back from the quay will look for it here. If it doesn't exist, say so ("Return times: see …").
- The journey time is the same on every row (12 min). Saying "Crossing takes 12 minutes" once would let the arrival column shrink in visual weight or go entirely.
- Check that the times use tabular figures and are right-aligned, so the column of digits lines up (Bringhurst 4.4.3). I can't tell from this capture.

**What's working**
- The type scale is clean: three sizes (title, grey subtitle, table text), with bold carrying the headers rather than another size. Keep it.
- The column headers are plain and use verbs ("Leaves north", "Arrives quay"), so each one says what happens, not a category. Keep that voice when you add the day columns.
- 24-hour times with leading zeros remove any AM/PM ambiguity.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- How the table reflows on a phone. Four wide boxed columns are likely to overflow or squash, so check it at around 375 px wide.
