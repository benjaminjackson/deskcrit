**First read.** The eye goes to the bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", and that part reads cleanly. Then it drops into three grey blocks where the lines of text sit on top of each other: descenders hit the ascenders of the line below, and some words get clipped. You have to stop and pick each line apart. The page feels broken more than unfinished. Under the paragraphs, the whole right half and bottom half of the canvas are empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a passenger information page (web or a printed notice) that tells riders when the ferry runs, how bikes work and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** It doesn't work. The body text has a line height below its font size, so the lines overlap and the paragraphs that carry all the information can barely be read. Behind that, the copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on: actual times.

**What would move it most**
1. **The lines of text overlap. Defect.** This is in all three paragraphs. The space from one line to the next is smaller than the type itself: in the first paragraph, "every" runs into "forty minutes" and "takes about" collides with the line above. A reader has to work out each line before they can read it, and someone glancing at it on a phone or at the quay will give up. Body text needs leading above the type size, roughly 1.3–1.5× for text this size (Bringhurst; Butterick, *Practical Typography*). Check the CSS for a `line-height` below 1 or a fixed pixel value smaller than the font size. Once that's fixed, put a clear paragraph gap between blocks. At the moment the gap between paragraphs looks about the same as a normal line space would, so the three topics will blur together.
2. **The facts a passenger acts on are vague. Defect.** "Between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "one boat each hour" are what people plan around, and none of them gives a time. Someone reading this can't tell whether they'll catch the last boat home. They'll take a guess, and if it's wrong they're stranded. Give the first and last departure times for summer and winter, and say which months count as "winter". Make the times the most visible text after the headline. A small timetable or two lines of times will scan faster than prose.
3. **Nothing on the page can be scanned. Taste, with a practical reason.** The page is three paragraphs of the same weight and size with no subheads. Readers come to a notice like this with one question ("can I take my bike?", "when's the last boat?") and can't jump to the answer (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Add a short label to each block, such as "Times", "Bicycles" and "Winter". That gives a three-level scale: headline, label, body.

**Smaller things.**
- The text block is about 75–80 characters wide, at the top of the comfortable range (Bringhurst: 45–75). Narrow it a little when you fix the leading.
- Fares for foot passengers aren't mentioned, only that bikes cost nothing extra, which suggests there's a fare somewhere. Say what it costs or where to find out.
- "A little longer when the tide turns against the boat" doesn't say how much longer. Give a rough figure ("up to 20 minutes").
- "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" is ambiguous: does it mean leave your bags on the bike, or don't bring them up to the passenger deck? Say what the rider should do.

**What's working.**
- The headline is clear and set at a good size and weight against the body. That step works, so keep it.
- The copy is plain and friendly in tone. Once the times are added it will read like a person explaining the service, which suits a local ferry.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the overlap shows up at every viewport width or only this one, and whether a web font loading late changed the line metrics. Check it in a live browser at a few widths.
