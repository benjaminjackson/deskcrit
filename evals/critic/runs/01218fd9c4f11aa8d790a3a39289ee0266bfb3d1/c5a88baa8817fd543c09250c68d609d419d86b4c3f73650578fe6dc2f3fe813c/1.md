**First read.** The eye goes first to the bold "Landing stage rebuild" headline, then to the blue "Sign up" button, and then straight to the identical blue rounded bar in the illustration. It reads as a ferry operator's news page with a timetable sign-up in a sidebar. It feels calm, plain and orderly, and close to a default template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a ferry operator's update page that tells passengers about the rebuilt landing and gets them to sign up for timetable change notices.

**Verdict.** The article half works. The sign-up, which is the only thing the page asks the viewer to do, doesn't work: it never asks where to send the notes, so as drawn it can't deliver what it promises.

**What would move it most**

1. **The form has no way to reach the subscriber.** The "Get timetable changes" card asks only for "Your name" and "Home landing", then says "We send one short note when the timetable changes." A note sent where? Someone who fills in both fields and presses "Sign up" has given you nothing to contact them on. Either they notice the gap and give up, or they sign up and never hear anything. This is a **defect** in the page's one conversion. Fix: add the contact field the note goes to (email, or phone if it's a text) and say which in the helper line: "We email you once when the timetable changes." Then ask whether "Your name" is needed at all. Every extra field costs sign-ups, and a timetable alert doesn't need one.

2. **The blue bar in the illustration looks like a second button.** In Figure 1, the rounded rectangle at the right of the waterline uses the same blue, the same corner radius and roughly the same proportions as "Sign up". That blue exists nowhere else on the page except that button. So the picture's most saturated shape reads as something to click, and it pulls the eye away from the real button. Things that don't do anything shouldn't look interactive (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, affordances). This is a **defect**. Fix: draw the boat in a colour from the illustration's own muted palette, or give it a shape that reads as a hull, and keep the brand blue for the one action.

3. **"Home landing" is an open text box for what is really a short fixed list.** People will type "North", "north landing", "N. Landing" or misspell it, and either the system can't match the answer or it fails quietly. Inputs should accept only valid answers (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 4, constraints). This is a **convention**, and breaking it costs accurate alerts here. Fix: use a dropdown or radio list of the landings. If the label means "the landing I want alerts for", say that. Even "Your landing" would be clearer than "Home landing".

**Smaller things.**
- Copy: "Get timetable changes" names a category, not what happens. "Get a note when sailing times change" tells the viewer what they're signing up for.
- Figure 1's caption describes the scene, but the picture is grey blocks, so it shows no ramp, benches or kiosk. Either show the ramps meeting the deck, which is the claim that matters to pram and wheelchair users, or drop the figure.
- The key fact for many readers, "ramps now meet the deck at every tide", sits last, under the figure. Move it up into the opening paragraph.
- There's a large empty band between the last paragraph and the footer note. That's fine if the page really ends there, but it makes the article look unfinished.
- The grey caption, form labels and helper text look small and light. Measure their contrast against 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3).

**What's working.**
- The type scale is tight and clear. I count about four sizes (headline, card heading, body, small grey labels), each with its own job and no near-duplicates. Keep it.
- The labels sit above the fields, stay visible, and sit closer to their own field than to the one above. The form reads in one clean column.
- The body copy is concrete and speaks to the passenger: "prams and wheelchairs roll straight on" lands the benefit on the reader. Keep writing like that.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- What "Sign up" does on submit (confirmation, errors, required-field handling), and how the sidebar card reflows on a phone, where it should probably follow the article or sit just under the key fact.
