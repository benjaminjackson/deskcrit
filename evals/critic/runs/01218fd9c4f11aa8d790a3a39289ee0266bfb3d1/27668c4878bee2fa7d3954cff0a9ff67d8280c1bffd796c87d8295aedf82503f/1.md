I've read the brief and the image and checked the typeface entries in the references. Here is the critique.

**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans headline, then hits a wall of heavy brush script. It slows to a crawl, word by word, on lines like "minutes between early morning and late evening." The page reads as a practical notice for ferry passengers, but it looks like a café chalkboard or a wedding favour tag. It's friendly in tone and hard to read.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a passenger information page (web or print notice) that someone reads quickly to find out when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. Every fact a passenger needs is set in a connected display script that is slow and tiring to read in paragraphs. The typeface is the problem, and nothing else matters until that's fixed.

**What would move it most**

1. **The body text is set in a display script.** *Defect.* This is all three paragraphs, about 110 words. The script's letters join, slant and change shape, so the reader has to decode each word instead of recognising it. Words like "timetable thins," "lower deck" and "twelve minutes" take a second look, and older readers, low-vision readers and anyone reading off a phone on a windy landing will skip it or misread it. A script like this is made for a few words at a large size, not for running text. The heading and body are in clear contrast, so this isn't a conflict in Dair's sense (*Design with Type*, ch. 8). The roles are simply inverted: the plain, legible face is on the six-word title and the hard-to-read face is on the information. **Fix:** set the body in a plain text face, either the headline's sans in its regular weight or a sturdy serif. If you want the warmth of the script, give it at most a few words, such as a short tagline, and never the times or rules.

2. **The facts are buried in prose and some are vague.** *Defect* for this job. A passenger wants three answers: when does it run, how long does it take, and when is the last boat. Here those answers sit mid-sentence. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last departure. "Before dusk" in winter gives no clock time, so a passenger can't tell whether 4:30 is safe. "A little longer" for crossings against the tide gives no amount. The page also sends you to a printed timetable "in the shelter at each landing," which you only see once you're already there. **Fix:** pull the schedule out as scannable lines: frequency, first and last sailing, crossing time, and winter hours with an actual last-departure time. Keep the prose for the bicycle rule. If exact times change, say where to check them before travelling, not only at the landing.

**Smaller things.**
- Line spacing in the body looks tight for a face with such tall loops and long descenders. In "lower deck" and "after foot passengers" the descenders nearly reach the line below. This becomes moot once the face changes, but give the new face enough leading for its measure (Bringhurst, §2.2.1).
- At about 70–75 characters per line, the measure is at the top of the comfortable range (Bringhurst, §2.1.2). That's fine for a sans at this size; just don't let it widen.
- The column sits in the top-left with roughly 40% of the width and the lower third of the frame empty. That's acceptable for a web page, but on a printed notice it looks unfinished. Size the content to the format.

**What's working.**
- The headline is clear and plainly worded, and it's one obvious step above the body in both size and weight. Keep it.
- The type scale is disciplined: two sizes, each with an obvious job.
- The bicycle paragraph says who does what and in what order ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). That's the standard the other paragraphs should meet.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The medium. If this is a web page, check how the column reflows on a phone, where most passengers will read it.
