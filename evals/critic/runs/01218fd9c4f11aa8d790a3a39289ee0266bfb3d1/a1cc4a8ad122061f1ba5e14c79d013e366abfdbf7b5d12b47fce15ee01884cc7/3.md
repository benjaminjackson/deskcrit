**First read.** The eye goes straight to the white serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops to the search field, and then moves down into a tidy 4×2 grid of topic cards. It reads at once as a software company's help centre: calm, competent and trustworthy. It also feels anonymous. Nothing on the screen tells you what Harbor is or what its users usually get stuck on.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing page of a product help centre, where a customer with a problem should find the answer, or the right section, in as few steps as possible.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clean, the conventions are right, and the craft is careful. What holds it back is that the page only sends people somewhere else. The search field doesn't look as clearly like a search as it should, and the grid organises topics without answering a single question. A customer who arrives with a problem always needs at least two more clicks.

**What would move it most**

1. **The search field undersells itself.** The white bar in the hero (360–920 px) has no magnifier icon and no submit button. Its placeholder, "Search for articles", is the most generic line it could carry. It still reads as a field, because white on navy is strong. But a search box is one of the patterns people recognise by its icon, and without one it looks a little like a banner or a text block (Jakob's law, Nielsen). *Convention.* Fix: add the magnifier at the left and make the field a little taller so it clearly owns the hero. Then give the placeholder a real example from the user's world, such as "e.g. reset my password, download an invoice". That tells people what kind of question works and what this product's help covers.

2. **The grid sends people away but never answers anything.** Each of the eight cards shows a title and a one-line scope, so every visit costs a click into a category and then another into an article. Most traffic to a help centre goes to a handful of questions ("reset password", "where's my order", "cancel plan"). Nothing above the fold puts those in front of the customer (Nielsen 10 and Krug: don't make people hunt). *Taste, argued:* list two or three of the most-read articles under each card's description as direct links, or add a "Popular answers" row above the grid. Either one turns the first screen from a directory into an answer page for most visitors.

3. **The eight cards are all equal, in an order that has no visible reason.** "Troubleshooting" is probably the card most people in trouble want, and it sits last, bottom right, where the eye reaches it last. Every card has the same weight, so the grid reads as a table of contents and not as a set of priorities. *Taste.* Fix: order the cards by how often they're used (check the analytics). If you want one card to stand out, give it the first slot, not a louder style.

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: the logo (~20 px bold), "Browse by topic" (~18 px bold) and the card titles (~16 px bold) sit only a couple of pixels apart, so they look like three near-identical levels. Make the section label clearly smaller with muted colour, or clearly larger. Don't leave it in between.
- Copy: "How can we help?" plus "Browse a topic or search the guides" could sit on any company's help page. The subline just describes the page below it. Drop it, or use it to say something specific, such as where to go if you're locked out.
- Topic mix: "Orders and shipping" (physical goods) next to "workspace", "Integrations" and "calendars" (SaaS) makes a stranger ask what Harbor actually sells. If both are real, a word in the hero would set the right mental model.
- Card descriptions: "How we protect and store your data" and "Choose which alerts you get and where" each wrap onto a short second line. Trim them to one line or let the cards be a little wider so the rag is less ragged.
- The cards have no signifier that the whole card is clickable (no chevron, no link styling). That's fine if hover handles it, but it's worth confirming.

**What's working.**
- One clear focal point and a clean path through the page: headline, then search, then grid. The navy band holds the hero against the pale-grey body without needing any extra decoration.
- The serif headline set against the sans-serif UI text is a contrast of structure that clearly reads as deliberate (Dair). It gives the page a little character without making it noisy. Keep it.
- The icon set is consistent: one stroke weight, one level of detail and one colour. The card grid keeps even gutters on a single column structure that lines up with the logo and the "Browse by topic" label at the left margin.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the cards, the nav links and the search field. Check that the search field has a visible focus ring against the navy.
- Whether any popular articles, a contact route or a footer sit below the fold. If they do, part of finding 2 may already be handled, but it would still belong above the fold.
- How the 4-column grid reflows on mobile.
