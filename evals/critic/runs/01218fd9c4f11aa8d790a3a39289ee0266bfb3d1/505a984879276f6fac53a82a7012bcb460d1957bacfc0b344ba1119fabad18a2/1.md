**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, moves down two plain paragraphs, and stops at the grey box, which is the only shaped thing on the page. It reads as a calm, tidy notice from a ferry operator or a council page. It's quiet and competent, but nothing on the page tells you which line matters most.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a winter service notice that riders scan to learn when the ferry runs, whether it might be held, and what they'll pay.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and readable, but its one piece of emphasis is on the wrong content. The grey box highlights the fares, while the ice-delay warning, which is the reason this winter page exists, sits as an ordinary paragraph. The schedule is also written in words, so it's hard to scan and can be read more than one way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The emphasis is on the wrong paragraph (defect).** The tinted box puts the bicycle and van fares forward. The ice paragraph is the one that changes what a rider does: the boat may be held, so check the board first. As it stands, a skimming rider takes in the fare note and can miss the warning. The fix is to swap them. Put the ice warning in the box (or in a stronger warning style) and make the fares ordinary text. The box should mark the exception, not the routine.
2. **The timetable is prose with gaps in it (defect).** "Every forty minutes… first crossing is at seven in the morning and the last at six in the evening" can't be scanned, and it leaves out things a rider acts on. Do the times apply only to departures from the north landing? When is the last boat back from the other side? Is "six" the last departure, or the last chance to board? Is that the same on weekends? A rider who reads "last at six" as the return crossing gets stranded. Write the times as numerals (07:00, 18:00, every 40 min), and say which landing each time applies to, along with the last return. A small two-row table (north to south, south to north) would answer all of it at a glance.
3. **The fares line can be read two ways (defect).** "Bicycles ride free on weekdays" leaves weekend cyclists guessing whether they pay full fare or a lower rate. "Cars pay the standard fare" gives no amount. Both are lines riders hold the operator to later. State the weekend bicycle rule, and give the fare or link to it.

**Smaller things.**
- **Body lines run too long.** Body lines run to about 100–105 characters, against a 45–75 target (Bringhurst). Narrow the text column to about 640–680 px, or raise the type size.
- **The box padding is uneven.** There looks to be about 24 px above the text and about 55 px below, so the box looks like content went missing or the box has a fixed height. Make the padding equal on all sides.
- **"Check the board at the ticket hut" assumes local knowledge.** Say what the board shows, for example "the board shows whether the next crossing is held."
- **The headline promises more than the page delivers.** "How the river ferry runs in winter" sounds like it covers the whole service. Something like "Winter ferry times and ice delays" tells the reader up front what they'll find.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is restrained.** There are two sizes, a bold headline and regular body text, with a clear step between them and nothing that clashes. Keep it.
- **Dark text on white.** Contrast is high and the spacing between paragraphs is comfortable. The page is easy to read once you're in it.
- **A soft tint as the highlight.** A light tint with a gentle radius is the right strength of emphasis for a page this quiet. It just needs to sit on the right paragraph.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't tell whether the empty lower half of the frame is the end of the page or a capture of a page that hadn't finished loading. If the page has more content (fares, a map, live status), check it in a browser.
