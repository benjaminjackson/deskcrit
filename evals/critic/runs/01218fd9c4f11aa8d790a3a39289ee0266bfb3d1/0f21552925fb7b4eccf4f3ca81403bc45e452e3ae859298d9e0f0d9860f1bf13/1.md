**First read.** The eye lands on "Plan the whole trip in one place", drops to the subhead, then stops at two green buttons and has to work out which one to press. It reads as a travel-planning startup's landing page: calm, tidy, cream and dark green. The right half of the screen and the bottom third are empty, so the page feels finished on the left and unfinished overall.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a marketing landing page, and its job is to get someone who plans group trips to start using Wayfold.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are competent, but the hero does not do its job. Its one decision point offers two buttons that mean the same thing. Its words are generic, and the one line that makes Wayfold different is set in the smallest text.

**What would move it most**

1. **Two buttons for one action.** "Get started" (filled) and "Start now" (outlined), below the subhead, are synonyms. The viewer stops to look for a difference that isn't there, wonders whether one is a trial and one is a purchase, and the second button weakens the first. This is a **defect**: Nielsen heuristic 8, and Krug's "get rid of half the words". Keep one button, or give the second a different job, such as "See how it works" or "See a sample trip", so a viewer who isn't ready to commit has somewhere to go.

2. **The headline could be on anyone's page, and the real idea is hidden in the subhead.** Many travel and itinerary tools could say "Plan the whole trip in one place". What sets Wayfold apart is in the subhead: a *group* can *agree* on a trip without a dozen chat threads. That is the pain the viewer recognises, and it is set at body size in grey. The ask has the same problem. "Get started" doesn't say what happens when you click, and nothing on the page lets the viewer judge the value for themselves. The page asks them to take "in one place" on trust. This is a **defect** in what the page has to do. Put the group-agreement promise in the headline, ideally set against what they use now (the chat thread). Make the button name the specific thing they will do, for example "Start a trip and invite your group". If a product visual belongs on the right, it should show that comparison: one shared plan with the group's votes, instead of a chat log.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "Plan the whole trip in / one place", leaving "in" hanging at the end of the line. Break it as "Plan the whole trip / in one place" so each line is a whole phrase.
- "Destinations" in the nav suggests a booking or inspiration site, which muddies what Wayfold is. Consider "Features" or "For groups", unless destination content really is a main path.
- The nav links are small, mid-grey, and lighter than the body text. They look like they pass contrast, but check them against 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3).

**What's working.**
- **One strong left edge.** The logo, headline, subhead, and buttons all hang from the same left margin, so the column reads as one deliberate unit. Keep it.
- **A clean type scale.** Nav, body, and headline are three clearly separate levels. The headline gets its weight from size *and* boldness, so nothing collides.
- **A restrained palette.** The dark green is used only for the logo and the actions, so it already signals "you can click this". Don't spend it on decoration when you add content.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I can't tell whether the empty right half and lower third are intended, or a hero image or illustration that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken. Check in a live browser. If they're empty by design, the space is leftover rather than shaped, and the page shows nothing of the product.
