**First read.** Your eye lands on the big serif quote and stays there. It's warm and has a human voice, and it reads like a feature opener in a local newsletter. Next the eye drops to the bold attribution, and the grey paragraph comes last. After the quote, my first thought was "why is *old* in quotes?" The page never answers that.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the lead story of a library's email or web newsletter, telling regular patrons that the reading room reopens and making them want to come.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound and it feels right for a library. Two things hold it back. First, the quote can't be understood without facts the page leaves out. Second, the actual news (it reopens Monday) is the quietest line on the page. Third, at this size the typewriter punctuation is the first craft flaw anyone will see.

**What would move it most**

1. **The quote's point depends on a name the reader is never told. Defect.** The quote says "It's the 'old' reading room – nobody calls it anything else." A stranger reading it has to wonder what else people are supposed to call it. Was it renamed? Is the "new" room somewhere else? The single quotes around *old* signal that this is a joke or a quarrel, but the setup isn't on the page, so it falls flat and the biggest thing on the page reads as a puzzle. The fix is to give the official name in the line under the quote, e.g. "...for thirty years. Officially it's now the [X] Room." Then the quote reads as warm stubbornness, which is what it's meant to be.

2. **The news is the faintest text on the page. Defect.** "Reopens on Monday" is the one fact a reader would act on, and it sits in the smallest, lightest grey line, below a bold attribution that outweighs it. Someone skimming the email gets the quote and the volunteer and misses the reopening. "Monday" also has no date, so if the newsletter is read late or forwarded, it can be read as two different Mondays and someone turns up a week late. Fix: give the date in full ("Monday 2 December"), and make the reopening line at least as strong as the attribution. Either darken it to the same ink colour, or swap the weights so the attribution goes regular and the news gets the emphasis.

3. **Straight quotes, a hyphen used as a dash, and a quote mark that doesn't hang, all at about 90 px. Convention for the dash, common sense and taste for the rest.** At body size these would be nitpicks. At this size they are the most obvious marks on the page:
   - The `"` and `'` are straight typewriter marks, not curly quotes. They make a crafted serif headline look like it was pasted in from a plain-text field. No source I can check covers this one, so call it common sense: curly quotes are what a printed quotation looks like.
   - "room - nobody" uses a hyphen with spaces where it needs an en dash with spaces or an em dash (Bringhurst, 5.2.1).
   - The opening `"` sits inside the text edge, so "It's" starts to the right of "room", "anything", the kicker and the body copy. The left edge looks dented exactly where the eye first lands. Hang the mark out into the margin so the letters line up (taste; it's an old display convention, but I have no checked source for it).

**Smaller things.**
- The display lines look set at text spacing. "reading" and "nobody calls it" feel slightly loose for this size, so tighten the tracking and word spacing a little (Bringhurst 2.1.1; Hochuli).
- The trailing comma after "else," hangs off a very big line with nothing after it. It's grammatically right because the attribution finishes the sentence, but the attribution is in a different face and much smaller, so the hand-off is weak. Bring the attribution closer under the quote, or end the quote with a full stop and set the credit as a separate byline ("— a volunteer who has…").
- There's no next step: no hours, no link, no "come and find the puzzle table". If this is the lead story, give the reader one specific thing to do.

**What's working.**
- The pairing of a big serif quote, a sans for supporting text and a rust small-caps kicker on warm paper. It feels like a library and not a template, so keep it.
- Leading with a person's voice and their thirty years of stamping books is the right instinct. It gives the reopening a reason to care that a plain notice wouldn't.
- The type scale is tight: four sizes with clear jobs (kicker, display, attribution, body). Body lines run about 55 characters, which is comfortable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the display quote reflows on a phone-width email. At this size it may break into six or more lines and push the reopening news below the fold, which would make finding 2 worse.
