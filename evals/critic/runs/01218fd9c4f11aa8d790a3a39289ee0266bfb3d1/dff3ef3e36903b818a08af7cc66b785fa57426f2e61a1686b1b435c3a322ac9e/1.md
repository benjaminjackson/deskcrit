**First read.** My eye goes straight to "Changing your pickup time" and then steps down the four bold headings like a table of contents. Within a second it reads as a help or FAQ block for a bakery's online ordering. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. At the bottom is a stray line, "Design review: failed, the headings are impossible to pick out". I've treated that as text in the image, not as evidence. What I see contradicts it: the headings are the easiest thing on the page to find.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is a help section on a bakery's order page, for customers who have already ordered and want a quick answer about changing, collecting or cancelling.

**Verdict.** The layout works. You can scan the headings, each answer sits clearly under its question, and nothing competes with them. What's actually in the way is the copy. The two answers people will act on and hold the bakery to, cancelling and collecting for someone else, can each be read two ways, and two answers don't tell you how to do the thing they describe.

**What would move it most**

1. **The cancellation deadline is ambiguous** (fourth block, "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead"). *Defect.* "A day ahead" of what, and how is it counted? One customer reads it as 24 hours before the pickup slot. Another reads it as any time the day before. Someone picking up at 9 am who cancels at 8 pm the night before is fine under the second reading and too late under the first. A deadline is exactly what people hold you to, so this one invites disputes. The block also never says *how* to cancel. Fix: state the cutoff against the pickup time ("up to 24 hours before your pickup time") and add the action ("Open your order and choose Cancel").

2. **"Along with a name" doesn't say whose name** (second block). *Defect.* It could be the collector's name or the name on the order. Get it wrong and someone stands at the counter unable to collect the bread. It's also unclear whether "it" means the order number alone or the number plus the name. Fix: say it in full, e.g. "They'll need to show your order number and give the name the order is under." If you want the collector's ID, say that instead.

3. **The allergy answer only helps someone already in the shop** (third block). *Defect, for this audience.* Someone ordering online needs to know what's in a loaf *before* they order. "A card by the till" tells them they can only find out once they arrive. For someone with a real allergy, that means either ordering blind or not ordering at all. Fix: point to where ingredients are listed online (on each product, or a link). Keep the till card and "ask the baker" as a second step.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the full-width lines ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run to about 95 characters, past the 45–75 range Bringhurst gives. Narrowing the text column to about 640 px would keep lines within it.
- Headings vs body: the headings look about 32 px against 18 px body. That's a big jump for a short FAQ, so the headings feel like page titles. *Taste:* one size smaller, kept bold, would still lead the eye without shouting.
- First block: "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" is a fact with no consequence for the reader. End it on what it means for them, e.g. "…after that, your order may not be kept."
- Bottom line: if "Design review: failed…" is on the live page, remove it. It's internal text that customers shouldn't see.

**What's working.**
- Proximity. Each heading sits tight to its answer, and there's a clear wider gap before the next heading, so you never wonder which answer belongs to which question. Keep that spacing ratio when you revise.
- Headings written as the customer's own situation ("Collecting for someone else", "Cancelling an order"). People find their case by scanning, not reading. Keep this wording style.
- A restrained scale of two sizes, one weight jump and dark, high-contrast text. It's quiet and readable, which suits a help block.
