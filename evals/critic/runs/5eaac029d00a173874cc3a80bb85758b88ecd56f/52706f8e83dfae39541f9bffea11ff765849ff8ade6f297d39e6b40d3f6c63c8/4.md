**First read.** My eye goes straight to the big stacked-area chart at the bottom, because it's the only large block of colour. Next it jumps to the bold sidebar headings ("Also on Energy Explained", "Also on EIA.gov") rather than to the article's own heading. The 2025 sources chart at the top is small, washed out, and mostly empty space, so I skipped past it. Overall it reads as a reliable but dated government reference page: easy to trust, hard to take in quickly.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is an EIA "Energy Explained" page meant to tell general readers, students and journalists what the US generated its electricity from in 2025 and how that mix has changed.

**Verdict.** The text does its job. The charts are the weak part. The chart for 2025, the year the page is about, is a blurry picture of text that you can't read without clicking. The two charts also give the same fuels different colours. The headline number (58% fossil) is never shown in a picture you can read at a glance.

**What would move it most**

1. **The 2025 sources chart can't be read where it sits (defect).** The stacked bar drawn as a plug socket (upper left) is a low-resolution picture of text. The renewables breakdown box, the source line and the note look like 5–6 px type, and the faint socket drawing runs behind "nuclear 17.7%" and "coal 16.6%". It fills less than half the column width and leaves a dead white block on the right, so the viewer has to "Click to enlarge" to get the main fact of the page. This breaks WCAG's rule against images of text and its contrast minimums, and it's the opposite of Tufte's data-ink principle: the socket costs legibility and carries no data. Fix direction: rebuild it as a real chart, like the one below it, at full column width. Drop the socket metaphor. Group natural gas, coal and petroleum together so the 58% fossil share in the heading can be seen at once.

2. **The two charts give the same fuels different colours (defect).** At the top, nuclear is light blue, coal is pink and natural gas is grey-white. At the bottom, nuclear is red, natural gas is blue and coal is brown. A reader comparing the two will match blue to blue and read nuclear as natural gas. Fix: one colour per fuel across the whole page, and across all Energy Explained pages.

3. **The numbers in the text don't line up with the chart (defect).** The chart says "petroleum and other 0.7%". The text says petroleum alone was 0.7%, then that "other gases and other sources" were another 0.2%. A reader adding them up gets 0.9% and doesn't know which figure to trust. The scope also changes from sentence to sentence. The gas and "other" sentences say "utility-scale"; the coal, petroleum and heading sentences say "U.S. electricity generation". That leaves open whether 58% includes small-scale solar. The text also calls coal "third-highest" without ever naming second place (nuclear, which only appears in the chart). On a page whose whole purpose is figures, each of these makes the reader stop and check. Fix: make the chart and text categories match exactly, state the scope once and keep it, and name nuclear as second.

4. **The long-run chart shows the story but doesn't tell it (convention, off-list source).** The heading makes a point (58% fossil). The chart title only names a topic ("…by major energy source, 1950–2025"). The two events worth seeing are coal peaking around 2007 and natural gas overtaking coal around 2016. Neither is marked, so the reader has to find them alone. The legend sits below the x-axis, so every colour needs a look down and back up. The tan petroleum band and brown coal band are close in hue, and the red nuclear band sits right against the green renewables band, a pair that red-green colour-blind readers will struggle to tell apart. Fix direction: give it a title that states the finding, label each band directly at its 2025 end, and add one or two notes on the chart.

5. **The sidebar outranks the article (taste, backed by Refactoring UI's point about bold weight).** "Also on Energy Explained" and "Also on EIA.gov" look larger and heavier than the article's own heading ("Fossil fuels accounted for…"). The navigation therefore pulls the eye before the content does. I count about five text sizes on the page, and the sidebar headings and the main heading are close enough in size that they compete. Fix: make the content heading clearly the top level, and set the sidebar headings a step down.

**Smaller things**
- Body text lines look like about 90–95 characters, past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the text column or set the paragraphs a size up.
- The petroleum paragraph has 7 links, shown by colour alone (no underline). They break up the reading and fail the WCAG rule against colour as the only cue. Underline them, and keep only the links people will actually want.
- The current item in the sidebar ("▸Electricity in the United States") is pale grey and looks disabled. Show the current page with weight or a marker, not by fading it.
- The rules don't follow one system: a thick grey rule sits above each chart, but only the first chart has a thin rule below it.
- The charts use Arial-like type while the body uses a geometric sans. This is minor, but it makes the charts feel pasted in.
- The two "Click to enlarge" links with a magnifier icon are only there because the charts are too small. Fixing #1 removes the need for them.

**What's working**
- The section heading makes a claim instead of naming a topic ("Fossil fuels accounted for about 58%…"). Keep that approach, and apply it to the chart titles too.
- The stacked-area chart's band order matches its legend order and puts coal at the base. That makes coal's rise and fall the most readable shape on the page.
- The paragraphs are short and each starts with the fuel it covers, so the text is easy to skim.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- I couldn't crop the image to look at it at full resolution (Bash was blocked), so my judgments about the smallest text in the first chart come from the reduced view. Check that note text at its real size.
- Whether the Highcharts chart has hover values or a data table behind the ≡ menu. If not, the exact figures exist only in an image.
