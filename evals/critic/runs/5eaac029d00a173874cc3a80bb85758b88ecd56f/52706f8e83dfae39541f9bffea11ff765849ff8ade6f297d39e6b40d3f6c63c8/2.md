**First read.** The eye goes first to the bold heading "Fossil fuels accounted for about 58%…", then down to the large coloured area chart, which is the heaviest thing on the page. The small outlet-shaped chart at the top barely registers. It looks like a grey thumbnail with text too small to read. This reads as a government explainer page (EIA *Energy Explained*). It feels trustworthy and plain, a little dated, and dense with text.

**What it's for.** My assumption: it helps a general reader understand where U.S. electricity comes from, using a current snapshot and the long-term trend, with links for going deeper.

**Verdict.** The prose does its job. The two charts that should carry the page work against it. The 2025 snapshot is a shrunken picture of text that nobody can read in place. The two charts also give the same energy sources different colours, so a reader who learns the colours from one chart misreads the other.

**What would move it most**

1. **The two charts give the same sources different colours (defect).** In the top chart, nuclear is light blue, coal is pink and natural gas is grey. In the area chart, natural gas is blue, nuclear is red and coal is brown. A reader who learns "blue = nuclear" at the top will read the big blue band in the lower chart as nuclear growing since 2005, which is the wrong story. A colour should mean the same thing everywhere on a page (Nielsen heuristic 4, consistency and standards). **Fix:** use one colour per source across both charts, and across the site if possible.

2. **The 2025 snapshot is an image of text, shrunk until it can't be read (defect).** The chart sits at about half the column width, with empty white space to its right. Its segment labels look about 7 px tall. The renewables breakdown box (wind, hydro, solar, biomass, geothermal) and the five-line note and source block are a grey blur. A reader has to click "Click to enlarge" to get any number beyond the four big segments. This breaks WCAG 1.4.5 (images of text) and fails the readable-caption basics. **Fix:**
   - Rebuild the chart as live HTML or SVG at full column width.
   - Set the labels at a readable caption size.
   - Move the note into real caption text under the chart.

   The outlet-shaped outline and the plug-slot graphic under the "nuclear" and "natural gas" labels add nothing and make the labels harder to read (Tufte, chartjunk). Drop them, or keep the outlet as a faint outline at most.

3. **The stacked area chart hides the main change in the data (defect for this job).** The big story from 1950 to 2025 is that natural gas overtook coal and renewables grew. In a stacked chart, only the coal layer (at the bottom) and the total (at the top) sit on a flat baseline. Gas, nuclear and renewables float on wavy baselines, so you can't see when gas passed coal. The title is also a topic label ("…by major energy source, 1950-2025") rather than a finding. **Fix:**
   - Show each source as its own line on one axis (or as small multiples), so the crossing points are visible.
   - Write a title that states the takeaway, for example "Natural gas overtook coal in 2016".
   - Label each series directly at its right-hand end instead of using a legend you have to look back at (Tufte, Few).

   The lower chart also has two problems:
   - **Red next to green:** red nuclear sits directly against green renewables, which colour-blind readers struggle to tell apart (WCAG 1.4.1).
   - **Two browns:** petroleum's light brown and coal's dark brown are close in colour.

4. **"Coal was the third-highest source" doesn't match the chart above it (defect).** The snapshot shows renewables at 24.1% and nuclear at 17.7%, both larger than coal at 16.6%, so a reader comparing them sees coal in fourth place. The sentence only holds if renewables are split into wind, solar and so on, and the page never says that. The base also shifts: the natural gas and "other" paragraphs say "utility-scale generation", while the coal and petroleum paragraphs don't. A careful reader will wonder whether the percentages use different denominators. **Fix:**
   - Say "third-highest single source, after natural gas and nuclear".
   - Use one wording for the base throughout.

**Smaller things.**
- **Line length:** main-column body lines look about 90–95 characters long, above the 45–75 range (Bringhurst). Narrow the column or increase the type size.
- **Stray dash:** the coal sentence has an unpaired em dash ("about 17%—of U.S. electricity…"). Delete it.
- **Inline links:** the petroleum paragraph has seven links marked only by colour. Underline inline links (WCAG 1.4.1).
- **Current-page item:** in the sidebar, "▸Electricity in the United States" is pale grey and looks disabled. It needs stronger contrast and a clear "you are here" treatment.
- **Mixed typefaces:** the charts use Arial/Helvetica, while the page uses a geometric sans (Futura-like), so the charts look pasted in. Set chart text in the page face.
- **Inconsistent divider rules:** a thick grey rule sits above each chart and a thin one above the heading, with no clear reason for the difference. Use one rule style.
- **Detached link:** the lower "Click to enlarge" sits about 50 px below the chart's source note, so it looks cut off from its chart. Tuck it up under the chart.
- **Unexplained menu icon:** the area chart's hamburger icon gives no hint of what it does. Label it, for example "Download data".

**What's working.**
- **The section heading states the finding** ("Fossil fuels accounted for about 58%…"). Readers get the point without reading the paragraphs, so keep that pattern and extend it to the chart titles.
- **Links on source names:** each paragraph opens with the source name as a link (Natural gas, Coal, Petroleum), which makes the section easy to skim.
- **The sidebar is well grouped:** clear headings and plain link lists, separated by coloured rules.
