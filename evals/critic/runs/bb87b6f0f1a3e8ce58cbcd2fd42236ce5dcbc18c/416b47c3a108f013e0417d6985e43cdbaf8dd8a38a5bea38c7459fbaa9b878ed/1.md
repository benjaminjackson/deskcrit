**First read.** The eye lands straight on "Harbour Lights Festival", the big cream italic that fills the upper two thirds. It then jumps up to the amber date and drops down to the two-line description. It reads as the opening screen of a small coastal-town event site, and it feels calm, evening-lit and a bit literary. After the paragraph the eye has nowhere to go: the bottom quarter is empty navy, and I'm left asking "where is this?"

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a web page for a free local festival, and its job is to get local people and day-trippers to turn up.

**Verdict.** The typography is strong and the mood is right, but the piece leaves out where the festival happens and gives no next step. A viewer who gets excited has nothing to act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The piece never says where the festival is.** "Harbour" suggests a waterfront and "the old net shed" and "the quay" suggest one particular place, but no town, harbor or address appears anywhere. The date and time are the first thing you read, and the place, which a visitor needs just as much, isn't there at all. A local might fill it in. Anyone else can't decide whether to come. This is a defect in the copy, because the piece fails at its basic job. Fix: put the place on the same line as the date, in the same amber label style, so "when" and "where" read as one unit. Only the organizer can supply the place name. If a site header above this frame already names the town, this becomes a minor point, but this frame should still say it.
2. **The eye path stops at the paragraph, with no ask after it.** "Free entry for everyone" is a good closing line because it removes the cost question. Then nothing follows. There's no way to see the program, find out when the lantern boats go out, or get directions. The empty lower quarter looks like space left over rather than space held for something. On a page whose whole goal is to get people to show up, that's the spot for one specific next step. Fix: add one ask about a concrete thing the viewer would want to know, such as the timings or how to get there. Keep it to one, so the calm, spare feel survives. This is a defect: the viewer is interested and has nothing to do about it.

**Smaller things.**
- The date line is set as a small, uppercase, widely letterspaced label above the hero headline. That's a stock landing-page move, so the most useful fact on the page looks like any template's decorative label (unslop, "Uppercase labels with wide tracking"). Set it in sentence case at normal or slightly open spacing, and keep the amber color and the position.
- "Saturday 14 June" has no year. The weekday narrows it down, but if the page stays up after the event, people will misread it. Add the year if the page will be live for more than one season. This is common sense, not a published rule.

**What's working.**
- **The display type.** A large serif italic set against a plain sans body gives the headline real character and a sense of a hand-lettered evening. It's a clear contrast in face, size and color, not a near match (Bringhurst on typefaces in concord or clear contrast). Keep the scale. It makes the headline unmistakably the starting point.
- **Palette and value.** A navy ground tinted toward night water, cream text and one warm amber accent. With so few colors, each one has a clear job, and the amber reads like lantern light without anyone having to explain it.
- **The body copy is specific.** "Lantern boats on the water at dusk, music on the quay, and a fish supper at the old net shed" names real things in a real place. It couldn't sit on any other festival's page, and it does more selling than the headline. The paragraph is about 62 characters wide, a comfortable reading length (Bringhurst's 45 to 75).
