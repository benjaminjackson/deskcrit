**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold sans headline, then drops into the left column and starts reading down. Three equal serif columns with no titles look like one article that keeps going from column to column, the way a newspaper story does. It feels calm, plain and well made, like a printed info sheet. The right quarter and the bottom third of the frame are empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a short information page about a river ferry, read by passengers who want to know how the crossing works (when it runs, what they can bring, what changes in winter).

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and orderly, but the headline promises something the text doesn't deliver, and the columns are too narrow for comfortable reading while a band of width sits empty. A passenger skimming for "can I bring my bike?" or "when's the last boat?" has to read all three columns to find out.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline and the untitled columns send the reader to the wrong place.** The headline says "through the seasons", but only the third column is about a season (winter). The first is about the route and the crossing time, and the second is about bicycles. Readers come expecting a spring-to-winter walk-through and get practical rules instead. The three columns are the same width, the same face and the same color, with no titles. That makes them read as one text running on (similarity grouping, Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*), but each is really a separate topic. Someone hunting for one answer can't find its column without reading. **Defect.** WCAG 2.4.6 says a heading should describe what its section holds. The fix has two parts:
   - Make the headline name what the page covers, the crossing and its rules, or else make the content actually go through the seasons.
   - Give each column a short label that names its topic, set clearly smaller than the headline and clearly apart from the body text, so there are three distinct levels.
2. **The columns are too narrow, and the room to widen them is sitting empty.** Each column runs about 25–32 characters a line. That is below the 40–50 Bringhurst gives for text set in several columns (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). Column 1 takes 13 lines for two sentences, so the eye jumps back to the left edge every few words, and the ragged right edge is choppy. Meanwhile the block stops about three quarters of the way across the frame. That leaves about 270 px empty on the right against a 120 px left margin, so the page sits heavy on the left with no reason in the content for the empty band (Arnheim, chapter on balance). **Convention.** Widen the columns into that space until lines reach roughly 40–50 characters. If that isn't possible, use two columns. Measure the line length in the live page.

**Smaller things.**
- Column 2 ends with "stairs." alone on its last line, a runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Fix it with a small change to the column width or a non-breaking space before the last word.

**What's working.**
- The two faces differ clearly in kind, a bold sans for the headline and a reading serif for the text. The headline outranks the body by size, weight and form at once, so the starting point is never in doubt. Keep that contrast when you add column labels.
- The grid is strict. The columns start at equal intervals, the gutters match, and the first lines share one baseline across all three columns. That order is what makes the page feel calm.
- The body text is a comfortable size and dark enough on white. The line spacing suits the text.
