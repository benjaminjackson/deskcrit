**First read.** The eye lands first on the row of four big numbers, then jumps to the one saturated blue bar (Friday). Only after that does it reach the map, where two frosted callouts float over the streets. It reads as an operations dashboard for a city bike-share. It's calm, tidy and competent, but nothing on it says "here's the problem to deal with right now."

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the home screen for a bike-share operations lead. They glance at it to see the fleet's state and decide which stations need bikes moved to them now.

**Verdict.** It's clean and well built, but it doesn't yet do its job. The most urgent fact on the screen, a station that has been empty for 40 minutes, is the least clearly marked thing. The map hides which dot it is and the table offers no way to act on it. Some of the numbers also compare against the wrong reference, which makes them hard to trust.

**What would move it most**

1. **You can't tell which dot is the empty station or where the van is.** The "Canal Basin, 0 bikes" callout floats in the upper left with no pointer. The only blue dot (the "Empty" marker) sits in the middle of the map, right beside the "Van route 2" callout. A viewer will naturally read that blue dot as the van, which has no marker of its own, and will have to work out that Canal Basin is actually the blue dot. The callouts also cover the streets and station dots under them. This is a **defect**. Things that sit close together and look related get read as one group (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*), and here that grouping is wrong. The fix:
   - Tie each callout to its own point with a pointer, or place it right against that point.
   - Give the van its own marker.
   - Put the station name next to the empty dot itself, so the dot doesn't rely on blue alone (WCAG 1.4.1).

2. **The comparisons point at the wrong day, and the chart has no time period.** The header says "Today, 24 November 2026", which is a Tuesday. Yet "Rides 3,184" is compared to "2,960 last Monday", and the same goes for average ride and bikes in repair. Comparing a Tuesday to a Monday isn't like for like, so the viewer can't tell whether 3,184 is good. "Rides by weekday" shows a full Mon–Sun week, but it's only Tuesday. The chart is either last week or an average, and nothing says which. This is a **defect**. A KPI needs a stated period and a fair comparison, or the reader can't tell good from bad (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). The fix:
   - Check what the comparison really is. If it's the same weekday last week, say so: "last Tuesday".
   - Name the chart's period in its title or a caption.

3. **The restock list is a dead end.** "Stations to restock" is the reason this screen exists, but the three rows (Canal Basin, Hartley Park gate, Market Cross) offer no action. You can't assign a van, open a station or sort by urgency. Nor does the table say which station a van is already heading to, even though the map knows ("Next stop Canal Basin"). The operator has to carry that information from the map to the table in their head. Meanwhile the four KPI cards, equal in weight, take the strongest spot on the page. This is **taste / common sense**: on an ops screen, the thing that needs doing should come first, and the means to do it should sit next to it. The fix:
   - Make the restock problem the strongest element on the page, for example by moving the restock list up or making the empty count prominent.
   - Give each row its next step and its status ("van on the way").

4. **Blue means two different things.** On the map, blue means "Empty", the one alarming state on the screen. In the chart, the same blue marks Friday as the peak day. In the sidebar, the active "Fleet" item uses no blue at all. Readers learn a color's meaning once and carry it across the screen. Here the strongest color is spent on a trivia point (the best day for rides) and diluted for the alarm. This is a **convention** breach (Apple HIG, Color: status colors keep one meaning). The fix: keep one alert color for "needs action", used only on the map and the table, and make the chart's highlight neutral or drop it.

5. **The map callouts use a stock look.** The two callouts are frosted glass with a soft, blue-tinted blurred shadow. Every other surface on the page is a flat card with a hairline edge. This is a template tell (Sokolovsky, unslop: "glass cards with an inner highlight and a colored shadow"). It reads as one of many dashboards, and it makes the map's most important labels look ornamental. The fix: draw them as plain, opaque labels that match the cards, attached to their points.

**Smaller things**
- "Rides by weekday" only has a value on Friday (3,410). The other six bars can't be read or compared to today's 3,184. Label every bar or add a light scale, and let the title say what the chart shows rather than just naming it.
- Nav icons: "Rides" uses what looks like a shopping cart, and "Settings" uses a sun or crosshair glyph instead of the gear people expect. Swap in icons whose meaning is obvious.
- The gray station dots on the gray-beige map look close to the 3:1 minimum for meaningful graphics (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure them, and darken them if they fall short.
- "Docks" in the table and "24 docks" in the callout don't say whether this is total docks or free docks. Use one clear label.

**What's working**
- The KPI row's structure is right: a label, a big number and a comparison line under each, at three distinct sizes. Once the comparisons are corrected, keep it exactly like this.
- The restock table is set well: numbers right-aligned in columns, light row rules and generous row height. It scans in one pass.
- Restraint: one type family, a neutral palette, a single accent and a clear grid with even gutters. That calm is a good base for making the urgent thing stand out.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the map callouts can be moved or dismissed, and whether the table rows are clickable. That decides how bad finding 3 really is.
