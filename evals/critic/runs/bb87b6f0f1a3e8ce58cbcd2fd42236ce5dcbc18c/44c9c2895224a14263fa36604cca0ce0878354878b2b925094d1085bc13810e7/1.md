**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three short serif paragraphs and reads them top to bottom. It reads as a plain information page from a small local service. It feels calm, quiet and trustworthy, and it's easy to read. Nothing pulls at you, and nothing tells you the one thing you'd come for: when the next boat leaves.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming it's a web page that tells people using a river ferry how and when to travel, read on a laptop or phone before they set out.

**Verdict.** The typography and layout are sound. The page fails at its job because it gives passengers no times they can plan around. Every fact about when the boat runs is vague, and the one firm source of times is a printed sheet at the landing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times are too vague to act on. Defect (copy).** All three paragraphs talk about time without giving one: "between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes… a little longer when the tide turns", "one boat each hour", "before dusk". Someone planning a trip can't tell when the first or last boat leaves, which landing the forty-minute rhythm is counted from, or when "winter" starts and ends. Those are the details a reader will act on and hold the service to. "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can be read as "the last boat is at dusk" or "it could be well before dusk". If they guess wrong, they're left on the wrong bank. The page then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which means they only find the times once they've already made the trip. The fix: put in the actual first and last departure from each landing, which landing the forty-minute interval starts from, and the dates of the winter timetable. Or link to the timetable from this page. Those facts are yours to supply; I won't make them up.
2. **The schedule change that can strand someone is buried last, at the same weight as the bicycle note. Defect.** The winter paragraph (one boat an hour, last crossing before dusk) changes whether you can get home. It sits at the bottom, set exactly like the paragraph about locking bicycles to the rail, so a quick reader takes in the summer schedule and stops. The fix: move the schedule facts up next to the first paragraph so all the timing information sits together. If you want the winter rule to stand out, separate it with a short heading or position, not with more decoration.

**Smaller things.**
- Heading copy: "Notes" names a category rather than what the page gives the reader. Once the times are in, a heading that says it's the timetable and rules would tell people they're in the right place. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- **The type pairing.** A bold sans heading over a reading serif gives clear contrast in both form and weight. The page has two levels and the eye never has to guess between them. Keep it to two or three levels when you add content.
- **The paragraph setting.** The body text looks about 20 px, with lines of roughly 65 to 70 characters, generous but not loose line spacing, a gentle rag, and paragraphs marked by space alone. It's comfortable to read and should be kept as it is.
- **Restraint.** Near-black on white with no boxes, ornament or template effects. That suits a public service notice and makes it feel credible.
