**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left and drops straight into three even paragraphs of prose. The right 45% of the frame is empty, and so is the bottom quarter. It reads as a plain information page for people who use a river ferry, and it feels calm, quiet and well set. But to find out when the boat goes, you have to read every sentence.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming it's a web page that gives passengers what they need to catch the ferry: when it runs, how long it takes, what to do with a bike, and what changes in winter. Correct me if it's something else.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and does its job. The page fails at its real job: the facts a passenger plans around are vague and buried in sentences. Someone who comes here to find out when the boat leaves goes away without a time.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times you act on are vague, buried, and not on the page.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" give the reader nothing to set a clock by. "In winter" doesn't say which dates the change starts and ends. And the exact times are only "kept in the shelter at each landing", so a reader on this page has to go to the landing to learn what the page could have told them. These are the lines a passenger will hold the operator to. If they read "before dusk" as 5 pm and the last boat leaves at 4:15, they're stranded on the wrong bank. That's a **defect**.
   - **Direction:** put the real first and last departure times on the page, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends. You have those facts; I don't, so I won't make them up.
   - Lift the schedule out of the prose into a short list or small table, so a passenger can scan to it in a second (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web": people scan for the fact they came for). The bicycle and winter paragraphs can stay as prose. They give guidance, not times.
   - Smaller copy gaps of the same kind: "at no extra charge" points to a fare the page never states, and "Notes" in the heading names a document type rather than what's inside it. Both make the reader fill in a gap the page could close.

**What's working.**
- **The setting reads easily.** The lines run about 68 to 73 characters, the line spacing is generous, the text is dark on white, and each paragraph is marked by space alone. Keep this if you add a schedule.
- **There are two type levels and nothing more.** One bold heading over one body size gives a clear place to start, with no competing sizes. If you add a list, use weight or position for its labels, not a third size.
- **Each paragraph holds one topic** (crossings, bicycles, winter), so the prose that stays is already grouped well.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see how the column behaves at other widths. On a wide screen, the empty right half could look either intentional or unfinished, depending on the rest of the site.
