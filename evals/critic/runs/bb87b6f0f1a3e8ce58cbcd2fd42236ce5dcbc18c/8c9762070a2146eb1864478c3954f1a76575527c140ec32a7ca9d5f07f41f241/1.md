**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "Send an invoice in the time it takes to pour coffee.", then drops to the dark green button. Only after that does it cross to the small invoice card on the right, and last it reaches the three feature cards along the bottom. It reads as the landing page for an invoicing tool for freelancers and small studios. It feels calm, warm and competent, and a little quiet.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage hero of a SaaS invoicing product, and its job is to get freelancers and small-business owners to start the 14-day trial.

**Verdict.** The page mostly works: clear hierarchy, one ask, a restrained palette. The biggest thing in the way is that the only visual, the invoice card, shows a finished invoice that any invoicing tool could show. It doesn't back up the headline's promise or show what makes Tallyfold different.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invoice card restates "invoice" but doesn't prove the claim.** *(Taste.)*
   - **Where:** the card on the right, about 340 px wide, sitting in the hero's right half.
   - **The problem:** the headline promises speed. The subhead names the two things that set the product apart: it fills in your clients, rates and tax, and it chases late payments. The card shows neither. It's a static, completed invoice. A viewer sees "this makes invoices", which they already assumed, and nothing that shows how this one is faster or less work than what they use now. So the right half of the hero takes up space without making the case.
   - **Fix:** make the visual show the part that's different, such as which fields Tallyfold filled in versus what the user typed, or a late invoice being chased. Then give it enough size to hold its half of the hero. That's the strongest place to let viewers compare the product with how they invoice today, on their own terms.
2. **The headline breaks badly.** *(Convention: Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.4.2, on a short word alone on the last line. The rule on phrase breaks in headings is my own common sense; the source isn't confirmed.)*
   - **Where:** it currently breaks as "Send an invoice in the / time it takes to pour / coffee."
   - **The problem:** this is the biggest text on the page. "in the / time" splits the phrase that carries the comparison, and "coffee." sits alone on line three. The line breaks fight the meaning of the line, and the lone last word makes the block look unbalanced.
   - **Fix:** break at phrase boundaries, for example "Send an invoice / in the time it takes / to pour coffee." Or narrow or widen the text box until it breaks that way.
3. **The ask has nothing to compare against.** *(Taste, on the copy.)*
   - **Where:** the button, "Start free for 14 days".
   - **The problem:** the button is clear about the deal. But nothing near it lets viewers weigh Tallyfold against what they use now: a spreadsheet, a Word template, their accountant's tool. So the decision rests on the coffee line, which they have to take on trust.
   - **Fix:** put the comparison near the ask, in terms the viewer already trusts, such as the time or steps against their current way of invoicing. That fact has to come from you; I'm not supplying it.

**Smaller things.**
- **Empty feature cards:** each of the three cards is a fixed height, and its text fills only the top half, leaving a band of dead white under each. Shorten the cards to fit their text, or drop the box and let space set the three apart.
- **Near-miss alignment:** the invoice card's left edge looks about 4 px off the left edge of the third feature card below it (around x 820 vs 824). Align them exactly, or move the card clearly off that line.

**What's working.**
- **The palette:** a warm cream ground, near-black type and a single deep-green button. The ask is the only colored thing on the page, so the eye finds it immediately. Keep that restraint.
- **The invoice data looks real:** the figures are right-aligned and the totals add up ($640 + $45 = $685, 8% tax = $54.80, total $739.80). The item names are specific ("Poster run, 200 units", Harbor Print Co.). That kind of detail earns trust, so keep it when the visual changes.
- **The hierarchy:** headline, then subhead, then button, then three feature titles with their body text. Each level is plainly different from the next, and the feature copy is concrete ("Clients pay from the email with two taps") rather than filler.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't check the keyboard focus indicator on the nav links and the button, or whether a sticky header would cover a focused element.
