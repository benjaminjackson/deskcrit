**First read.** The eye lands on the row of four big numbers at the top, then drops to the one blue bar (Saturday) in the right-hand chart, the only strong color on the screen. Within a couple of seconds this reads as the back-office dashboard of a small ferry operator on the west coast of Scotland. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, with nothing shouting.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a month-at-a-glance overview for office staff at a ferry operator, to check sales, demand and route performance against last month.

**Verdict.** The layout and hierarchy are solid and nothing structural is broken. What gets in the way is the numbers: one chart can't be read, one headline comparison hides the change it exists to show, and the currency is probably wrong for the place. On a dashboard, the numbers are the job.

**What would move it most**
1. **The "Sailings sold out by weekday" chart (right, middle) can't be read except for Saturday.** Only the Saturday bar has a value (19). There is no axis, so Monday through Sunday are shapes without numbers. The viewer can say "weekends are busier" but not "Friday had about a dozen", and can't tell how far Sunday trails Saturday. Every chart should label its marks with values and units (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few). The title also doesn't say whether these are totals for November or an average per weekday. Fix: put a value on every bar (seven fit easily) and keep Saturday blue as the highlight. Say in the title or a caption that the counts are for the month shown. *Defect.*
2. **The currency is probably wrong.** Every money figure ($142,630, $15, the Revenue column) uses $, but the routes are Oban to Craignure, Lismore and Kilchoan, and the footer says "Harbour office, Oban". A Scottish operator works in pounds, so staff would either distrust the screen or copy wrong figures into reports. If the product really bills in dollars this is fine. Otherwise use the operator's currency everywhere. *Defect (common sense), unless dollars are intended.*
3. **"Average fare: $15, $15 in October" hides a real change.** Using the card's own figures, November is about $15.15 (142,630 ÷ 9,412) and October about $14.79 (131,200 ÷ 8,870). Rounding makes them look identical, so the viewer reads "flat" when fares rose about 2%. The other three cards also give October as a raw number, so the viewer has to subtract in their head to see the change. Fix: show the fare to the cent, and next to each headline number show the change from October as well as October's figure (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). *Defect.*
4. **"Daily passengers" doesn't plot daily data.** Each line has about 11 points across 30 days, so a point every three days or so. Anyone reading a single day's value, or looking for a weekend dip, will be misled about what each point means. This is fine if the data really is sampled: then the title should say so ("every 3 days", or whatever the interval is). Otherwise plot all 30 days. *Defect, unless the sampling is intended and labeled.*

**Smaller things.**
- The November/October legend sits top right, away from the lines. Label the two lines directly at their right ends instead (Tufte; Wilke).
- The line chart's date ticks are unevenly spaced (1, 10, 19, 30 Nov: gaps of 9, 9, 11 days). Use even intervals such as weekly, or label the start and end only (Tufte).
- The chart titles name the topic, not the finding. That's normal for a live dashboard, but a one-line takeaway under "Sailings sold out by weekday" would save the reader a step. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- **Restrained color.** Gray for context and one blue for the thing that matters: October in gray against November in blue, and Saturday in blue among gray bars. It points the eye without a legend war. Keep this discipline when adding value labels.
- **The routes table is set properly.** Text columns are aligned left, number columns aligned right in what look like tabular figures, and the rules are hairline thin. Revenue and sailings can be compared down the column at a glance.
- **Clear structure.** KPIs first, then trend and pattern, then detail. The card edges share one grid and the sidebar is quiet, so the eye goes top to bottom without help.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a date picker. It looks like static text. If staff can change the period there, it needs to look like a control.
