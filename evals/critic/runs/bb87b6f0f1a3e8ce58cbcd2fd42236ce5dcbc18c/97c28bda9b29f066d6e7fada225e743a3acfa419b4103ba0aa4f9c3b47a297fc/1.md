**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif "Harbour Lights festival" and then drops straight into three gray columns of equal weight. Nothing in them stops the eye. It reads as a town notice or a newsletter page, and it feels calm and literate but flat, like a council memo rather than an invitation to a night of lanterns and paper boats. The bottom 40% of the screen is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling local families what's on at the festival, when and where, and asking for volunteers.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The setting is tidy, but every fact a visitor needs (the day, the time, the place) is buried mid-sentence in three solid paragraphs, so the page has to be read in full to plan a visit. It also looks nothing like the event it's selling.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts are locked in prose (defect).** All three columns are single unbroken paragraphs with no subheads. A visitor scanning for "when is the lantern walk?" has to read column 2 from the top to find "Saturday night… at dusk… town hall". The same goes for "Sunday afternoon… long hall… by one o'clock… at four" in column 3. The boat race has no day or time anywhere. People skim web text, and a dense block loses them (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Fix: give each column a short heading that names its event, and pull the day, time, and place out of each paragraph into a short line or list the eye can catch. The prose can stay underneath for color. The missing boat race time is a fact only you have, so add it.
2. **The look contradicts the event (taste, argued).** A festival of lights, lanterns on the river, a pie contest and seagulls is set entirely in near-black and gray on white, with no image, no color and no sign of evening or celebration. Color and value should support the mood the subject calls for (Itten, *The Art of Color*). Here they make a joyful event read like a planning notice, and nothing invites a family to come. Fix: bring in a color or an image that belongs to this event, such as lantern light or the harbour at dusk, and use it with restraint so the type stays as clean as it is now.
3. **The volunteer ask goes nowhere (defect, copy).** "Volunteers are still needed to hand out programmes, carry chairs, and guard the cake tent" is the only call to action, and it's buried in column 1 with no way to act: no name, no place, no link, no deadline. Someone willing to help is left to work out how. Fix: give the ask its own line and say exactly what to do and by when. Those details are yours to supply.

**Smaller things.**
- Column 1 ends with "seagulls." alone on its last line. Rewrap or edit slightly so the last line carries more than one short word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The apostrophes in "o'clock" and "children's" look straight rather than curly. At this size, few readers will notice. This is common sense, not a sourced rule here.

**What's working.**
- The text face is a good reading serif at a comfortable size, with generous leading and dark, high-contrast text. Keep it for the body when you add structure.
- Baselines line up across all three columns, and the thin rules divide them quietly. The three-column grid is a good frame for three events, so keep it and give each column a heading.
- The copy has real local voice ("the pie that makes the judges laugh", "guard the cake tent against hungry seagulls"). It's specific to this town and could sit on no one else's page, so keep it while you pull the logistics out.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the three columns reflow on a phone. Check that each event still reads as its own section once they stack.
