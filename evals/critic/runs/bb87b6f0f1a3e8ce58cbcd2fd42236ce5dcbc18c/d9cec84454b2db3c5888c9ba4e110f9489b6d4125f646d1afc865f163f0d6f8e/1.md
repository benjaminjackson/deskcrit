**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading at top left, then down into three even paragraphs. It reads as a plain information page for a small river ferry: calm, quiet and easy to get into. Nothing pulls at you. The only thing that trips you up is that you finish reading and still can't say when the next boat leaves.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web notice that tells passengers how to use the ferry (when it runs, how bikes work, what changes in winter) so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The layout and type do their job well, and there is almost nothing to fix visually. The problem is the copy. The facts a passenger acts on are given in vague words where a reader needs exact ones, and one of them can be read two ways. That ambiguity could leave someone on the wrong bank.

**What would move it most**
1. **The service hours and the winter cutoff are too vague to plan by.** This is a defect. In the first paragraph, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives a frequency but no start, no end, and no departure minute to count from. In the third paragraph, "in winter" has no dates and "before dusk" has no clock time. These are exactly the lines a passenger will act on and later hold the operator to. Each reader guesses their own version of "late evening," and someone who guesses late misses the last boat. The page also points to "printed timetables… in the shelter," so the times it leaves out are only on the bank, not on the page itself. The fix is to put the real times into these sentences: first and last departure from each side, the minute past the hour the boats leave, the date range for "winter," and the actual last-departure time (or "X minutes before sunset" if it really moves). Only the operator has those numbers.
2. **"The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" can be read two ways.** This is a defect. "The quay" could be the market quay, the only place the page calls a quay. Or it could be a loose word for both landings. Reading one means the north landing's last boat may leave at a different time. Reading two means one time covers both sides. A passenger who crossed to the market in the morning and guesses wrong is stuck on the far bank. The fix is to name the landing, or say "from each landing," and give the time as in finding 1.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what the page tells you. That's taste: a heading that names the content (times, bikes, winter service) tells a scanning reader in one look whether their question is answered here. Don't add anything; just reword it to describe what is already on the page.
- "Bicycles ride… at no extra charge" implies there is a base fare, but the page never states it. If this page is the main notice, the fare is the missing kind of fact a passenger will want next to the times.

**What's working.**
- **The type is disciplined.** There are just two sizes, a bold heading of about 36 px and regular body text of about 19 px, with a big step between them. Weight and size both do the separating, so the hierarchy is clear at a glance without a third size (Refactoring UI; Bringhurst). Keep it to two levels.
- **The text is comfortable to read.** One flush-left column runs about 65 to 72 characters per line (Bringhurst's range), with generous line spacing. Paragraphs are marked by space alone, the right edge is gently ragged, and none ends on a lone word. Dark gray text on white looks well above 4.5:1.
- **Each paragraph opens with its topic** ("The river ferry runs…", "Bicycles…", "In winter…"). That lets a reader skim three short blocks without needing subheadings. Keep that order if the times are added.
