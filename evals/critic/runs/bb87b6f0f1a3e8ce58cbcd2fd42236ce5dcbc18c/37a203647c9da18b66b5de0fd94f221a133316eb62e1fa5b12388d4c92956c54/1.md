**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading at top left, reads down two paragraphs, and stops at the gray box. It reads as a plain web page for passengers about a river ferry's winter service. It feels calm and trustworthy, but the text is pushed hard against the left edge with the right third and bottom half empty, so it looks broken rather than finished.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public web page that tells ferry passengers the winter timetable, what happens when there's ice, and what each kind of vehicle pays.

**Verdict.** The content is clear and the type is quiet and readable, but the page isn't laid out yet. The text runs from x=0 in a column about 100 characters wide, so it looks broken and reads worse than it should. The timetable and the fare are also written in a way that's hard to look up and act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no margin, so the page tips left.** The heading, both paragraphs, and the gray box all start at the left edge of the frame. The content fills the top-left 880 × 410 px, and the right 400 px and the bottom half are empty. The viewer's first thought is that the page didn't load or the CSS broke, which costs it trust before they read a word. Müller-Brockmann places text inside a type area with a margin around it. Arnheim's point is that a heavy side with nothing to balance it makes a page look about to tip. Here the empty space is just left over: it isn't gathered into a shape that does any work (Ruder). The fix: set a real page margin, and either center a narrower column or give the empty right side a job. If this is a component captured without its page wrapper, check it in the live page, but as shown it's a defect.
2. **Lines are too long.** The body lines run about 100–105 characters ("From November to March … The first crossing is at"). Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 as comfortable and 66 as ideal. At this width the eye loses its place going back to the start of the next line, and this is text people scan for a time. The fix: bring the column down to about 600–650 px at this size. That also makes the current line spacing comfortable, since long lines need more of it (Bringhurst, Hochuli). Defect, convention.
3. **The timetable and the fare are hard to use.** "Every forty minutes", "seven in the morning", and "six in the evening" are spelled out, so someone checking whether they'll make the last boat has to read the full sentence instead of spotting the number. "Cars pay the standard fare" names a price without giving it or saying where to find it. Prices are what people act on and later hold you to, so a driver here can't tell what they'll pay. The direction: set the times as numerals (an editorial edit only), and either state the car fare or point to where it's listed. Only you have that figure. The spelled-out times are taste and common sense; the missing fare is a defect.

**Smaller things.**
- The gray fare box has roughly 28 px of padding above the text and 45 px below, so it looks like it holds an empty line. Make the padding even.
- "The crew may hold the boat … until the channel is clear" doesn't say how long a hold usually lasts. If you have that, one clause on what to expect would help people waiting. If you don't, the instruction to check the board does the job.

**What's working.**
- There's a clean two-level type scale: a bold heading clearly above regular body text, one sans family, and dark text on white. Keep it that simple when you fix the layout.
- The pale tinted box sets the fare rules apart from the schedule without a border or rule. That's the right weight for a side note.
- "Check the board at the ticket hut before you queue" is a good ask: one exact thing, in a place the reader will actually be.
