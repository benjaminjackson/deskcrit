**First read.** The eye lands straight on the big serif headline, then drops to the two-line paragraph, and stops there. It reads as the top of a local bakery's website: warm, calm and old-fashioned in a good way. Then it just ends. Nothing shows the bread, nothing says when the shop is open, and nothing tells you what to do. The bottom third of the screen is empty cream.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a small local bakery's homepage. Its job is to make a nearby person want to come in or order, and to tell them how.

**Verdict.** The typography is well made, but the screen doesn't do its job. It sets a mood and then gives the visitor no way to act on it. The biggest problem is that there's no next step: no hours, no directions, no way to order, nothing to click.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no ask, and the eye path ends at the paragraph.** *Defect.* The eye goes headline, then paragraph, then into empty space. All the content sits in the top-left 60% of the frame, and the lower third and right half carry no weight at all. Visitors who came to find out whether they can buy a loaf today, and where, leave without an answer or scroll hoping for one. The empty bottom doesn't look like chosen breathing room. It looks like the page hasn't finished loading, because nothing balances the heavy headline (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Fix: add one specific action that a bakery customer picks without thinking, such as when and where to come in, or how to order. Put it where the eye stops after the paragraph, so it fills the space now going to waste. Leave the actual hours, address and ordering method to the maker. I'm not going to invent them.

2. **The headline could be on any bakery's site.** *Defect, copy.* "The finest bread in the valley" is a claim every bakery makes. Readers take it on trust or skip it, and it tells them nothing about this shop. The proof is already there in the smaller line below it: sourdough, rye and tin loaves, baked every morning in a wood-fired oven on Mill Street, since 1962. That's specific and believable, and it's buried at 20 px while the generic boast gets 110 px. Fix: move what is particular to Hartley's up into the largest type, using the facts already on the page (wood-fired, every morning, Mill Street, since 1962). Or cut the superlative and let the headline point at the bread. Don't add new claims.

3. **Showing no bread is a choice that costs this piece.** *Taste.* Food is sold by appetite, and a bakery's first screen with no crust, no oven and no loaf asks the words to do all the work. Type alone can work for a brand with a strong voice, but this copy isn't strong enough to carry it yet (see 2). Fix: if the maker has real photos of their own bread or oven, one would give the eye a second stop and help balance the empty right side. Don't use stock bread.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "The finest bread in the / valley", which leaves one word alone on line 2 and splits the phrase "in the valley". Rebreak it as "The finest bread / in the valley" so each line is a whole phrase. *Convention, but the entry's source isn't confirmed, so treat it as common sense.*
- The "HARTLEY'S BAKERY · SINCE 1962" line above the headline is a stock uppercase, letterspaced eyebrow. Visitors have seen this exact move on countless landing pages, so the bakery's name, its most personal element, reads as boilerplate (unslop, Damir Sokolovsky). Set it in sentence case, or let the name act as the masthead.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing and scale are right. A Garamond-style display serif carries the old-craft feel and a plain grotesque handles the reading text. There are only three sizes with big jumps between them (about 14, 20 and 110), so every level is obvious at a glance. The large serif is set tight, with a real "fi" ligature. Keep this.
- The palette fits the subject: cream ground, near-black text, one bread-crust brown accent. It feels warm without any effects. Keep it to these three colors.
- The supporting line is good copy. It's concrete and has a verb, and it names a street. It's the best thing on the page, so give it more of the page.
