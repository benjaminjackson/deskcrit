**First read.** The eye goes straight to "River ferry timetable," a heavy dark headline at the top left, and then looks for the timetable. There isn't one. Below the headline is a small gray smudge of three paragraphs about 4 px tall, and the other ~85% of the frame is empty white. It feels unfinished, like a page whose styles failed to load or got scaled down by mistake.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a public web page where people who use the ferry look up when the next boat leaves.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. Nobody can read the body text, and even if they could, the page has no departure times on it. A page titled "timetable" leaves people with nothing to plan a trip from.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is too small to read** (defect). The three paragraphs at x≈120, y≈140–195 have letters about 4 px tall in a column about 100 px wide. Even zoomed to full resolution, I couldn't reliably read a single word. So the page gives the viewer exactly one line of information: its own title. Body text should be at least as large as the interface text around it, and Material's body size is 16 (Material Design 3 type scale). Measure the rendered `font-size` on these paragraphs. It looks like a missing or broken style, or a transform scaling the block down, rather than a choice. Either way, the paragraphs need to be set at normal body size. The column should then grow with them to about 45–75 characters per line, which is roughly the count each line holds now.
2. **The page has no timetable on it** (defect). The heading promises a schedule, but the only thing under it is prose. A heading has to describe what its section holds (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.6). People who come for a timetable want to look up one exact value: the next departure from their landing. That calls for a table with times in aligned columns, one row per sailing (Few, *Show Me the Numbers*, ch. 3). If the times live somewhere else, like a printed sheet or another page, the heading should say what this page actually is, and the page should link to the times. Either way, the departure times are the piece's main content and only the maker has them.
3. **The scale has two levels and no middle** (defect, with a convention behind it). I count two sizes: a headline of about 44 px and body text of about 4 px, roughly a 10:1 jump. The headline's job is to lead into the text. Here it's the only thing on the page that reads at all, so the eye lands, finds nothing to go to next, and stops. Sizes should come from a small, deliberate scale where every step reads as a level (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Fixing finding 1 mostly fixes this too. If the page gets a table, it will probably need one middle level for things like landing names or column heads.
4. **All the weight sits in the top-left corner** (defect, caused by finding 1). The headline and the shrunken block take up about a 360×120 px patch. The rest of the 1280×800 frame is blank, so the page looks like it's tipping into that corner, and the empty area isn't doing anything (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I; Ruder on gathered space). Don't fix this separately. Once the text and the times are at real size, they'll fill the column. Then check balance again.

**Smaller things.**
- The small text is in a regular weight that breaks up into gray at its current size. Once the text is a reading size this goes away, so there's nothing separate to fix.

**What's working.**
- The headline is plain, says what the page should be, and has a clear left edge at x≈120 that the body text shares. Keep that single left alignment when the table goes in.
- The palette is near-black on white with nothing extra. That's the right restraint for a utility page that people consult under time pressure.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The copy itself. At this size I couldn't proofread the paragraphs or check what they promise (times, days, fares). Read them in the source.
- Whether the tiny text is a capture or rendering fault, such as a CSS transform, a zoomed-out screenshot, or a missing stylesheet. Check the computed body `font-size` in a live browser before treating it as a design decision. If the source is fine, the earlier findings still apply to what this page actually displays.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 6 yes, 2 no (purpose clear in one sentence; heading names what the section holds)
What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (distinct text levels)
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 10 yes, 3 no (white space gathered; balance; eye path)
Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (body text large enough)
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: skipped, no pictures
Charts and data: 16 lines, 15 yes, 1 no (table for exact values: the schedule isn't shown as a table)
Interface: skipped, no controls
Template tells: 10 lines, 10 yes
