It reads as the "about" section of a small heritage lighthouse's site, written for possible day-trippers. It feels quiet, friendly, and well set, though a little plain.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is an editorial and visitor section that should make someone want to visit and tell them how.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The text is clean, readable, and well grouped, and the headline leads. Two things hold it back. The picture floats next to the text with no line in common with it. And it is a generic flat-vector lighthouse, which says far less than the words beside it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The picture lines up with nothing.** The image's top edge (about y 230) misses both the headline's cap line (about 145) and the first line of body text (about 207). Its bottom edge (about 573) sits well below the last line of text (about 500). It isn't centered on the text block either. So the two columns look as if they were placed separately, and the pair reads as a little loose and unfinished instead of one composed unit. **Convention** (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "Construction of the grid"; Arnheim on ambiguous placement). Fix: give the image an edge it shares with the text. The usual choice is to align its top with the headline's cap height or with the first body line. Or make a clear, deliberate offset that doesn't look like a near-miss.
2. **The illustration is any lighthouse, and the copy is about this one.** The text gives ninety steps, brass plaques in the lamp room, a gallery, a ferry crossing the bay, and a harbor wall. The picture is a stock flat tower with one red band on two blue waves, in a square tile whose pale-blue sky makes a hard box on the white page. It repeats the headline literally ("light" next to a lighthouse) and adds nothing of its own, so the reader has seen this picture on a hundred pages and the place stands out less than it should. This is a **template tell** (unslop: generic picture as the main visual), and it's also **taste**: picture and headline should complete each other, not echo each other. Fix: use an image from Gull Point itself, such as a photo of the tower or a drawing of a detail the copy names. If you keep the illustration, give it something specific to this place and let it sit into the page instead of in a pale box.
3. **The visitor facts are buried in prose, and they're vague.** This only matters if the section is meant to bring visitors. "Open to visitors on summer weekends" and "Tickets are sold at the cottage by the car park" are the lines a visitor acts on. They sit mid-paragraph in the last two paragraphs, set the same as the history. "Summer weekends" gives no dates or hours, so a reader can't tell whether a given Saturday in late September counts. They may make the trip and find the door shut. This is a **defect** for that job. Fix: pull the practical details out of the story into a short, scannable block of their own. Name the kind of fact that's missing: the opening months, the hours, and the ticket price. Only you have those facts.
