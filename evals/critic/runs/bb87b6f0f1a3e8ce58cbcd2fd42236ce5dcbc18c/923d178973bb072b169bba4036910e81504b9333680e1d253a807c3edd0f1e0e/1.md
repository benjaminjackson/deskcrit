**First read.** The eye goes to the headline "Send invoices in minutes, not hours", reads down the five-line paragraph, and stops on the blue "Start free trial" button. Then it notices the large empty white area to the right of the hero, and after that the row of three beige cards. It reads as a calm, tidy page for a small-business invoicing tool. It's legible and easy to trust. It's also anonymous: there is nothing to look at except words, and nothing that tells this product apart from any other invoicing app.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of an invoicing SaaS. Its job is to get owners of small design and photo studios to start a free trial.

**Verdict.** It works as a clear, readable page, but it doesn't sell. The product is never shown, the right half of the hero is empty, and the cards repeat the paragraph above them. A studio owner leaves knowing what Quillfern claims but having seen no reason to believe it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero shows no product, and its right half is empty.** From about x=700 to x=1096 there is nothing next to the headline and paragraph. All the weight sits on the left, so the hero looks like it's tipping over or like something failed to load (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). This matters more than a usual layout note. The headline promises speed and the paragraph promises "clear invoices", and the audience is designers and photographers, who judge with their eyes. They aren't shown a single invoice. The direction: put a real preview of the product in that space, ideally the finished invoice a client receives or the "what each client owes" view. Then the picture proves the claim the headline makes. If an image was meant to be there and didn't load in this capture, check it in a live browser; as captured, it's a defect.

2. **The copy could belong to anyone, and the ask gives the viewer nothing to compare against.** "Send invoices in minutes, not hours" is a line any invoicing tool could use. "Minutes" and "hours" aren't tied to anything the studio owner does today, so the viewer has to take the claim on trust. "Start free trial" asks them to commit before they've seen anything specific. The paragraph already holds the better material: one shared link for the accountant, a template you reuse every month, nothing to install. The strongest version would set Quillfern next to what these studios use now for invoices and chasing payments, so the time saved shows up on terms the viewer already accepts. Name that comparison; it's a fact only you have. This is a defect: without it, nothing pushes the viewer to act.

3. **The three cards repeat the hero paragraph.** "Send invoices from a template", "Chase late payments politely" and "Know what each client owes" say again what the paragraph says ("send clear invoices, follow up on late payments, and see what each client owes… reuse it every month"). So the whole first screen makes one point twice, and the section titled "Why studios use Quillfern" never gives a reason that's specific to studios (Nielsen heuristic 8, aesthetic and minimalist design). The direction: either shorten the hero paragraph to the one-line promise and let the cards carry the three features, or keep the paragraph and use the cards for something it doesn't say. That could be the accountant link and the no-install point, or something that applies only to studios. This is a defect, because the space is spent and nothing new is said.

**Smaller things.**
- Too many text sizes: I count about six. The eyebrow is about 13px, nav and card body about 16, card titles about 17 bold, hero body about 18, the section heading about 24, and the headline about 44. Card title, card body and hero body are only a point or two apart, so they look like accidents rather than separate levels (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). Bring the body text to one size and let weight alone set the card titles apart. Measure the real sizes to confirm.

**What's working.**
- The hero is left-aligned on the same edge as the logo, the section heading and the first card. That one clean left edge makes the page feel organized; keep it.
- There is exactly one primary action. The solid blue "Start free trial" sits next to the underlined text link "Book a demo", so the eye always knows which comes first.
- Restraint with color: neutrals, one warm gray tint for the cards, and blue used only on the main button. Once a product visual goes in, the blue will still stand out.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right side of the hero is the design or a hero image that hadn't loaded when this was captured. Check it in a live browser.
- How the hero and the three cards reflow on a phone, and the hover and focus states of both actions.
