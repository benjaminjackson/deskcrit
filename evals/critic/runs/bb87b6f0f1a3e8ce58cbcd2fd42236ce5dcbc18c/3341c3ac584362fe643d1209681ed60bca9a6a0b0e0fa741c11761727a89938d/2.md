**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", then drops to the paragraph, skims the three short lines and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a website for a small local ferry company, and it feels calm, plain and trustworthy. Then the eye drifts right into a large blank area and finds nothing there.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you gave no brief: this is the top of a ferry operator's home page. It should get a local or a day visitor to decide to take the boat and find out when to go.

**Verdict.** It's clean, well set and easy to read, but it holds back the facts a ferry passenger came for. The ask then sends them off to find those facts. The empty right half makes the page look unfinished, not calm.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page withholds what the viewer came for (defect).** A ferry visitor wants to know where the boat goes, when it leaves and what it costs. This page gives "the north landing" with no destination named, "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" with no first or last time, and no fare. "Bicycles travel free" suggests people pay, but never says how much. These are exactly the facts people plan around and later hold you to, and here they're vague, so the viewer has to click "See the timetable" to find out whether the trip suits them at all. That's my common-sense judgment, not a cited rule. The fix: put the times and the fare on this screen as plain figures, and name both ends of the trip. Only you have those facts. The button then confirms what the visitor has already decided instead of standing between them and a decision.
2. **The right half of the frame is empty, so the page tips left and looks unfinished (convention).** All the content sits in a column across the left ~60%. The right half and the bottom third hold nothing, and a heavy side needs a counterweight (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). On a ferry page that space is the obvious place to show the bay, the boat or the route. Without that, the piece only describes a pleasant crossing and never shows one. If a picture is meant to sit there and didn't load, this finding goes away; check it in a live browser. If nothing is meant to go there, give the space a job: a picture, the timetable summary from finding 1, or a wider column.
3. **The ask is generic (taste).** "See the timetable" is clear, but it asks the viewer to go and look something up rather than act on a trip they can picture. It also offers no comparison: the headline's "twelve calm minutes" means more set against how the viewer gets across the bay now (the road round, say), and the page never makes that comparison. If such a comparison is true, you're the one who can supply it. Once the times are on the page (finding 1), the button can point to the next step in the viewer's own plan.

**Smaller things.**
- The body paragraph (~20 px) and the three-line list (~18 px) look only a couple of pixels apart, so they read as a mistake rather than two levels. Set them at the same size, or make the list differ by weight or color instead (Bringhurst, §3.1.1).
- The headline breaks as "twelve / calm minutes", which pulls the number away from its unit. Break it as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" so the promise reads as one phrase.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a specific, checkable promise (twelve minutes) with a feeling attached (calm). It's the right thing to lead with, and it's clearly the dominant element. Keep that order.
- There's one left edge, one typeface and one blue used for both the brand name and the button. The restraint fits a calm crossing, and the single blue button is clearly the action. Keep the palette this small.
- The paragraph runs about 65–70 characters a line with generous spacing, so it reads easily.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty right half is a picture that failed to load or a band that animates in. Check in a live browser.
- Hover and focus states on the button.
