**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes," then down through the paragraph, three loose lines and the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the top of a small ferry operator's website: quiet, clean, a little sparse. The type does its job well. But nearly half the screen to the right and the whole lower third are empty, so it looks like a hero image never loaded.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the landing hero of a local ferry website. Its job is to get someone, whether a day-tripper or a commuter, to decide to take the boat, or at least to open the timetable.

**Verdict.** It's competent but thin. The type and the hierarchy at the top work. The biggest problem is that the page holds back exactly what a would-be passenger needs to decide: when the boats run, how much it costs and where it goes. The pitch is all mood, and the viewer has to click before they can judge anything. The empty right half makes it feel unfinished on top of that.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts a passenger acts on are vague or missing.** This is a defect, argued as common sense; the checklist's source for it, Tognazzini on anticipation, isn't on the approved list. "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" gives the interval but no first or last departure. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:30, and someone planning a commute or a last boat home can't tell which. The page also gives no fare, and nowhere says where the boat lands; "the north landing" is the only place named. These are the details people remember and later hold you to, and here they are the whole decision. Hiding them behind "See the timetable" turns a decision into a chore. Direction: put the first and last sailing times right in the paragraph, and add the fare and the destination if you have them. Keep them short, in the same voice. Then the button confirms a choice instead of starting the research. Only you have those facts, so I haven't made any up.

2. **The right half of the frame is empty, so the page tips left and looks broken.** This is a defect if it's a capture problem and a convention issue if it's deliberate. Every element sits in a column from about x=160 to x=800, and nothing on the right weighs against it. Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance") says a heavy side needs a counterweight. Here the viewer reads the blank space as a missing picture, not as calm. On a page selling "the harbour slide past from the open deck," that's also the one place where showing would beat telling. Direction: if a hero image goes there, check that it loads in the capture. If not, either give the right side something that belongs to this crossing (the view, the boat, a simple map of the route) or bring the content toward the center so the empty space reads as intended.

3. **The feature lines are almost the same size as the paragraph, so they don't read as their own level.** This is a convention issue. The paragraph looks like about 20 px and the three lines ("Bicycles travel free", "Seats on the open deck", "Hot drinks on board") look like about 18 px, in the same weight and color, with no bullets or markers. They read as body text that lost its periods, not as a list of benefits. Sizes only a step apart look like a mistake, not a level (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). Direction: make the list clearly different through weight, a small marker or color, not another near-size. Or match the paragraph size and let spacing do the grouping. "Bicycles travel free" is the strongest selling point on the page, and right now it's the quietest.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line break: "twelve" ends line 1 and "minutes" starts line 2, so the number is split from its unit and the main claim takes two glances. Break after "bay" ("Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes") so the twelve minutes read as one unit.
- There's no header, logo area or navigation. A visitor can't get to fares, routes or contact from here (Nielsen, Jakob's law). This is fine if the image is a crop of the hero component only; otherwise add a standard top bar.
- The text logo "Coastal Ferry Co." is set like a label, small and blue. It's fine as an eyebrow, but if it's the only brand presence on the page, it's too slight to be a logo.

**What's working.**
- The headline makes a concrete, specific promise ("twelve calm minutes") instead of a slogan. It's the one line that couldn't sit on anyone else's page. Keep the number in it.
- The top of the hierarchy is clean: one dominant headline, a clear step down to the body, one primary button with an action-specific label. Nothing competes.
- The paragraph is set well: about 60–68 characters per line, comfortable line spacing, flush left. "Bring a bicycle, a pram or a picnic" speaks to real people, in their own words.

- The focus state of "See the timetable" (it needs a visible ring of at least 2 px at 3:1 contrast).
- How the single column reflows on a phone.
