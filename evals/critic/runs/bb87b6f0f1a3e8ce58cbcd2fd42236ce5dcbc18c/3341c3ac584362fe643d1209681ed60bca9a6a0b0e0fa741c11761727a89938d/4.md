**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops to the paragraph, slides past three plain lines and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads at once as a ferry service's landing page: plain, quiet, sure of itself. Then you notice that the whole right side and the bottom third of the screen are empty. It feels like something didn't load.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the first screen of a ferry operator's website, meant to get locals and day-trippers to plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The message and the order of reading are clear, and the headline is good. Two things hold the piece back. Half the screen is empty, so the page looks unfinished rather than calm. And the facts a traveler needs to act (when the boats run and what they cost) are hidden behind the button.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half of the screen is empty, so the page tips left and looks broken.** All the content sits in the left 60% and the top two-thirds. Nothing on the right (from about x=800 to the edge) or at the bottom (below y=570) balances it. A visitor will take that space for a failed image, not for calm. If a hero picture belongs there, this is a loading problem, and you should check it in a live browser. If the space is meant to be empty, it isn't doing any work. A large empty area works when it is shaped against something dense (Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*, "Contrasts"). Here it is simply leftover, and the page leans because nothing counters the weight of the text (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Fix direction: put something on the right that belongs to this crossing, such as the boat, the bay or the route. Or rework the composition so the empty space is clearly intended, for example by setting the text larger to own the frame. **Defect** (it reads as broken), with a **convention** behind it.
2. **The page keeps back the facts the visitor came for.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" makes the visitor guess the times. Someone planning a trip needs the first and last sailing, and there is no fare anywhere. They are the details a traveler acts on and holds you to, and the page makes them click to find any of them. Fix direction: put the first and last sailing times and the fare (or "from" price) next to the paragraph or the button, so a visitor can decide without clicking. Only you have those facts. **Taste / common sense**: the anticipation rule has no approved source, but the cost to the visitor is clear.

**Smaller things.**
- **Headline break:** the headline splits "twelve" from "calm minutes", so the number and its unit sit on different lines and the key fact gets read in two pieces. Rebreak so "twelve calm minutes" stays together, for example "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" (Bringhurst, 2.4.6).
- **List vs. paragraph size:** the three feature lines look only a little smaller than the paragraph above (about 18 vs. 20 px). They read as a stray second paragraph, not as a list. Either match the paragraph size and set the list apart with markers or weight, or drop them clearly to a smaller step (Bringhurst, 3.1.1).
- **No header:** there's no site header, logo or navigation. The small blue "Coastal Ferry Co." is the only branding, and there's no route to tickets or other pages. That's fine if a header exists above this view. If it doesn't, visitors lose the place they expect to look for their next step (Nielsen, Jakob's law).
- **Copy, the missing comparison:** "twelve calm minutes" implies a comparison with how the visitor gets across now, but the page never says it. Naming what the ferry replaces (the trip around the bay, if that's the real alternative) would let visitors judge the time saved on their own terms. That's for you to supply.

**What's working.**
- **The headline:** it gives a concrete number plus a feeling ("calm"), and it sells the experience, not the company. Keep both the number and the adjective.
- **The paragraph:** "Bring a bicycle, a pram or a picnic" names real cases from the visitor's own life, so they can picture themselves aboard. That is exactly the right kind of specific.
- **The button:** "See the timetable" names exactly what happens when you press it, and it's the only action, so there's no doubt about what to do next.
