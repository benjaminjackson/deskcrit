**First read.** My eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers," then drops into three plain paragraphs. It reads as a calm, tidy information page for people about to take a small river ferry. The text sits in the left of the frame with nothing to its right or below it, so it feels quiet, maybe a bit unfinished, but easy to read.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that people check before or while traveling, to find when the ferry runs, how long the crossing takes, and whether they can bring a bike.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are sound and need little work. The page falls short on its job because the copy never gives a time you could plan around. Every schedule fact is approximate ("early morning," "late evening," "before dusk") and sits inside a sentence, so a passenger reads the whole page and still can't tell whether they'll make the last boat.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule has no clock times.** *(Defect.)* The first and third paragraphs give "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and, for winter, "one boat each hour" with "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk." These are the lines people act on. If they misread one, they miss the boat or wait on a cold quay. "Before dusk" can be read two ways: the boat leaves right at dusk, or some unknown time before it. The date winter starts is also missing. Readers have to fill in these gaps themselves, and each person will fill them differently. The fix is to put in the first and last departure times, the date the winter timetable starts, and the exact last winter departure. Only you have those facts. Then show the times so readers can scan them, for example a short timetable instead of prose. A reader looking for "when is the last boat" should find it without reading every sentence.
2. **The most useful fact isn't the first thing you see.** *(Taste.)* The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names the page's category but not the answer people came for. The three paragraphs all have the same weight, so the winter change, which can actually leave someone stranded, looks no more important than the bike rule. My suggestion is to lead with the schedule, current season first, and let the bike rules follow as secondary detail. You could also make the heading name the route (north landing to market quay) instead of calling the page "Notes."

**Smaller things.**
- "every forty minutes" and "twelve minutes" are written in words. People compare numbers faster as figures (40 minutes, 12 minutes), and the times you add in finding 1 will be figures anyway.

**What's working.**
- The text measure is right: lines look like they run about 65 to 75 characters at a comfortable body size and line spacing, so the paragraphs read easily. Keep that column width when you add the timetable.
- The type uses one family with two clear levels: a bold heading and regular body text, in a dark near-black on white. The order of importance is clear without any decoration. Don't add more sizes when you restructure.
- The empty space to the right forms one large open area instead of scattered gaps. For a single reading column that's the right choice, not leftover space.
