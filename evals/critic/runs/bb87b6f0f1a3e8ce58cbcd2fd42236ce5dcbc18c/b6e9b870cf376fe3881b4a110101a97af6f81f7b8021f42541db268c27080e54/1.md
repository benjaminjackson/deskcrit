The page is clean and well built, but it fails at its riskiest job: changing a paid plan. It doesn't show which plan you're on now, and a plan change is committed by a generic button under a different section, with nothing saying what it costs or when you'll be charged.

**First read.** My eye goes to "Plan and billing" first, then to the Studio card with the teal outline, then down to the teal "Save changes" button. It reads straight away as the billing settings page of a small business tool, apparently a booking product for tattoo studios. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy. The calm runs out at the plan cards: I can't tell if Studio is the plan I'm paying for or just the one I last clicked.

**What it's for.** My assumption is that it's for a studio owner who wants to check what they pay, change their plan, keep their billing details current and find past invoices.

**Verdict.** It mostly works: the layout, type and table are tidy and conventional. The one serious defect is in the plan picker, where money changes hands and the page shows the least.

**What would move it most**

1. **The page doesn't show the current plan, or what happens when you switch.** *Defect.*
   - **Where:** the three plan cards at the top. The only thing marking Studio is a filled radio button and a teal border. That cue looks exactly the same whether Studio is the plan you pay for now or one you just picked.
   - **What the viewer runs into:** someone who clicks Multi-studio sees the same cue move to that card. They still can't see:
     - that the switch hasn't happened yet;
     - what the new price will be on their next bill;
     - whether the change takes effect today or at the next billing date;
     - whether they'll be charged a part-month amount now.
   - **Why it's serious:** a price and a start date are what a customer remembers and later holds you to. A choice that can be read as either "my plan" or "my selection" is a defect here, not a style note. It goes against Nielsen's first heuristic (visibility of system status) and his sixth (recognition rather than recall): to know where they stand, the owner has to remember what they're paying or scroll down to the invoices.
   - **Fix:** give the current plan a lasting label of its own, separate from the radio selection. When someone picks a different plan, show next to the cards what changes and when. The timing and any part-month charge depend on your billing rules, so you'll have to supply those facts.

2. **"Save changes" sits under the billing details but also commits the plan.** *Defect.*
   - **Where:** "Save changes" and "Cancel" sit right under the "Email a copy of each invoice" checkbox. The rule under the plan cards and the "Billing details" heading split the page into two sections, so the button seems to belong only to the billing fields.
   - **What the viewer runs into:** some will pick a new plan, see no confirm button next to it, and leave thinking they've switched. Others will edit their invoice address, press Save, and switch plans without realizing it.
   - **Why:** Norman's natural mapping says a control should sit next to the thing it changes.
   - **Fix:** separate the two actions. The plan change should get its own explicitly named action next to the cards. Changing a plan isn't just "saving changes", so the button text should say what happens. "Save changes" can then cover only the billing details.

3. **The plan cards describe each plan in different terms, so you can't compare them.** *Defect, in the copy.*
   - **Where:** the one-line summaries on the three cards:
     - Guest spot: "One artist, 30 bookings a month"
     - Studio: "Up to 6 artists, deposits online"
     - Multi-studio: "Up to 4 studios, one client list"
   - **What the viewer runs into:** each card uses a different measure. Does Studio limit bookings? How many artists does Multi-studio allow? Does Guest spot take online deposits? The owner can't put their own studio's numbers against the three and pick one at a glance. They end up guessing, or leaving the page to find out.
   - **Fix:** describe every plan on the same few measures, in the same order. Only you know the real limits, so the missing facts are yours to fill in.

**Smaller things**
- **Invoice table:** the invoice numbers are plain text, and no row offers a way to view or download the invoice. Getting a copy of an invoice is the usual reason to come to this table. Give each row a visible view or download action. (Taste and common sense: the checklist entry here has no approved source.)
- **Billing form:** nothing says which fields are required. Every field is filled in here, so the cost is low. But a user who clears the invoice address can't tell if that's allowed. Mark the required fields, or put "optional" after the optional ones (Nielsen Norman Group, Budiu, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").

**What's working**
- **The type scale is clear:** the page title, section headings, field labels and body text are four distinct levels, and none of the sizes nearly collide. Field labels are set apart by weight, not by a fifth size, as *Refactoring UI* recommends. Keep it this tight when you add the plan-change information.
- **The invoice table:** it has light rules, a right-aligned Amount column with figures that line up, status spelled out as "Paid" rather than shown only by color, and a clear date order.
- **Color is used sparingly:** one teal, used only for "selected" or "primary" (the chosen card, the checkbox, the Save button), so it means something every time. "Settings" is bold in the top menu and "Plan and billing" is shaded in the side menu, so you always know where you are.
