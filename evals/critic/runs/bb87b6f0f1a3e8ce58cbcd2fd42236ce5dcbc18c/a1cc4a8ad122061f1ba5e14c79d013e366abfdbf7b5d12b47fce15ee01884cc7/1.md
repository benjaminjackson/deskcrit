**First read.** The eye lands on "How can we help?", the white serif headline on the navy band. It drops straight to the search field, then to the 4×2 grid of topic cards. In two seconds it reads as a help center for a software product. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, but it also feels anonymous: apart from the word "Harbor", nothing on the page belongs to this product.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a product's help center, where a customer with a specific problem should find the right article fast, by search or by topic.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The search is the obvious first move and the topic grid is easy to scan. Two things hold it back. The topics don't add up to one product, so a visitor can't be sure they're in the right place. And the page offers only drawers to open, with nothing that answers the most common problems directly.

**What would move it most**
1. **The topic list describes two different products** (defect). "Getting started: Set up your workspace and invite your team", "Integrations: Connect calendars, chat tools, and storage" and "Notifications" describe a team workspace tool. "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" in the top-right card describes a store. A stranger reading the grid stops to wonder which Harbor this is and whether "Orders" means their own orders or orders they handle for customers. Someone who came for a shipping problem may wonder whether this is even the right help center. The page should show what the product is and how its parts relate (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). If Harbor really does both, the fix is in the copy: make the shipping card say whose orders they are, and let the order of the cards follow the product's structure. If the card is left over from a template, take it out.
2. **No fast lane for the visitor with a specific problem** (taste / common sense). Everything below the search is a category, so anyone who doesn't search must guess a drawer and dig. People reach a help center with a few recurring problems, and the page puts none of them in front of the visitor. Status, which answers "is it me or is it you?", is only a small nav link. The direction: under the search or beside the grid, add a short list of the most-read articles from the maker's real data, and bring Status out when it matters. That turns many visits into one click. I can't see below 800 px, so if this exists further down, it belongs higher.

**Smaller things.**
- Card descriptions end on single words: "data." (Privacy), "where." (Notifications), "messages." (Troubleshooting), plus "recovery." and "receipts.". Across four columns this makes a ragged, stuttering bottom edge. Rebreak them or trim the wording so the last lines aren't single words (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- The search field's only label is its placeholder, "Search for articles", which disappears as soon as you type. The subtitle above it partly does that job. Add a magnifier icon or a Search button so the field still says what it is once text is in it (NN/g, "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful").

**What's working.**
- **The search-first structure is the right convention.** The white field on navy is the brightest, highest-contrast shape on the page, so the main tool gets found first without a big button. Keep that contrast.
- **The type pairing has a clear plan.** A bold serif sets the single welcoming headline, and a plain sans carries everything else in a few clearly separate sizes and weights. Card title vs. description is done by weight and gray, not by another size. Keep the serif to this one line.
- **The grid is disciplined.** The logo and the first card column share a left edge, the nav ends on the last card's right edge, and the outline icons share one stroke and size. That's why the page feels orderly at a glance.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I couldn't see hover and focus states on the cards and search field. The faint card borders mean the hover cue has to carry the "this is clickable" signal.
- I couldn't see what sits below the fold (popular articles, contact options).
