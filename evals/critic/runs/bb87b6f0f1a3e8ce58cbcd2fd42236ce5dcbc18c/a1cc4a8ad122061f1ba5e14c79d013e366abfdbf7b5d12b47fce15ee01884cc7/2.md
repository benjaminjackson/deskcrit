**First read.** The eye lands on "How can we help?", the white serif headline on the navy band, and drops straight to the white search field under it. Then it moves to the eight topic cards on the pale gray ground. Within a second it reads as the help center for a software product called Harbor. It feels calm, orderly, and trustworthy, and also familiar: this is what most help centers look like.

**What it's for.** No brief was given, so I'm assuming this is the home page of a product's help center, for existing customers who have a problem and want to search or browse to the answer quickly.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The order of importance is clear, the layout is disciplined, and the topic copy is written in the user's own words. The biggest problem is in the words, not the layout. The page uses three names for its own content and seems to describe two different products, so a stranger can't be sure what they're searching or which Harbor this is.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page's own vocabulary doesn't agree with itself (defect).** The subtitle says "search the guides", the field says "Search for articles", the nav offers "Guides" as a separate place, and the section is "Browse by topic". A first-time visitor has to wonder whether guides and articles are different things, and whether the search covers what the "Guides" link leads to. The topics also describe two kinds of product. "Getting started: Set up your workspace and invite your team" and "Integrations: Connect calendars, chat tools" read as team software. "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" reads as a store. If Harbor really does both, the page never says so, and a viewer may decide they've landed on the wrong help center. Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2: the naming and grouping should let a first-time user build a correct picture of the system. Fix: pick one word for the content and use it in the subtitle, the placeholder, and the nav (for example, "search the guides" with "Search the guides"). If shipping is a real part of the product, the subtitle or the "Getting started" card should say what Harbor covers. Only the maker knows which is true.

**Smaller things.**
- Five of the eight card descriptions end on one word alone on the last line ("your team.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages."). Across a grid that repeats it reads as careless. Widen the text box slightly or prevent breaks before the last word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- "Browse by topic" (about 18 px bold) is barely bigger than the card titles (about 16 px bold), in the same weight and color, so the section heading and the item titles read as nearly one level. Make the heading clearly larger, or set it apart by color or weight (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger).
- The card edges look very faint: a white fill and a pale gray border on a near-white ground. The cards are the click targets, and on a dim or low-contrast screen their edges may disappear. Measure the border against the background and bring it toward 3:1, or give the cards another cue (WCAG 1.4.11).

**What's working.**
- The hero does exactly one thing. One headline, one sentence, one field, and no competing buttons, so a user with a problem knows to type within a second. The single serif headline against an otherwise all-sans page gives it warmth without clutter. Keep that restraint.
- The grid is strict. The logo, "Browse by topic", and the left column of cards share one left edge, the right column ends where the nav ends, and the gutters are even. That's why the page feels calm.
- The card copy is specific and uses verbs from the user's world ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Fix sync problems and error messages"). People can match their problem to a card without guessing. The icons come from one outline set with matching stroke and size.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- What's below the fold. If there are no popular articles, recent issues, or a contact route further down, users whose problem doesn't fit a topic will have to guess. Check the full page.
- Focus states on the search field and the cards, and whether the cards highlight on hover.
