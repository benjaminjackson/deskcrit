**First read.** The eye lands on the white serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops straight to the white search field, then spreads across an even grid of eight topic cards. It reads at once as the help center for a SaaS workspace product called Harbor. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy. It also feels like a help center you have seen before, which suits the genre more than it hurts it.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the landing page of a product help center, there to get a user with a problem to the right article fast, by search or by topic.

**Verdict.** It does the job. Search is the obvious first move, the topics are clear and well worded, and the build is clean. The one thing in its way is that the first screen gives only doors (a search box and eight categories) and no answers. The user who came with a common problem still has to search or click twice to reach it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The screen lists categories but no answers.** Between the search field and the topic grid, nothing names an actual question or article. Someone who arrives with the most common problem (a sign-in failure, a missing invoice, a sync error) has to guess which of the eight boxes holds it, open it, then scan a list. Most help-center traffic goes to a small set of questions, so putting those on the landing page lets many visitors leave with their answer in one click. The "Troubleshooting" card's "Fix sync problems and error messages" hints that the maker knows what those questions are. Fix direction: add a short list of the top questions or articles, in the users' own words, either under the search field or above the grid, and keep it short so search stays dominant. If outages are a common reason people come here, a one-line status note does the same job better than the "Status" link in the nav. Which questions go there is for you to supply. This is common sense, not a cited rule (defect: it slows the most common visitor).

**Smaller things.**
- **Too many type sizes.** By eye I count six text sizes: roughly 34 (headline), 20 (logo), 18 ("Browse by topic"), 16 (subhead and card titles), 15 (nav) and 14 (card text). The 20/18/16/15 sizes are only a step or two apart, so they read as near-misses rather than levels. Keep the headline, one heading size, one body size and one small size, and use weight or color for the other differences (Bringhurst, 3.1.1; Refactoring UI).
- **One-word last lines.** Six of the eight card descriptions end on a single word: "your team.", "recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages.". The cards look ragged as a set. Widen the text slightly, trim a word, or use balanced wrapping so each line pair breaks evenly (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- **"Orders and shipping" sits oddly** next to workspace, team, calendar and integration topics. A first-time user may wonder whether this is the same product. That's fine if Harbor really ships physical goods. If it doesn't, the card is a leftover; if it does, nothing else on the page hints at it (Norman, conceptual model).
- **The search field has no magnifier icon and no submit button.** It reads as a field, but adding the usual search icon makes its job clear without reading the placeholder. This is common sense, not a checklist failure.

**What's working.**
- **The serif headline against the sans body.** It is the one typographic voice on the page and gives Harbor some character inside a stock layout. Keep the contrast between the two faces.
- **The card copy.** Each description names concrete things the user does or has ("Track a delivery or start a return", "Passwords, two-step sign-in, and recovery"), not category filler. A user can match their own problem to a card without opening it.
- **Restraint and consistency.** There is one navy plus grays, one icon set at one stroke weight, and one card treatment repeated exactly. Nothing competes with the search field.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indicators on the search field and cards, whether a sticky header would cover focused cards, and the hover state that tells users the cards are clickable.
