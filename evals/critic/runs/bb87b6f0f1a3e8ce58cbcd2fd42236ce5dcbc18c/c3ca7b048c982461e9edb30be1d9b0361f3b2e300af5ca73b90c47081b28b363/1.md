**First read.** The eye lands on the big headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and three short lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads as the landing page for a local passenger ferry, aimed at day-trippers and commuters. It feels clean, calm and legible, but also bare and generic: a white page with black text that could belong to any product.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the top of a ferry operator's home page. Its job is to get someone to take the crossing, and the next step is checking departure times.

**Verdict.** It's competent and readable, and it has one clear action. But it doesn't sell the trip. The copy promises a calm crossing past the harbor, and the page shows none of it. The rider also has to click through to learn where the boat goes and when it leaves.

**What would move it most**
1. **The stock centered stack, with nothing of the place in it.** The wordmark, headline, paragraph, list and button all sit on one centered column on a blank white field, and the bottom third of the screen is empty. Visitors have seen this exact hero on countless software sites, so it looks like anyone's page. The one thing that makes this offer different, a boat crossing a bay with an open deck, appears only in words. This is a template tell (unslop, "Hero stacked on one centered axis"), and it echoes Tschichold's objection to the central axis as a default. Direction: let the crossing carry the page. Either set the text over a large view of the harbor or the deck, or split the hero asymmetrically with text on one side and the picture on the other, so the empty space does a job. *Taste, argued: on a page selling a scenic trip, the scenery is the strongest argument you have, and right now it's missing.*
2. **The facts a rider acts on are missing or vague.** "Cross the bay" never says where to. Only one end, "the north landing", is named. "From early morning until late evening" is a schedule nobody can plan around: someone deciding whether they can make the last boat home needs a time, and they'll hold you to it. There's no fare either, though "Bicycles travel free" suggests there is one. The button is a good, specific ask, but it makes the viewer click before they can tell whether the ferry fits their day. Direction: name the other landing, and replace "early morning until late evening" with the actual first and last departures. If you can, show the next departure or the fare near the button. Only you have these facts. *Common sense, not a cited rule.*

**Smaller things.**
- The three-line paragraph is centered, so each line starts at a different place and is slower to read. Set it flush left (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, 2.1.3), or cut it to one or two short lines that can stay centered.
- The paragraph (about 20 px) and the three feature lines (about 18 px) look almost the same size. With no bullets or other marks, the list reads as leftover paragraph text rather than its own level (Bringhurst, 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). Either make them the same size and set the list apart some other way, such as weight, color or a marker, or make the step between them clearly bigger.
- The headline breaks as "twelve / calm minutes", which splits the key phrase. A break such as "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes" keeps the promise in one line.

**What's working.**
- The headline is concrete and benefit-led, with a number ("twelve calm minutes") and a feeling ("calm") that a rider can test against their own trip. Keep that specificity.
- There's exactly one action, and its label names what happens next ("See the timetable"), not a vague "Learn more".
- The palette is restrained: one blue ties the wordmark to the button, and everything else is near-black on white. Contrast is comfortable throughout.
