I've finished the checklist and the deep read; writing up the critique now.

**First read.** The eye goes to the headline, "Fill every market pitch before Saturday", then jumps right to the rust F4 cell in the layout panel. From there it comes back down to the matching rust button. In about two seconds it reads as booking software for whoever runs a street market. It feels calm, practical and local, and nothing about it looks like a generic SaaS page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a product landing page that gets market managers to start setting up their market in Stallkeep.

**Verdict.** It does its job, and it does it well. The headline is specific, the product panel shows the real thing, and there is one clear action. The one thing in the way is a short stretch of the lede that stops talking to the manager and can be read two ways. After that, the panel makes its own key point, the free pitches, hard to see.

**What would move it most**
1. **The lede loses the reader halfway through.** It opens to the manager ("shows *your* market layout") and then switches to "the market manager stops taking bookings by text", as if that were someone else. The phrase "stops taking bookings by text" can mean two things. One is the intended relief: you no longer have to handle texts. The other is a policy: the manager refuses bookings sent by text. That second reading worries a manager whose regulars text today. Keep it in the second person and say the consequence to them, for example "…and you stop taking bookings by text." This is a defect in the copy (common sense, no catalog rule). It's the one line that sets the product against what the reader does now, so it's worth getting exactly right.
2. **The free pitches are the faintest thing in the panel, but they're what the headline is about.** The headline promises to fill every pitch, so E3, F6 and G5 are the story. In the panel they show only as white cells, a white that is close to the off-white panel. Their dashed borders are so pale they almost disappear, and nothing labels them as free. The "18 pitches, 3 free" count lets a careful viewer work it out, but at a glance they look like gaps. Give the free pitches a cue beyond a pale fill, such as a visible border or a "free" label in the cell. Make that cue stronger than the faint dashed border, and keep the rust highlight on the selected pitch. This is a convention issue: meaning shown by color alone (WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Color). It also weakens the message.

**Smaller things.**
- Grid: the panel starts at about x=652, and the feature columns start at about 80, 469 and 859. The bottom half of the page doesn't share a column line with the top. Put the panel's left edge on the feature grid, or the feature columns on the hero's split (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).

**What's working.**
- **The headline names a deadline and an outcome from the reader's own week** ("before Saturday"). Next to it, "Plan your next market" is an action the reader can picture. Keep both concrete.
- **The panel is a real, specific artifact**, not a generic hero image. It has named rows, a footway band, a selected pitch with its size, its trader and its paid status. Repeating the button's rust on F4 ties the picture to the action.
- **The feature row compares the product with what managers do now** ("nobody walks the rows with a cash bag", "the trader list it asks for each quarter"). That lets readers judge the value on terms they already accept. It also avoids the stock icon-and-bold-title feature card.
