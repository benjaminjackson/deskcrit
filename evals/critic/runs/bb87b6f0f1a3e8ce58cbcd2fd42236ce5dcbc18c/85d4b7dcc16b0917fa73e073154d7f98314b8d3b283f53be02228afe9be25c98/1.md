**First read.** My eye goes to "Overview", then along the four big numbers, then to the one saturated thing on the page: the blue Friday bar. It reads as a calm, well-made monthly report for a small hostel's owner or manager. It feels tidy and trustworthy. The trust lasts until you add up the numbers.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's or manager's monthly snapshot of Fellside Hostel: how November went against October, and where the business came from.

**Verdict.** The layout, type and restraint are good, and the page is easy to read at a glance. But its figures don't agree with each other, and its units are unclear. A report whose numbers can't be squared loses the trust it exists to earn. Fix the data and labels before anything visual.

**What would move it most**
1. **The line chart and the "Beds sold" card disagree (defect).** "Beds sold per night" runs from about 50 to 95 and looks like it averages about 74 a night. Over 30 nights that's roughly 2,200, but the card above says 2,846. The October line is worse: "Up 9% on October" puts October near 2,600, about 87 a night, yet the gray line sits around 55–65. The chart also seems to plot about 11 points for 30 nights, under a title that says "per night". The x-axis ticks (1, 10, 19, 30 Nov) are unevenly spaced. An owner who checks one number against another will stop trusting the whole page. The size of the effect drawn has to match the data (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2), and tick spacing has to be even. To fix it, plot one point per night from the same source as the card, or retitle the chart to say what each point is. Use even ticks. Check the numbers against the card in a live build before anything else. These are values read by eye, so confirm them with the real data.
2. **"Nights" in the table can be read two ways, and neither works (defect).** Skiddaw dorm shows 212 nights in a 30-night month, so the column can't be room-nights unless there are several such dorms. Read as bed-nights, $19,080 ÷ 212 comes to about $90 a bed. That is far above the $25 a bed implied by $71,150 ÷ 2,846 beds sold, and the same as the twin room's $90. So under either reading the column misleads. It should use the same unit as the rest of the page, which counts in beds (Nielsen, heuristic 2: use the audience's words). Name the unit in the header (bed-nights, or room-nights) and make sure the revenue is for that same unit. Only the maker knows which unit is meant.
3. **"Beds sold up 9%" next to "Beds filled down 3 points" is left unexplained (defect, copy).** Selling more beds while filling a smaller share only works if capacity grew. The page doesn't say so, so the reader either assumes an error or has to go and find out. "Down 3 points" is also the only bad news on the page, but it's set in the same small gray as the good news, so it's easy to miss. Add the missing fact, such as the bed count for each month, in the card's sub-line. Let the one negative change stand apart by more than its wording. Only the maker can supply that fact.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart titles name topics, not findings. "Check-ins by weekday" could say that Friday is the busiest day, which the blue bar already shows (taste: a finding in the title saves a read).
- The line chart uses a legend in the top right. Label "November" and "October" at the right-hand ends of the lines so nobody has to look back and forth (Tufte; Wilke).
- The weekday chart has no scale and labels only Friday (212). Saturday looks almost as tall but has no value, so the gap can't be read. Label each bar, or at least Saturday.
- "1 to 30 November 2026" looks like plain text. If the period can be changed, make it look like a control, and offer a way to pick another month or comparison (Nielsen, heuristic 7).

**What's working.**
- The restraint in color: one blue for "this month / the thing to look at" and gray for context, in both charts. Keep that rule when adding anything.
- A clear four-level type scale (page title and figures, card titles, body, small print) with plenty of air. It reads fast and calm, which suits an owner checking in once a month.
- Every headline figure comes with an October comparison written in words ("Up 6% on October"). Keep that pattern. It just needs to tell the truth (see 1 and 3).

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover values on the line chart, and whether the date range is interactive.
- The contrast of the pale axis labels and sub-lines looks adequate. Measure it to be sure.
