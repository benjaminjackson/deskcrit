**First read.** The eye lands first on the headline, "Let the harbour book its own berths". It goes next to the navy S4 berth in the pontoon card, then down to the navy "Set up your harbour" button, so the navy links the example to the ask. You know within a few seconds that this is booking software for small harbours and marinas, sold to the harbour office. It feels calm and practical, with a quiet nautical palette and nothing shouting.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the marketing home page for Moorly. Its job is to get harbour managers to start setting up their harbour, or at least open the sample.

**Verdict.** It does its job, and better than most product heroes: the right-hand picture shows the product's own thing instead of a stock image, and the headline, picture and ask tell one story. The one real problem is the berth map: its color code reads backwards at a glance, so the picture that should sell the product makes the viewer stop and decode it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The berth map's colors suggest the opposite of what they mean.** Fifteen berths are filled sage green and three (N3, S6, W5) are white with a dashed outline. Green means taken here and white means free. In booking maps of seats, rooms or berths, green usually means "available", so a harbour manager's first reading is "mostly free", which is the reverse of the truth. Only "18 berths, 3 free" in small gray type at the top right corrects it, and only if the viewer counts. The dashed outline is the one cue besides color that marks a free berth, and it looks far below 3:1 against the off-white card, so the free berths almost dissolve into the card (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.11). This is a **defect**: the color reverses a common convention (Apple HIG, Material on status color), and the picture's message ("you can see who is moored where") arrives a second late. Direction: give taken berths a neutral or muted fill that doesn't read as "go". Make the free berths the ones that stand out, with a firmer outline or a "free" label. Keep the navy S4 as it is.

**Smaller things.**
- Hero paragraph: the last line is the lone word "where.". Rebreak the lines or adjust the width so the last line carries a few words (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- Headline (copy, taste): "Let the harbour book its own berths" hides who does the booking. The subhead then says it's the boat owners. The line has character and I'd keep the idea, but check that a harbour master doesn't read "the harbour" as their own office doing the work. Naming the boat owners would close that gap.
- The ask: "Set up your harbour" is good because it names the viewer's own harbour. "Open a sample pontoon" asks them to grade a demo. Nothing on the page sets Moorly against how the office handles bookings today. If you have that fact, a comparison would let the viewer judge the value on terms they already trust.
- Feature copy: "the berth shows as taken at once" stops at the fact. End it on what that spares the harbour office, in "you" terms, using a consequence you can stand behind.

**What's working.**
- **The hero picture is the product.** A real pontoon plan with named berths, a walkway to the slipway, and a booked berth with its size, season and "$380 paid" says more than any feature list. Keep it specific like this.
- **One accent color doing one job.** Navy appears only on the booked berth and the primary button, so the eye moves from example to action. Everything else stays neutral, and the hierarchy is clear: headline first, then picture, then ask.
- **Plain, concrete copy.** "Visitors book the night before" and "A plan the harbour master can print" sound like the audience talking. The hero paragraph runs about 55 characters per line with generous line spacing, so it reads easily.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the berth map responds to hover or click, and what "Open a sample pontoon" opens. Check both in a live browser.
