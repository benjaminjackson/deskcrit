**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left, then drops into three paragraphs set in one gray-black size that run nearly the full width of the frame. Nothing in the text stands out, so the eye just starts reading line one. The bottom 45% of the frame is empty. It reads as a calm, plain information page for a small river ferry, more like a letter than a timetable.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page that helps passengers work out when they can cross and how to bring a bicycle, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do that job well. The type is clean and calm, but a passenger who comes to find out when the boat goes has to read the whole page and still gets no times. Lines about 140 characters long make that reading harder than it needs to be.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger came for aren't on the page.** Defect, in the copy. "Early morning and late evening", "every forty minutes", "about twelve minutes", and in winter "one boat each hour" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" give no clock time. Nobody can plan a crossing from these lines. The page also sends readers to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which means they have to be at the ferry already to learn when it runs. The winter line can be read two ways. "The quay" could mean the market quay only, or both landings. "Winter" has no dates, and "before dusk" moves by an hour or more across the season. Someone who reads it wrong waits at the landing for a boat that has already gone. Direction: state the first and last crossing times, the summer and winter dates, and which landing each last boat leaves from. Only you have those facts, so they have to come from you. If the times really do change too often to print, say where the current ones are posted online, not only in the shelter.
2. **Lines are far too long to read comfortably.** Defect. Each paragraph runs from the left margin almost to the right edge, about 135 to 145 characters per line. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2) gives 45 to 75. At this width the eye loses its place on the way back to the next line, so people reread lines or start skimming, and on this page skimming means missing the details. Direction: limit the text column to about 66 characters. The heading can stay where it is.
3. **The line spacing is too tight for that width.** Convention. About 1.5 would suit a normal measure, but on lines this long the lines run together. Bringhurst (2.2.1) and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 47) both say longer lines need more space between them. Fixing the line length in item 2 mostly fixes this too. Only add space if you keep the wide column.
4. **Facts a reader will look up are written as running prose.** Defect. Departure frequency, crossing time, the bicycle rules, and the winter changes are all in three paragraphs with no subheads, so someone looking for "can I bring my bike?" has to read from the top (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Direction: give each paragraph a short subhead that names its topic, such as times, bicycles, and winter and changes. Then set the schedule as a short list or a small table, not as sentences.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- One sans-serif family in two clear levels: a bold heading at about twice the body size, and body text in near-black on white. The hierarchy is plain and the contrast is solid. Keep this restraint when you add subheads: use a bold weight at body size or a little larger, not a third large size.
- The paragraphs are separated by space alone, with no rules or boxes. That suits a page this quiet.
- The heading says plainly who the page is for.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column behaves at narrower widths. If it is already fluid, check that it doesn't simply keep filling wide screens.
