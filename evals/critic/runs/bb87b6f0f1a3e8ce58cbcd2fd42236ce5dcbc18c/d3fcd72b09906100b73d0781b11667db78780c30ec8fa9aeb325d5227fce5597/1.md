**First read.** The eye lands on the "Sales" title and the row of four big figures, $27,640 first. It then drops to the darkest bar in "Orders by weekday" and the one number shown on that chart, 126 over Saturday. It reads as a monthly sales overview for a small fabric shop (Bolt & Bobbin, Norwich) in a back-office tool. It feels calm, orderly and competent, but generic: it could be any shop's dashboard in blue.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the shop owner opens it to see how November went against October, when people buy, and which fabrics sell.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and the KPI row works. But the numbers don't hold together and the weekday chart misleads, so an owner who looks closely will stop trusting the page. Data integrity is the biggest problem, not styling.

**What would move it most**

1. **The figures don't add up, and "Daily orders" isn't daily.**
   - **Where:** the "Daily orders" line chart, measured against the KPI row and the weekday bars.
   - **The daily line:** the November line has about 11 points across 30 days, roughly one every three days, so the title promises something the chart doesn't show (WCAG 2.4.6: a heading names what its section holds).
   - **The totals:** the line hovers around 20 to 35, which comes to roughly 800+ orders for the month. Revenue ÷ average order ($27,640 ÷ $46) is about 600. The weekday bars also look like they add up to about 600 (only Saturday's 126 is printed; the rest are read from bar heights). The October line has the same mismatch against $24,110 ÷ $44.
   - **What the viewer experiences:** an owner who does this sum once, and owners do, can't tell which number is real. Every other figure on the page then loses its authority.
   - **Fix:** check the query behind the line. Either plot one point per day, or rename the chart to match what it plots (say, a three-day total or average) and state that in the title or axis. The line, the bars and the KPIs should come from the same order count.
   - **Defect.** I'm reading values off a screenshot, so confirm against the data.

2. **The weekday chart's colors say Sunday, but the data says Saturday. Only one bar can be read.**
   - **Where:** the "Orders by weekday" bars.
   - **The ramp:** the bars run pale to dark from Monday to Sunday, so the shade follows the order of the days, not the values. Sunday is the darkest bar, but Saturday is the tallest. Tuesday is lighter than Monday, yet Tuesday is also the shorter bar. The color fights the bar heights.
   - **Missing labels:** there is no axis and no value except "126" on Saturday. The viewer can rank the days but can't say how big the weekday gap is.
   - **Missing comparison:** this is the only panel with nothing to compare against, while everything else shows October.
   - **Why it matters:** a light-to-dark ramp should follow the order of the values (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*). Every mark needs a readable scale or a direct label (Tufte; Few).
   - **Fix:** draw all bars in one neutral and give the accent only to the bar that matters (Saturday). Either label every bar with its value or add a light axis.
   - **Defect.**

3. **Dollar signs on a Norwich shop's page.**
   - **Where:** every money figure: the Revenue and Average order cards and the Revenue column of the table.
   - **The clue:** the shop is "Bolt & Bobbin, Norwich", the page says "Metres", and dates are written day-month ("1 to 30 November 2026"). All of that reads as British, yet money is shown in $.
   - **What the viewer experiences:** if the shop trades in pounds, every figure carries the wrong currency, and an owner reporting to an accountant or a bank will quote it. If it really is a US business, the metres and date style are what's odd.
   - **Fix:** use the shop's own currency symbol wherever money appears.
   - **Defect**, unless the business really does trade in dollars.

4. **The page reports numbers but never says what they mean.**
   - **Where:** the chart titles "Daily orders" and "Orders by weekday", and the KPI sublines.
   - **Chart titles:** both name the topic, not the finding. November ran above October all month, and the weekend is the peak, but the viewer has to work that out.
   - **KPI sublines:** "$24,110 in October" makes the reader subtract to learn revenue rose about 15%. All four metrics went up, and nothing on the page says so.
   - **Fix:** let each chart title state its finding, and show the change next to each KPI, using only figures the page already has. The words stay the maker's.
   - **Taste:** no approved source covers this, but on a page an owner checks monthly, the takeaway is the reason to open it.

**Smaller things.**
- **Line chart legend:** November and October are told apart only by blue shade, via a legend in the corner, and the pale October line looks weak against white. Label each line at its right end and darken or neutralize October (Tufte; WCAG 1.4.1, 1.4.11).
- **Color scheme (template tell):** both charts are drawn in shades of the accent, the stock dashboard look (unslop). Nothing in the color says "fabric shop", so the page looks like anyone's. Use neutral steps and keep the accent for the one thing that matters in each chart.
- **Uneven x ticks:** the line chart's x ticks sit at 1, 10, 19 and 30 Nov (gaps of 9, 9, 11). Space them evenly (Tufte).
- **Crowded corner:** the y label "0" and the x label "1 Nov" nearly touch at the line chart's bottom left. Pull them apart. This is common sense; no source needed.
- **Missing unit:** the line chart's y axis has no unit. Is it orders per day or per bucket? The title has to carry that once finding 1 is fixed (Few).

**What's working.**
- **The KPI row:** four equal cells, a large figure, a small label above and last month's figure beneath. It's the right structure, and putting October beside each number is exactly the context a headline figure needs.
- **The table:** numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures under right-aligned headers, rules are light, and the columns read cleanly. Keep it.
- **Restraint:** one sans serif in about four clearly different sizes, gray borders, no shadows or effects, and a sidebar that marks "Sales" as the current page. The calm is earned, not empty.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a control for changing the period. It looks like plain text; if it isn't clickable, the owner can't look at any other month.
- Focus states and how the layout reflows on narrow screens.
