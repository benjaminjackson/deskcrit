**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is an internal doc or web page that shows a project team the stages of a design project and what happens in each.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The type is clean, the levels are distinct and every line reads easily. Two things get in the way. The borders shout over the content, and nothing on the page tells the viewer that these five items are steps in order, so the one thing the page is for has to be inferred.

**What would move it most**
1. **The five card borders outweigh the content (convention).** Every stage sits inside a dark border about 3 px wide, the same near-black as the text. Ten dark horizontal bars are the strongest marks in the column, so the page reads as a stack of compartments before it reads as a list of stages. The text inside has to compete with its own frame. The 18 px gaps between items already separate them, so the boxes add weight without adding information. Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, says rules and boxes "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell"; Tufte makes the same case against non-data ink. Fix: remove the borders and let space separate the stages, or swap them for a pale tint or one hairline rule. Either way the stage names become the strongest things after the title.
2. **The sequence isn't shown, and the heading doesn't name it (defect).** Kickoff → Research → Sketches → Review → Handover is a process, and the copy itself depends on the order ("Pick one direction" only makes sense after "Draw two rough directions"). Yet the five items are visually equal, with no numbers, no connecting line and nothing to say "first, then". "Project notes" calls them loose notes, which works against reading them as steps. A stranger could take this for an unordered set of reminders. Fix: show the order with numbers on the stage names or a simple step marker. Then make the title name what the list holds, such as the project's stages or process (WCAG 2.4.6, headings describe their content). The exact wording is yours.

**What's working.**
- **A clear, small type scale.** I count three sizes: a title around 34 px, stage names around 20 px bold, and descriptions around 16 px regular. The steps between them are big, so each level has an obvious job. Keep this when you remove the boxes, because it's what will carry the hierarchy then.
- **The copy.** Each description is one short sentence that starts with a verb and says who does what ("Visit three sites and write up what the teams there need"). Nothing is left for the reader to fill in.
- **A disciplined column.** One left edge for the title, cards and text, with consistent padding and even gaps.
