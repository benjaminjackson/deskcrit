**First read.** My eye has nowhere to land. Eight lines of nearly the same gray text start at one left edge, so I read from the top as if it were one paragraph. Only the spacing tells me that "Changing your pickup time" is a question and not the first sentence. It reads as a bakery's help or FAQ page, and it feels calm, plain and a little unfinished.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help section on a bakery's online ordering site, for customers checking how pickup, collection, allergies and cancelling work.

**Verdict.** The page doesn't do its job well yet. A customer who comes with one question can't scan for it, because the four headings look the same as the answers. The words are mostly clear. The type gives the reader no structure to find their way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings are no different from the text they introduce.** Look at "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order". They're in the same face, weight and color as the answers, and they look about a pixel smaller. So headings and answers read as one group, and a viewer scanning for "cancel" has to read every line to find it. A heading the same size as its text, or within a pixel or two, is the worst case Dair describes: a difference that small looks like an accident, not a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). There's also no page title above the list, so nothing tells the viewer where to start or what page this is. Fix: make each heading differ from its answer in a way you see at a glance. A clearly heavier weight is the usual way, and a step up in size as well if you like. Then give the page one top-level title so the order is plain. *Defect.*
2. **"Up to a day ahead" can be read two ways, on the one line a customer will hold you to.** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" could mean 24 hours before the pickup time or any time before the day of pickup. A customer who reads it the second way and tries to cancel the evening before an early pickup gets refused, and that becomes an argument at the counter. The line also never says how to cancel, though the pickup-time answer does ("Open your order and…"). Fix: say what the day is measured against, and add the step, in the same way as the pickup answer. Only you know the actual rule. *Defect.*
3. **"Along with a name" leaves the stranger guessing whose name.** It could be the name on the order or the collector's own name. The person sent to pick up the bread has only this sentence, so they'll guess, and if they guess wrong they leave without the order. Fix: say whose name. Restoring the missing word is enough. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- The one-line answers ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run about 95 characters, past the 45 to 75 range (Bringhurst, 2.1.2). Narrow the column to roughly 65 characters. This also stops the two-line answers wrapping at a different point from the one-line ones.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Narrowing the column will probably fix it. If not, keep the last two words together.

**What's working.**
- The spacing is right. The gap between a heading and its answer (about 10 px) is clearly smaller than the gap between sections (about 25 px). That spacing is the only structure the page has now, so keep it when you strengthen the headings.
- The answers are short and written in plain, direct words. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" gives the reason for the rule in the customer's terms, so the rule feels fair rather than arbitrary.
- The single quiet typeface, the dark gray on white, and one left edge all suit a small bakery's help page. Fix the hierarchy without adding color or boxes.
