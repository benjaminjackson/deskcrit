**First read.** The eye goes to the headline, "Hold a real conversation in ten weeks", then drops straight to the orange "Start your free week" button. Then it trips on the gray "Keep your streak going" card under the button before it reaches the three steps. It reads as a consumer language-learning app's home page. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, but also a bit anonymous: the right half of the hero has nothing about this product in it.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: this is the marketing home page of a speaking-first language app. Its job is to get a first-time adult visitor to start the free week.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The promise is clear, there's one obvious action, and the three steps explain the method. Two things hold it back. Half the hero is spent on a generic chat-bubble picture instead of showing the thing that makes Parlo different. And a card meant for existing users sits right under the signup button.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero picture is a stand-in, not the product.** Two outlined chat bubbles with gray lines in a pale teal box take up the whole right half of the hero. They only repeat the word "conversation" from the headline and add nothing to it. This is the stock "empty box or generic picture as the hero visual" move (Sokolovsky, unslop): visitors have seen chat bubbles on every messaging, support and AI product, so Parlo looks like any of them. Meanwhile the page's real difference is spelled out in the copy: "a tutor that listens" and "feedback on each word". The visitor never sees that, so they have to take it on trust.
   - **Fix:** put a real preview of a lesson in the box, such as the speaking step with its word-by-word feedback, so the picture shows what the headline promises.
   - Template tell, and a missed opportunity for the main pitch.

2. **"Keep your streak going" is talking to the wrong person, in the most valuable spot.** The gray card sits between the button and the steps, the second thing the eye meets after the headline. A visitor who hasn't signed up has no streak, so the heading reads like an app notification that ended up on the wrong page. It also doesn't name what the card holds, which is a statistic (WCAG 2.4.6 asks headings to describe their content). The stat leaves the reader to fill a gap: "finish the course twice as often" as whom? Learners who practice less, or everyone else? A filled gray box also pulls attention from the button.
   - **Fix:** if the stat is meant to persuade new visitors, rewrite the heading so it states the finding to them, and say what "twice as often" is measured against. Otherwise move the card into the product. Either way, get it out of the gap under the button.
   - Defect.

3. **The commitment button doesn't say what happens after the free week.** "Start your free week" is the line visitors will later hold you to. It can be read two ways: the trial ends on its own, or it rolls into a paid plan. If they guess the first and get billed, you've lost their trust. "Ten weeks" in the headline has the same gap: ten weeks at what daily effort? "Short daily lessons" gives no number.
   - **Fix:** put the terms in one short line under the button, such as whether a card is needed and what happens on day 8, and give the daily time behind the ten-week claim. The facts are yours to supply.
   - Defect (Nielsen 6: the user shouldn't need to find this on the Pricing page).

**Smaller things.**
- The illustration box doesn't follow the grid. Its right edge stops about 150 px short of the right edge shared by the nav and the step row. Its left edge matches none of the three step columns, so it looks placed by eye and leaves a dead strip on the right (Müller-Brockmann). Fix: take its edges from the step-row columns and the page margin.
- The hero splits at about 620/676 px, a split that relates to nothing else on the page, while the step row below sits on clean thirds. Tie the hero split to those columns.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** It's a concrete outcome with a time frame, set large, bold and tight on two lines, so it's the obvious place to start.
- **One clear action.** The orange button is the only warm, saturated thing on a neutral page, so nothing competes with it. Keep it the only one.
- **The three steps.** "Listen / Speak / Use it", each with one plain sentence, explain the method in a few seconds. The thin rules and the shared left edges give them a calm rhythm.
