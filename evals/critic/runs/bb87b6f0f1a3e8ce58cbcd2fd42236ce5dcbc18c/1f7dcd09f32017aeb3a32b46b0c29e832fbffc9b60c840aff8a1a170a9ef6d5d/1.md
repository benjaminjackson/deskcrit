**First read.** The eye goes first to the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month." It then drops to the tall June bar and jumps right to "$4.82M." This reads as a credit union's half-year report page for its members. It feels calm, sober and trustworthy. Nothing fights for attention, and the page tells a simple growth story.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public member-report page, read on a desktop browser, that shows members how savings deposits grew in the first half of 2026 and why that's good news.

**Verdict.** This is a clean, well-built report page. It does its job. One thing could mislead: the biggest feature of the chart is a June jump that comes mostly from a one-off bonus payroll, and only a small gray footnote below the chart card says so. The "Average deposit" card also probably means something different from what its label says.

**What would move it most**
1. **The June spike gets explained only in fine print, outside the chart.** Each month from January to May adds about $40k to $80k. Then June jumps $282k, to $1,150k. Because that bar is the tallest, the eye goes there second, and the headline and the "$4.82M, up 6.1%" card make it read as strong ongoing growth. The reason, "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll," is the last clause of a small gray source line *below* the chart card, which most viewers won't read. A member who reads only the chart takes away a stronger trend than the money shows. Put the explanation at the bar: a short note on the June bar inside the card, or a different tone for the bonus part of that bar if you have the split. Then the source line only has to give the source. This is a **defect**: the chart leaves a false impression. The rule that the takeaway and key points belong on the chart itself is my own judgment, not a cited convention.
2. **"Average deposit, Jun 2026: $214" probably doesn't measure what it says.** $1,150k ÷ 5,374 accounts = $214. So the figure is June deposits per account that made a deposit, not the average size of one deposit. If any account deposited more than once in June, the label misnames the number, and members will remember and quote a wrong "typical deposit." The fix is to relabel it to say what was divided by what. It's only fine as it stands if every account made exactly one deposit, which only you can confirm. In the same cards, "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025" use "on" in the British sense of "compared with." A US reader can read "on Jun 2025" as a date. Changing it to "from June 2025" or "vs June 2025" removes the second reading. **Defect** (copy).

**Smaller things.**
- Numbers are in mixed formats: the June bar says "$1150k" (no thousands comma), the axis tops out at "$1200k," and the card says "$4.82M." Pick one money format for the page, e.g. $1.15M on the bar or a comma in the thousands (Sokolovsky, unslop).
- The small type uses four sizes about 1 px apart: the eyebrow and axis at ~13 px, card labels at ~14, the chart title at ~15, and the subtitle at ~16. These read as one level picked four times. Merge them into one or two sizes and let weight and color do the rest. Also, the ~32 px card figures match the headline's size, so "$4.82M" competes with the headline for first look. Making the figures a step smaller would keep the headline clearly on top (Bringhurst, scale).

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding as a full sentence with a verb, and the subtitle defines the measure and the period. A stranger knows what they're looking at in five seconds. Keep that pattern.
- The chart is honest and quiet: bars start at zero, there's one hue, gridlines are faint, the months are in order, values sit on the bars, and the chart title gives the unit ("US dollars per month"). There's nothing to decode.
- Each stat card pairs its number with a year-on-year comparison, and the "Up" deltas use both green and a word, so the comparison doesn't depend on color alone.
