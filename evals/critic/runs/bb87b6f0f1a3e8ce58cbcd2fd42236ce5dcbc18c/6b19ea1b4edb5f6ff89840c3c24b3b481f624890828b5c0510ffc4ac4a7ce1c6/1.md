**What would move it most**
1. **The purple-to-blue gradient on "Set up your harbour" and on berth S4** (template tell; Sokolovsky, unslop). The rest of the page has its own palette: sage berths, a sand-colored walkway, a warm off-white panel. It looks like a harbour chart. The gradient comes from a different visual world, and visitors have seen it on a hundred other software sites. So the two most important marks, the main button and the booked berth, are the ones that look borrowed, and the page loses the local, nautical character that sets it apart. Fix: take the gradient out of both. Pick one flat accent that belongs with the sage and sand palette, and use it for both the button and the selected berth so they still visibly match.

2. **Green means "taken" in the berth plan** (convention: Apple HIG and Material on status colors; defect for this audience). Booked berths are filled sage green, and free ones (N3, S6, W5) are white with a dashed border. Most viewers read green as "available", and a harbour manager looking at a plan will too. The first reading is "lots of free berths", which the "18 berths, 3 free" counter then contradicts. There's also no key, so the viewer has to work out the code from that count. Fix: make taken berths a neutral or muted fill and let the free berths carry the signal, or add a small key next to "18 berths, 3 free".

3. **The ask gives the visitor nothing to compare against what they use now** (copy). "Open a sample pontoon" is a good, specific second action, and it lets the visitor judge the product with their own eyes. But nothing on the screen sets Moorly against how a harbour office works today, whether that's a paper plan, a spreadsheet or phone bookings. A manager has to imagine the gain without help. The third feature, "A plan the harbour master can print", comes closest. Direction: somewhere near the hero, name the tool or routine this replaces. That fact has to come from you.

**Smaller things**
