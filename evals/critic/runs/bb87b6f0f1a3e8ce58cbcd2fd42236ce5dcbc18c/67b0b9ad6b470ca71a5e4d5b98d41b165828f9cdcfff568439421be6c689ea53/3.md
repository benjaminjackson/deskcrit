**First read.** The eye goes first to the bold title, "Trial to paid conversion by plan", then to the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%. From there it moves along the four labeled bars and over to the small table on the right. It reads as a clean, calm internal analytics page. It looks trustworthy and finished, but it is quiet: it shows numbers and leaves you to decide what they mean.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal dashboard page for product, growth or finance people who want to know how well each plan's trials convert in Q3 2025, and which plan is doing best or worst.

**Verdict.** This is competent and honest charting. It doesn't yet do the analyst's job: it never says what the numbers mean, it gives nothing to compare them against, and its definition can be read two ways. The biggest gap is the missing meaning. A reader sees four bars between 3.1% and 4.0% and can't tell whether that is good, bad, changed, or just noise.

**What would move it most**
1. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** Defect, argued as common sense. "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells the viewer what the chart is about and leaves them to work out the conclusion. With bars this close (3.1, 3.6, 4.0, 3.4), different readers will reach different conclusions, or none. The direction: let the title or a one-line caption say what the maker wants the viewer to take away (which plan leads, and whether the gap matters). Leave the current line as the subtitle that defines the measure. Only the maker knows what that takeaway is.
2. **The rates come with no comparison.** Defect (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte). Each rate has a unit and a period but nothing to judge it against: no previous quarter, no target, no trend. On a page called Conversion, "is 3.1% for Starter good?" is the first question, and the page can't answer it. Fix: put a comparison next to each rate, such as the change since the last period or the target (Few). That could be a ghost bar or tick for Q2 on each bar, or a delta beside each value label. Which comparison to use is the maker's call.
3. **The definition gives two readings of the conversion window.** Defect: this is the number people will quote. The subtitle says "became paid accounts within 30 days". The definition says "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." The definition can be read two ways:
   - Each trial gets its own 30 days.
   - Every trial has until one cutoff date, so a July trial gets three months and a late-September trial gets one.

   If the second reading is true, plans whose trials cluster early in the quarter look better than they are. If the first is true, the definition's sentence is confusing more than it is clarifying. Fix: restate the rule so it says who gets how long, ending at what point. For example, keep "within 30 days of the trial start" and say what the 30 October date is for, if anything.
4. **The chart ranks plans more firmly than the counts support.** Common sense, backed by Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16 on showing uncertainty. The table gives the sample sizes, which is good: Enterprise has 38 paid out of 1,120 trials. At that size, Enterprise's 3.4% could plausibly sit anywhere from the mid-2s to the mid-4s. Team's 3.6% vs. Enterprise's 3.4% is probably no real difference. Bars of exact heights with crisp one-decimal labels invite the viewer to read a ranking that may not exist. Fix: show the range on each bar, such as a thin interval line. Or at least say in the caption which differences are big enough to trust.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title (cap top around y≈97) and "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" (around y≈106) sit a few pixels apart. They look like they were meant to line up but don't, so the two columns don't start on a shared line. Align their tops or baselines.
- The table is named "Trials by plan" but also holds Paid counts. A Rate column would tie it to the chart, so the reader doesn't have to match the two by eye. That is taste.
- The value labels on the bars make most of the y-axis redundant. Dropping the axis or thinning it to 0% and one or two ticks would quiet the chart further. That is taste, not a defect.

**What's working.**
- The chart is drawn honestly and simply. Bars start at zero, there is one color, gridlines are faint, every bar is labeled at its data, and there is no legend. Keep this restraint when adding comparisons and ranges.
- The counts are on the page, and the rates match them exactly (286/9,210 = 3.1%, 38/1,120 = 3.4%). The sample sizes are right next to the rates. That is the right habit, and it is what makes finding 4 fixable.
- The source line and the definition block are the right supporting pieces. Once the window wording is fixed, they will do real work.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the top navigation, and whether the bars show more detail on hover.
