**First read.** My eye goes first to the four purple bars and then to the tallest one, Growth at 4.0%. After that it moves up to the title and across to the small table on the right. It reads as an internal analytics page for a SaaS team. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy: one color, light gridlines, honest axis. The page shows numbers but never says what they mean, so after a few seconds I have the figures but no conclusion.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal report page for product and growth people. They need to compare trial-to-paid conversion across plans and decide whether any plan needs attention.

**Verdict.** It's a well-made, honest chart. As a report it stops one step short. It never says what the comparison shows or whether the gaps are real, and it defines the metric in two ways that disagree. A reader who acts on "Growth converts best" may be acting on noise.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page shows the gaps but not whether they are real (defect).** In the chart, the four bars span only 3.1% to 4.0%. The table gives the sample sizes: Enterprise rests on 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials, Growth on 118 out of 2,950. At those counts, a gap of a few tenths of a point between Enterprise (3.4%) and Team (3.6%), or Enterprise and Starter (3.1%), may not mean anything. Even Growth's lead may not hold up. The chart draws every bar as an exact, equally solid value, so the viewer will rank the plans and may act on that ranking. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says to show a sample with its uncertainty. Fix: put a range on each bar, or at least a note near the chart saying which differences hold up. Keep the bars as they are.

2. **The title names the topic, not the finding (common sense; the source for this rule isn't on the approved list).** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" says what the chart is about. It leaves the reader to work out what it shows, and with bars this close together, different readers will reach different conclusions. Fix: once item 1 tells you what is actually true, state that finding in the title or the subtitle. Saying "Growth converts best" or "no plan stands out" is the maker's call, and it must match the uncertainty.

3. **Two definitions of the 30-day window (defect: a line the reader will act on that can be read two ways).** The subtitle, under the title, says trials "became paid accounts within 30 days". The definition, on the right, says "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." Reading one: each trial gets 30 days from its own start date. Reading two: every Q3 trial has until 30 October, so a 1 July trial gets about four months and a 30 September trial gets 30 days. Under reading two, the rates mix very different windows, and a plan whose trials started early in the quarter looks better than it is. Fix: make both lines state the same rule. If it is per trial, say "within 30 days of starting" in the definition and drop or explain the 30 October date. If it is a cutoff, take "within 30 days" out of the subtitle.

4. **No point of comparison (convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").** Every rate stands alone. There is no previous quarter, no target, no trend, so the reader can't tell whether 3.1% for Starter is a problem or normal. Fix: put a comparison next to each rate, such as last quarter's value as a tick or a faint bar, or the target. Then the page answers "is this good?" and not only "what is it?".

**Smaller things.**
- The table on the right gives Trials and Paid but no rate, so anyone checking the chart has to do the division. A rate column would tie the two halves together.

**What's working.**
- **Honest chart.** The axis starts at zero, the gridlines are pale and clearly behind the data, there is one color for one series, and the values are labeled on the bars. Tufte and Wilke would approve, so keep all of it.
- **The numbers add up, and the chart cites its source.** The table counts reproduce every bar exactly (286 / 9,210 = 3.1%, 38 / 1,120 = 3.4%), and the source line names the data and the date range. That is why the page reads as trustworthy.
- **Clear structure.** The chart sits on the left with the table and definition on the right. The nav shows the current page (Conversion, bold and purple), and the type uses a few clearly different levels. Nothing competes with the chart.
