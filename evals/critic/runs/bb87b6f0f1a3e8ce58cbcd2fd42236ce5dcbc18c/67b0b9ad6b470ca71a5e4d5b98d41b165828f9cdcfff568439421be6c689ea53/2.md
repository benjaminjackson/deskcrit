**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title, then straight to the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%, then across the other three. It reads as a clean product-analytics dashboard page for a growth or product team: calm, credible, and well made. Then it stops. Nothing says whether 4.0% is good, or whether a gap of under one point between plans means anything, so you're left with the question "so what?"

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal analytics page that shows a product or growth team how trial-to-paid conversion differs by plan, so they can decide where to act.

**Verdict.** It's well crafted and honest, but it stops at showing the numbers and doesn't help anyone act on them. The biggest problem is that it presents small differences with no comparison and no sense of how reliable they are, so a reader will rank the plans on gaps the data may not support.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bars look more certain than the data is.** The four bars run from 3.1% to 4.0%, and the eye reads them as a clear ranking. The table shows how thin some of that is: Enterprise is 38 conversions out of 1,120 trials. Five conversions either way would move its bar by about 0.4 points, which is enough to reorder it against Team and Starter. Only Growth vs. Starter has enough volume behind it to look solid. A reader who sees "Growth best, Enterprise behind Team" will act on a ranking that may be noise. This is a defect: it misleads the intended viewer (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Fix: show the range on each bar, such as error bars or a confidence band, or at least mark the low-volume plans in a note at the bar, so the reader can see which gaps are real.
2. **No number has anything to be compared with.** "4.0%" sits alone. There's no previous quarter, no target, and no overall rate across all plans, so the viewer can't tell whether any plan is doing well or badly, or whether anything changed. The Conversion tab is presumably where someone comes to find out whether things are getting better. This is a defect (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). Fix: put a comparison next to each rate, such as the change from the previous quarter or the target, or add a reference line for the all-plans rate. The maker has to supply which comparison is right.
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells you what the axes are, which the chart already shows. The viewer still has to work out the point. I'm marking this taste: the source for this rule isn't on my approved list, but it's common sense on an analysis page. Once findings 1 and 2 tell you what's real, the title or subtitle should state the takeaway in words, in terms of what it means for the reader's decision. Don't claim more than the uncertainty allows.
4. **The conversion window is described two ways.** The subtitle says paid "within 30 days." The Definition says trials "still open on 30 October are counted as not converted." Those are two different rules. Take a trial that started on 1 July and paid on 15 August (day 45). It converted under the 30 October cutoff but not under the 30-day rule. The source line adds a third window, "1 July to 30 September," which suggests payments after September aren't in the data at all. These dates decide what the numbers mean, so the ambiguity is a defect: whoever reads it the wrong way will compare these figures with another report and get a mismatch. Fix: an editorial pass so all three lines describe one rule, for example by saying in the Definition that each trial gets 30 days and that 30 October is simply when the last window closes, if that's the intent.

**Smaller things.**
- The table's "Paid" column has no window or unit. Change the header so it names what's being counted, for example by matching whatever the subtitle settles on. I'd also add a rate column, so the exact percentages can be looked up next to their counts.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and lean. The bars start at zero, gridlines are pale, values are labeled directly above each bar, and plans stay in their natural tier order rather than being re-sorted. Keep all of that.
- The rates match the table (286/9,210 is 3.1%, 38/1,120 is 3.4%, and so on), and the table is right there. A skeptical reader can check the chart, and that's exactly where the uncertainty fix in finding 1 will plug in.
- The type is restrained. There are three or four clear sizes, numbers are tabular and right-aligned, and the one accent color is used only for the bars and the current nav item.
