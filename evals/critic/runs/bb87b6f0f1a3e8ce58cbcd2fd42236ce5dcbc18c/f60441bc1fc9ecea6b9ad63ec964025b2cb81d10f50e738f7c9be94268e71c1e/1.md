**First read.** The eye goes first to the three big numbers (3.8%, €64.20, 12.4k), then drops to the orange bars and lands on the tall Friday and Saturday pair and the short Sunday bar. It reads as a calm, well-made owner's dashboard for a small bakery that delivers. It feels tidy and trustworthy, and the orange of the brand name carries into the bars.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the shop owner checks this to see how the business is doing and to plan baking and delivery capacity by day.

**Verdict.** The layout, type, and KPI cards do their job well. The main chart doesn't: it compares raw September totals across weekdays that occur a different number of times, so it shows the owner the wrong weekday pattern. That is the one thing to fix.

**What would move it most**
1. **The weekday chart compares unequal samples, so it misleads (defect).** September 2026 starts on a Tuesday, so it has five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of every other day. The bars are monthly totals, so Tuesday and Wednesday get an extra day's orders each. Reading the bars roughly by eye, the per-day picture is different: Monday comes out around 160 orders per delivery day, ahead of Tuesday (about 142) and Wednesday (about 152). The neat steady climb from Monday to Thursday that the chart shows isn't real. Tuesday and Wednesday are the weakest weekdays. An owner who plans staff or bake volume from this chart would plan for the wrong days. The size of the effect shown doesn't match the size in the data (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2, graphical integrity). Fix: plot the average orders per delivery day, or use a window with equal counts of each weekday, and say which one it is in the title or source line.
2. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding (taste and common sense; the catalog source here isn't on the approved list).** "Orders by delivery day" tells the owner what the chart is, but they still have to work out what it means. The point that matters for planning is the weekend peak and the Sunday drop, and once fix 1 is in, whatever the corrected weekday pattern turns out to be. That point is left for them to find. Fix: once the data is corrected, have the title or a one-line caption state what the owner should take away, using the corrected numbers. Don't write it from the current bars, because they would give the wrong takeaway.

**Smaller things.**
- The three cards use two comparison bases: "vs previous 30 days" twice and "vs 31 August" once. That's defensible for a point-in-time count like subscribers, but the different wording makes the viewer stop and check whether the three numbers cover the same period. Keep the wording as parallel as the data allows.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards.** Each figure is large and heavy, and its unit (%, €, k) is set smaller and lighter, so the number reads first and the unit doesn't compete. Each one comes with its period and a change against an earlier period, marked by a triangle and a + sign as well as green, so the meaning doesn't depend on color alone. Keep this structure.
- **Restraint.** There's one accent hue, used for the brand name and the data, on neutral grays. Gridlines are faint, there are no shadows or effects, and the source line names the system and the period. Almost everything on the screen is data or labels for data.
- **The grid.** The cards and the chart panel share the same left and right edges and gutters, and the current page is clear in the nav. The page reads as one composed whole.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the nav links, and whether the chart bars offer exact values on hover. Without that, the owner can't read an exact daily count anywhere.
