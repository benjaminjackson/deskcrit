**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, which is huge and black. It drops to the gray subline, then stops at the blue button. After that there is nothing: the right half of the page and the bottom third are blank. It reads as the landing page for a team-planning SaaS tool. It feels clean, confident and finished, but also bare, like a template with the picture left out.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero of a marketing homepage for Weekplan, and its job is to get team leads or managers to start a trial.

**Verdict.** The words do their job but the page doesn't. It promises "one honest picture of the next five days" and then shows no picture, so visitors have to take the main claim on trust while half the screen sits empty. Fix that first. Everything else is small.

**What would move it most**
1. **The promised picture is missing, and the layout leans because of it** (defect). All the weight sits in the upper left: the headline, the subline and the button end around y=530 and x≈1030. The right half and the bottom 270 px hold nothing. That empty space doesn't read as planned; it looks like the spot where a picture should be. Arnheim's point in *Art and Visual Perception* ("Balance") applies here: a heavy side needs a counterweight, and here nothing balances it. The cost to the viewer is bigger than the balance problem. The subline makes a concrete, checkable claim (tickets, meetings and leave turned into one picture of the week), and the one thing a visitor wants next is to see that picture. Leaving out the product view is a known template move (Sokolovsky, unslop: the hero visual should be "a product preview or an image from the product's world"). It also means Weekplan looks like any other plain hero. Fix: put a real Weekplan week view in the empty area, to the right of the text or below it, ideally with all three inputs from the subline (a ticket, a meeting, someone on leave) visible in it. That counterweights the headline and shows the claim at the same moment.

2. **The button asks for commitment before giving visitors a way to judge the value** (defect, copy). "Start a free trial" could be on any SaaS page. It doesn't say how long the trial is, what it costs to start, or what the visitor will see first. The headline sets up a good comparison: the week as planned versus the week "your team actually works". But nothing on the page lets visitors make that comparison against what they already have, such as their own tickets and calendar. Fix: point the button at the specific first result the visitor gets, and say what the trial involves. Those facts (trial length, whether a card is needed, what connects first) only you have, so I'm not writing the line.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks inside the clause, "your team / actually works", so the first line reads "Plan the week your team" on its own. Breaking after "week" keeps the phrase "your team actually works" together. Judging where a line breaks this way rests on reading the words; no source confirms it.
- The headline looks set at weight 800 or so. That extra-heavy grotesque is a stock template move (Sokolovsky, unslop: "Headings at weight 800 or 900"). Its size already makes it dominant, so a lighter weight would keep the rank and look less like anyone else's page. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- The copy has a real point of view. "The week your team actually works" admits that plans and reality differ, and "one honest picture" names exactly what the product produces. Keep both lines as they are.
- The hierarchy is clear. There are three distinct levels (headline, subline, button) with big steps between them. The left edge is flush and shared by the logo, the headline, the subline and the button. The subline runs about 60 characters per line.
- There is one primary action in one accent color, and the nav links stay quiet, so nothing competes with the button.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether there is content further down that answers "show me", and whether the button's focus state is visible.
