**First read.** The eye lands on the title "New sign-ups by channel", drops straight to the tall Organic search bar, then steps down the staircase of bars to Events. Only after that does it find the right-hand column. That column holds the actual point: organic search grew after the guide pages were rewritten, and the events budget is moving to referrals. It reads as a calm, competent internal growth report for a marketing or leadership audience. The design is clean and quiet, maybe too quiet about its own conclusion.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a quarterly growth page that tells the team which channels brought sign-ups in Q3 and backs up a budget decision for Q4.

**Verdict.** The chart is honest and well made, but it doesn't make the page's argument. The finding and the decision sit in a side column the eye reaches last. The chart shows only Q3, so the "up from a quarter in Q2" claim can't be seen in it at all. This is solid work held back by a title and a chart that describe the data instead of saying what it means.

**What would move it most**
1. **The title labels the topic, and the finding sits off to the side.** "New sign-ups by channel" tells the viewer what the bars are, which the axis already tells them. The two things a reader needs, that organic search now brings a third of sign-ups and that events money is moving to referrals, are in a small 15 px-looking column at the right that the eye reaches last. Someone skimming the page sees "here is a chart" rather than "here is what happened and what we're doing about it." Direction: move the finding into the title or subtitle, so the title states the takeaway and the chart becomes the evidence. The sidebar's own first sentence already has the words for it, so this is an edit of existing copy, not new copy. Taste, argued as common sense: a reader who stops at the title should still leave with the point.
2. **No comparison, so the chart can't show the change the text claims.** Every bar is Q3 alone. The sidebar says organic search went "up from a quarter in Q2", but nothing on the chart lets the viewer see that rise or check how any other channel moved. With no comparison, 1,840 or 260 can't be read as good or bad. Direction: put the previous period next to each bar, as a paler Q2 bar or a marker for the Q2 value, so the shift the text describes can be seen on the chart (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*). Convention.
3. **The chart gives no emphasis to what the argument is about.** All six bars are the same steel blue, so Organic search, the channel behind the Q4 decision (Podcast ads plus Events), and the referral channel the budget is moving to all look equally important. Direction: keep one color, mute the bars that don't matter to the argument, and keep the full color only on the bars the text talks about. Taste.

**Smaller things.**
- The "What changed this quarter" heading (at about y 174) lines up with nothing on the left: not the title, not the subtitle, not the chart's top gridline (about y 195). The column looks dropped in rather than placed. Align its top to the chart's top or to the subtitle (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and well set up. The bars start at zero, are sorted by value, are labeled directly with their exact counts, and sit on gridlines pale enough that the data is the darkest thing in the chart. Keep this restraint if you add a Q2 comparison.
- The numbers hold together. The six values add up to the 5,400 in the source line, 1,840 really is about a third, and 410 + 260 really is under 700. A reader who checks will trust the page.
- The source line gives the system, the exact date range, and the total, which is exactly the context a growth report needs.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Nav link target sizes and focus states. The links look well spaced, but measure them in the browser.
