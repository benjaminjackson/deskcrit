**First read.** My eye goes to the bold sans heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then straight down into a calm serif column of four paragraphs. It reads as an information page from a small river ferry operator: quiet, tidy and trustworthy, like a well-set notice board. There's nothing to fight through. It feels considered, a little bookish, and unhurried.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells current and would-be passengers how the ferry runs: when it goes, how long it takes, what they can bring, and where to wait.

**Verdict.** The typography does its job well. The page fails passengers on content: it never gives the times they need to catch a boat, and the facts they would act on are buried in prose with no way to scan for them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule has no times** (defect, copy). Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", and paragraph 3 says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger can't plan around "early morning" or "before dusk". The last-boat line is the one people will hold the operator to. It can be read as "a fixed time that happens to fall before dusk" or as "a time that changes daily with sunset", and if they guess wrong they're stranded on the wrong bank. The page then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which they can only read once they're already there. Direction: give the first and last departure times, the winter last-departure time (or the rule that sets it), and the date winter service starts. Only the operator has these facts.
2. **Rules that matter are hidden in running prose** (taste). A passenger comes back to this page with one question, like "can I bring my bike?" or "when's the last boat in winter?", and has to read paragraphs to find the answer. The four paragraphs already cover four separate topics: crossings, bicycles, winter service, and landings. Short subheads or bold lead-ins naming each topic would let people jump straight to their answer. Keep the serif body and the calm tone, which suit the piece.
3. **"Benches face the water while the boat is loading"** (defect, copy, minor but real). As written, it says the benches only face the water while the boat loads. A reader can tell that isn't the intent, but the sentence makes them stop and work it out. Reordering fixes it: "While the boat is loading, you can wait on benches facing the water." The same goes for "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle". Saying it to the reader ("keep your bags on your bicycle") makes it read as an instruction to them, not a description of the crew.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 2 ends with "crossing." alone on its last line, a short-word runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). It's minor on the web, but a non-breaking space before the last word, or a small rewrap, would fix it.
- The lines look to be at the top of the 45–75 character range (about 72 on the third line). That's fine now, but don't let the column get any wider.

**What's working.**
- **The type pairing.** A bold grotesque heading over a warm text serif gives two clearly different levels, with contrast in weight, form and size, so there's no doubt where to start. Keep that, and add no more sizes.
- **The body setting.** The text size is comfortable, the leading is generous, the paragraph spacing is clear, the rag is even, and the contrast is near-black on white. It's easy, pleasant reading.
- **The restraint.** There are no boxes, rules or ornaments, and the empty space is gathered on the right and at the bottom instead of scattered. That gives the page the calm of a notice.
