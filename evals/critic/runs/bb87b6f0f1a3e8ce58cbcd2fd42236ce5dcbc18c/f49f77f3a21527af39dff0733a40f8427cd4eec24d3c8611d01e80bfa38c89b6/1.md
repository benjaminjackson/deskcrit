**First read.** The eye goes first to the one saturated mark on the page, the blue Saturday bar, then up to the row of four big numbers, then to the two blue-and-gray lines. It reads as a calm, tidy monthly report for a small café or bakery owner, Halfmoon Bakery. It's neutral and easy to scan. Nothing shouts, and nothing seems wrong until you start adding up the numbers.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner opens this to see in a few seconds how November went against October and where the business comes from. That means the figures have to agree with each other and be trusted at a glance.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and conventional and would work, but the numbers contradict each other, and on a dashboard that is the failure that counts most. Once the owner sees that the charts don't match the headline figures, they can't trust any number here.

**What would move it most**

1. **The charts and the headline figures disagree.** *Defect.*
   - The Tickets card says 1,032 tickets for November.
   - The "Daily tickets" chart puts every day between about 400 and 700 on a 0–900 axis, which would be 15,000 or more for the month.
   - The "Tickets by weekday" bars are unlabeled except Saturday's 148. Read against that, they add up to about 700, not 1,032.
   - "Daily tickets" draws about 11 points across 30 days, so it isn't daily either.

   An owner who checks one number against another finds they don't reconcile, and from then on doubts all of them, including the true ones. Tufte's graphical integrity principle (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2) is the rule here: what a chart shows has to match the data. If this is placeholder data, it's fine only once real data is wired in. Before it ships, check that each chart sums to its card. Then either plot a point for every day or rename the chart for what it plots.

2. **Two charts don't say what their marks measure.** *Defect, by the convention in Tufte ch. 2 and Few, Show Me the Numbers.*
   - "Tickets by weekday" has no axis and only one value label. The viewer can't tell whether a bar is a month's total for that weekday or an average day. That's exactly the gap that hides the mismatch in finding 1.
   - The daily chart's y-axis has no unit.

   Direction: label each weekday bar with its value, which is cheap with seven bars. State the measure in the title, for example whether it's an average or a total. Then the two charts and the Tickets card can be checked against each other.

3. **The Average ticket comparison hides the change it exists to show.** *Defect.* The card reads "$9 / $9 in October". From the page's own figures, the values are about $9.17 and $8.93. Rounding to whole dollars turns a real rise in average spend into "no change". That is the one KPI where cents matter to a café (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": a headline number needs a comparison the reader can use). Direction: show cents on this card. On all four cards, consider showing the change itself next to the October figure, so the owner doesn't have to subtract in their head.

**Smaller things.**
- "Loyalty card visits 36%" doesn't say 36% of what: tickets, customers, or visits. Restore the missing words, such as "share of tickets with a loyalty card" or whichever is true (WCAG 2.4.6).
- The nav icons come in five colors (blue, orange, green, pink, gray), and the Menu icon is filled and heavier than the outline icons. They look collected rather than designed, a stock template move. Use one set, one weight, and the text color, and mark the active item by its background as it already is.
- The Saturday highlight uses the same blue as the "November" line, so it reads as part of that series. That's harmless here because both are November, but only by accident. Pick a highlight that doesn't share a series color, or accept the link on purpose.
- The chart row splits at about 891/908 px, matching none of the KPI dividers at about 511, 756, and 1002 px. The table's Category column at about 689 px lines up with nothing either. Put the cards and columns on one set of column lines (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).
- The daily chart's legend sits top right, away from its lines. Put "November" and "October" at the right ends of the lines themselves (Tufte; Wilke).
- The chart titles name topics, not findings. This is taste: "Saturday is the busiest day" or "November ran ahead of October every day" would do the reading for the owner, once the data is right.

**What's working.**
- The KPI strip pairs every figure with its October value in small gray text directly below it. That's the right structure for a monthly report, so keep it and only fix the rounding.
- Gray for October and context, blue for November and the one standout bar. This restraint means the single highlight actually lands.
- The best-sellers table is quiet: right-aligned numbers, thin row rules, no zebra striping or boxes. It reads as easily as a printed report.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the date range "1 to 30 November 2026" can be changed, and whether the charts show exact values on hover. Both matter for checking the reconciliation problem above.
