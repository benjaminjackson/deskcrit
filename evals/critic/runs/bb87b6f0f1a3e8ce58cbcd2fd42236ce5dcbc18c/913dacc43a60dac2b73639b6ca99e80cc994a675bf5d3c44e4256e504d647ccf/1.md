**First read.** The eye lands on the three blue bars, then goes up to the title "Rainfall at the reservoir", then drifts right to "Getting there". The bars give no numbers, no units, and no names, so after a few seconds you know rainfall is the topic and nothing else. The block beside the chart is about buses and parking, and because it touches the chart it reads at first as the chart's caption. It feels like an unfinished page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public information page for a reservoir site that shows visitors recent rainfall and tells them how to get there.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The chart is the biggest thing on the page and it says nothing. A viewer can't learn how much rain fell, when, or whether that's a lot. On top of that, the directions are jammed against the chart, so the two unrelated topics blur into one.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart has no data a reader can see.** There's no value axis, no numbers, no unit (mm? inches?), and no labels on the three bars (months? years? gauges?). The reader can only tell that the middle bar is tallest, but not by how much, in what period, or compared with what. Every chart needs labeled axes or direct labels, with units, a readable scale, and a date or source, so the reader never has to guess what a number measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). Without a scale you also can't confirm that the bars start at zero (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, 17.1). You can't tell whether three bars cover enough time to mean anything, or whether they're in a natural order such as time. Fix: name each bar under it, put the value with its unit on or above each bar, and state the period. **Defect.**
2. **Neither the title nor the chart states a finding.** "Rainfall at the reservoir" names a topic. Even with labels, the viewer is left to work out what the bars mean for them: is the reservoir full, is this wetter than usual, does it affect a visit? Fix: once the data is labeled, turn the title or a one-line caption into the finding the bars show, put in terms of what it means for the visitor. Comparing against something visitors already know, such as a normal year or last year, would let them judge for themselves. That comparison is the maker's to supply. **Taste**, argued on common sense: a chart with no stated finding asks every reader to do the analysis.
3. **The directions block touches the chart and has no space of its own.** "Getting there" starts at the chart's right border (around x=640) with no gap. Its heading lines up with the top of the chart's frame. Its column width matches nothing else on the page. With no gutter, the directions read as part of the chart, and two unrelated groups merge into one. The gap between groups should be clearly larger than the gaps inside them (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, ch. II). Blocks should also take their edges from one grid (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*). Fix: give the directions a real gutter on a shared column grid, or move them into their own section below the chart. They're a separate topic, so their own section is probably right. **Defect.**

**Smaller things.**
- The chart sits in a 3 px near-black frame on a blue-gray tint. That frame is the strongest line in the chart, stronger than the baseline, and it fights the bars. Remove the frame, or lighten it to a pale tone, so the bars are the darkest marks (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, 23.2).

**What's working.**
- The type is clean and well set. One sans family carries three clear levels: a bold title of about 30 px, a bold heading of about 20 px, and body text of about 17 px. The body runs about 45 characters per line with comfortable line spacing. Keep this scale.
- One restrained blue for one series, on a neutral page, is the right amount of color. Once the chart has labels, it won't need more.
- The directions are good copy: specific and concrete (bus number, gate, frequency, the time parking becomes free, where the cycle racks are). Keep that level of detail.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the chart was meant to have labels or tooltips that didn't render, or only show on hover. Check in a live browser. Even if hover labels exist, the static view needs a scale and units.
