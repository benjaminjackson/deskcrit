I've finished the checklist and the deep read, and I'm writing up the critique now.

**First read.** My eye lands first on the headline, "Quote a bathroom from the doorway". It then crosses to the quote table on the right, drops to the brown button, and ends on the three short features at the bottom. In about two seconds it reads as a quoting tool for tilers. It feels calm, warm and trade-like, closer to a well-made invoice than to a tech startup.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the home page hero of a quoting app for self-employed tilers, and it should get them to start a first quote.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The page has one clear focal point, copy that sounds like the trade, one primary action and a real example of what you get. What holds it back is the quote panel. It is the only picture on the page, and it proves the itemized quote but not the two promises the headline and paragraph actually make: you send it from the doorway, and the customer accepts it from a link.

**What would move it most**
1. **The quote panel stops short of the claim.** The headline sells speed on site, and the paragraph ends on "the customer accepts it from the link". The panel shows a finished line-item table and then "Sent 14:12, opened 14:30".
   - A tiler sees a tidy quote, which any spreadsheet can make. Nothing in the panel shows that it was made in the doorway.
   - The story ends at "opened", one step before the payoff the copy promised. The viewer has to take the best part on trust instead of seeing it.
   - **Direction:** let the panel's last state be the one the paragraph ends on. If the product records acceptance, show it. If the maker has a true time for the visit or the measuring, put it next to "Sent" so the speed can be judged and not just claimed. I'm leaving the facts to the maker.
   - *Taste*, but it is the piece's main argument.
2. **Half a fake window around the quote.** In the top right there are three gray "traffic light" dots and a faintly tinted title bar with a full-width rule under it. Below that there is no edge, background or shadow, so the frame starts and then disappears into the page.
   - The dots do no work except to say "screenshot of a desktop app".
   - That cuts against the story the page tells. The tiler is standing in a doorway, and the third feature says "the customer reads the quote on a phone".
   - **Direction:** remove the dots and the tint. Either present the quote plainly as the document the customer receives, keeping the "Quote 118, Mrs Okafor, Lime Street" line as its title, or frame it as the thing the copy describes.
   - *Taste / common sense*. The catalog's sources for "every ornament does a job" aren't on the approved list.

**What's working.**
- **The copy speaks the trade's language and ends on what it means for the viewer.** Examples: "so you do not run short on day two", "You get a text when they do", "Set your day rate and the tiles you buy most once". The headline makes a specific, physical promise rather than a category claim. Keep this voice through any revision.
- **The hierarchy is restrained and clear.** There is one large dark headline, a single terracotta button as the only saturated color, and an underlined secondary link that is plainly lower in rank. The eye never has to choose where to start.
- **The sample quote is real data, set well.** It uses true units (m², × in tile sizes), prices right-aligned in tabular figures, a total that adds up ($1,731.00), and a named customer and street. It makes the product concrete in a way a generic dashboard picture wouldn't.
