**Verdict.** The typography is calm, well-proportioned and does its job. As an invitation, though, it stops short. It makes you want to turn up but doesn't tell you when, where or how. That missing information is the biggest problem. The layout is secondary.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invitation has no specifics a stranger could act on (defect, copy).** The right paragraph makes the ask: "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings… a spare apron on the hook by the door for anyone who wants to help." A reader who wants to come still has to guess three things:
   - **What time:** "Thursday evenings" has no hour.
   - **Where:** "the end of the lane" names no lane, town or shed.
   - **How:** there is no contact, no "just turn up," and no link.

   These are the details people act on and hold you to. Someone who shows up at 6 when you meet at 7:30, or can't find the lane, won't come back. The copy is lovely and should stay as it is. Add the missing facts in the same plain voice, either as one closing line under the right paragraph or as a short "Thursdays, [time], [place]" line set apart below the two columns. Only you have those facts. Putting them in a separate block also gives the eye a destination after the paragraphs.

**Smaller things.**
- **The page looks top-heavy and unfinished (convention, Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"):** all the content sits in the top ~300 px, and the lower ~480 px is empty, so it reads as a page that stopped loading. If more content follows below the fold, ignore this. If it doesn't, the block of practical details from finding 1 is the natural counterweight. Otherwise, move the text block lower in the frame.

**What's working.**
- **A clean two-level hierarchy:** a bold display headline and a regular-weight text serif from what looks like one family. The contrast comes from size and weight together, so there's no doubt where to start and nothing competing with it. Keep it to these two levels when you add the details line, and don't introduce a third size for it.
