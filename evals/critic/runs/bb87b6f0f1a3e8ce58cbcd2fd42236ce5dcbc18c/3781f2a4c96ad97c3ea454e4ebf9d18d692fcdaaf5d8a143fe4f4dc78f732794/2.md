**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project" at top left, drops to the two-line promise under it, then moves right into the white card and down three fields to the blue "Send message" button. It's clearly a design studio's contact page, aimed at a prospective client. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, with nothing in the way. It also feels a little generic.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the contact page of a small design studio, and its job is to get a prospective client to send a first inquiry.

**Verdict.** It does its job: you can tell in a second what it is, what to do and what happens next. The weak spots are faint field borders and a right edge that matches nothing else on the page. Neither breaks the page, but both make it look less finished than it should for a studio selling design.

**What would move it most**
1. **The field borders look too faint.** This is a defect. The gray outlines on the three inputs look roughly #AAB0BC on white. That's probably under the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for on the edges of form fields. Someone with low vision, or anyone on a washed-out laptop screen, sees three labels floating over a white card and has to guess where to click. The labels carry it for most people, which is why this isn't worse. Measure the border against white. If it's under 3:1, darken it until it reaches 3:1 without changing anything else.
2. **The form card's right edge matches nothing.** This is a convention (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). The card stops at about x=1068, while the header rule, the nav and the "Contact" link run to about x=1224. That leaves a dead strip about 156 px wide down the right side, so the card looks placed by eye rather than set on the page's columns. The empty strip also pulls the page's weight left. Choose a deliberate relationship between the card and the page's margins: run the card to the same right margin as the nav, or size both columns from one column grid so the gap on the right reads as intended.

**Smaller things.**
- **Nav doesn't mark the current page.** "Contact" looks the same as Work, Services and About, so nothing confirms where you are. Mark the current item with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an accent bar (Nielsen Norman Group, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- **Required fields aren't marked.** If all three fields are required, the cost is small, but a stranger can't tell. Either add an asterisk to each required label with a one-line key at the top, or mark any optional field "optional" (Nielsen Norman Group, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- **The help is far from the field it's about.** This is a copy note. "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them" sits in the left column, about 450 px from the Message box it refers to. Moving that sentence under the Message label, as is, would put the help where people type.
- **The headline could be any studio's.** This is a copy note. "Tell us about your project" is the stock contact-page line, so the page says nothing about what Fernwick is like to work with. The intro does better: "reply within two working days" is a real, checkable promise. If the headline should do more, it needs a fact only the studio has, such as the kinds of projects it takes. That's for the maker to supply.
- **The footer is stranded.** Privacy, Terms and Careers sit about 180 px below the card with nothing in between, so they float. That's fine at this height, but check that the gap doesn't keep growing on taller screens. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- **The intro copy is concrete.** "We read every message and reply within two working days" answers what happens after you send, which is the real worry on a contact form. Keep it.
- **The form is set up correctly.** It's a single column with labels above the fields, not placeholder-only, and the gap between each label and its field is clearly smaller than the gap between fields, so every label sits with its own input. There's one primary button, and its label says exactly what happens.
- **The hierarchy is restrained.** The bold, tightly set headline is the only large type. The body text, labels and links are close in size and are told apart by weight and color rather than extra sizes. That's a clean scale with nothing fighting for attention.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Focus rings on the fields and button.** On a page that is one form, keyboard users depend on them. Check them live.
- **Input types and validation.** I couldn't see whether the email field uses an email input type or what happens if someone submits an empty or invalid form.
