**First read.** My eye lands on the headline "Tell us about your project", drops to the two-line promise under it, then moves right to the white form card and down to the blue "Send message" button. It reads as the contact page of a small design studio, meant for prospective clients. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also anonymous: apart from the wordmark, nothing on it belongs to this studio.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a design studio's contact page and its job is to get prospective clients to send a useful first inquiry.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The order of reading is clear, there is one obvious action, and the reply promise is specific. What holds it back is small: the page asks for a budget and a timeline in one place but collects the message somewhere else, and it never says which fields are required.

**What would move it most**
1. **The budget and timeline request is far from where the visitor types.** "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them" sits in the left column, about 450 px away from the Message box. People read the intro once and then put their attention on the field. By the time they are writing, the request is out of view, so many inquiries will arrive without the two facts the studio most needs, and the first reply becomes a round of questions. Put that line as hint text directly under the "Message" label, where the visitor reads it at the moment it matters. The intro can then keep only the reply promise (Nielsen, heuristic 10: help sits next to the step it concerns). *Defect.*
2. **No field is marked as required or optional.** Name, Email address and Message all look the same. A visitor can't tell whether they can skip their name, and if they find out only through an error after pressing Send, that is a moment of friction on the one action the page exists for. Either mark the required fields with an asterisk and explain it once, or add "(optional)" after any optional label (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms"). If all three are required, one line saying so is enough. *Convention.*

**Smaller things.**
- Navigation, top right: "Contact" looks the same as Work, Services and About, so nothing shows which page you are on. Mark the current item with one cue, such as bold, a heavier underline or a color change (Laubheimer, NN/g, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Input borders: the light gray outlines on the white card look like they are below 3:1 contrast. Low-vision visitors may struggle to see where the fields are. Measure the border color against white and darken it to at least 3:1 if needed (WCAG 1.4.11).
- The card's right edge, at about 1067 px, lines up with nothing. The nav ends at about 1224 px, and no column explains where the card stops, so it looks placed by eye. Either run it to the right margin the nav sets, or narrow it to a column edge you can name (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- Headline break: "Tell us about your / project" leaves one word alone and splits "your project". Widening the text column slightly or adding a manual break, such as "Tell us about / your project", keeps the phrase together. This is my taste; the source for this rule isn't confirmed.
- Headline copy: "Tell us about your project" could be on any agency's site. If the studio has a specialty, an edit that names the kind of project it takes on would tell the right visitor they're in the right place. That fact has to come from you.

**What's working.**
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a concrete promise with a deadline. It answers the visitor's real worry, whether anyone will read this, better than any reassurance would. Keep it, and keep it specific.
- The hierarchy is clean. There is one large headline, the body text is a readable size at a comfortable line length (about 50 characters), the labels are small and bold and sit above their fields, and there is a single strong primary button. Nothing competes with the form.
- Links follow the usual convention: blue and underlined in both the nav and the footer, so nobody has to guess what is clickable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The focus ring on the fields and the button, and the error and validation messages, matter on a form this short. Tab through it in a browser and submit it empty to check.
- Whether the email field uses an email input type.
