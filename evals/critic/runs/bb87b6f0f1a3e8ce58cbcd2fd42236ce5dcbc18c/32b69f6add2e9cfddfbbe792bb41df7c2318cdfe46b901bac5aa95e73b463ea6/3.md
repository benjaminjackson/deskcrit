**First read.** My eye goes to the three big numbers across the top, then drops to the three colored lines in the chart. It reads as a clean, calm marketing analytics overview for a small team. Then it stalls: the chart is titled "Visits by channel", but nothing says which line is which channel, what a line's height means, or which week is which. The page looks finished and tells you less than it seems to.

**What it's for.** You gave me no context, so I'm assuming this is a dashboard home screen where a marketing or growth person checks traffic, sign-ups, and conversion over the last 12 weeks, and sees which channel is driving the change.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The chart takes up two-thirds of the screen but says almost nothing, because it has no series labels, no axis values, and no dates. The conversion card also reports a rise that the cards beside it rule out. Both have to be fixed before anything else matters.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart can't be read** (defect). In the "Visits by channel" panel, the blue, orange, and green lines have no names, no legend, no y-axis values, no unit, and no week labels on the x-axis. You can see that blue is highest and everything rises a little. You can't tell which channel is blue, how many visits any line stands for, whether the gap between lines is 2× or 20%, or when the dip around week 8 happened. Since the y-axis has no values, you also can't check where it starts, so the rise could be exaggerated and you'd never know (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2, on clear labeling and graphical integrity; Few). The empty strip left of the plot (about 70 px) and the blank band under the baseline look like room left for tick labels that never rendered. If they exist in the live product, this is a capture problem. In this image, though, the chart is unreadable. The fix: put each channel's name at the right end of its line instead of in a legend (Tufte; Wilke). Add a few y-axis values with the unit, and week or date labels along the bottom. Direct labels also solve a second problem: the series are now told apart by color alone, which fails for anyone who can't tell orange from green (WCAG 1.4.1).

2. **The conversion delta contradicts the other two cards** (defect). Visits are up 6.1% and sign-ups up 2.4%. When sign-ups grow more slowly than visits, conversion must fall. Working back from the cards' own numbers, the previous period was about 2.20%, so the current 2.12% is down about 0.08 points. The card shows "+0.1 pts" in success green. This is the figure a reader will repeat in a meeting, and it may be the wrong way around. Either the delta is wrong or one of the other two percentages is. Whichever it is, someone who checks the arithmetic will stop trusting every number on the page. Fix the calculation, and make sure the color follows the sign: green for a gain, not green by default (Apple HIG and Material, on status colors).

3. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding** (taste). The second thing someone opening this dashboard wants to know, after the headline numbers, is which channel moved them. "Visits by channel" makes them work that out from the lines, and right now they can't. Once the chart is labeled, the title or a short caption should say what it shows, in terms of the channels on the chart. The maker has to supply that sentence. The chart has plenty of room for it, so the space ought to pay off in an answer.

**Smaller things.**
- The type scale has collisions. "Overview" (about 24 px) and the KPI numbers (about 26 px) are nearly the same size, and the brand name and "Visits by channel" look the same size too. I count about six sizes where four would do. Make the KPI numbers clearly the largest, or make "Overview" clearly smaller and let weight carry the difference (Bringhurst, scale; Refactoring UI, bolder not bigger).
- The chart panel's white space is left over, not designed: the blank gutter on the left, the blank band under the baseline, and an unused top gridline. Labeling the chart fills most of it. Then tighten the panel so the plot fills it.

**What's working.**
- Each card carries its own comparison ("vs previous 12 weeks") with a signed change. That's the right context for a headline number. Keep the pattern, and fix the conversion figure.
- The chart has very little non-data ink: pale gridlines, one baseline, no boxes or shadows. Once labels are added, the lines will still be what you see first.
- The layout is calm and orderly. The three cards and the chart share one set of edges and one gutter, so the page reads top to bottom with no searching.
