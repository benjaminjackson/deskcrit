**First read.** The eye lands straight on the big serif quote, and that's the right place. Then it stops: “It’s the ‘old’ reading room—nobody calls it anything else” is charming, but on its own it doesn't say what the news is. The eye drops to the bold attribution, and only at the faint gray line at the bottom does it learn the actual story: the room is reopening. It feels warm, calm and literate, like a good small-town paper.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the lead story of a library newsletter. Its job is to tell members that the reading room is reopening and get them to come.

**Verdict.** It's a handsome, well-made page, but the order is backwards. The one fact a reader has to act on (the room reopens Monday) is the smallest and palest text on the page, below a bold attribution that outranks it. As set, the warm-up gets the weight and the news gets a footnote.

**What would move it most**
1. **The news is the weakest element on the page.** The order of importance runs quote, then bold attribution, then the gray two-line paragraph. That paragraph holds everything a reader needs: what is happening, when, and why to come. A reader who scans the headline and the bold line, which is how most people read a newsletter, comes away with a nice sentiment and no event. Krug's test is that a page should say what it is within seconds. This one only does so if you read to the faintest line. Keep the quote dominant, since it's the hook and it works. The direction is to swap the two lower levels: either set the attribution quieter (regular weight, or the gray) and give the reopening line the stronger weight and color, or bring the reopening into the first thing read after the quote. *Defect.*
2. **“Monday” is a date with nothing to anchor it.** “Reopens on Monday” is the one line a reader acts on, and a newsletter gets read days after it goes out. One reader takes it as the coming Monday, another as one already gone, and either may arrive to a closed room. This is an editorial fix: the line needs the actual date, which only you have. If opening hours apply, those are a fact only you can add too. *Defect.*
3. **The line break splits “reading / room”.** The quote is about what people call the room. The break falls inside that very name, so the reader meets “reading” and has to carry it to the next line before the name lands. At 95-odd px, every break is a beat. Rebreak so “reading room” stays on one line, for example “It’s the ‘old’ / reading room— / nobody calls it anything else,” or by changing the measure. This is a convention (break headings at phrase boundaries, Bringhurst and Hochuli on line breaks), and here it costs the joke its punch. *Convention.*

**Smaller things.**
- The opening quotation mark isn't hung. The “ sits on the text edge with “room” and “anything”, so the I of “It’s” starts about 40 px to the right and the headline's left edge looks ragged at display size. Hang the “ into the left margin so the letters line up. This is taste, backed by common practice; the reference source for it is unconfirmed.

**What's working.**
- The scale contrast. A big text serif against small sans gives one obvious starting point and two plainly different voices, and there is no fight between near-identical faces or sizes. Keep it.
- The palette: warm cream, near-black ink and one rust accent on the kicker. It's quiet, it feels like the institution, and it leaves the type to do the work.
- The page sits on a firm left margin, the white space is gathered to the right and below rather than scattered, and the body text runs a comfortable measure (about 55 characters).
