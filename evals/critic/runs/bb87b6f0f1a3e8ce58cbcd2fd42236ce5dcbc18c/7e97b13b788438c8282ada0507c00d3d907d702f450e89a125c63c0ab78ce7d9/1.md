**First read.** My eye goes first to the big navy 38% wedge, then up to the serif headline, and then it gets stuck in the fan of thin colored slivers at the top left of the pie, going back and forth between them and the legend. It reads straight away as a page from a company's annual report showing where the money went. The page around the chart looks calm, well made and editorial. The chart itself looks like a rainbow.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is one page of a printed or PDF annual report, for shareholders and staff who want to know what drove operating costs this year.

**Verdict.** The page around the chart is well built. The chart is the wrong kind for this data: a 14-slice pie with 14 hues means that, apart from the four biggest categories, the reader has to match color chips against the legend. It also never says what the reader should take away, or how this year compares with last.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pie has 14 slices, and ten of them are slivers of 1–4% that only a color chip can identify.** Salaries, rent, cloud hosting and marketing are labeled inside their slices. Travel through Postage are told apart only by color, so the reader has to match each sliver against the legend on the right. Several colors are hard to tell apart at sliver width: two greens and a teal (Insurance, Bank fees, Equipment repair), and a red, brown and orange (Travel, Training, Cloud hosting). For a color-blind reader, that matching mostly fails. A pie can't compare 3% with 2%, and that comparison is the only one the lower half of the data offers. (Convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design," pitfall 5; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1; Tufte on labeling at the data; WCAG 1.4.1 on color carrying meaning.) **Fix:** the legend is already the better chart. It's sorted, right-aligned and labeled. Put a bar in each row, sized by its share, drop the pie, and color everything one neutral except the bars you want to stand out. If the pie has to stay, keep the four or five largest slices and group the rest into one labeled Other slice. **Defect.**
2. **The headline names the topic but not the finding.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" tells the reader what the chart is about, then leaves them to work out the answer. That answer is plain in the data: people cost more than the next three categories combined (38% against 37%). An annual report reader skimming nine sections will remember the sentence, not the slices. **Fix:** make the headline or subtitle say that finding in the page's own numbers. (Taste. The usual sources for this are off my approved list, but the case stands on its own.)
3. **Nothing shows how this year compares with last.** This is an annual report, and the first question a shareholder asks of a cost breakdown is what changed. With shares alone, the reader can't tell whether 12% on cloud hosting is up, down or flat, or how big the total is. (Convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls," pitfall 2.) **Fix:** next to each share, add a comparison only you have, such as the fiscal 2025 share or the change in points. Put the total operating cost in the subtitle or source line. **Defect** for this audience.

**Smaller things.**
- The 1–3% callout labels around the top left of the pie are packed into a tight arc with very short leader lines, so the reader has to hunt for which label belongs to which sliver. Grouping the small categories or switching to bars removes the problem.

**What's working.**
- The type plan: a bold serif headline, a lighter serif subtitle and a sans for the data and labels. There are three clear levels and no sizes that nearly collide, so the page reads in the right order.
- The legend is set like a good table: sorted by value, with right-aligned tabular figures in bold and thin rules. It already does the comparison work the pie can't, so keep it.
- The framing is honest and complete. The eyebrow ("4.2 Costs") matches "Section 4 of 9", the subtitle gives the period ("twelve months to 30 June 2026") and the source line explains the rounding. The shares add up to 100.
