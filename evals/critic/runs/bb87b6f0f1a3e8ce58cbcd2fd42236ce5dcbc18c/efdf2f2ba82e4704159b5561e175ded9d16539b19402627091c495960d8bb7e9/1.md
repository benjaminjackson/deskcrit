**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers", drops into the paragraph below it, and then has nothing to grab: fifteen lines of even gray text with no breaks, no bold, and no numbers that stand out. It reads as a plain, calm, trustworthy information page for people who ride a river ferry. It feels like a document, not a guide. The right half of the screen is empty, so the text column sits to the left like a page of notes.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells riders, including first-timers and cyclists, when the ferry runs, how to board, and how to pay, so they can plan a trip and get on without asking anyone.

**Verdict.** Typographically it's clean, but it doesn't do its job well. Everything a rider needs is here, but it's poured into one unbroken paragraph, and the facts they plan around (times, last boat, winter schedule) are vague or can be read two ways. Someone checking "when's the last boat?" has to read the whole block, and even then they don't get an answer they can act on.

**What would move it most**

1. **One paragraph holds five separate jobs, so nothing can be scanned.** The single block covers five things: the schedule (lines 1–4), boarding order (lines 4–7), bicycle rules (lines 6–11), tickets and passes (lines 11–14), and winter and timetable notices (lines 14–16). A rider almost always arrives with one question, such as "can I bring my bike?" or "where do I buy a ticket?". They can't find it without reading from the top, because nothing marks where each topic starts. On the web, people scan for the part they need and skip the rest, and a long unbroken block defeats that (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). *Defect.* Fix: split the block into short sections under plain subheads named for the rider's question, such as times, boarding, bicycles, and tickets. Set the bicycle steps, which are a real sequence (wait at the gate → wheel to the lower deck → lock to the rail → keep bags on → keep stairs clear), as a numbered list. The type, size, and measure are fine as they are; only the structure is missing.

2. **The times riders plan around have no times in them.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines people act on. Neither gives a clock time, so a rider can't tell whether a 7:00 or a 21:30 crossing exists. "Before dusk" moves by more than an hour across the winter. "In winter" doesn't say which months. *Defect* (common sense, not a cited rule): a rider who guesses wrong misses the last boat. Fix: put in the actual first and last departure times and the dates the winter timetable applies. Only the operator has these facts. Then pull them out of the prose so they're the second thing the eye hits after the headline.

3. **Two lines that commit the rider can be read two ways.** "The last crossing leaves the quay" could mean the market quay only, or both landings, and the paragraph names two ("north landing", "market quay"). If a rider at the north landing reads it the wrong way, they could be stranded on the wrong bank. "A season pass covers any number of crossings for a month" could mean a calendar month or 30 days from purchase, and the rider is paying for one of those. *Defect.* Fix: name the landing ("leaves the market quay", or "leaves each landing"), and say when a pass starts and ends. These are editorial fixes that use the operator's own facts.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Notes for ferry passengers" is accurate but generic. Once the page has sections, a title naming the route (north landing ↔ market quay) would tell a searcher at once that they're on the right ferry's page. This is taste.
- Tickets say where they're sold but not what they cost. If price is part of what a rider decides on, that's a missing fact for the operator to supply. This is a note on the copy, not a checklist failure.

**What's working.**
- The type setting is sound. The lines run about 65–75 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45–75 range. The leading is comfortable for that length, the text is dark on white with no contrast trouble, and the heading is clearly bolder and larger than the body. Keep all of this when you add structure.
- The voice is plain and concrete ("Lock bicycles to the rail along the side of the deck", "Leave the stairs clear"). Each sentence says who does what, which makes the bicycle steps easy to turn into a list without rewriting them.
- It's free of stock decoration. Nothing competes with the information, which suits an operator's notice page.
