**First read.** The eye lands straight on "Before you board", drops to the first paragraph, and reads down the three short paragraphs with no detours. It reads as a ferry operator's info page for passengers, maybe a section of a larger site. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, like good public-service signage, and slightly unfinished because the content stops in the upper half of the screen.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: a web page that tells river ferry passengers what to expect and what to do before they board.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound. Hierarchy, spacing and reading comfort all do their job, and nothing visual is in the way. What lets it down is the copy. The page gives approximate times where a passenger needs exact ones, and it sends them to a printed sheet at the landing for the one fact they most need before they leave home.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on are vague (defect).** The first paragraph says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", and the third says "In winter the timetable thins to one boat each hour." A passenger planning a trip can't tell when the first or last boat leaves, which minute past the hour the boats go, or which months "winter" covers. These are what people remember and later hold the operator to. "Late evening" can mean 9 pm to one reader and 11 pm to another, and the reader who guesses late is stranded on the wrong bank. The fact that would settle each phrase isn't on the page, so I won't invent it. The page needs:
   - the first and last departure from each landing
   - the departure pattern (which minutes past the hour)
   - the date range of the winter timetable

   Either state them or link to the timetable that has them.
2. **The last line points to the timetable but doesn't give it (defect).** "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" tells the reader where the timetable is, but they can only reach it once they're standing at the landing. So the one thing they came to the page for still isn't in their hands. Either put the timetable (or a link to it) right there, or end the line on what it means for the reader, in second person. An edit of the existing words could read: "You'll find a printed timetable in the shelter at each landing." That is still weaker than giving the times.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run about 80 to 85 characters. The first line, ending "every forty minutes", is roughly 83. That is a little over the 45 to 75 Bringhurst gives (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). Narrow the column by about a tenth so lines land nearer 70.

**What's working.**
- There are three clear type levels with big steps between them: a small gray label, a large bold heading and body text at a comfortable size. Each size has an obvious job and nothing competes with the heading. Keep this scale if you add subheads, for example by setting them in body size and bold, not by adding a fourth in-between size.
- Spacing groups the text correctly. The heading sits closer to the text it introduces than to the top of the page, and the gaps between paragraphs are clearly larger than the line spacing within them, so each paragraph reads as one topic: the route, bikes, winter.
- The copy is plain, short and active, which suits a public service. "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail along the side of the deck" says who does what, when and where, with nothing left for the reader to fill in. Hold the timetable lines to that standard.
