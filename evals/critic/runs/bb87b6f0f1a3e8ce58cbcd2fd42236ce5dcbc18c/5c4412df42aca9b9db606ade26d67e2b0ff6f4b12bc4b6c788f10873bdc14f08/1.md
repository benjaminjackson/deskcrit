**First read.** The eye lands on the word "Pricing", then drops to three equal cards and scans the prices from left to right: $9, $29, $79. Then it runs out of things to do. None of the cards has a button, so the eye drifts to "Start free" in the top-right corner, and that button doesn't say which plan it starts. It's clean and calm, and it reads as a stock SaaS pricing page that was left unfinished.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the pricing page for a scheduling or booking product, and its job is to help a visitor pick a plan and start on it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. You can read the prices, but you can't act on them from where you read them. The page also doesn't say what each price covers, so you can't choose with confidence. The biggest problem is that no plan has its own action.

**What would move it most**

1. **No plan has a way to choose it (defect).** The three cards end in feature lists with nothing after them. The only action on the screen is "Start free" in the header, about 280 px above the nearest card and outside the column of cards. When someone has just decided "Team is mine", they have to leave the card they chose to act, and then guess whether "Start free" will start Team. The label adds to the doubt. No plan on the page is free, so "Start free" reads two ways: a free trial of a paid plan, or a free tier that isn't shown here. If they read it the wrong way, they find a card form or a different plan after the click, which is where pricing pages lose people. The action should sit next to the thing it acts on (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, natural mapping). Give each card its own action at the foot of the card. Make the label say what happens, for example by restoring the missing noun in "Start free" so it names what is free and for how long. Only the maker knows those facts.

2. **The price doesn't say what it buys (defect).** "$29 / month" could be per person or per team, and the page doesn't say whether it is billed monthly or yearly. "Teams of every size" in the subtitle makes per-user pricing a real possibility. If someone reads $29 as the team total and it turns out to be per seat, they find a bill several times larger at checkout and will feel misled. The feature lists have a similar gap. "Shared availability" and "Reports" appear only under Team, and "Single sign-on" and "Priority support" only under Business. A viewer can't tell whether Business also includes reports, or whether Solo includes booking pages on the higher plans, so they have to piece the plans together in their head (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Put the billing unit next to each price, and state plainly that each tier includes the one before it, if that's true.

3. **The page's loudest element says nothing, and the plans tie (defect).** "Pricing" is the largest, heaviest thing on the page. It tells visitors only what they already knew when they clicked the tab. Below it, the three cards match in size, border, weight, and color, so no plan stands out and nothing guides the choice (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the logical order by differences of size). The price figures (about 42 px) are also nearly the same size as the "Pricing" heading (about 46 px). They read as two versions of one level rather than two levels (Bringhurst, section 3.1.1). Let the plans carry the page. Make one plan clearly the first thing seen, probably the one most visitors should pick, which only the maker knows. Make the heading either smaller or worth reading. The subtitle has a similar problem: "Simple plans for teams of every size" could sit on any SaaS site. The half that matters, "Change or cancel whenever you like", is the part that would reassure someone who is about to commit. Give it more weight.

**Smaller things.**
- Type sizes crowd together. I count about seven: the heading (~46), the prices (~42), plan names (~20), the subtitle (~18), nav and "/ month" (~16), and feature lines (~15). Cut these to three or four levels with clear steps, and use weight and color for the finer differences.
- The subtitle breaks to a short last line, "you like." Rebreak it, or widen the measure so the sentence holds together (Bringhurst, section 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The cards are disciplined. Plan names, prices, and feature lists line up on the same baselines across all three cards, so comparing across them is easy. Keep that alignment when you add buttons.
- The restraint with color: everything is neutral grays except one blue, which marks the brand, the current tab, and the one action. Once each card has an action, that blue can make clear which action is primary.
- The current-page underline under "Pricing" in the nav clearly shows where you are.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus indicators on the nav and button.
- Whether the empty band below the cards is the end of the page or a section that hadn't loaded yet. If it's the end, a pricing page usually needs some answers below the cards (billing, cancellation, what counts as a "calendar"). Check this in a live browser.
