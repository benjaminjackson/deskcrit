**First read.** My eye lands on the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then hits a wall. All three paragraphs below it are set in a heavy, bouncy brush script, and my reading slows to a crawl as I work out the letterforms one word at a time. It reads as practical ferry information dressed as a party invitation. The heading feels like a timetable and the body feels like a café chalkboard.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page of practical service notes: when the ferry runs, how long it takes, and the bicycle and winter rules, for passengers planning a crossing.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. Information people need to scan quickly and act on is set in a display script built for a few words, so every sentence costs effort. The vague times in the copy make that worse.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in a script face (defect).** All three paragraphs, which are the whole content of the page, are in a heavy connected brush script. In this face, "landing", "quay" and "timetable" have to be decoded rather than read, and a passenger skimming for "how often" or "bikes" can't pick words out at a glance. Readers with low vision or dyslexia have it worse. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1) says a text face is "unlikely to be carrying excess ornament". The fix is to set the paragraphs in a plain face made for reading, such as the heading's sans or a text serif. If you want the script's warmth, keep it for a few words of display at most.
2. **The two faces belong to different worlds (taste).** The heading is a crisp, neutral sans and the body is a casual hand-lettered script. They share nothing: not era, not mood, not stroke. The page reads as two pieces pasted together, and the script's festive tone undercuts information people use to catch a boat. This is my judgment, not a published rule. The fix from point 1 solves this too: once the body is a plain text face that matches or clearly partners the heading, the page reads as one calm, reliable notice.
3. **The times a passenger acts on are vague (defect, in the copy).** People plan around "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". Neither gives a time they can hold the ferry to. "The quay" in the winter paragraph can be read two ways. It could mean the market quay only, or "the landing" in general, which would include the north landing. Someone reading the second way may be stranded on the north side. The fix:
   - State the first and last departure times and the winter cutoff, which are facts only you have.
   - Name which landing the last winter crossing leaves from.
   - The pointer to the printed timetables in the shelters helps, but it sends people to the landing to find out what this page should tell them.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing separately. Line length, contrast, margins and alignment are all sound.

**What's working.**
- The heading is clear and plainly says what the page holds. Its weight makes it the obvious place to start.
- The single left-aligned column with even paragraph spacing is the right calm structure for short notes. Keep it when the body face changes.
- Each paragraph covers one topic (service, bicycles, winter), so the content is already chunked well.
