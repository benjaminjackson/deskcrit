**First read.** The eye lands on the title "Notes for the summer fete", then jumps straight to the bold phrases: "labelled with their ingredients", "collected by four", "on the field only". After that it goes back for the italics. It reads as a simple, calm notice for fete helpers and stallholders. The serif type feels friendly and suits a village event. But on a quick scan you pick up the bold fragments, and the times split between bold and italic.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a short notice (web page or printout) so stallholders and helpers know when to arrive, what to do with cakes and raffle tickets, and where to park.

**Verdict.** The setting is clean and nothing in it is broken. It fails at its main job, though: someone who skims it, which is everyone who reads a notice, comes away with the wrong set of facts. The emphasis has no single rule. The arrival time, the most important instruction, is in quiet italic, while lesser points are in bold.

**What would move it most**
1. **The emphasis follows no single rule, so a skim picks out the wrong things.** Two of the times, "arrive before ten" and "three o'clock sharp", are italic. The third, "collected by four", is bold, and so are two instructions that aren't times ("labelled with their ingredients", "on the field only"). Bold catches the eye from across the page; italic only shows once you're reading. So a skimmer collects "ingredients, collected by four, field only" and misses the 10 o'clock arrival, which is the one instruction that makes the day work. The bold phrases also form their own pattern that pulls the eye around out of order. Things of one kind should look the same (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*), and things that look alike are read as a group (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*). Here the look cuts across the meaning. Fix: choose one way to mark "must-do" items, probably bold, and apply it to every deadline and rule, including the arrival time. Drop the second style, or keep italic only for the softer asides. *Defect.*
2. **Six separate instructions are set as two prose paragraphs.** Arrival time, cake labels, where cakes go, raffle time, the ticket deadline, and parking are each a separate thing someone will need to look up on the day. In running prose, finding "where do I park?" means re-reading. Text people skim should be broken into chunks or lists (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Fix: a short list, one instruction per line, ideally with the time or rule at the start of each line. That also fixes most of finding 1. *Defect* for a notice like this.
3. **Some of the copy can be read two ways, and these are lines people will act on.** "Before ten" has no a.m. or p.m. Obvious to the writer, but it's the one line people hold you to. "All tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box" can be read as "winners must claim prizes by four" or as "helpers must gather in unsold tickets by four". If someone takes the second reading, a winner goes home without a prize. "By the time the gates open" refers to a time the notice never gives. "The trestle by the door": which door, if the fete is on a field? Fix: make these lines clear with the facts only you have, such as the gate opening time, who collects what, and which door. For example, restore the missing actor: "winners must collect their prizes by four". *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- "o'clock" uses a straight apostrophe, which shows up in the italic. Use the curly ’. It's a tiny detail at body size (Bringhurst 5.2 area; the curly-quote point is common sense, not a checked rule).

**What's working.**
- The title clearly leads. It's a much larger, heavier serif from the same family, so it reads as one piece with a clear starting point.
- The body is comfortable to read. The size is generous, the line spacing open, the lines run about 75 characters at most (the top of Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range), and dark text on white is easy on the eyes. Keep all of this when you turn it into a list.
- The tone fits. A calm book serif with no decoration suits a community notice. It doesn't try to look like an app.
