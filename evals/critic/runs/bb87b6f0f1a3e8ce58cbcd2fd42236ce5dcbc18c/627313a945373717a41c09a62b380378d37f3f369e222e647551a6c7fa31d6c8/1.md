**First read.** The eye lands on "Create your account", runs down a stack of six empty fields, and stops on the navy "Create account" button. It reads as a standard SaaS sign-up page for a notes app called Quillnest. It feels calm, tidy and orderly, but it could belong to almost any product.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the sign-up screen where a new visitor creates a free Quillnest account, and its job is to get them through with as little effort and doubt as possible.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the labels are correct, required fields are marked and there is one obvious action. What gets in the way is extra effort: the form asks for more than "a free workspace for your notes" needs, and it hides the one rule that most often makes people fail a sign-up, the password rule.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form asks for things the user doesn't need yet.** "Workspace name" and "How did you hear about us?" take up two of the six fields and have the same visual weight as the four that actually create the account. Someone who came for a free notes app sees six boxes and decides whether it's worth the effort before typing anything. The referral question helps you, not them, and it sits right before the button, at the moment they're about to commit. The dropdown is also blank, with no "Select one" prompt, so it isn't clear whether it's empty, broken or required. Direction: move the referral question to after sign-up or drop it. Give the workspace a default the user can rename later, or ask for it on the next screen, so this screen holds only what creates the account (Nielsen, heuristic 8, aesthetic and minimalist design; Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure"). *Defect.*
2. **The password field gives no rules until it's too late.** "Password" is a bare label and a box. If there's a minimum length or a required character type, the user only finds out from an error after pressing "Create account", which is the most common way a sign-up fails. Direction: put a short hint under the field with the actual requirement, in the same small gray style as "* Required field" (Nielsen, heuristic 10, help and documentation; the help belongs next to the step it's about). If there truly are no rules, this point doesn't apply. *Defect.*
3. **The subhead could appear on any product's page.** "Start a free workspace for your notes" names a category and doesn't say what's different about this one. On the page that decides whether someone signs up, the only line of persuasion gives them no reason to choose Quillnest over the notes app they already use. Direction: keep it to one line, but make it say the specific thing Quillnest does for the user's notes that their current tool doesn't. Only you can supply that fact. *Taste*, and I think it matters more than it looks: this is the only sentence on the page doing any selling.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Sign in" link at the bottom has the same color and weight as "Already have an account?" and no underline, so nothing shows it can be clicked. Underline it or give it the brand blue (WCAG technique G183; NN/g, Moran, "Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention").

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean: one heading, one filled button and nothing competing with them. Keep the button as the only strong color in the card.
- The form follows the conventions: labels sit above their fields and close to them, it's a single column, and only first and last name share a row, which is the right exception for a short related pair. Keep that structure when you cut fields.
- Required fields are marked with both an asterisk and a written legend, so color isn't the only signal.
