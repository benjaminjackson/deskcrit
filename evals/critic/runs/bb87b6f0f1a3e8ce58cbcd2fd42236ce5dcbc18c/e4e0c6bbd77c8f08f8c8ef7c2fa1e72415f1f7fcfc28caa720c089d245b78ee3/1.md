**First read.** The eye goes straight to the large serif headline, "The finest bread in the valley", then up to the small brown line naming the bakery, then down to the short paragraph. After that there is nothing. It reads as the top of a local bakery's homepage: warm, calm, old-fashioned in a good way. It also feels unfinished, as if the rest of the page hasn't loaded: there's no bread, no address or hours, and no next step.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a neighborhood bakery's homepage, and its job is to get locals to come in or order.

**Verdict.** The type is well made, but the screen doesn't do its job. It makes a claim and then leaves the visitor with nothing to look at and nothing to do. The biggest problem is that there's no next step: no hours, no way to order, no directions to Mill Street.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no next step, so the eye stops dead.** The eye moves from the headline to the paragraph and then reaches empty space in the bottom 40% of the screen. Nothing tells a hungry visitor when the bakery is open, how to get there, or how to order. This is a **defect**: the person most likely to act leaves this screen without the one fact they came for. Arnheim's eye path should run from the focal point to an end. Here the end is missing. Fix: put one exact action under the paragraph, and say what the visitor gets from it. Something like opening hours with directions to Mill Street, or ordering for pickup, whichever the bakery actually wants. Plain "Learn more" or "Contact us" won't do. The empty band below already has room for it.

2. **The headline could be on any bakery's website, and nothing backs it up.** Every bakery in every valley says it has "the finest bread". A stranger can't tell what's different here, and the claim asks them to take it on trust. Your strongest material is in the small paragraph: wood-fired, baked every morning, Mill Street, since 1962. Those facts are specific and believable, and they're set at the smallest size. This is a **defect** in the copy. Fix: let the specific facts carry the large type, or move the eye to them right after the headline. The edit should come from words already on the page, for example leading with the wood-fired oven or the 1962 date. Don't invent new claims.

3. **The block hangs in the top left and the bottom of the screen is empty.** All the weight sits in the top 60%, and nothing below or to the right balances it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Because there's no image, the empty space doesn't look like calm breathing room. It looks like a section that never loaded. This is a **convention** issue, and here it costs the page. Fix 1 mostly solves it: an action, hours, or a photo of the actual bread placed in the lower part gives the page weight where it's missing. A real photo of their own loaves would also show the claim to the visitor, which the words alone can't do (taste).

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "…in the / valley". That leaves one word alone on line 2 and splits "the valley". Rebalance the two lines, for example by breaking before "bread" or by tightening the measure (convention; the source for breaks in headings is unconfirmed, so this is common sense).
- The eyebrow, "HARTLEY'S BAKERY · SINCE 1962", is set in small capitals with wide letter spacing above the headline. That's the stock line many template heroes use, so it makes the page look like anyone's (template tell, per Sokolovsky's unslop). The bakery's name and its 1962 date matter and deserve better. Try sentence case, or make the name a real wordmark.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing: a warm old-style serif at display size with a plain sans for the body. The contrast is clear and fits a decades-old bakery. Keep it.
- The palette is restrained: cream background, near-black text, and one brown accent. It reads as baked goods without being literal. Most of the area stays neutral.
- Everything shares one clean left edge, and the body text runs about 60 characters per line with comfortable line spacing. The structure is sound, so the fixes are about content, not rebuilding.
