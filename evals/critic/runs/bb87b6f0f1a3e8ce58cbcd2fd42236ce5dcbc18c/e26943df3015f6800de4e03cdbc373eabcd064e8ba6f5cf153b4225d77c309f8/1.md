**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold two-line headline, "Update the card that pays for your subscription", then down into the body text. It reads as a calm, well-made help-center article for a billing product, and it feels trustworthy. Then the eye looks for the steps or a button and finds three paragraphs of policy instead. It never learns where to click.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a help article for an account owner or billing admin who wants to swap the card on file. The page should get them to the right screen fast and tell them what happens to their invoices.

**Verdict.** The typography and layout are solid, but the page doesn't do its main job. It explains the rules around changing a card and never shows how to change one. The second problem is the decline timeline, which is the part people will act on and can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**

1. **The article never tells you how to update the card.** "You can replace the card on file at any time from the billing page" is the only instruction on the page. "The billing page" is not a link, and the page gives no path to it, no steps and no button. Someone who came here to change their card has to leave and find the screen from memory. Every other blue link points sideways to another article ("roles and permissions", "paying by invoice"), not to the place where the task gets done. Nielsen's sixth heuristic (recognition rather than recall) asks for the needed action to be on screen, and this is the action the title promises. *Defect.* Fix: make the path the first thing under the headline. At minimum, link "the billing page" directly. Better, give the actual route or short numbered steps (the maker knows the menu names) before the explanation of open invoices. The "Only an account owner…" paragraph should come before the steps too, so a member without the role finds out before they go looking.

2. **The decline timeline can be read two ways, and it decides when you lose your plan.** "We try a declined card again after three days and after seven days" can mean day 3 and day 7, or day 3 and then seven days after that (day 10). "If the card is still declined after the second attempt" is also unclear. The first charge plus two retries makes three tries, so "second attempt" could mean the first retry or the last one. This is a deadline the reader will plan around and hold the company to. If they read it wrong, they either rush or get downgraded to the free plan without expecting it. *Defect (copy).* Fix: make it say the days from one fixed point, for example "three days and seven days after the first decline", and use "second retry" or "final retry" so the count is clear. A related gap: the first paragraph says an open invoice "keeps the old card until it is paid". It doesn't say what happens when the old card is the reason you're changing, for example because it expired. Name that case, but only the maker knows the answer.

3. **The sidebar doesn't mark the current page.** Under "In this section", "Update a payment card" is the same weight and color as its four neighbors (crop at left column, y ≈ 178). Nothing on the page connects the sidebar to the article you're reading. Nielsen Norman Group's menu checklist (Laubheimer, guideline 5) calls this the most common menu mistake. *Convention, and it costs something here:* the reader can't see where they are among the billing tasks. Fix: mark the current item with one clear cue, such as bolder weight, a left bar, or a tint.

**Smaller things.**
- One page, three names: the breadcrumb says "Payment methods", the sidebar says "Update a payment card", and the title says "Update the card that pays for your subscription". Pick one name, or at least make the breadcrumb end in this page's name, so the reader knows the sidebar item and the page are the same thing. (Common sense; Norman, conceptual model.)
- Body lines run about 80–85 characters ("You can replace the card on file at any time from the billing page. The new card is"), above Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the text column a little; the empty right side has room for it.
- The headline breaks between "your" and "subscription", splitting the phrase. A slightly wider heading or shorter title would keep "your subscription" together. (Taste.)
- A help center with no search box in the header makes every reader browse. Add search to the top bar.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: a large bold title, a clearly smaller bold section heading, readable body text around 17 px, and smaller gray text for the navigation. Each level has an obvious job, and the hierarchy works because size and weight change together. Keep it.
- Links are underlined and blue, so you can find them without relying on color, and their wording says where they go ("roles and permissions", "paying by invoice"). Keep that pattern when you link "the billing page".
- The page is plain, with no boxes, icons or decoration. The calm tone suits someone worried about a declined card.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus styles on the sidebar and links, and how the two columns reflow on a phone. On a phone, check whether the sidebar pushes the article below the fold.
