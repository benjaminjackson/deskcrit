**First read.** The eye goes to "Create your account," then down a tidy stack of six empty boxes to the navy button. It reads at once as the signup screen for a notes app called Quillnest, and it feels calm and competent but generic. Nothing on it says why this notes app over any other. The second thing you notice is how many boxes there are for something called "free."

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the web signup page for Quillnest, and its job is to get a new visitor from landing to a created account with as little friction as possible.

**Verdict.** It works, and the visual craft is clean. What holds it back is the form itself: six fields, with nothing marking which are required, an empty marketing question in the main path, and no password rules. Each of those adds a pause or a failed submit right where people drop off.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form asks for more than signing up needs, and doesn't say what's optional.** "How did you hear about us?" sits in the main path, between the password and the button, and no field has an asterisk or "(optional)." A visitor can't tell whether the empty dropdown will block them, so they either stop to fill in a marketing survey or submit and maybe hit an error. This is a defect. Nielsen's "Aesthetic and minimalist design" says every element should support the user's main task, and Budiu (Nielsen Norman Group, "Marking Required Fields in Forms") says to mark the required fields. To fix it, take the attribution question out of the signup path: ask it after the account exists, or put it behind a visible "tell us how you found us" control. Then mark the fields that remain, either with an asterisk on each required label and a one-line legend, or with "(optional)" after each optional label. "Workspace name" is a judgment call (taste, common sense): a new user often doesn't know yet what to call a workspace. A sensible default, such as one built from their name and editable later, would remove a decision at the worst moment.
2. **Password has no stated rules.** The field sits bare under its label. If there's a minimum length or character rule, the user only learns it from an error after submitting, which is the most frustrating place to learn it. This is a defect (Nielsen 5, error prevention; Nielsen 10, help near the step). Put a short line of helper text under the field that states the rule before they type.
3. **"Sign in" doesn't look like a link.** At the foot of the card, "Sign in" is the same gray, weight, and style as "Already have an account?" with no underline. A returning user who landed here by mistake has to guess that part of the sentence is clickable. This is a convention break (WCAG technique G183 and F73; Nielsen Norman Group, "Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention"), and here it costs the people you most need to route away. Give it the brand blue and an underline, or at least the blue and a heavier weight.

**Smaller things.**
- The input borders are a pale blue-gray on white and look like roughly 2:1, below the 3:1 WCAG 1.4.11 asks for field edges. Measure the border color, and darken it if it falls short.
- The dropdown is an unstyled native select: an empty box with a hard black chevron jammed against the right edge, unlike the padded text fields above it. Give it the same padding and a chevron in the field's own gray. If it stays, also give it a "Choose one" prompt so it doesn't look broken.
- "Start a free workspace for your notes." could sit on any notes app's signup page. If Quillnest has a specific promise, this line is where a hesitant visitor would read it. Saying what that promise is falls to you.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear. One bold heading, bold labels sitting tight above their fields, and a single filled navy button as the only primary action, so there's never any doubt about the next step.
- The type uses one family in about four sizes with clear jobs: heading, subtitle and body, labels, and the small footer line. Weight, not size, does the fine distinctions. Keep that discipline when adding helper text.
- Labels sit above every field, closer to their own field than to the one above. Pairing first and last name side by side is the right exception to the single column.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't check whether focused fields show a visible focus ring at least 2 px thick with 3:1 contrast, what the error states look like, or whether the email and password fields use the right input types. Check all three in a live browser.
