I've finished the checklist walk and the deep read, and I'm writing up the critique now.

**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold near-black headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then has nowhere to go. Everything under it is the same pale gray, so the two paragraphs read as a faded block, almost like disabled text. The one line telling you to do something ("Check the noticeboard…") is the smallest and easiest to miss. It reads as a plain, calm information page for people taking a small river ferry, but it looks unfinished, as if someone forgot to set the body color.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers, including people with bikes, when the ferry runs, how long it takes, how bikes board, and where to check for changes.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The words are mostly clear, but the body text looks too faint to read comfortably, and the one line the reader has to act on is set as the least important thing on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **All the body text looks too faint to read.** *Defect.* Both paragraphs and the footnote are set in a light cool gray (it looks like about #9CA3AF) on white. By eye that's around 2.5:1, well under the 4.5:1 WCAG 1.4.3 asks for normal text, and at the bottom of the range where this becomes a real failure. Readers in sunlight on a landing, on a phone, or with older eyes will struggle, and it's exactly the schedule and bike details they came for. It also flattens the page: only the headline is dark, so in grayscale the page is one dark line over a pale smear (Albers; Refactoring UI). Fix: darken the body text until it reaches at least 4.5:1, so it sits clearly between the headline and white. Measure the actual hex to confirm the ratio.
2. **The line you have to act on is set as the least important.** *Defect.* "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel." is the only instruction on the page, and the thing that stops someone missing a ferry. It's smaller than the paragraphs above it (about 16 px against about 19 px) and just as pale, so it reads as fine print. The two sizes are also too close to read as separate levels; they look like an accident rather than a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). Fix: promote this line rather than demote it. Give it the body size or more, plus a clear difference in weight or color, so a skimming reader can't miss it.
3. **The times you'd plan around aren't stated.** *Defect, copy.* "Between early morning and late evening" and "in winter" are what a passenger holds you to, and both can be read in different ways. Is the first ferry at 6 or 8? Does winter start in November or on a fixed date? The wrong guess means a missed last crossing. Fix: replace these with the actual first and last departure times and the dates the winter timetable applies. Only you have those facts. "Every forty minutes" and "about twelve minutes" are fine as they are.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing on their own. The size collision is part of finding 2, and the flat value range is part of finding 1.

**What's working.**
- **The headline.** It's one weight, one size, near-black, and clearly above everything else, so the page has an obvious starting point. Keep it.
- **The reading column.** Lines run about 65–75 characters with comfortable spacing, it's flush left, and there's a clear gap between paragraphs. Once the color is fixed it will read easily.
- **The bike paragraph.** It says who does what and in what order ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). A stranger could follow it without guessing.
