**First read.** The eye goes to the headline, "Book every kennel run without a phone call". From there it jumps right to the one rust-brown tile, L4, in the run plan, and back down to the rust "Set up your kennels" button. It reads as booking software for boarding kennels. It feels calm, warm and practical, and it's built around a real picture of the product rather than decoration.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of a booking product for kennel operators, meant to get them to set up an account or open the sample run plan.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the product picture is specific and the ask is concrete. Two things get in the way. The run-plan picture colors its runs in a way that a first glance reads backwards. And the headline gives the operator a verb (booking) that the operator's customers do.

**What would move it most**
1. **The run plan's colors say the opposite of the label above them.** Fifteen tiles are sage green and three (K3, L6, M5) are white with a dashed edge. In booking grids, green usually means "available". So the first read is "lots of free runs", and only the small gray "18 runs, 3 free" in the corner reverses that. Nothing on the grid says what white means, or what the brown L4 means: you only work out that L4 is selected from the "Run L4, large dog" line below it.
   - **Why it matters:** this panel is the page's proof that the product makes a run plan readable at a glance. If it needs a second look, the proof is weaker.
   - **Convention:** this breaks the usual meaning of green as a status color (Apple HIG, Material). It also leans on color alone for "free" and "selected" (WCAG 1.4.1). The dashed border helps, but it isn't explained.
   - **Fix:** make occupied runs read as taken (a neutral or muted fill, with the dog's name on the tile) and free runs read as open. Label the states directly on the tiles or with a short legend, so color isn't carrying the meaning alone.
2. **The headline gives the operator a verb that belongs to the dog owner.** "Book every kennel run without a phone call" is an order to the reader, and the reader is the kennel operator ("Set up your kennels"). But it's owners who book runs, as the paragraph below says ("Owners book a run for the dates they are away"). A stranger rebuilds this as "a tool for me to make bookings" and only straightens it out in the body text.
   - **Fix:** an editorial edit that puts back who does what. For example: "Let owners book every kennel run without a phone call", or "Owners book every run without a phone call". That keeps the strong "without a phone call" ending and puts the benefit on the operator. (Defect: a misreading on the line that frames the whole page.)

**Smaller things.**
- **Hero paragraph:** "noon." sits alone on the last line. Rebreak the paragraph or adjust the measure slightly so the last line carries more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- **"A walk rota for the day":** "rota" is a British word, while the "$210" and "3 to 10 August" suggest a US audience. Pick one market's words (Nielsen heuristic 2). The maker knows which market it is.

**What's working.**
- **The product picture is the real thing.** A named block ("Brackenhill, east block"), a dog called Biscuit, dates and "$210 paid" show the actual job in the operator's own terms, not as abstract dashboard decoration. Keep it this specific when you fix the colors.
- **One accent, used with discipline.** The rust brown appears only on the logo, the primary button and the selected run. The button is clearly the one primary action, and "Browse a sample run plan" is properly lower as a text link. It's also a specific, low-commitment second ask.
- **"Without a phone call" sets the product against what operators do today.** That comparison lets them judge the value on terms they already know. The three feature lines below are plain and concrete, with a heading and one sentence each, and no icon or emoji cards.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the run plan is interactive (hover or select on tiles), and how the two-column hero stacks on a phone.
