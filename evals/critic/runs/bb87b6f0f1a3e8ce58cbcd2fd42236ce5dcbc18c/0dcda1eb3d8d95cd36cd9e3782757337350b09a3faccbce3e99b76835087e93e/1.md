I've finished the checklist and the deep read, and I'm writing up the critique now.

**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", drops to the bold header row, then runs down the Leaves column. It reads as a plain web timetable for people catching one river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, with nothing extra on the page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this: a rider looks up the next ferry from North landing on a given day and checks when it reaches market quay.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The type is clear, the order is chronological and nothing fights the data. What holds it back is the layout: the table is stretched across the full width, so the rider sees a departure time a long way from the day it runs. The copy also leaves out the dates the timetable covers.

**What would move it most**
1. **The time and the days it runs are 600 px apart.** The four columns are spread to fill about 1080 px, with text starting at roughly 121, 359, 657 and 951 px. One trip's values sit about 300 px apart, while rows are only about 57 px apart. Proximity tells the eye that columns are the groups, when the real groups are the trips (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on grouping by proximity). This matters because "Runs" decides whether a boat exists today. Three of the seven sailings don't run every day, and Runs is the farthest column from Leaves. On a Saturday, a rider has to follow every row across the page to find out that 12:40 and 17:10 don't run. **Defect.** Fix: size the columns to their content so the table is only as wide as it needs to be, and put Runs next to the departure time. Then a row can be read in one glance.
2. **"Spring season" gives no dates, and "Weekdays" leaves holidays open.** This is what riders act on. They will hold the operator to a timetable, and a timetable that doesn't say when it applies can be read two ways. Someone checking in a shoulder week can't tell whether this table or another season's applies, and the wrong guess means a missed boat. "Weekdays" also doesn't say whether public holidays count. **Defect.** Fix: put the season's start and end dates in the subtitle, and say how holidays are treated if they differ. Only the operator has those facts.

**Smaller things.**
- The times use proportional digits. In the Leaves and Arrives columns, "11:00" is narrower than "06:40", so the colons wander down the column. Set tabular figures so the times line up (Bringhurst 4.4.3).
- The Period column (Morning, Midday…) repeats what the times already say, and it takes the first column, where the eye starts each row. Consider dropping it, or turning the periods into quiet group breaks between rows (common sense, Nielsen heuristic 8).
- The headers "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" drop the place names that the subtitle spells out as "North landing" and "market quay". Using the full names ("Leaves North landing", "Arrives market quay") would match the subtitle and read less like a compass direction (taste).

**What's working.**
- There is one clear focal point, then a quiet, single-family type system: a bold title, a gray subtitle, bold headers and regular body text. Every level is obvious, and no size competes with another.
- The rules are pale and horizontal only. They hold each row without boxing the cells, which is how Lupton recommends ruling a table (*Thinking with Type*). Keep them this light.
- The 24-hour times, strictly chronological order and short "Daily / Weekdays / Weekends" values make a lookup table, the right form for exact values.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the four-column table reflows on a phone. On a timetable like this, mobile is likely the main use, so check it in a live browser.
