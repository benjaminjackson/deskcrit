**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops straight into the body text. Nothing else competes for it. It reads as a plain, calm information page for people who use a local river ferry: tidy, quiet and easy to read. It also reads like an essay, not something you can check while walking to the landing.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page that tells passengers when the ferry runs and what the rules are, and that they come to it to answer one practical question ("when's the next boat?", "can I bring my bike?").

**Verdict.** It's well set and doesn't really fail as typography. It does fail at its job. A passenger can't get a departure time from it, because the page never gives one, and the facts it does give are buried in sentences with nothing to mark which topic is where.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy promises a timetable and never gives one (defect).**
   - In the first paragraph, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives a rhythm but no starting point. A passenger still can't work out when the next boat leaves.
   - In the third paragraph, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "in winter" have no clock time and no dates. The viewer has to guess both.
   - That same line can be read two ways: the last boat leaves the market quay before dusk, or the last crossing in either direction does. Someone standing at the north landing at dusk who picked the wrong reading misses the boat. This is the sentence people will plan around, so the ambiguity matters.
   - Fix direction: put in the first and last departure times from each landing, the dates when the winter timetable starts and ends, and the winter last-boat time, all as numbers. Only you have those facts. If the times change too often to print, at least say so, and say where the printed timetable can be seen before you reach the landing.
2. **The three topics are unlabeled prose, so skimming doesn't work (convention, Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** The paragraphs are short, which is good. But timetable, bicycles and winter changes are three separate questions, and nothing marks them. A cyclist has to read the timetable paragraph to find out that the second paragraph is the one they want. The figures that matter most ("forty minutes", "twelve minutes", "one boat each hour") sit mid-sentence in body weight.
   - Fix direction: give each paragraph a short heading that names its topic. Pull the times out into a small table or list where the eye can find them without reading the sentences.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing.

**What's working.**
- The reading setup is right. Body text looks about 18 px, lines run about 65 to 72 characters with generous line spacing, the text is near-black on white, and the paragraphs are flush left and separated by space only. Keep all of this when you add structure.
- The page has one clear starting point. The bold heading is the only heavy thing on the page, and with just two type levels there's no scale muddle. Any headings you add should sit between the title and the body in weight, not add new sizes.
- The page is restrained: no ornament, no stock template moves, nothing pulling the eye away from the text.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, where most passengers will read this. Check that the text column fills the width with a side margin.
