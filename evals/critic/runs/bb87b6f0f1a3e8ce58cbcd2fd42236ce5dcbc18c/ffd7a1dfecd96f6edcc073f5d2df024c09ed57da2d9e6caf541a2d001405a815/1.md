**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Notes for ferry passengers," then goes straight into three tidy paragraphs of gray-black text. It reads as a quiet information page from a local transit service or town site. It feels calm and trustworthy, and also like a letter: there is nothing to scan, so you have to read every sentence to find out when the boat goes.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: someone about to take the ferry needs to know when it runs, how long it takes, and what to do with a bike. It's a web page they'll glance at, often on the way to the landing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound and I found nothing to fix there. The page still falls short of its job, because it gives no actual times. Everything a passenger will act on is written as loose prose ("early morning," "late evening," "before dusk"), and the one real timetable is in a shelter at the landing.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on are vague, so the page can't answer "when is the next boat?"** (defect, on the copy). Look at "between early morning and late evening" in paragraph 1 and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" in paragraph 3. These are the lines people plan around and hold the operator to, and each one can be read more than one way. If the reader assumes "late evening" means 10 pm and the last boat is really at 8:20, they're stranded. "Before dusk" moves by hours across the winter, and the reader can't tell whether "winter" means a set date range or "when it gets dark." Then "Printed timetables are kept in the shelter at each landing" sends the reader to the dock for the information the page should give. The fix is direction only, and the facts have to come from you: give the first and last departure times, the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, and the winter last-departure time. Then say which landing each time departs from. Paragraph 1 says the boat runs "from the north landing to the market quay" and paragraph 3 says the last crossing "leaves the quay," so a reader can't tell whether return trips run on the same schedule.
2. **The key numbers are buried in running prose, so you can't scan the page** (defect, common sense; Krug's "don't make me think" covers scanning). "every forty minutes," "about twelve minutes," "one boat each hour": the numbers are spelled out mid-sentence, so the eye skims past them. On a page whose whole value is a handful of facts, the reader has to read all of it to pull out four of them. The fix is to lift the facts out of the prose: write the numbers as digits ("every 40 minutes," "about 12 minutes"), and set the schedule (frequency, crossing time, summer and winter hours) as a short list or small table above the prose. The bicycle rules can stay as a paragraph.

**What's working.**
- **The type is set for reading:** one plain sans at two sizes, with a bold title clearly above body text that looks about 19 px. Lines run about 70 to 75 characters with generous leading, and the paragraphs are marked by space alone. Keep this setting when you add the schedule.
- **The bicycle paragraph is clear:** it says who does what, in what order: wheel on after foot passengers, lock to the rail, keep bags on the bike. Every sentence there names an action. Keep it as written.
- **The restraint suits the subject:** no stock effects, no ornament, near-black on white. It reads like a civic notice, which is the right tone.
