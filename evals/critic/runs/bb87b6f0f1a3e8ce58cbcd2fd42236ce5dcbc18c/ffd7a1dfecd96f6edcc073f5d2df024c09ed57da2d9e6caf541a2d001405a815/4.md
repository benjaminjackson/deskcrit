**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading at top left and drops straight into three gray paragraphs of the same weight. It reads as a plain web page for a river ferry service. It feels calm and legible, and a little like an unstyled document: the right 40% of the screen and the bottom third are empty.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boats run, how bikes work and what changes in winter, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound and very easy to read. The page fails at its job anyway, because it never gives the facts a passenger acts on: what time the first and last boats leave. The design is fine. The words are what's in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **No actual times, and the page sends readers elsewhere for them.** *Defect.*
   - Paragraph 1 says boats run "between early morning and late evening." Paragraph 3 says the winter last crossing leaves "before dusk." Someone planning a trip gets no time they can plan around.
   - Paragraph 3 then says printed timetables are "kept in the shelter at each landing." So the person reading online has to go to the landing to find out when to go to the landing.
   - The consequence is a wasted trip or a missed last boat.
   - Fix: replace "early morning," "late evening" and "before dusk" with the real times. Say which months count as "winter." If times change week to week, as the noticeboard line suggests, say where online to find the current ones. Only the operator has these facts.

2. **"The last crossing leaves the quay" can be read two ways.** *Defect.*
   - The page calls one end "the north landing" and the other "the market quay." So "the quay" most likely means the market side only.
   - A reader on the north side can't tell whether their last boat also goes before dusk, or whether "the quay" just means "the pier" in general.
   - This is the line people get stranded by. Name both ends, or say "the last crossing in each direction."

3. **The three answers people come for are buried in even prose.** *Taste, argued from Krug's "don't make me think."*
   - A rider asking "can I bring my bike, and does it cost extra?" has to read paragraph 1 to find out that paragraph 2 is the one about bikes. Every paragraph looks the same, so nothing signals which one holds which answer.
   - Fix: add short subheadings that name each topic, such as times, bicycles and winter. Or lead each paragraph with its key fact, so a scan of the left edge finds it.
   - This is an edit of what's already there, not new content.

**Smaller things.**
- **Lopsided screen (taste).** The 630 px text column sits on the left of a 1280 px screen with nothing on the right, so the page looks unfinished rather than chosen. Either center the reading column, or give the right side something real, such as the timetable from finding 1.

**What's working.**
- **Comfortable reading text.** Lines run about 68 to 70 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range. Line spacing is generous, and the dark gray text on white is strongly legible. Keep all of this when adding structure.
- **One clear focal point.** The heading stands out by size and weight together, so the eye knows where to start. That's enough contrast; it doesn't need more.
- **Plain voice.** "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" sounds like a person talking. Keep that tone when you put the real times in.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether there's site navigation or a way back outside this crop.
- How the page reflows on a phone, which is where someone standing at a landing will read it.
