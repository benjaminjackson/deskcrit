**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold heading, then down into three even paragraphs. Nothing else on the page pulls at it. It reads as a plain information page for people who use a small river ferry. It feels calm, orderly and a little anonymous, like a well-kept document more than a guide you'd check while hurrying to the landing.

**What it's for.** Assumed, since I was told nothing: a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes, and the rules for bicycles, so they can plan a crossing.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and does nothing wrong. The page still fails at its main job, because it never gives a passenger a time they can plan around. "Early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk" are all you get, and the one deadline on the page can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times can't be acted on, and the last-boat line is ambiguous.** This is a defect. Every time on the page is either vague or has no anchor:
   - "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say forty minutes from when.
   - "One boat each hour" in winter doesn't say which months count as winter.
   - "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" (paragraph 3) can be read two ways. It could mean the last boat from the market quay only, so the north landing's last boat is at some other time. Or it could mean the last boat of the day, from either side.

   The last boat is the one fact a passenger will plan around. A reader who takes the wrong meaning ends up stuck on the wrong bank at nightfall. The page also sends readers to the printed timetable in the shelter, which they only see once they're already at the landing.

   Fix direction: replace each vague time with the exact one, i.e. the first and last departure from each side and the months the winter timetable runs. The maker has these facts; I don't. Also name the landing in "leaves the quay" so the line has only one reading.
2. **The facts a passenger came for are in the wrong order, and buried in prose.** This is taste, argued on its own terms. The page reads as three paragraphs of equal weight. The bicycle rules come second, and the winter hours and last crossing come last. Someone checking whether they can still get back has to read all of it to find that. The bicycle paragraph is fine as prose, because it's a procedure. The timetable facts are things to look up, not read.

   Fix direction: put the times first, set as lookup items (short labeled lines or a small table of departures), so the answer can be found at a glance. Leave the bicycle rules as the prose they are.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not this ferry. Using the route the body already gives (north landing to market quay) would tell a visitor at once that they're on the right page. This is an editorial edit using words already on the page.

**What's working.**
- **One tight type system.** There's one sans family and two sizes with a big step between them. The heading is set apart by bold weight as well as size. There's no third size competing, so the order is never in doubt.
- **Comfortable reading setting.** Lines run about 70 characters at roughly 1.5 line spacing, flush left with an even rag. Paragraphs are separated by space alone, and the gap is clearly larger than the line spacing, so each paragraph reads as one unit. Keep this measure and spacing when you add structure.
- **Plain, concrete voice in the bicycle paragraph.** "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail" says who does what, and when. The rest of the copy should match that precision with real times.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the page reflows on a phone. Many passengers will check it on a phone at the landing, so the column and any timetable you add need to work at narrow widths.
