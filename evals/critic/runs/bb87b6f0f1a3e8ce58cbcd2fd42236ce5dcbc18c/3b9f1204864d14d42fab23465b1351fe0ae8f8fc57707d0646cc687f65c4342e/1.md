**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, then drops into a row of four prices that all hit at the same weight: $0, $12, $39, $99. Nothing pushes forward, so the eye moves left to right like it's reading a spreadsheet and stops nowhere. It's a clean, calm SaaS pricing section for a collaborative design or doc tool. It's competent and generic, and once you start comparing the columns, the numbers don't quite hold together.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a pricing page that helps a small team or a freelancer pick a paid plan and click through, with Team most likely the plan the business wants to sell.

**Verdict.** The table is tidy, but it doesn't help anyone choose. All four plans get the same weight and the same button, and the feature ladder contradicts itself: Free has more editors than Solo. A careful buyer will stop at that point and start doubting the rest.

**What would move it most**
1. **The numbers contradict each other, and they're what a buyer acts on.** In the "editors" row, Free gives 3 editors and Solo, the paid plan above it, gives 1. Read the way a stranger would, either it's a typo or paying $12 takes away two seats. Free also caps at 2 projects against Solo's 20, so a real trade-off may be intended, but nothing on the page says so. The Company card shows a firm "$99 / month" and then asks you to "Talk to us". Is $99 the price or a starting point? And "/ month" never says what it's per: per workspace or per editor. At 100 editors that's the difference between $99 and something like $9,900. Prices and seat counts are what people hold you to, so this is a **defect**, not a style note. Fix: check the editor counts. If Free really has more editors, say why in the line itself. Say what the price covers next to "/ month". Then either make Company's price firm and give it a direct button, or label it as a starting price. Only you have these facts. (Nielsen 6: what the user needs to decide has to be on the screen.)
2. **No plan leads, and every button is primary.** All four cards have the same border, size, and white fill, and all four have the same solid blue button. The eye can't tell which plan is for "most people like me," and the buttons lose their signal because they all shout equally. That's a **convention** break (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance by contrast; Material Design and Apple HIG: one primary action per view). Here it costs real decisions. Fix: make one plan clearly first, probably Team since it fits the headline about teams, with a stronger frame, tint, or label. Keep its button filled, and drop the others to an outline or secondary style.
3. **The labels assume the reader already knows the product.** The subhead promises "unlimited viewers", the rows count "editors", and nothing says what separates them. "Priority help" vs "Email help" and "Full history" vs "1 year of history" are categories rather than outcomes. A buyer counting seats has to guess who on their team counts as an editor. This is a **defect** for the job of choosing. Fix: put the editor/viewer distinction in a few words where it's first used, for example in the subhead line that already mentions viewers. Make each row say what the buyer gets. Don't add new features.

**Smaller things.**
- The gutter between Team and Company looks about 36 px against roughly 24 px between the others, so the Company card sits slightly right and the row's rhythm breaks. It reads as an error, not a choice. Make all gutters one interval (Müller-Brockmann).
- Every card has an empty band of about 70 px under its button, which reads as fixed-height cards waiting for content. Close it up, or use it for something real.

**What's working.**
- The prices are large and bold, with the light "/ month" set beside them. The price is the first thing read in each card, and the unit stays out of the way. Keep that contrast.
- Each feature sits in the same row position in every card (projects, editors, history, help), so you can compare across the cards by reading along a row. Keep that strict row order when you fix the content.
- "For freelancers / For small studios / For larger groups" names who each plan is for, which is the right instinct for helping someone self-select.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Button hover and focus states, and how the four columns reflow on a phone, where a "recommended" plan matters even more once the cards stack.
