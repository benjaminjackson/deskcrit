**First read.** My eye goes straight to the three big figures, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and then stops, because none of them says what it is. Is 3.8 a percent? Is 64.20 in dollars? Is 12.4 a count of subscribers, or thousands of them? After that the eye drops to the orange bars, where Saturday is tallest and Sunday falls off. It reads as a tidy, calm overview page for a small bakery's online shop. It looks finished, but the numbers aren't.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the owner's or manager's landing screen for checking at a glance how the shop is doing and when deliveries peak.

**Verdict.** The layout is clean and well built. But the three numbers it makes biggest can't be read: they have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them against. So the top of the page doesn't yet tell the owner whether things are good or bad.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI figures have no units.** This is a defect, in the three stat cards across the top. "3.8" could be a percent or a ratio, and "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" for *active subscribers* can't be a head count, because you can't have a fraction of a subscriber. It must mean thousands, or a rate, but the card doesn't say. The viewer has to guess, and two people will guess differently. The largest, heaviest type on the screen is spent on the least readable content. Put the unit right on each number (%, the currency sign, "k" or the full count) so each figure reads on its own (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2).
2. **The KPIs have no period and no comparison.** This is a defect, in the same cards. Nothing says whether these figures cover September, the last 30 days or all time. The only date on the screen is in the chart's source line, and the viewer shouldn't have to assume it also applies to the cards (Nielsen, heuristic 6: recognition rather than recall). Even with units, a bare "3.8%" can't tell the owner whether to worry. Next to each number, add the period and one point of reference: the change from the previous period or a target (Few, pitfall 2). That turns three facts into three judgments.
3. **The chart compares days that aren't equal.** This is a defect in the "Orders by delivery day" chart. It shows September totals. September 2026 had five Tuesdays and five Wednesdays but only four of every other weekday. So the Tuesday and Wednesday bars include an extra delivery day, and they're inflated next to the rest. The Saturday peak is still real, but the gentle climb from Monday to Thursday may partly be a calendar effect. Plot average orders per delivery day, or say in the caption that the totals are raw and how many of each weekday the month had. This is common sense rather than a cited rule; the entry's source isn't on the approved list.
4. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding.** This is taste, but it matters here. "Orders by delivery day" makes the viewer find the point themselves: Saturday is busiest, Friday is close behind and Sunday is under half of either. The owner will probably use that to plan staffing and baking. A title or caption that says it in words, built from what the chart already shows, saves the reread. Don't add claims the data doesn't make.

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is clear.** I count four levels: KPI figures, page title, card and chart titles, and labels. There are big steps between them and finer distinctions come from weight and gray. Keep this when you add units and comparisons; set them in the label size or gray, not a new size.
- **The chart is honest and quiet.** It's a single series in the brand orange, the bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint and the ticks are even (0 to 1,200 in steps of 300). Weekday order is the right sort for this question, and there's a source line. Almost all the ink is data.
- **The structure is disciplined.** There's one grid, the three cards share their gutters with the chart panel below, and margins are consistent. The bold "Overview" in the nav shows where you are. The page has room for the extra context without getting crowded.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't see focus or hover styles on the nav links, or whether a date-range control exists elsewhere. If one does, its current setting belongs on this screen.
