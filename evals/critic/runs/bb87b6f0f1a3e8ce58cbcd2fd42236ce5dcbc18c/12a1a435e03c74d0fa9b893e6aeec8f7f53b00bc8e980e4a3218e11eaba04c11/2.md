**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and then down to the Saturday bar, the tallest thing in the chart. It reads as the home screen of a small online bakery's admin, built for the owner to check how the shop is doing. It feels calm, tidy and on-brand. Then you try to say what any of the three numbers means and can't: 3.8 of what, 64.20 in what currency, and 12.4 subscribers can't be a head count.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's at-a-glance health check: in a few seconds, are sales and subscriptions on track, and which days are busy.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are well made, but the top half fails at the one thing it's for. The three headline numbers have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them with, so the owner can't read them or tell whether they're good.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline numbers have no units, and one can be read two ways (defect).** In the three cards across the top, "3.8" under *Checkout conversion rate* is probably a percent but doesn't say so. "64.20" under *Average order value* has no currency. "12.4" under *Active subscribers* is the worst: you can't have 12.4 subscribers, so it's likely 12,400 shortened to "12.4k" with the "k" dropped, but it could be read as a rate or a percent. The viewer has to guess the meaning back in and may guess wrong, and these are the numbers the owner acts on and repeats to others. Every figure should say what it measures (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few). The fix: put the unit on each number (%, the currency sign, "k" or the full count) and write all three in a style that can't be misread.
2. **No number has a period or a comparison (convention, and it costs the main job here).** The cards don't say whether they cover today, this month or all time. Only the chart's source line says September 2026, and it doesn't clearly cover the cards. None of the cards shows a previous period, a target or a direction, so "3.8%" can't be judged good or bad. A bare number on an overview tells the owner nothing they can act on (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2). Direction, per the approved fix: next to each headline number, put the unit, the time period and a comparison such as the change from last period or the target. Use whichever comparison the owner actually steers by; that's the maker's call.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Orders by delivery day" names the topic but leaves the finding to the viewer: Friday and Saturday carry the week and Sunday is about a third of Saturday. This is my taste and common sense, not a published rule. If the copy can follow the data, a caption that says what the shape means, for example for baking or staffing, would turn the chart into something to act on. Because the data is live, a static takeaway title may not be possible, and the current title is the safe fallback.

**What's working.**
- **The grid.** The logo, the page title, the left card and the chart panel all share one left edge. The three cards split the panel's width evenly with matching gutters, so the page reads as one calm block. Keep that when you add units and comparisons to the cards, and don't let the cards grow uneven.
- **The chart is honest and plain.** The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, there's one hue and no legend, the days are in their natural order, and the data source and month are stated. Nothing gets between the viewer and the weekly shape.
- **Color restraint.** The bakery's orange appears only on the wordmark and the bars, so the one place it carries data is also the one place your eye goes after the numbers.
