**First read.** The eye goes straight to three big numbers: 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4. None of them says what it is. 3.8 is probably a percent, 64.20 is probably money in some currency, and 12.4 active subscribers can't be right unless it means thousands. Next the eye drops to the orange bars, where Saturday is tallest and Sunday is a deep dip. Overall it reads as a calm, tidy owner's dashboard for a small bakery's online shop.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the shop owner uses this home screen to check at a glance how sales and subscriptions are doing and when orders peak.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are solid, but the three headline numbers, the most prominent thing on the page, can't be read with confidence. They have no unit, no period and no comparison. A dashboard whose top row needs guessing fails at its main job.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline numbers have no units** (defect). In the three cards across the top, "3.8", "64.20" and "12.4" stand alone under their labels. The owner has to supply the meaning: percent or ratio? Dollars, pounds or euros? 12.4 people, or 12,400? "Active subscribers: 12.4" is the worst, because a count of people can't be fractional, so the viewer either distrusts the number or invents a unit. These are the figures the owner will quote and act on, so two possible readings is a real cost. Tufte (*Visual Display*, ch. 2) and Few both say the reader should never have to guess what a number measures. Fix: put the unit on the figure itself (%, the currency symbol, "k" or the full count), at the same size or one step down.
2. **No period and no comparison, so good can't be told from bad** (convention, Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2). Even with units, 3.8% means nothing on its own. Is it this week, this month, or all time? Is it up or down? The chart's source line says September 2026, but the cards say nothing, so the owner can't even tell whether the cards and the chart cover the same window. Fix, from Few: next to each headline number, show its time period and one comparison, such as the change from the last period or a target. The cards have plenty of empty space to the right of each figure for it.
3. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding** (taste / common sense; no approved source). "Orders by delivery day" makes the owner work out the story, and the story is the point: demand builds through the week, peaks on Saturday, and falls by about two-thirds on Sunday. That shapes baking and staffing decisions. A title or one short note that states the pattern, using only what the bars already show, would let the chart pay off in one glance.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, there is one hue with no legend, the days run in their natural order, and the source and month are stated. Keep that restraint.
- The structure is clear. One grid and one margin serve the title, the card row and the chart panel, and the gaps make the groups obvious. The bold KPI figures properly dominate, so once they carry units the top row will do its job.
- The palette is small and on brand: the burnt-orange wordmark and bars on warm neutrals suit a bakery without shouting.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the nav links, and whether the period is meant to be set by a control that isn't shown.
