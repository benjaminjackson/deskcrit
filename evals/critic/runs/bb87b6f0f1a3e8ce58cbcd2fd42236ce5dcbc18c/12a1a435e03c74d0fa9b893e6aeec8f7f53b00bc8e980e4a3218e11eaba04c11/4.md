**First read.** The eye goes to the three big numbers across the top: 3.8, 64.20, 12.4. It then drops to the orange bars, where the tall Saturday bar and the short Sunday stub are what stand out. It reads as the back-office overview for a small bakery's online shop, likely one that sells subscriptions. It feels calm, tidy and on-brand. Then you try to read the numbers and find you can't.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the bakery's owner or staff check this screen to see at a glance how the shop is doing and when orders land.

**Verdict.** The layout and chart are well made, but the three headline numbers fail their basic job. None has a unit, a time period or a comparison, so the owner can't tell what any of them means or whether it's good. Fix those three cards and this is a solid dashboard.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units, so they can be read more than one way.** In the three cards at the top, "3.8" is presumably a percent, but nothing says so. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" active subscribers can't be a count of people, so it is probably 12.4 thousand, but it could also be a rate or a percent. These are the largest type on the screen and the first thing anyone reads. A viewer has to guess the meaning, and a wrong guess about subscribers (12 people or 12,400) changes every decision made from this screen. Every number needs to say what it measures (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*). Fix: put the unit on each figure, as %, the currency symbol and k (or the full count). Then the decimals will stop looking random as well (see Smaller things). *Defect.*
2. **Each headline number stands alone, with no period and no comparison.** Even with units, "3.8%" doesn't tell the owner whether that is up, down, on target or for which month. Only the chart's source line says "September 2026", and it sits at the bottom of a different card, so the KPIs seem to have no time frame at all. A bare big number can't tell good from bad, which is the point of an overview. Fix: next to each number, give its time period and one comparison, such as the change from last period or a target (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2). *Convention, and it costs the screen its purpose.*
3. **The chart title names a topic, not what the chart shows.** "Orders by delivery day" makes the viewer find the story alone. Friday and Saturday carry most of the week, and Sunday drops to about a third of the weekday level. That is the thing a bakery would plan staff and baking around. Fix: let the title or a short caption state that finding in the words of the data. *Taste:* the source for this rule isn't on my approved list, but on a screen that is opened to make a decision, the conclusion should not be left for the viewer to work out.

**Smaller things.**
- The number formats are mixed: 3.8 and 12.4 have one decimal, while 64.20 has two (it looks like money). Once units are added, use one format per kind of number so that they read as a set.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and quiet. Bars start at zero, there are only four light gridlines, it uses one color, and the days are in their natural Monday-to-Sunday order. The source line names the system and the month. Keep all of that.
- The type has clear levels: the page title, then the card labels in regular weight, then the big bold numbers, then the small gray axis text. Each size has an obvious job.
- The palette is restrained. Neutral gray and white carry the page, and the one warm orange is shared by the wordmark and the bars, so the brand color does real work instead of decorating.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the KPI cards or bars show exact values or periods on hover, and whether nav items show focus. Neither fixes the missing units, since the headline has to read without interaction.
