**First read.** The eye goes to the headline, "Know which rack every boat is on," then slides right into a big beige box holding nothing but a generic image icon. It reads as a clean, quiet SaaS landing page for rowing or sailing clubs. It feels calm and trustworthy, but half the first screen is a blank. You can tell what the product claims to do, but you never see it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the home page hero of a product for club boathouses. It should get a club captain or volunteer to understand the product and start mapping their racks, or at least open the sample.

**Verdict.** The words and type do the job well. The page still falls short because the right half, where a viewer looks for proof, is an empty placeholder. Until that box shows a real rack plan, the page asks people to trust a claim they could have seen for themselves.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero visual is an empty placeholder (defect, and a template tell).** The 550 px beige panel to the right of the headline has only a stock image glyph in it. It's the second thing the eye lands on, and it says "nothing to see here" at the exact moment the viewer wants to see the rack plan the headline promises. An empty or generic hero box is a stock template move (Sokolovsky, unslop). Viewers have seen it on countless unfinished pages, so it makes this page look unfinished and like anyone's. It also wastes the strongest argument the product has. "Know which rack every boat is on" practically asks for a picture of a boathouse rack plan with boat names on racks and a few marked free or private. Image and headline should make one point together: the headline makes the claim and the picture proves it. If this is just an image that didn't load in the capture, it's fine once the real image appears. Check that the live page has one and that it shows the product itself, not a stock photo of boats.

2. **The asks don't compare Slipway with what clubs use today (copy, taste).** "Map your boathouse" is a good primary ask: one concrete action, in the club's own words. "See a sample boathouse" is better still, because it lets the viewer judge for themselves. Nothing on the page sets Slipway against what the captain uses now, though. The third feature, "Print the rack plan for the wall," hints that there's already a plan on a wall somewhere. A line or visual that puts Slipway next to the club's current way of tracking racks and fees would let viewers see the gain on terms they already accept. The maker has to supply what that current method actually is.

**Smaller things.**
- "Rack fees each spring": "Send every rack holder the yearly fee … with a card link" can be read as paying the fee out, and "card link" leaves the viewer to work out what it is. This is a money line, so make it unambiguous, for example "Bill every rack holder their yearly fee on one day, with a link to pay by card."
- Hero paragraph: "glance." sits alone on the last line. Rewrap or adjust the measure so the last line carries more than one word (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- Headline: the break "every / boat is on" splits the noun phrase. "Know which rack / every boat is on" breaks at the phrase and reads more easily.

**What's working.**
- The copy is specific to its audience: "rack plan," "private boats," "yearly rack fees," "the captain." A rowing club volunteer knows straight away this was made for them. Keep that vocabulary.
- The hierarchy is clear and restrained: one dominant headline, one navy primary button, one underlined secondary link, and three feature columns where weight, not extra sizes, separates heading from body. Don't add sizes or colors when you revise.
- The second ask, "See a sample boathouse," lets a cautious viewer look before committing. Keep it next to the primary button.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hit areas for the nav links and the "See a sample boathouse" link: they look about text height. Check that each is at least 24×24 px (WCAG 2.5.8).
- Whether the hero image is missing by design or failed to load: check in a live browser.
