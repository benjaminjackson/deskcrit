**First read.** My eye lands first on the bold headline, "Plan the week with your whole team", then jumps right to the solid blue "Create account" button, and then works back up through the form. I take it to be the sign-up page for a team planning tool, aimed at small-team leads. It feels calm, clean and trustworthy, but also anonymous. Nothing on the page shows what planning in Tallyboard looks like.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the desktop sign-up screen for a SaaS team planner, and its job is to get a new visitor to create a free workspace.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The layout is clear, there is one primary action, and the labels are proper. What holds it back is the words. The free-plan line can be read two ways on the point people care about most, the password rule disappears as soon as someone starts typing, and the form never says which fields are required.

**What would move it most**
1. **The free-plan promise can be read two ways (defect).** The headline promises "your whole team". The subhead then says "for up to five people. You can invite the rest of the team later." A lead with a team of eight can read that two ways:
   - Adding people later is still free.
   - Free stops at five, and the rest of the team costs money.

   This is the line people will hold you to. If they read it the first way and then hit a paywall at person six, you lose their trust right after sign-up. If they read it the second way, the headline sounds like bait, and they may never fill in the form. The fix is to say plainly what happens at person six: whether it's free, paid, or a limit. Only you know which. Then check that the headline doesn't promise more than that.

2. **The password rule lives only in the placeholder (defect, Nielsen 6: recognition rather than recall).** "At least 10 characters" sits inside the Password field, so it vanishes with the first keystroke. People then have to remember it, or find out at submit. Password is the field that fails most often on sign-up forms, so that hint needs to stay visible. Move the rule into helper text under the field, or next to the label, and leave the field empty or use a neutral placeholder.

3. **Nothing says which fields are required (convention, Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").** There are five fields for a free workspace, and Company is the one people are most likely to doubt: a freelancer or a team inside a big company won't know whether to fill it. With no markers, people either fill in everything or stop to wonder. Either way the form feels heavier than it is. If every field is required, put an asterisk on each label and explain it once at the top. If any field isn't required, add "(optional)" after its label. If Company isn't needed to create a workspace, consider asking for it later. Whether it's needed is your call.

**Smaller things.**
- "Sign In" at the foot of the card is the same gray, size and weight as "Already have an account?", with no underline. Returning users can't see that it's a link. Underline it or give it the link color. Also change it to "Sign in" to match the sentence-case "Create account".
- The input borders are a pale gray-blue on white and look like about 2:1 contrast, below the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for on field edges. Measure them, and darken the border if it falls short.
- Type scale (taste plus convention, Bringhurst; unslop "Too many type sizes"): I count about seven sizes on one screen, roughly 38, 22, 20, 17, 15, 14 and 13 px by eye. The 17 px subhead, 15 px input text, 14 px labels and 13 px footer are only a step or two apart, so they look like sizes picked one at a time. Cut it to three or four sizes and let weight and color carry the small differences.
- The ask is a generic "Create account". Nothing on the page sets Tallyboard next to what the viewer plans with today, such as a spreadsheet, a whiteboard or a standing meeting, so they're taking the headline on trust. The large empty space under the left column could hold that comparison, or a glimpse of a planned week. That content is yours to supply.

**What's working.**
- There's one clear primary action. The solid blue button is the only saturated block on the page, and it repeats the blue of the wordmark, so there's no doubt about what to do. Keep it that way when you add anything.
- Every field has a real label above it, bold and close to its field, and First name and Last name share a row as a short related pair. This is good, conventional form structure.
- The alignment is quiet and exact. The wordmark lines up with the card's title, the left column and the card share a clear top line, and the empty space sits in one large area instead of scattered gaps.
