**First read.** The eye lands on "Help for ferry passengers," drops to "Before you board," and runs down the left bullets before crossing to the right column. It reads straight away as a plain help page from a ferry operator. It feels calm, clear and unbranded, close to a default document style.

**What it's for.** You didn't say, so I'm assuming this is a web help page that tells ferry passengers how to board smoothly and how to report a problem.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The layout and type are clean and easy to read, and there is nothing to fix at the structural level. The weak spots are in what the page puts first and where it sends people. The one fact that can leave a passenger stranded is hidden in the middle of a bullet. The "something goes wrong" steps end by sending the reader to an address they can't see from this page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The fact that can strand someone is set like everything else.** It's in the first bullet, top left: "winter sailings run once an hour and the last boat leaves before dusk." It sits in the middle of a sentence, in the same style as the advice about prams and bicycles. Someone skimming reads "Check the timetable board" and moves on. They miss the only line on the page where getting it wrong costs them a night on the wrong shore. "Before dusk" is also a deadline people will act on, and it can be read two ways: the time the boat leaves, or roughly when it gets dark, which changes through the winter. If a reader takes it the loose way, they arrive at dusk and the boat has gone. Fix: give the last-sailing warning its own line, ahead of the other bullets, so it reads first. Tie "dusk" to something the reader can check, such as a time or a pointer to where the exact time is posted. Only you know that fact. *Defect.*
2. **The "If something goes wrong" steps send the reader somewhere they may not be.** Step 3, right column: "send it to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter." Someone reading this page at home or on their phone after the crossing can't see that noticeboard. The last step, the one that actually gets the complaint sent, is the one they can't do. The heading also covers everything from a lost ticket to someone hurt on board, and the only route it offers is a note. Nothing says what to do if the problem can't wait. Fix: put the address on the page itself. Add a line saying what to do when it's urgent or the reader is still on board, such as finding the crew. Both are facts you have to supply. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- The lines in the two columns don't sit on the same rows. The right column's second and third items fall level with the gaps between the left column's lines, because the space between items isn't a whole number of lines. To fix it, make the space between items equal one line (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). *Convention.*

**What's working.**
- **The copy is written well for its readers.** Every item starts with a verb, and most give the reason too ("because winter sailings…", "so that the gangway is kept clear"). People follow a rule more readily when they know why it's there.
- **The list styles match the content.** The boarding tips can be done in any order, so they're bullets. The reporting steps happen in order, so they're numbered. Keep that distinction.
- **The type is easy to read.** There are three clear sizes (title, bold section heads, body). Body lines run about 60 characters, line spacing is generous, and bullets hang outside the text so the left edge stays clean.
