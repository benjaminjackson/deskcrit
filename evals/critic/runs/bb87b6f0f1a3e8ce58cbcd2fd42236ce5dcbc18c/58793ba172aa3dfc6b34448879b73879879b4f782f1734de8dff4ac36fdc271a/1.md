**First read.** The eye lands on "Low-light houseplants", then drops into the row of four cards. The four button colors pull at it next: teal, purple, orange, blue. It reads as a small, tidy plant shop's category page. It feels calm and competent until the buttons, which look like they came from four different sites.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a shop category page where someone who doesn't get much light picks one of four plants and adds it to the cart.

**Verdict.** The structure is solid, but the page fails at the one thing a plant shop most needs, which is showing the plants. All four pictures are the same stand-in drawing of a terracotta pot with a couple of leaves, so the shopper chooses on name and price alone. The four-color buttons are the second problem: they make one action look like four different things.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures don't show the plants (defect).** Each card's image area shows nearly the same generic pot with two or three leaf shapes. A snake plant has stiff upright blades, a ZZ has glossy leaflets on stems, and a pothos trails, which its own card says ("trails to 6 ft"). Here the pothos is drawn upright. Someone choosing a plant buys mostly on looks, and this page gives them no way to compare four. That leaves the heading's real promise ("more than six feet from a window") without visual proof. It is also a template tell (unslop, "Empty box or generic picture as the hero visual"): a placeholder in the most important spot on each card. The fix is a picture of each actual plant, shot or drawn in one consistent style on the same pale ground. If these are placeholders waiting for photos, the page isn't ready to judge until they arrive.
2. **Four colors for one action (defect).** "Add to cart" is the same action on every card, but it comes in teal, purple, orange, and blue. Elements that look different read as different things (Wertheimer; Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*), so the shopper wonders whether the purple button does something the blue one doesn't. Nothing on the page explains the difference. The four hues also have no relation to the brand's green-and-terracotta palette or to the calm mood the plants set, so they break up a page that otherwise holds together. Give all four buttons one shared color, as Apple's Human Interface Guidelines ("Color") direct for elements that do the same job, and draw it from the plant palette, for example the leaf green. That green would then be the one accent on the page.

**Smaller things.**
- The top navigation draws "Houseplants" like its neighbors, so nothing tells the shopper where they are. Mark the current item with one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").

**What's working.**
- **The subhead is specific.** "Four plants that do well more than six feet from a window" turns a vague category into a test the shopper can check against their own room. That is exactly the kind of reference point good copy gives. Keep it.
- **Each card has a clear order:** name, then a one-line spec, then a large bold price, then the button. Size and weight separate the levels cleanly, and the spec line ("6 in pot, grows to 2 ft") answers the two questions a buyer asks before price.
- **The layout is quiet and disciplined.** It uses one four-column grid with equal gutters and consistent padding, and content aligns to the logo's left edge. There's nothing to remove.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus and hover states on the buttons and nav links, and how the four columns reflow on a phone.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 5 yes, 3 no (main image and headline make one idea; each kind of element looks the same; colors carry the mood)
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 12 yes, 1 no (only related elements look alike)
Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
Color: 9 lines, 7 yes, 2 no (small palette; saturated color kept to a small share)
Pictures: 12 lines, 11 yes, 1 no (every image shows its content)
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 31 yes, 1 no (screen shows where the user is), 1 unsure (focus indicator)
Template tells: 10 lines, 9 yes, 1 no (pictures specific and set into the page)
