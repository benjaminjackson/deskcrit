**First read.** The eye lands on "Changing your payment card", drops to the one-line intro, then moves down the three bold subheads. It reads at once as a help-center article on billing: calm, plain, and easy to trust. Nothing pulls the eye away from the text, and nothing feels like a template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a help article for a customer who wants to swap the card they're billed on, or who has just had a payment fail. They need to finish the task without contacting support.

**Verdict.** The typography and layout do their job. The weak point is the copy: it leaves out the one step that links adding a card to removing the old one, and it states the failed-payment deadline in a way that can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The article never says how the new card becomes the one you're charged.** The intro says "The new card is used from your next invoice". "Add a new card" stops at entering the details. Then "Remove an old card" says a card can only be removed "once another card is set as the default". A reader who follows the steps in order adds a card, tries to remove the old one, and gets stuck. They also can't tell whether their next invoice goes to the new card or the old one. *Defect:* the task in the headline can't be finished from the page. Fix direction: in "Add a new card", say whether adding a card makes it the default. If it doesn't, add the step that does, naming the control the way the other steps do. Only you know which is true. (Common sense; no catalog source.)
2. **The failed-payment timeline can be read two ways, and it's a deadline.** "We try again after three days… After a third failed try the account moves to read-only". Reading one: the third try overall, which is the original charge plus two retries. Reading two: the third retry. The two readings put the lockout days apart, and the reader will plan around whichever one they picked. The section also stops at the fact. It never tells the reader what to do, and "the account owner" leaves a reader who isn't the owner unsure whether anyone will warn them. *Defect.* Fix direction: count the tries in one clear way. Then end the section by pointing the reader to the fix the page already gives, for example "add a new card (above) before then", so the consequence is said to them.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the longest body lines look like about 80 to 85 characters (for example "We place a hold of one pound… within three working"). That's a little over Bringhurst's 45 to 75. Narrow the column slightly. *Convention.*
- Runt: "days." sits alone on the last line of the hold paragraph. Rewrap it, or let a narrower column fix it. *Convention* (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The control names in the steps ("Settings", "Billing", "Add card", "Remove") are buried in running sentences. Setting them in bold, or turning each section's steps into a short numbered list, would let someone with the app open beside the article find them at a glance. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- A clear three-step type scale. The title, the bold subheads, and the body are plainly different sizes, and the subheads also differ by weight, so the page scans in one pass.
- Spacing groups the sections well. Each subhead sits tight to its text and well clear of the section above, so the three tasks read as three separate blocks.
- The intro sentence answers the reader's real worry first: "nothing already paid is moved." Keep it.
