**First read.** My eye goes to the "Overview" title, then across the four large numbers in the top strip, then to the one blue bar (Friday, 212). Within a second it reads as a monthly report for one small hostel ("Fellside Hostel, Keswick"), for the owner or manager. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy: gray, one blue, nothing shouting. That calm is a problem, because the one piece of bad news, "Down 3 points on October" under Beds filled, looks exactly like the good news next to it.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a hostel manager opens this monthly to check how November went against October and spot what needs attention.

**Verdict.** The layout and styling are competent. But as a report it fails at the one thing that matters: its numbers contradict each other, so the manager can't trust any of them. After that, the bad news is camouflaged and the charts leave the reader to work out what they show.

**What would move it most**

1. **The figures don't reconcile, so the report loses trust on first check.** (Defect; common sense.)
   - **Beds sold vs the line chart.** Beds sold says 2,846 for November, which is about 95 a night. "Beds sold per night" peaks at about 95 and averages around 70, which adds up to roughly 2,100.
   - **The change vs October.** The strip says "Up 9% on October", but the November line sits about a third above October's the whole month.
   - **Beds filled.** "Beds filled 74%, Down 3 points" can't be true if beds sold rose 9% on a 30-day month vs a 31-day one, unless capacity grew. Nothing on the screen says it did.
   - **What it costs.** A manager who checks one number against another, which is what managers do with this screen, finds the mismatch and stops believing the rest.
   - **Fix.** If this is mock data, replace it with one consistent dataset before anyone judges the design. If it's live, every tile and chart has to come from the same query and the same period.
2. **The one figure that needs action looks like all the others.**
   - **Where.** All four comparison lines under the big numbers ("Up 9%", "Up 6%", "Down 3 points", "Same as") are the same small gray text. A manager scanning the strip has to read all four to find that occupancy fell.
   - **What it costs.** That is the one line that should send them somewhere.
   - **Fix.** Make direction visible at a glance: an up or down mark, a word weight, or a restrained status color. Always keep the words, never color alone. (Taste, argued: this is the main question the screen exists to answer.)
3. **The sparklines are decoration, and they're a template tell.**
   - **Where.** Every stat card has a small gray squiggle in its top-right corner, with no scale, no period and no endpoint. Average stay's is almost flat.
   - **What it costs.** The viewer can't read a value or a direction from them. The Beds sold sparkline repeats the big line chart right below it. A row of identical sparklined cards is something people have seen on many other dashboards, so this one looks like anyone's (unslop, "Sparklines on every stat card").
   - **Fix.** Take them out and let the number, the label and the change carry each card. Keep a sparkline only where it shows something the card can't, and then give it a period.
4. **The charts make the reader work out what they mean.**
   - **No units or scale.** The line chart's y-axis is bare numbers with no unit. Its 11 or so points don't match "per night" over 30 nights, and the ticks at 1, 10, 19 and 30 Nov are unevenly spaced (Tufte; Cairo's rule on even ticks). The weekday bars have no scale, and only Friday has a value, so Monday through Sunday can only be compared by eye (Tufte, Few: label axes and units).
   - **Legend instead of labels.** The line chart sends the reader to a legend in the corner when "November" and "October" could sit at the ends of the lines (Tufte; Wilke).
   - **Titles name topics, not findings.** "Beds sold per night" and "Check-ins by weekday" say what the chart covers, not what happened. Common sense: on a monthly review, a title that states the result ("Fridays bring the most check-ins") saves the reader the work. Write it only from what the data actually shows.

**Smaller things**
- **Grid.** The top strip splits into four equal columns, but the chart row below splits at about 65/35, which matches none of those column lines. Put the chart split on the strip's grid, for example 3 + 1 or 2 + 2 columns (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- **Blue means two things.** It's "November" in the line chart and "busiest day" in the bar chart right next to it. Give it one job (taste).
- **"Nights" in Rooms most booked has no unit.** Skiddaw dorm shows 212 nights in a 30-day month, so it must mean bed-nights. Derwent twin's 58 points the same way. A reader could take it as room-nights and misjudge each room's demand. Name the unit in the header (Nielsen 2).
- **Nav icons.** A shopping cart for Bookings is a shop convention, not a hostel one. The Settings glyph looks more like a sun or crosshair than a gear. Use icons that match the words (Nielsen 2).

**What's working**
- **Restraint.** One blue on gray, with gray used for October and the other weekdays. Where the blue lands, it means something, and the screen reads in grayscale.
- **Clear numbers.** The big figures carry their own units ("$71,150", "74%", "2.4 nights"), and every tile has a comparison period. Keep both.
- **The table.** Text columns are left-aligned, numbers are right-aligned with figures that line up, and rules are light. It scans cleanly.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a period picker. If it is, it looks like a plain label. On a monthly report, changing the period is the most likely next action, so check it in the live product.
- Hover values on the charts, focus states, and how the layout reflows on narrower screens.
