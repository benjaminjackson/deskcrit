**First read.** The eye lands on "Overview" and the row of four big numbers, then jumps to the one blue bar (Saturday, 148), then follows the two lines in "Shop visitors." It reads as a calm, tidy monthly sales dashboard for a small farm shop: neutral, well built, and quiet. After a few seconds the quiet becomes the problem. The screen never says whether November was a good or bad month, and I had to do the subtraction myself to find out that it was worse.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner or manager of Cedar Hollow Farm Shop checking how the month went and deciding what to look into. Correct me if it's meant for someone else.

**Verdict.** The build is clean and competent, but as a dashboard it doesn't do its main job yet. It reports numbers without saying what they mean. The month's real story is that visitors rose while orders and revenue fell, and nothing on the screen shows that.

**What would move it most**

1. **The main news is never stated, and nothing stands out.** The four KPI cards in the top strip are the same size, weight, and color, so none of them is read first (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance by differences of size). Each one also leaves the viewer to do arithmetic. "$31,270" over "$34,105 in October" is about an 8% drop, and "2,214" over "2,390" is about 7%, but they're set in the same neutral gray as the flat "$14 / $14" card. A bad month and a steady one look identical. The October figures do count as a comparison, so Few's "bare number" pitfall doesn't strictly apply, but the viewer still can't tell good from bad at a glance. Meanwhile the strongest mark on the page is the blue Saturday bar, which is the least urgent fact here. **Defect.** Fix: put the change and its direction next to each headline figure, using the figures already on the page and marked by more than color. Then make the month's key result the first thing seen, so the rest sit behind it.

2. **The charts name topics and leave the findings for the viewer to piece together.** "Shop visitors" shows November above October on every day, probably 30% or more higher by eye. Two cards to the left, orders and revenue are both down. Put together, that means more people came and fewer bought, which is the most useful thing on this screen. But it only appears if the viewer connects two separate cards. Titles like "Shop visitors" and "Orders by weekday" name a category, not a result. This is my own view, not an approved rule, and I'd argue it hard: a dashboard that makes the owner do this synthesis will be glanced at and closed. **Taste / common sense.** Fix: let the titles or a short note say what each chart shows. Put the visitors-versus-orders contrast where the eye goes first, with a direct path to the Orders view to find out why.

3. **Two labels can each be read two ways.** "Farm box subscribers 18%" doesn't say 18% of what: customers, orders, or revenue. The viewer can't act on a number they can't interpret. "Best-selling produce" is sorted by revenue, but apple cider sold the most units (231) and sits third. An owner reading "best-selling" as units would restock the wrong item first. **Defect** (Nielsen, heuristic 2: labels in the audience's own terms). Fix: name the base of the percentage, and say what the table ranks by, for example "Top produce by revenue". Both are edits to the existing words, not new content.

**Smaller things.**
- "Orders by weekday" has no value axis and only Saturday is labeled (148). The heights are relative, so the viewer can't read Tuesday's count. Add a light axis or label each bar (Tufte: label what a number measures).
- "Shop visitors" doesn't give its unit. The 0–900 scale reads as visitors per day, but say so.
- The two-entry legend at the top right of "Shop visitors" makes the eye go back and forth. Label "November" and "October" at the right end of each line instead (Tufte).

**What's working.**
- Gray for context, blue for the current month: October is gray against November's blue, and in the weekday bars one blue bar stands against gray. That restraint keeps the charts readable and lets color mean "look here." Keep it, and use the same move for the KPI findings.
- The grid and spacing are disciplined. The KPI strip, the two chart cards, and the table share edges and gutters, and the table's right-aligned numbers line up. Nothing needs reorganizing.
- Each KPI shows its previous-month figure right under it, which gives the comparison material. It only needs to be turned into a direction.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a control that changes the period or just a static label. A dashboard like this needs a period switch, so check it in the live product.
- Hover values on the charts and keyboard focus on the sidebar navigation.
