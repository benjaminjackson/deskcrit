**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, then jumps to the one brown tile (L4) in the run plan, then back down to the brown "Set up your kennels" button. Within a few seconds it reads as booking software for boarding kennels, sold to the people who run them. It feels calm, warm, and practical. The sage, cream, and brown suit dogs and kennels, and the run plan shows a real product instead of a stock picture.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the hero of a marketing homepage that gets kennel operators to sign up (the button), or at least to look at a sample run plan (the link).

**Verdict.** It mostly works. It's clear, quiet, and specific to its trade. The biggest problem is the headline, which tells the operator to do something their customers do. After that, the run plan uses green for booked runs, the opposite of what staff will expect.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline speaks to the wrong person** (defect, copy). "Book every kennel run without a phone call" tells the reader to book. But the button says "Set up your kennels", so the reader is the operator, and operators don't book runs; their customers do. The body copy makes it worse: "Owners book a run" means dog owners, but a reader who owns a kennel can easily take "owners" to mean themselves. A first-time operator has to work out who does what, and the real promise ("your customers stop phoning you") is never said to them. Fix: an editorial edit that names who books and lands the benefit on the operator, for example by putting back the missing subject: "Let owners book every run without phoning you." Also make "Owners" in the body say dog owners. The comparison against the phone call is the strongest thing in the copy, because it measures the product against what the operator does today. Keep it.
2. **In the run plan, green means booked** (convention, Apple HIG and Material status colors; WCAG 1.4.1). In the "Brackenhill, east block" panel, the 14 taken runs are sage green and the 3 free runs (K3, L6, M5) are white with a dashed border. Staff, and anyone using booking software, read green as "available." So at a glance the plan suggests nearly everything is open, which is the opposite of the truth. Only "18 runs, 3 free" in small gray type at top right corrects that. The second cue for "free" is weak: the dashed borders look far below 3:1 against the cream panel (WCAG 1.4.11), and white against sage is a small step in lightness. Fix: either make the free runs the marked state, or move booked runs to a neutral fill. Then give "free" a cue beyond fill, such as a label or a clearly visible border. Check the border against 3:1 in the real product, where these tiles are controls.

**Smaller things.**
- The glossy orange orb logo at top left is a stock template mark (unslop, "Logo mark as a glossy orb"). It's also the only shaded 3D object on an otherwise flat page, so it looks like it came from somewhere else. Use a flat mark in one color, or the wordmark alone.
- The hero paragraph ends with "noon." alone on its last line (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Adjust the measure or the wording so the last line carries more than one word.
- "Vaccinations checked at booking" doesn't say who checks: the text below only says the owner uploads the card. Restore the subject so the claim matches what actually happens.

**What's working.**
- **The run plan is the product, not decoration.** The selected tile L4 links to the "Run L4, large dog · Biscuit, 3 to 10 August · $210 paid" row underneath. That row shows the whole idea (run, dog, dates, payment) in one glance and backs the headline instead of repeating it. Keep it that specific.
- **One accent, used only for meaning.** The brown appears only on the primary button and the selected run, so it connects the demo to the action. Everything else stays neutral.
- **The layout is calm.** One primary action and a plain-text secondary link ("Browse a sample run plan", a good low-commitment ask). The hero text sits vertically centered against the panel, and the three feature columns below are clean, with no emoji or badges.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the run-plan tiles are interactive on the live page, and how they look on hover and focus. How the two-column hero stacks on a phone.
