**First read.** The eye lands on the big headline at left, then jumps across to the one oxblood seat (B4) in the map, then back down to the oxblood "Put on a show" button. Within about three seconds it reads as booking software for small community theatres and village halls. It feels calm, warm and trustworthy. The muted sage and oxblood suggest a curtain and a parish hall rather than a startup. The trip snags on the map: at a glance most of the hall looks green and available.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a landing-page hero that gets volunteer organizers of amateur shows to start an account or try a sample plan.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, restrained page that mostly does its job: it's clear what the product is, who it's for and what to click. The biggest problem is the product picture. The seat map uses color backwards and has no key, so the one image meant to show "audiences pick their own seats" takes a second read to decode. The headline also makes a claim that the picture doesn't show.

**What would move it most**
1. **The seat map's colors say the opposite of what they mean.** This is a defect and breaks a convention. In the card on the right, 14 seats are filled sage green and 3 (A3, B6, C5) are white with a faint dashed edge. Only the small gray "18 seats, 3 free" in the corner tells you that green means sold. Green normally means "go" or "available" (Apple HIG, Material Design: color roles), and on most seat pickers viewers have used, the open seats are the colored ones. So a first-time viewer reads a mostly empty hall, and the sell-out story is lost. This is a product whose audiences will use this exact map to choose seats, so the demo is also a sample of how readable the real thing is. Fix: make sold seats look used up, for example a muted gray or a neutral sage close to the card. Make the free seats the obvious, inviting state. Add a small key ("Sold / Free / Your seat") next to the count.
2. **The headline promises something the picture doesn't show.** This is taste, argued on its own terms. "Sell every seat in the hall, not just the front row" relies on a problem it never states: why would only the front row sell? A stranger has to guess (phone bookers ask for the front? back rows feel unsold?). The map next to it doesn't help, because the front row has a free seat (A3) and the empty seats are spread across all three rows. As a result the headline and image sit side by side instead of making one point. Fix: either restore the missing step so the line says what goes wrong today and what changes for the organizer, or have the map show the back rows filling up. The maker has to supply the actual cause. I can't.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "row" sits alone on the third line, which leaves a one-word runt and a lopsided block (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rebreak it, for example "Sell every seat / in the hall, not just / the front row", or adjust the column width.
- "Put on a show" is a good, warm ask in the viewer's own words, but it doesn't say what happens when clicked (an account? a cost?). A short line under the button saying what the first step is and what it costs would remove the hesitation. The maker has to supply those facts.
- In the card footer, "Seat B4, row B" says the row twice. "Seat B4" is enough.

**What's working.**
- The copy talks about the organizer's actual chores: drawing the hall, a door list per night, cast holds, concessions, "the box office stops taking orders by phone". That last line compares the product to the phone-and-paper routine the viewer already knows, which is the strongest kind of ask. Keep it.
- The asymmetric two-column hero, with the real product picture standing in for a stock illustration, makes it look like this product and nobody else's. There's no template badge, centered stack or emoji cards.
- The palette is disciplined. Oxblood appears only on the primary button and the buyer's seat, so it reliably means "this one". The three feature columns line up on clean thirds with tight heading-to-body spacing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indicators on the nav, the button and the "Try a sample plan" link.
