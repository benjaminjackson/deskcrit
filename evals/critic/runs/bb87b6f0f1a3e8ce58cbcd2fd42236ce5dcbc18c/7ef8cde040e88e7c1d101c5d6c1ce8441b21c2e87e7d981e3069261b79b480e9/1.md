**First read.** The bold serif headline lands first, as it should. Then the eye drops to the large left paragraph about the shed. The right paragraph comes last and looks like a footnote, because it is set noticeably smaller. That right paragraph is the invitation to volunteer. The piece feels calm, literary and warm, a bit like a parish newsletter. The bottom half of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a short web page or post for a community boat shed, and that its real job is to get local people to come and help on Thursday evenings.

**Verdict.** It looks tidy and has a consistent voice, but the type sizes work against it. The atmosphere text is the biggest thing on the page and the invitation is the smallest, and the invitation never says how to join. A reader finishes it charmed but with nothing to act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The two body paragraphs are set at two sizes, and the invitation gets the smaller one** (defect). The left paragraph looks like about 22 px and the right like about 17 px. They're in the same face and weight and neither is a heading, so they read as one level that doesn't match, not as two deliberate levels. Bringhurst (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale") and Gerstner (*Designing Programmes*) both say elements of one kind should share one style unless a rule calls for a variant. The cost is that the paragraph asking people to come is the one a skimmer is most likely to skip. Fix: set both paragraphs at one body size. If you want one to stand out, it should be the invitation, and you'd do that with weight or position, not by shrinking the other.
2. **The invitation doesn't say how to join** (defect, copy). "Thursday evenings" has no time. "The end of the lane" has no named place. "Anyone who wants to help" doesn't say whether to just turn up or contact someone first. Read as a stranger, it's a nice idea with no way in. This is the line people act on, so the gaps decide whether they come. Fix: the right paragraph needs a start time, a place a stranger could find, and the first step (turn up, or contact someone). Only you have those facts. "Nobody needs experience" is the best line here, because it answers the reader's own doubt; keep it.
3. **The baselines don't line up across the two columns** (convention). The left lines look about 34 px apart and the right about 28 px, so after the first line every line sits at a different height across the gap. Müller-Brockmann (*Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, "Construction of the grid") asks for shared baselines in side-by-side columns. Here it mostly makes the size mismatch more obvious. One body size with one line height fixes both problems.

**What's working.**
- The headline sets the hierarchy clearly: it's much bigger, bold, and in the same serif family, so it stands apart without adding a second typeface.
- The left paragraph's copy is concrete and sensory (clinker dinghies on trestles, tar and cut cedar). It belongs to this place and no other, so keep that specificity when you revise.
- The palette is restrained, with near-black on white and generous margins. That suits a quiet, handmade subject.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The lower 55% of the frame is blank. That's probably just the end of a short page, but check in a browser that nothing is meant to load there, such as a photo, map or contact block.
