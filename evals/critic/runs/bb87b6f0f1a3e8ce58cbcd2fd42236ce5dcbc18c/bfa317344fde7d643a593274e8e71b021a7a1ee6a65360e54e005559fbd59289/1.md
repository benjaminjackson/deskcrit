**First read.** The eye goes first to the serif headline, then drops to the big navy slice and its 38%. Next it hits the gray 25%, the second-biggest shape on the page, and only then crosses to the legend on the right to find out what any of it is. It reads as a calm, well-made page from an annual report. It's sober and trustworthy, but it reads like a reference page, not a point being made.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: this is a page in a company's annual report. It tells shareholders and staff how fiscal 2026 operating costs were split, so they can see where the money went.

**Verdict.** The page is clean and correct, but it shows the split without saying what it means. The chart makes readers decode colors through a separate legend. It also gives them nothing to measure the shares against: no total, no prior year. Its second-largest slice is "Other," so a quarter of the answer to "where did costs go" is unexplained.

**What would move it most**

1. **A quarter of the costs are in "Other", the second-largest slice.** The gray 25% at upper left is bigger than rent, cloud hosting, and marketing. The headline asks "where did costs go", and for one dollar in four the answer is "elsewhere." Anyone reading closely, like a shareholder or an analyst, will notice that before anything else. **Defect** (there's no checklist line for this; I'm arguing it on its own terms). The fix: split out whatever makes up most of that 25% as its own category, so "Other" ends up smaller than the smallest named slice. If it can't be split, a short footnote on what "Other" includes would help. Only the maker knows what's in it.

2. **No total and no comparison, so the shares can't be judged.** Every number here is a share of a whole the page never states. A reader can't tell whether 12% on cloud hosting is a lot of money, or whether it went up. In an annual report the questions are "how much" and "compared with last year." Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") asks that headline figures come with a unit, a period, and a comparison. This page has the period but not the other two. **Defect.** The fix: put the total operating cost near the subtitle, and add each category's change from fiscal 2025 in the legend table, which already has room for another column.

3. **The names are in a legend away from the pie, matched only by color.** To read a slice you look at its color, cross about 300 px to the legend, find the matching swatch, and come back. The slices already carry their percentages, so the legend mostly repeats them, and the only thing it adds (the names) is what the slices lack. Navy, teal, and purple are the three most likely to blur for a color-blind reader, and color is the only link between slice and name. Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*) and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*) both say to label at the data; WCAG 1.4.1 says color shouldn't be the only carrier of meaning. **Convention, with a real cost here.** The fix: put each category name with its percentage on or beside its slice, and drop the swatches. Or, if the table stays because it will hold totals and the year-on-year change (see 2), make it the main display and turn the pie into a small sorted bar chart beside it, in the same row order.

4. **The headline names the topic but not the finding.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" is a label; the reader still has to work out the point themselves. Whatever the maker wants remembered (salaries are the biggest cost, or something changed) isn't said. **Taste**, though a strong one for a report page that has room for one sentence. The fix: keep the headline's frame and make it say what the chart shows, using a claim only the maker can make. The subtitle already handles the definition, so the headline is free to carry the takeaway.

**Smaller things.**
- The legend column (about x 620–1060) lines up with nothing else on the page. Its right edge stops well short of the header's right margin at 1216, so it looks placed by eye, and the lower right is a dead area. Put it on a column the header shares, or widen it to the right margin if it picks up a comparison column (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).

**What's working.**
- The type plan is clear: a bold serif headline, a lighter serif subtitle, a plain sans for the data, and a small gray source line. That's four levels with obvious jobs and no near-duplicate sizes. Keep it.
- The slice order is right. It runs clockwise from 12 o'clock, largest first, with "Other" last, and the legend matches that order with right-aligned figures. The sourcing line with the rounding note is a good, honest detail.
- The palette is restrained, and gray is correctly kept for "Other", so the leftover category doesn't compete with the named ones for color. The real problem with it is its size (finding 1).
