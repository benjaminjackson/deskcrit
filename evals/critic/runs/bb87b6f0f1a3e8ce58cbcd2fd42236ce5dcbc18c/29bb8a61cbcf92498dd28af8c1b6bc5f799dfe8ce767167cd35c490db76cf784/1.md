**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "Travel lighter with the Wayfarer pack", drops to the gray subhead, then to the black "Shop the pack" button. Then it goes right, looking for the pack, and finds a blank white half-screen. It reads as a retail product hero for a carry-on backpack. It feels clean and competent, but empty, as if the picture hasn't loaded yet.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a product launch page. It should get a traveler to want this specific bag and click through to buy.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. The page sells a physical object and never shows it. The left half is well made, but nothing on the screen lets the viewer see the bag they're being asked to buy, and the empty right half makes that the first thing they notice.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no picture of the pack, and the right half of the hero is empty.** The text column ends at about x=850 and everything to its right is white, as is the bottom third of the screen. Someone deciding on a bag judges it by eye first: its shape, its size against a body or a seat, how it opens flat. All of that is left to the words. The headline names the Wayfarer, and nothing shows what a Wayfarer looks like. *Defect.* A product hero should show the product or something from its world; the page shows nothing (Sokolovsky, unslop, "Empty box or generic picture as the hero visual"). The empty half also leaves all the visual weight in the upper left with nothing to balance it, so the layout looks lopsided and unfinished, not deliberately open (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). **Fix:** put the pack in that right half, large enough to match the headline's weight. Ideally pick a view that proves one claim from the subhead, such as the bag under a seat or opened flat, so the picture and the words make one argument. If a picture is meant to be there and didn't load, this finding is about the capture, so check it in a live browser.

2. **The specific claims sit in the quietest text, and the loudest text is generic.** "Travel lighter" could go on any bag's page. What makes this bag different is the 22 litres, fitting under an airline seat, opening flat like a suitcase, and weighing less than a kilo. Those are all in the mid-gray subhead at about a quarter of the headline's size. Many viewers will read the headline, skip the gray line, and leave knowing only "a light bag." *Taste.* The subhead is the strongest copy on the page because it measures the bag against things the viewer already knows: the seat in front of them, a suitcase, a kilo. Give it more presence: darker text, or let the headline carry one of those concrete claims. Don't add new copy.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline looks set at weight 800 or heavier. That's the stock template heading, and it makes the page look like many others (Sokolovsky, unslop, "Headings at weight 800 or 900"). A bold or semibold weight, with size carrying the rank, would read just as clearly and look more like this brand's own page.

**What's working.**
- The subhead copy. Each clause checks the bag against something the viewer already knows (an airline seat, a suitcase, a kilo), so the viewer can judge the value without taking a claim on trust. Keep that wording through any revision.
- The type scale is clean. There are four distinct levels (eyebrow, headline, subhead, button/link), with big steps between them, all on one sharp left edge. Nothing collides.
- The actions are ranked correctly. "Shop the pack" is the only filled button, and "Compare sizes" is clearly secondary, an underlined link that still looks clickable.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- There's no logo, navigation, or header in the frame. If this is the real top of the page, the viewer can't tell who sells the bag. Check whether the capture left out a site header.
- If an image is supposed to fill the right half, check in a live browser whether it loads, or appears late because of a scroll animation.
