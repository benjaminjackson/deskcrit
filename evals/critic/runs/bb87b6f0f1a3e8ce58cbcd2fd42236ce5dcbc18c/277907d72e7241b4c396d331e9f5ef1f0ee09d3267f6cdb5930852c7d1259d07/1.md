**First read.** The bold serif headline lands first. Then the eye jumps straight to the six underlined italic phrases and skips the sentences around them, the way you scan a page for links. It reads as a short web notice of practical instructions for people helping at a village summer fete. It feels calm and plain, with the underlines adding a nagging, scattered tone.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a quick briefing that tells stallholders and helpers when to arrive, what to do, and where to park, so they get the details right on the day.

**Verdict.** The setting is clean and readable, but the emphasis is wrong. Six phrases are set in italic *and* underlined, so they look like links and pull the eye in a broken path. Two of the facts the reader will act on are missing or can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The emphasized phrases look like links and stack two kinds of emphasis.** "arrive before ten", "labelled with their ingredients", "three o'clock sharp", "collected by four" and "on the field only" are each italic and underlined, and the underline cuts through the descenders of p, y, f and g. On a web page an underline means "click me." Readers will try to tap these phrases, or wonder where they lead, and when nothing happens they've lost trust in the page. If they really are links, the text gives no clue where they go. With six of them in five lines the eye hops from one to the next, and the sentences that hold them together (who, why, where) get skimmed. Lupton says emphasis within text "usually requires only one signal" (*Thinking with Type*, p. 94), and Hochuli warns that underlining "produce[s] a restless effect, and hinder[s] linear reading." **Convention, and a defect** because the underlines promise a link. Fix: take the underlines off. Keep italic, or a medium weight, on the few items people will be held to: the times and the parking rule. That's at most three or four phrases, not six.
2. **The two most important facts are incomplete or can be read two ways.** **Defect.** The page names no date for the fete and no audience ("Please arrive before ten": who must arrive? Stallholders, helpers, everyone?). A reader who isn't sure the note is for them, or which day it means, can't act on it. "All tickets must be collected by four or the prizes go back in the box" can mean *winners must claim their prizes by four*, or *organizers collect the ticket stubs by four*. A winner who reads it the second way loses their prize. Fix: add the date and who the note is for (only you know these), and rewrite the raffle line so it says who does what. For example, restore the actor: "winners must collect their prizes by four."

**Smaller things.**
- The second paragraph ends with "only." alone on its last line, which also splits the phrase "on the field / only". Rewrap or tighten the line so at least two words carry over (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The apostrophe in "o'clock" is straight. Use a curly apostrophe (’). This is common sense; no checked source covers it.
- Taste: these are times and rules people will check on the morning, so a short list of times (arrive by 10, raffle at 3, prizes claimed by 4) would scan faster than prose. The prose is short enough that this is optional.

**What's working.**
- The type plan is disciplined: one serif family and two sizes, with the headline set apart by both size and bold weight. Keep that restraint when you redo the emphasis.
- The measure and leading are comfortable. Lines run about 70 to 75 characters with generous line spacing, and the paragraph gap clearly separates the setup instructions from the raffle and parking ones.
- The headline names what the page holds, in plain words that fit the occasion.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the underlined phrases are real links. If they are, check where they go and whether the link text says so. If they aren't, that is the strongest reason to remove the underlines.
