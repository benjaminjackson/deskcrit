**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Know what is on the shelves before the van arrives". It moves right to the stock table, where the red "Baby formula, 8, 4 days" row catches it, and then drops to the green "Set up your pantry" button. Within a few seconds this reads as stock-keeping software for food banks, aimed at whoever runs the pantry. It feels calm, practical and unshowy, like a tool made by people who have stood in the storeroom.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the top of a marketing landing page meant to get food bank coordinators to start setting up their pantry in Pantrylog.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The headline is specific, the sample table proves the claim instead of just illustrating it, and there is one clear action. The main gap is that nothing compares Pantrylog with what a coordinator already uses. The value has to be taken on trust, so the page explains the product well but gives the viewer little reason to switch.

**What would move it most**
1. **The ask asks for trust instead of setting up a comparison** (taste, argued). "Set up your pantry" is a good, concrete ask, and "See a sample stock list" is a good low-commitment second option. But nothing on the screen places Pantrylog next to whatever the coordinator counts with today. The viewer has to imagine on their own why a shared scanned list beats it. The three cards describe features ("Counts from a phone", "Dates checked for you") without saying what they replace. Direction: in the subhead or the cards, name the current method the coordinator already knows and what it fails to show them, so the gain is judged on their terms. That fact has to come from you.

**Smaller things.**
- **Hero paragraph ends on "week." alone.** The last word sits by itself on line four. Rebreak or narrow the column slightly so the last line carries more than one word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- **Headline breaks "the van / arrives".** "arrives" sits alone on line three, which splits the phrase that gives the line its point. A break such as "Know what is on the shelves / before the van arrives" would keep the time clause whole (common sense; Hochuli on phrase breaks).
- **Table card nearly lines up with the left column.** Its top looks about 10 px below the headline's cap height, and its bottom about 15 px above the button's bottom edge. That is close enough to look like an attempted match. Align the card to the headline top, or offset it clearly (Müller-Brockmann).
- **"Orders sized to the gap" is the one title a stranger has to decode.** It isn't clear what "the gap" is until they read the line under it. That line already carries the meaning, so the fix is an editorial edit that brings "what will run out" into the title.

**What's working.**
- **The sample table does the selling.** Real-looking items with units ("Pasta, 500 g"), a count time ("Counted Tuesday 10:40"), a right-aligned "On shelf" column, and a single red row whose "4 days" also says why it's red. It shows the coordinator's view the subhead promises. Keep it this specific and this quiet.
- **The copy speaks the audience's language.** "Before the van arrives", "Scan a barcode at the door", "Tins near their best-before date". Each line names who does what and when, with no product jargon.
- **The restraint holds the hierarchy.** It uses one family, one green kept for the primary button, a warm gray ground for the cards, and one red reserved for shortage. Nothing competes with the headline. The cards use line icons rather than emoji.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the two-column hero reflows on a phone, and whether the table stays readable at that width.
