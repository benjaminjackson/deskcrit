**First read.** The eye lands on the black headline at upper left, jumps to the one dark-blue rack (S4) in the map, then drops to the matching blue "Map your bike store" button. It reads right away as a tool for whoever runs a residential building, and the map shows the product instead of describing it. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, not like a template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the hero of a marketing page that gets property managers or residents' committees to set up Rackspot for their building's bike storage room.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, specific hero. The layout works and the type and color are restrained. What stands in its way is words and one color choice: the main button can be read as being about a bike shop, the map doesn't show which racks are free, and the only price on the page can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **"Bike store" means a shop to many readers, and it's on the main button.** "Map your bike store", "View a sample bike store" and "maps the bike store in your building" use the British word for a storage room. The page also says "Flat 12" (British) but prices in "$" (US). A US property manager can read "Map your bike store" as a tool for bicycle retailers and leave before the body copy corrects them. This is the ask they're meant to act on, so the double meaning is a **defect** (Nielsen 2: use the audience's own words). Fix: choose one market and use its words all the way through, for example "bike room" or "bike storage" for a US audience, or a currency that matches "flat" and "bike store". The button should keep its good structure: a verb and one exact thing to do.
2. **The map doesn't show which racks are free, and its colors are backwards.** In the right-hand panel, taken racks are sage green and free racks (R3, S6, T5) are white with dashed edges that look faint against the beige panel. Green usually means available, so at first glance the green racks look open and the white ones look empty or disabled. Only the gray "18 racks, 3 free" label lets you work it out by counting. This map is the product demo, and if it takes a second look the product looks less clear than it is. This is a **convention** break (semantic color, Apple HIG; non-text contrast, WCAG 1.4.11), and it costs something here. Fix: make free racks the marked state, with a stronger edge or a word such as "free" inside the box, and keep the strong color for the selected rack. Or add a two-item key next to "18 racks, 3 free".
3. **"$6 a month" can be read two ways.** In the footer of the map card, $6 could be what the resident pays the building for rack S4, or what Rackspot costs. The nav has a "Pricing" link and this is the only price on the screen, so a manager may take it as the product's price and then feel misled, or not realize that charging rent per rack is a feature. A price is something the viewer holds you to, so this is a **defect**. Fix: put the payer and the payee into the line, for example "Flat 12 pays $6 a month", as an edit of the words already there.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline breaks: "Give every resident a / bike space they can / find" puts the "a" at the end of a line and leaves "find" alone on the last line. Rebreak it at phrase boundaries ("Give every resident / a bike space / they can find") or adjust the width (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- The grid: the hero splits at about 652 px, but the feature row's columns start at 469 and 859, so no feature column shares an edge with the map panel. Line the map's left edge up with a feature column, or set the feature row to the hero's two-column logic (Müller-Brockmann).
- The features name cargo racks ("Mark the wide racks"), and the map's footer says "cargo bike", but S4 is the same size as every other rack. If S4 were visibly wider, the demo would show the feature too (taste).
- Nothing in the ask compares the product to what managers use now, such as a spreadsheet, a paper list on the door, or an email thread. "View a sample bike store" lets them look but not compare. A line that sets the product against what they already do would let them see the value on their own terms. The maker should supply it.

**What's working.**
- The hero image *is* the product: a real-looking floor plan with a named building, a ramp and a selected rack. It shows what the headline promises instead of using a stock picture. Keep it specific.
- The copy is concrete and speaks to the reader. "sends each resident the number of their rack" and the three feature lines each say who gets what and when. "Map your bike store" is a real verb plus an object, apart from the word problem in finding 1.
- The hierarchy is disciplined. There's one dark headline, one saturated blue used only on the selected rack and the primary button (which ties them together), a text link as the second action, and a short type scale.
