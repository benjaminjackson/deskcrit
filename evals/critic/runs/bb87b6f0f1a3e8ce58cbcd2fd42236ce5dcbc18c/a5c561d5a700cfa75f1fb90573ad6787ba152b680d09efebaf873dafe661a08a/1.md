**First read.** The eye goes to the serif headline, "Savings deposits rose every month", then drops straight to the June bar. That bar is far taller than the rest and is the loudest mark on the page. Next it moves to $4.82M at top right. It reads as a calm, trustworthy credit-union member report, and the reading order is clear. But the first question it raises is "why did June jump?", and the answer sits in gray 12 px-looking type under the chart.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public member-report page telling credit-union members, who aren't analysts, how savings deposits went in the first half of 2026.

**Verdict.** This is a solid, well-built page that mostly does its job. The one thing in the way is that its most striking mark, June's jump, comes from a one-off bonus payroll, and the page only says so in the footnote. The hatched bar fill is the second problem.

**What would move it most**

1. **The June jump goes unexplained where the eye lands on it.** Monthly growth is about $40k–$80k until May. Then June adds $282k ($868k to $1150k), so the June bar is the visual peak and pulls the eye right after the headline. The headline says "rose every month", which is true, but a viewer looking at the chart sees a sudden surge and will take it as the story. The cause, "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll", sits in the smallest, palest text on the page, below the chart box, where most readers won't go. It also colors the cards: the $4.82M total and "Up 6.1%" both include that one-off, and the page doesn't say whether the 2025 comparison had one too. This is a **defect**: it lets members believe in a growth spurt that isn't there. Fix direction: put a short note on the June bar itself, or tint June apart from the others, so the cause sits next to the mark. Keep the footnote for the source line. Judgment on the merits (common sense); no approved source states it.

2. **The diagonal hatching on every bar is decoration that shows no data.** All six bars are filled with tight blue-and-white stripes. At this density they shimmer, especially on the tall June bar. They make the bars look lighter and busier than plain fills would, and they compete with the bold value labels above them. Hatching usually marks a difference, such as a forecast or partial data, so a careful reader may wonder what it means here, when it means nothing. This is a **convention** break that costs something (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 11; Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 5). Fix direction: plain flat blue. If you want to use pattern, save it for the June bar as the way of setting it apart in finding 1.

3. **"Average deposit, Jun 2026: $214" probably isn't an average per deposit.** $1,150k ÷ 5,374 accounts = $214, so this looks like the average deposited per depositing account. If any account made more than one deposit in June, the label is wrong. A member reading "average deposit" will compare it with a single deposit of their own, and that comparison could be off. This is a **defect** on a number people will quote. Fix direction: make the label say what was divided by what, for example "Average deposited per account, Jun 2026". If it really is per deposit, check the math against the deposit count.

**Smaller things.**
- Numbers of one kind are in two formats: the bars and axis use "$1150k" and "$1200k", and the card uses "$4.82M". Pick one: either $1.15M on the June bar and $1.2M on the axis, or thousands everywhere with a comma ($1,150k).
- "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025" use the British "up X on Y" phrasing. To a US reader, "on Jun 2025" reads like a date stamp. "Up $11 from June 2025" says the comparison plainly. This is an editorial edit only.

**What's working.**
- The headline states the finding, not the topic, and the subtitle and chart title give the measure and units exactly ("US dollars per month"). Keep that habit.
- Every stat card pairs its number with a comparison to the same period last year, and the green "Up" is spelled out in words, so it doesn't depend on color.
- The structure is restrained: one blue, one serif for the brand voice with a sans for the data, direct value labels on the bars, bars starting from $0, and a clear current-page state in the nav. It reads as one calm, credible piece.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the chart and the card column reflow on a phone (whether the cards stack above or below the chart), and whether the chart has a text alternative for screen readers.
