**First read.** The eye lands on "Plan and billing", drops to the teal-outlined Garden card, then goes straight to the teal "Save changes" button. It reads as the billing settings for a community-garden membership tool, used by a volunteer committee. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. It's well built. The one place the eye stops to think is the gap between picking a plan and pressing Save.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since nothing was given: a garden committee member or treasurer checks or changes the plan, keeps the billing contact current, and finds past invoices.

**Verdict.** This is a solid, mostly well-made settings page. The biggest thing in the way is money. The page doesn't say whether Garden is the current plan or a new pick, whether "Save changes" also commits a plan change, or what a change costs and when it starts. On a billing screen, that doubt is the part that matters.

**What would move it most**
1. **Picking a plan is ambiguous, and it costs money.** The three plan cards sit in their own section at the top. "Save changes" sits under a divider, inside "Billing details," directly below the invoice address. A treasurer who clicks Network can't tell three things:
   - whether Save applies to the plan as well as the billing fields
   - whether the outlined Garden card means "you're on this now" or "you just picked this"
   - whether moving from $22.00 to $60.00 a month takes effect today, is prorated, or starts on the next invoice

   One reading is "I'm only updating the email." The other is "I just tripled our bill." Getting it wrong means a surprise charge to a volunteer group, or a plan that quietly never changed.

   This is a **defect**. The page doesn't show the current state (Nielsen, heuristic 1: visibility of system status). The control is also separated from the thing it changes (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*: natural mapping). And the one complex step on the page has no short help next to it (Nielsen, heuristic 10).

   Direction of the fix:
   - Mark the current plan in words on its card.
   - Make the plan change its own clearly named action, or put Save visibly below both sections.
   - Add a short line by the cards saying when a change takes effect and how it's billed. Only you know those facts, so I won't supply them.

2. **Emoji in the count badges.** The sidebar has 👥 38, 🌱 12 and 🔔 3, and the Invoices heading has 🧾 12. This is a stock move from many AI-made templates. It makes the page look like anyone's product, and next to the restrained type and single teal it's the one element that looks off-brand. The emoji don't add meaning either, because the label beside each count already says what's being counted.

   This is a **convention** broken at a cost, a template tell (Sokolovsky, unslop: a count is a plain number). The fix: drop the emoji and keep plain numbers. If an icon is really needed, use one icon set.

**Smaller things**
- **Invoices table.** The heading says 12, but only three rows show, with no "view all." The invoice numbers (WF-0212 and the others) are set like plain text, so there's no visible way to open or download an invoice, which is the main reason anyone comes to this table. Add a visible link per row, or a download action, and a way to reach the other nine. This is common sense.
- **Required fields.** "Billing email" and "Invoice address" don't say whether they're required. Mark the required fields (Nielsen Norman Group: Budiu, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- **Pale borders.** The plan-card borders and input borders look pale gray on white and may fall below the 3:1 contrast that component edges need (WCAG 1.4.11). The radio buttons rescue the cards, but the inputs rely on their borders alone. Measure the border colors against white.

**What's working**
- **Restraint with color.** There's one teal, used only for the selected plan, the checkbox and the primary button. Because nothing else competes, the eye goes where it should. Keep it to those jobs.
- **The plan cards are well written.** Each one gives a name, one line on what the plan covers, and a price with its unit and period ("$22.00 a month"), so the plans can be compared at a glance.
- **The invoices table is clean.** Amounts are right-aligned so the figures line up, there are no heavy grid lines, and the dates are spelled out with no day/month ambiguity. Keep it that sparse.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Focus states on the plan cards and inputs.
- What happens after "Save changes": whether a confirmation step appears before the plan change goes through. That step matters a lot here.
