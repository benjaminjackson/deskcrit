**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the lead item in a library's email or web newsletter, telling regular patrons that the reading room reopens Monday, and what's new in it.

**Verdict.** It's a handsome, well-ordered page that buries its own news. The biggest and boldest lines are about what the room is called. The fact that it reopens Monday is in the smallest, palest text, so most readers come away with a feeling, not the news.

**What would move it most**
1. **The news is in the faintest text on the page (defect).** All the size and weight goes to the quote and the attribution. "Reopens on Monday", the only line a patron can act on, is the smallest, lightest-gray text at the bottom. On its own the quote doesn't say anything has happened: "nobody calls it anything else" could open a story about the room closing as easily as one about it reopening. A reader who stops after the headline, as most newsletter skimmers do, leaves without the date. A page's headline should state what its section is about (WCAG 2.4.6, headings and labels). A poster or lead item carries one message that reads at a glance, with the details clearly secondary (Müller-Brockmann). The quote can stay as the hook, but "reopens Monday" needs to show up in the first read. Either work it into the headline zone (a short line above or below the quote at a size and color that reads before the attribution does), or move it into the bold line and let the attribution drop to the gray level. "Monday" is also a commitment readers will hold you to. Unless the issue's date sits right next to it, it's ambiguous which Monday. Give the date if the newsletter might be read late.

2. **The headline's quote and dash marks are typewriter characters at 100 px (convention, plus taste).** The headline uses straight `"` and `'` marks and a hyphen for the dash ("room - nobody"). At body size you'd never notice. At this size they're among the largest shapes on the page. The straight marks look like plain code text, and the short hyphen makes a hole in the middle of line two. That clashes with an otherwise carefully set serif. Bringhurst (5.2.1) asks for a true dash. Curly quotes are a firm typesetting norm that no checked source here covers, so count that part as my judgment. Use curly quotes and apostrophes, and an em dash (closed up) or a spaced en dash. Then hang the opening quote mark into the left margin: right now it pushes the headline's visible left edge out of line with "LIBRARY NEWSLETTER" and the body text. Hanging it is my own call, not a sourced rule.

3. **The copy makes the reader guess twice (defect).** First, "the 'old' reading room": the single quotes read as scare quotes. A stranger can't tell whether the room is old, whether it's being renamed, or whether "old" is a nickname. If the point is the nickname, say that it's what people call it. Second, the attribution line has no verb that ties it to the news, so the quote and the announcement sit side by side and the reader has to connect them. A small edit fixes the first, such as making clear "old" is the name people use. For the second, the bold line or the gray line needs to say what that loyalty means for the reader this Monday. Only you know what that is, so I won't write it.
