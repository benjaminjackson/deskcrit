**First read.** The eye lands on the "Sales" title and the row of four big numbers, starting with $27,640. It then jumps to the one blue bar (Sat, 126) and follows the blue line. This reads as a monthly sales dashboard for a small fabric shop. It feels calm and orderly, and a little anonymous. Nothing on the page tells you what happened this month. You have to work it out.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I was told nothing: the shop owner opens this to see how November went against October and what to act on.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, well-built dashboard that shows its data but never states a conclusion. The biggest thing in the way is that every comparison and chart leaves the owner to do the math and draw the conclusion themselves. The weekday chart also can't be read beyond its one labeled bar.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page never says what happened.** Every KPI card puts a number next to October's ("$27,640" over "$24,110 in October"), so the comparison is there (Few). But the viewer has to subtract in their head to learn whether the month was good and by how much. Four cards means four subtractions. Each one gives the same answer, that all four metrics rose, and the page never says it. The chart titles ("Daily orders", "Orders by weekday") are bare topics, so the one pattern the design chose to point at, the blue Saturday bar, still has to be decoded. Direction: show the change on each card as a signed difference or percentage next to the October figure. Let the weekday title or a short note say what the blue bar means. Use only claims the data already supports. *Defect* for the KPI math, since it slows the main job. *Taste* for the takeaway titles (common sense: the owner came for the answer, not the topic).
2. **The weekday chart has only one readable number.** "Orders by weekday" has no value axis, and only Saturday carries a label (126). So the owner can see Saturday is highest but can't tell if Sunday is 20 below it or 60. The title also doesn't say if the bars are totals or averages. That matters here: November 2026 has five Sundays and five Mondays but four of every other day. If these are totals, Sunday and Monday get a free extra day and the comparison is skewed. Direction: label every bar's value, or add a light value axis, and say "total" or "average per day" in the title. If they're totals, switch to an average per day (Tufte, ch. 2: the reader should never need to guess what a number measures). *Defect.*
3. **"Best-selling" isn't defined, and the table contradicts its own ranking.** The table is sorted by metres, but by revenue, Wool melton ($4,508) beats Cotton lawn ($3,680). An owner who scans the Revenue column sees a list that looks out of order and has to work out why. Direction: say what "best-selling" means in the heading (by metres or by revenue) or mark the sorted column, so the order explains itself. With only three rows, add a visible way to see the rest. *Defect.*
4. **The currency may be wrong.** The shop signs itself "Bolt & Bobbin, Norwich" and measures in "Metres", yet every amount is in "$". A UK owner reading revenue in dollars would ask whether the figures were converted, and these are the numbers they'll act on. This is fine if the shop really trades in dollars. If not, the symbol, and possibly the numbers behind it, need checking. *Defect unless the dollars are intended.*

**Smaller things.**
- Daily orders chart: the series names are in a legend at top right, so the eye goes back and forth between the legend and the lines. Label the two lines directly at their right ends (Tufte; Wilke).
- Daily orders chart: each line looks like it has about 11 points across 30 days, which isn't daily. Check whether the data is sampled. Either plot every day or rename the chart.
- The "Sales" title (~24 px) and the KPI figures (~26 px) look almost the same size. They read as an accident, not two levels (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Pick a clearly separate size for one of them, or set the title apart by weight instead.
- The date range "1 to 30 November 2026" at top right is plain gray text. If the period can be changed, it needs to look like a control (Norman, signifiers). If it can't, nothing is lost.
- The Settings icon looks like a brightness sun rather than a gear, so the icon doesn't say what the item does. The label saves it, but use the expected glyph.

**What's working.**
- **Color restraint that means something.** The whole page is gray except one blue, and the blue always marks "this period" or "the standout" (the November line, the Saturday bar). The highlight carries a label too, not color alone. Keep this rule as you add things.
- **A grid you can feel.** The KPI row, the two chart cards, and the table share one set of edges and gutters, and the table's numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures. The page scans in one pass and fits on one screen.
- **Comparisons are built into every KPI.** The October figure under each number is the right instinct. It just needs to finish the thought (finding 1).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the date range or table rows can be clicked, and what keyboard focus looks like on the sidebar and cards.
