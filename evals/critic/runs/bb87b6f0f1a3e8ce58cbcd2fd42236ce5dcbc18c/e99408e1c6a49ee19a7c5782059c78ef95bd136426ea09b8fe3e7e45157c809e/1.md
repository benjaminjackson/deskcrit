I critiqued the ferry timetable. It works and needs no rebuild. What hurts it most is a heavy black grid that boxes every cell, and the "Runs" column, which hides which sailings run on the reader's day.

**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", then drops into a heavy black grid. The grid is the loudest thing on the page, louder than any departure time. It reads as a plain, honest timetable for people catching a ferry, but it feels like a spreadsheet printout. You read it cell by cell instead of scanning down for "my next boat". The "Runs" column catches you late: you only find out on the fourth row that not every sailing runs every day.

**What it's for.** My assumption: riders on the north landing to market quay route look up the next sailing that runs on the day they're traveling.

**Verdict.** It does its job, but it's clumsy. The title is clear, the times are legible and in order, and nothing is missing. Two things slow the lookup. Every cell sits in a near-black cage, and the information that decides whether a sailing runs at all is in the last column, so you have to read every row to answer "what runs on Saturday?"

**What would move it most**
1. **The grid is louder than the times (defect).** All four columns and seven rows sit in black rules about 2 px thick, as dark as the numbers. The lines compete with the data, and the eye reads boxes rather than running down a column of times. Each row also gets chopped into four separate compartments, so a departure and its arrival don't read as one sailing. Tufte calls this non-data ink. Lupton, *Thinking with Type* (p. 157), says table rules should "illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell." The fix: remove the vertical rules and either thin the horizontal ones to a pale gray hairline or replace them with space or a pale band on alternate rows. The times should become the darkest thing in the table.
2. **The day each sailing runs is the deciding fact, and it's buried (defect).** "Runs" is the far-right column, in the same regular weight as everything else. Daily, Weekdays, and Weekends mix in no pattern, so someone traveling on Saturday has to check all seven rows to rule out 12:40 and 17:10. That's the sailing a rider plans around and holds the operator to. A wrong read means a missed boat. The fix: make the weekday-only and weekend-only sailings stand out from the daily ones. Move "Runs" next to the departure time, mark the exceptions so they jump out (weight, a mark, or a note in the row), or split the table by day type. Pick whichever matches how riders ask the question.
3. **The Period column spends a full column on labels that repeat (taste).** "Morning, Morning, Midday, Midday, Afternoon, Afternoon" takes the widest left column, the first place the eye looks, for information the times already give. That pushes the departure times, the thing people came for, into the second column. Either drop it or turn it into group labels that span each pair of rows, with a little extra space between groups, so the periods mark sections instead of repeating on every line.

**Smaller things.**
- The rows are spaced evenly with nothing showing the period groups. Extra space between Morning, Midday, Afternoon, and Evening would make the day easier to scan. This folds into fix 3.
- The time columns are left-aligned. Set them in tabular figures and right-align or center them, so "11:00" and "17:10" line up digit for digit (Bringhurst 4.4.3).

**What's working.**
- The title and subtitle do exactly their job. "River ferry timetable" plus "North landing to market quay, spring season" gives the route, the direction, and when it applies in two lines, with a clear jump in size and weight between them. Keep it.
- Times are 24-hour and zero-padded ("06:40"), so there's no AM/PM confusion on a schedule people will act on.
- The type is one plain sans in black and gray, with nothing decorative. The restraint suits a transit notice, so don't add color for its own sake.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the table reflows on a phone. With four wide columns it could easily scroll sideways, so check it at phone width.

**Checklist.**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 7 yes, 1 no (every element does a job)
- What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 11 yes, 2 no (space shows the groups; boxes do not split groups)
- Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (tabular figures aligned)
- Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
- Pictures: skipped
- Charts and data: 16 lines, 15 yes, 1 no (data-ink)
- Interface: skipped
- Template tells: 10 lines, 10 yes
