**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "River ferry timetable," then drops into three paragraphs of gray-black prose and starts reading for a departure time. It never finds one. This reads as a calm, plainly set info page for local ferry riders. Because the heading promises a timetable, the first feeling is that the timetable failed to load or was left out.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page where a passenger checks when the next ferry leaves, from which landing, and whether it suits their trip today.

**Verdict.** As a timetable it fails. A page with that title gives no times, so a reader can't plan a crossing from it, and the vague time words in the prose make that worse. The typesetting is good, but the page is missing the thing it is named for.

**What would move it most**
1. **There is no timetable under the "timetable" heading.** The heading at the top promises departures. What sits under it is three paragraphs of description, so the heading doesn't name what its section holds (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.6, Headings and labels). A rider who wants "when is the next boat from the north landing?" has to read every line, then still can't answer it. Krug's test is whether a page is self-evident in seconds, and this one isn't. This is a **defect**. Fix: put the departures in a scannable form, a table of times for each landing and each season, set in tabular figures. Then let the prose become short notes under it. If the times really are only posted at the landings, rename the page to what it is (for example, ferry service information) and point clearly to where the times are.
2. **The time words a rider acts on can be read two ways.** This is a **defect**, because these are the lines people plan a trip around:
   - "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" has no first or last departure. A rider can't work out the next boat from it.
   - "Runs from the north landing to the market quay" doesn't say whether the forty minutes covers both directions. One reading is a boat each way every forty minutes; the other is a round trip every forty minutes, which halves the departures from either landing. A rider who guesses wrong waits up to forty minutes.
   - "The last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" moves every day and doesn't give a time. A rider who reads "before dusk" as "around dusk" misses the last boat home.
   - "In winter" doesn't give the dates when the hourly service starts and ends.

   Fix: put back the missing facts as the maker knows them: first and last departure times, which direction(s) the interval covers, the winter dates, and a clock time or rule for the last winter crossing. Keep the sentences. Just put the numbers into them.

**Smaller things.**
- The bicycle paragraph sits between the two schedule paragraphs, so you have to read past it to get from the summer frequency to the winter one. Put the schedule facts together and the bicycle note after them. This is taste.
- The single column hugs the left edge at this width. The right ~45% of the frame is empty and nothing balances it, so the page looks unfinished more than restful (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). A timetable table could use that width, or the column could sit in a deliberate frame. This is taste.

**What's working.**
- The text setting reads easily: body text looks about 18 px with roughly 1.5 line spacing, lines run about 70 characters (inside Bringhurst's 45 to 75), and it is set near-black on white. Keep all of this when the table goes in.
- The bold heading clearly outranks the body through both size and weight, so there is one obvious place to start.
- There is no decoration and no template styling. The plain look suits a public service notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page reflows on a phone. That matters here, because riders are likely to check it at the landing, and any added table needs to stay readable without scrolling sideways.
