**First read.** The eye goes to the headline first, then right to the blue B4 tile, then down to "Locker B4, top row / $10 deposit held". It reads at once as software for schools to manage lockers, keys and deposits, aimed at the school office. It feels calm, plain and competent, like a tool and not like a pitch.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing homepage that gets school office staff to start a trial ("Start with one corridor").

**Verdict.** It does its job. The purpose is clear in one look, the product picture shows the actual product, and there is a single, specific ask. What stands in the way is small but real: the one detail line in the product picture contradicts the plan above it. Also, the page never compares itself to the way the office tracks keys today.

**What would move it most**
1. **The picture contradicts itself on "top row". Defect.** The detail strip says "Locker B4, top row", but B4 is in the second row of the plan, under row A. A reader can take this two ways. Either it means the top unit of a stacked locker, which the plan doesn't show, or it's a mistake. The audience is office staff whose job is getting key and locker records right. For them, a demo record that doesn't match its own plan undercuts the claim that "the office sees who has which key". Fix: make the line agree with the plan, or say what "top" refers to if it means the upper unit.
2. **The ask doesn't show the value next to what the office already uses. Copy, taste.** "Start with one corridor" is a good ask: it names a small, exact first step the viewer can picture. But nothing on the page sets Lockerly against the office's current method, whatever that is: a spreadsheet, a paper key list, a plan on the wall. So the viewer has to take the value on trust. The feature lines get close ("a lost key is a quick lookup") without saying what it replaces. Direction: in the subhead or one feature line, name the current method the viewer already knows, so they can judge the gain on their own terms. You know the actual method; I'd only be guessing.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "bell" sits alone on the third line, splitting "the first / bell". Rebreak it so the last line holds a phrase, e.g. "lockers before / the first bell". (Bringhurst 2.4.2)
- "Deposits back in July" has no verb, so it could mean the school refunds, Lockerly refunds, or families get money back on a fixed date. The body says "at the end of the year", which only matches July in some school calendars. Because this is about money, put the verb back and tie it to the school's year-end, not a month.
- The free lockers (A3, B6, C5) are marked by a faint dashed outline and a white fill that is barely lighter than the cream card. The outline looks well under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11), so the lockers the product is about read as gaps more than as "available". Strengthen the edge, or make the fill difference larger.

**What's working.**
- The product picture is specific and earns its place. Named corridor, "Stairs to the labs" in the plan, key 2214, a $10 deposit, and the count "18 lockers, 3 free" agrees with the three white tiles. It shows the product's job without any explanation. Keep this kind of real detail.
- Restraint: one blue, used only for the primary button and the selected locker, on a warm neutral ground. Sage and cream carry the rest, and the hierarchy holds even in grayscale.
- The copy speaks the audience's language ("caretaker", "first bell", "when they enrol", "pupil") and the feature headings say what happens, not category names.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the locker plan is interactive (hover and selected states on the tiles), and focus styles on the button and link.
