**First read.** My eye goes to the title, then straight to the tall Growth bar. That bar looks about two and a half times as tall as Starter's, so the first impression is that Growth converts far better than the other plans. After that I read the table on the right, then the Definition note. The page is calm and clean, and it reads like an internal analytics dashboard for a SaaS product team.

**What it's for.** Assumption: product and growth staff use this page to compare how well trials on each plan turn into paid accounts.

**Verdict.** The page is tidy, clearly defined and well labeled, but the main chart misleads. Its bar axis starts at 2.5%, so differences of a few tenths of a percentage point look like a gap of several times, and that is what a viewer will remember.

**What would move it most**

1. **The bars start at 2.5%, not zero.** This is in the main chart, left column. Growth's bar is drawn about 2.5× as tall as Starter's (1.5 points of bar against 0.6). The actual values are 4.0% and 3.1%, which is about 1.3×. Tufte's lie factor here is roughly 5: the bars show a +150% difference where the data has +29%. Team (3.6%) against Enterprise (3.4%) looks like a real step but is only 0.2 points. A viewer who glances at the chart, which is how dashboards get read, will think Growth is in a different league and Starter is failing. They could make pricing or funnel decisions on a difference the data doesn't show.
   - Bar length encodes value, so a bar's value axis must start at zero (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, 17.1; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, ch. 2).
   - **Fix:** start the axis at zero so bar lengths keep the real proportions. If the small differences are the point, use dots on a labeled scale that doesn't start at zero (Wilke).
   - This also hides how uncertain the numbers are. Enterprise's 3.4% rests on 38 paid accounts. By my rough arithmetic from the table, the Team-vs-Enterprise gap and possibly more are within noise. A chart drawn to scale stops pushing that gap at the viewer.
   - **Defect** (convention broken, and here it misleads).

2. **The title names the topic but not the finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" says what is measured, not what it shows. With no takeaway written down, the viewer takes the one the exaggerated bars give them. Once the axis is fixed, a title that states the result in the page's own terms (which plan converts best, and by how much) sets the reading instead of leaving it to the bar heights. Any sentence like that has to come from what the maker concludes, not from the chart's look.
   - **Taste/common sense:** the usual sources for this point are not on the approved list.

**Smaller things.**
- **No comparison point.** The page has no prior quarter and no target, so a viewer can't tell whether 3.1% for Starter is good, bad or normal. Put last quarter's rate or the target next to each plan (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").

**What's working.**
- **The subtitle and Definition note say exactly what "converted" means:** the 30-day window, first invoice paid, and trials still open on 30 October counted as not converted. Most dashboards leave readers guessing at this. Keep it.
- **The table backs every rate with its counts.** The counts in the table work out to the rates on the bars (286/9,210 ≈ 3.1%, and so on). Its right-aligned tabular numbers can be compared down the column at a glance. Keep the table: it lets a careful reader check the chart and see how small each sample is.
- **The chart is restrained:** one hue, light gridlines, values labeled directly on the bars, plans in tier order, and a source line. Nothing competes with the data, so once the scale is fixed the chart will read cleanly.
