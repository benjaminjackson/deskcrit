**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, crosses to the purple B4 cell in the easel grid, then comes back down to the purple "Add your studio" button. Using the same purple for "your easel" and "your action" ties the mockup to the ask. It reads as a booking tool for life-drawing studios and feels calm, plain, and grown-up, not like a template.

**What it's for.** Assumed: the homepage hero of a booking product, meant to get people who run life-drawing studios or classes to sign their studio up.

**Verdict.** This is a good page that mostly does its job. The product mock makes the claim concrete, and the copy is specific to this trade. The main problem is that the hero talks to two different people: the headline speaks to the student who books, and the button speaks to the studio that signs up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline and the button speak to different people (defect).** "Book life-drawing easels without a paper list" puts the viewer in the student's seat, because the student is the one who books. "Add your studio" asks them to be the studio owner. The body copy names both in the third person ("Students book… the tutor sees…"), so it doesn't settle who "you" is. Then the nav offers "For studios", which suggests this page is *not* the studio page.
   - A tutor may read the headline as a student-facing app and leave.
   - A student who clicks the only strong button lands in a sign-up flow that isn't theirs.

   The fix is to pick the hero's reader. Going by the button, that's the studio. Make the headline name what the studio gets, for example by putting the actor back: "Let students book life-drawing easels without a paper list." Then make "For studios" either clearly the same audience or clearly something else (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, conceptual model). Keep "without a paper list" as it is. It sets the product against what the viewer already uses, which is the strongest kind of comparison a headline can make.

2. **In the easel grid, the color for "taken" looks like "available" (my judgment, not a published rule).** Fifteen cells are filled sage green and the three free ones (A3, B6, C5) are white with dashed outlines. In most seat and booking maps, a filled, colored cell means "you can pick this" and a pale or outlined one means "gone". At a glance, this grid says the studio is nearly empty, which is the opposite of the truth. Only the small gray "18 easels, 3 free" in the corner corrects that, and only if the viewer reads it and counts. This grid is the product's one demonstration, so a wrong reading costs more here than it would elsewhere. Make the taken easels the quiet neutral and give the free ones the visible color or treatment, or add a two-item key.

**Smaller things.**
- **Headline break:** the last line is "list" alone, and the break splits "paper | list" (Bringhurst, short last-line word). Change the headline's width slightly so "a paper list" stays on one line.
- **Small type sizes:** the logo, nav, the announcement link, the button, the lead paragraph, the feature headings, and the feature body look like six or seven sizes between about 14 and 20 px. Measure them. If they really are that close, cut to about three small sizes and use weight for the rest.

**What's working.**
- **The mock is a real scene, not a generic dashboard.** It shows a named studio and class, a model stand that places rows B and C, and the booked easel's detail: "good light", spring term, $140 paid. The "good light" detail sets up the "Easels by the light" feature below, so the mock and the copy tell one story. Keep that specificity.
- **The copy is in the trade's own words:** term, drop-ins, register, model stand, window light. It couldn't be on anyone else's page.
- **The calls to action are clearly ranked.** There's one filled button and one underlined text link. The announcement is a plain underlined link rather than a pill badge, and the features are a plain three-column row with no icons. It doesn't look like a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the grid cells react on hover or tap, which would show whether the mock is clickable.
