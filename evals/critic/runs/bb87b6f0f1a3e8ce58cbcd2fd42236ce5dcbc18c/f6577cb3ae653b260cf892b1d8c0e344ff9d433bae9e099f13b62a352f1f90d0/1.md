**First read.** The eye goes first to the one saturated blue bar, Saturday in “Bottles by weekday”, then left to the row of four large numbers, then to the blue line. Within a few seconds it reads as the November sales overview for a small wine shop (Two Rivers Wine, Portland). It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also a bit like every SaaS dashboard.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner or manager's monthly glance: how November went compared with October, and what sold. It's a desktop web app.

**Verdict.** This is a competent, well-built dashboard that mostly does its job. The KPI row is clear and every figure comes with a comparison. Its weak spot is the two charts. Neither says what its numbers measure, and they use the same blue for two different meanings. The panel that pulls the eye first is the one the viewer can least read correctly.

**What would move it most**

1. **The charts don't say what their numbers count.** This is a defect.
   - **The 148 in “Bottles by weekday” has no unit, no period and no axis.** It could be Saturday's total for the month or the average per Saturday, and the other six bars have no values at all.
   - **It can't be squared with the KPI above it.** The bars look like they sum to about 700 against 1,384 bottles sold, and Saturday can't be an average either. The viewer has nothing to settle which reading is right.
   - **The owner may act on this number.** Saturday is the one figure an owner might use for staffing or stock, and they will take one reading or the other.
   - **“Web shop visitors” has the same gap.** It plots 11 points over the month with no stated interval, so a value near 700 could be per day, per three days or something else. A viewer also can't tie it to a monthly total.
   - Tufte, *Visual Display*: the reader should never have to guess what a number measures.
   - **Fix direction:** put the measure and period in each chart title or a y-axis label, for example “bottles per …” and “visitors per …”, using the maker's real definitions. Give the bar chart a scale or label every bar.

2. **Blue means two different things in two charts side by side.** This is a defect (convention: Material Design and Apple HIG on color roles; Arnheim on grouping by similarity).
   - **The two blues don't mean the same thing.** In the visitors chart, blue means “November, versus October in gray”. Right next to it, blue means “the top day, versus the rest in gray”.
   - **The color pattern invites a misreading.** Because both charts pair one blue element with gray, a viewer tends to read the gray bars as “October” or “the past”.
   - **Fix direction:** give each meaning its own treatment. For example, keep blue for “this period” everywhere, and mark the top day another way, such as a darker shade of the bar gray plus its label.

3. **The gray marks look too faint.** This is a convention issue: WCAG 1.4.11 asks for 3:1 for chart marks that carry meaning.
   - The October line and its legend swatch are light gray on white and look under 3:1. The gray weekday bars look under 2:1.
   - The October line is the comparison the whole chart exists for. Anyone with low vision, or a laptop in a bright room, loses it.
   - **Fix direction:** measure both colors. Darken them until they reach 3:1 against white while staying clearly lighter than the blue.

**Smaller things.**
- **Legend:** the November/October legend in the top right of the visitors chart makes the eye travel. Label each line at its right end instead (Tufte, Wilke).
- **Chart titles:** both titles name a topic, not a finding. On a monthly overview, a title that says what happened would save the viewer reading the chart. This is taste.
- **“Wine club share”:** it doesn't say share of what (revenue, orders or customers), so 27% can be read several ways. Name the base. This is a copy edit, not new content.
- **Date range:** “1 to 30 November 2026” in the top right looks like plain text. If it's the period picker, it needs to look like a control. If it's static, the owner has no visible way to look at another month.
- **Settings icon:** it's a sun or crosshair glyph rather than the gear people expect, so Settings is harder to find by icon (platform conventions, Apple HIG and Material).
- **Table:** “Best-selling wines” stops at three rows with no route to the full list, and the wide Producer column leaves a big gap before the numbers.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI row.** There are four figures, each with its October value right under it in a quieter gray, so every headline number has a reference without any extra chrome. Keep that pairing.
- **The type scale.** It's disciplined: page title and KPI numbers, panel titles, body and table text, then small captions and axis labels. The levels are told apart by size and also by weight and color.
- **The table.** Numbers are right-aligned with tabular figures, and the rules are hairline. It reads quickly and stays quiet.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the date range and the chart points are interactive (hover values on the bars would partly fix finding 1).
- Focus states in the sidebar.
