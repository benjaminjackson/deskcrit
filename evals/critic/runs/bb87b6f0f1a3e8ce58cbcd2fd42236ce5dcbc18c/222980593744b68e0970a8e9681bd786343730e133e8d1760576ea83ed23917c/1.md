**First read.** The eye lands on the large headline "Quote a bathroom from the doorway", drops to the brown "Write your first quote" button, then crosses to the quote card, where the bold $1,731.00 total holds it. It reads straight away as quoting software for tilers and bathroom fitters. It feels calm, warm and practical, more like a tradesperson's notebook than a startup page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this: a landing page that gets tilers and bathroom fitters to start their first quote, judged here as the hero and the first feature row at desktop width.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, specific page that does its job. The type is disciplined, the copy is concrete and there is one clear action. The one thing holding it back is that the hero's own proof is set as the weakest text on the screen.

**What would move it most**
1. **The proof of the headline is buried at the foot of the card.** The headline promises speed on site: "from the doorway", "Send it before you leave the house". The only thing in the image that proves it is "Sent 14:12, opened 14:30" under the total. That line is the smallest, palest text on the card, below the bold total, where the eye has already stopped. So the card reads as "here is what a quote looks like", when it could read as "this quote reached the customer before you got back to the van". The body copy also says "the customer accepts it from the link", but the card stops at "opened", so the last step of the promise isn't shown. Fix: give the sent/opened timing enough weight or position that it reads as part of the card's story and not as metadata. If the product records acceptance, show that state on the card. That is the maker's fact to supply, not something to invent for the mockup. *Taste*, but it is the difference between showing the output and showing the claim.

**Smaller things.**
- The three gray dots in the card's title bar are window-mockup chrome. They do no job, and they frame the quote as a desktop window when the page says the customer reads it on a phone. Remove them, or frame the card the way the customer actually sees it. *Common sense, no approved source.*

**What's working.**
- **The copy is specific to this trade throughout.** "Quote a bathroom from the doorway", "cut waste for the tile size you pick, so you do not run short on day two", "You get a text when they do." Each line says who does what and why it matters to a tiler, in their own words. Keep this voice. It is what separates the page from every other "send quotes faster" product.
- **The quote card is real data, set like a real table.** It has real line items, real units (true × and m² characters), prices right-aligned in tabular figures, a bold total, and pale hairlines instead of boxes. "See a sample quote" then lets the viewer judge the output against quotes they already write, which is the strongest kind of ask.
- **The hierarchy and grid are clean.** There is one dominant headline, one filled primary button with an underlined secondary link, and a tight two-line headline. The card sits on the same column grid as the three-up feature row below it. The feature titles name exactly what each column holds.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus, hover and pressed states on the button and links; how the hero stacks on a phone, which matters here because the audience is on site.
