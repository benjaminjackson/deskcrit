**First read.** My eye goes straight to the dark band of weekday cells between about 09:00 and 16:00, then up to "When new tickets arrive." Next I go right to the three numbers. In two seconds it reads as an internal support report that answers "when are we busiest." It feels calm, sparse and trustworthy. It looks like a page you could act on.

**What it's for.** My assumption: support leads and managers at Northlight Desk use this to plan staffing around when tickets come in, so the numbers have to agree and be easy to act on.

**Verdict.** The chart is good: it uses the right form, it's quiet, and the peak shows at once. But the summary numbers next to it contradict the chart. A manager who staffs by the "Busiest hour" figure will staff the wrong hour. Until the numbers match the chart, the page can't be trusted.

**What would move it most**

1. **"Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" disagrees with the chart. (Defect.)** In the heatmap, the darkest cells (30+) are in the 11:00 column, on Monday and Friday. The 10:00 column is 25–29 on every weekday, and on Tuesday to Thursday 11:00 is also 25–29. So by the chart's own legend, 11:00 is the busiest weekday hour, not 10:00. The hour labels sit over the cells they name, so this isn't a reading-the-axis problem. A viewer who notices loses trust in every number on the page. A viewer who doesn't will plan cover for the wrong hour. Fix: check the stat against the data behind the cells and correct whichever is wrong. If "busiest hour" is worked out differently from the cells (for example a median, or start-of-hour versus end-of-hour), say so in its label.

2. **The 3,412 total can't be squared with the chart. (Defect.)** The cells show tickets per hour in an average week. Add up just the lower end of each weekday bin and Monday alone comes to about 200 tickets (5+10+20+25+30+25+20+25+20+15+5), with Tuesday to Friday similar. That's at least about 900 a week from weekdays alone, so 3,600 or more over the four weeks the chart covers. Yet the sidebar says 3,412 for all of September. Either the legend bins, the "per hour, average week" description, or the total is wrong. These figures are by eye from the legend bins, so check them against the data. But the gap is far too big to be rounding. Fix: make the bins and the total describe the same measure, and if the cells are something other than hourly counts, say what in the subtitle and the legend title.

3. **The chart and the numbers cover different periods. (Defect.)** The subtitle says "1 to 28 September." The stat label says "New tickets, September," which reads as all 30 days. "Up 4% on August" then compares against a 31-day month. A reader can't tell if 3,412 means 28 or 30 days, or if the 4% is like for like. Fix: give every number the same stated period, or label each with its own ("1 to 28 September"). If the comparison isn't like for like, say what it is compared against.

4. **The title names the topic, not the finding. (Taste, and common sense for a report.)** "When new tickets arrive" tells me what the chart is about, but I still have to find the answer myself. The chart already makes one point plainly: on weekdays, tickets build from about 08:00, peak late morning and tail off after 17:00, and weekends are light. Saying that in the title or subtitle, in words the page already supports, turns the page from a picture into an answer. Do this only after findings 1 to 3 are fixed, so the words and the numbers agree.

**Smaller things.**
- The legend title "New tickets per hour" repeats the subtitle almost word for word. That's fine, but if the measure changes under finding 2, update both together.

**What's working.**
- **The right chart, plainly drawn.** A day-by-hour grid on one blue lightness ramp is the honest form for "when." Seven ordered steps run from near-white to navy and still read in grayscale. There's no gridline clutter, and thin white gaps separate the cells (Tufte's data-ink point). Keep the single hue and the ordered ramp. Don't add a second color.
- **Each number comes with a comparison.** Each stat has a short label, one big figure and a change line ("Up 4% on August," "Down 6 min on August"). That's what makes a headline figure useful (Few). Keep that structure while you fix the values.
- **The source line does real work.** "Hours are when a ticket was opened, not when it was answered" heads off the most likely misreading. Keep it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cells give exact counts on hover or focus. A staffing planner will want exact counts, not seven bins.
