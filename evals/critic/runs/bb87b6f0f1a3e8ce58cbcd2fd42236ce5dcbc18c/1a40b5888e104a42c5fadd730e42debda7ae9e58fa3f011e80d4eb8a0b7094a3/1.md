**First read.** My eye goes first to the row of four big numbers, then straight to the one purple bar (Monday) on the right, because it is the strongest color on the page. Next comes the purple November line rising above the gray October line, and then the table. It reads as a calm, well-made monthly report for someone who runs a podcast. It feels quiet and trustworthy, close to a bank statement.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the host or producer of *The Long Table* checks how November went against October and decides what to do next, such as when to release and which episodes to make more of.

**Verdict.** As a layout it works. The structure is clear, it's restrained, and every headline number has a comparison next to it. What gets in the way is that the two charts don't say what they found, and the one that looks most certain is misleading. The Monday bar is the boldest claim on the screen, and much of its lead comes from the calendar, not from listeners.

**What would move it most**
1. **The weekday chart overstates Monday and hides how the other days compare (defect).** This is "Downloads by weekday", top right. The bars look like monthly totals: they add up to about 48,000, close to the 48,920 in the Downloads card. November 2026 has five Mondays and five Sundays but only four of every other weekday, so Monday and Sunday each get about 25% more days to collect downloads. The purple bar and its "9,860" label invite the reader to conclude "release on Mondays", and that is the decision this chart feeds. Measured per day, Monday's lead over Tuesday is much smaller, and Sunday drops from a clear second place to behind Tuesday. The size of the effect drawn doesn't match the size in the data (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2, graphical integrity). The chart also has no value axis, so Monday is the only bar with a number. Fix: plot the average per weekday, or say plainly that these are totals. Also give the bars a scale or put a value on each one (Tufte; Few, *Show Me the Numbers*).
2. **Neither chart tells you what it found (taste, argued).** The titles "Daily downloads" and "Downloads by weekday" name topics. The reader has to work out the takeaways alone: November ran above October all month, and the gap grew toward the end. Monday is highlighted, but nothing says why. This is a monthly report someone checks for an answer, so a one-line finding in each title, or a short note on the chart, would turn the page from "look at this" into "here's what happened". Use only facts the data already shows.
3. **"Daily downloads" isn't daily (defect).** Each line has about 11 points, one roughly every three days. Someone who reads the title will take each peak and dip as a single day's result. Either plot all 30 days or rename the chart to say what one point is. The x-axis ticks (1, 10, 19, 30 Nov) are also uneven, 9, 9, then 11 days apart, which makes the spacing harder to read (Cairo; Tufte: even tick intervals).

**Smaller things.**
- Line chart legend: "November / October" sits top right, away from the lines. Put each label at the end of its line (Tufte; Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*).
- Purple means two different things: "November" in the line chart, and "the highlighted day" in the bar chart, right next to it. Give each meaning its own treatment so the eye doesn't link Monday to November.
- The page title "Listens" and the four KPI numbers look about the same size, so the title doesn't stand as its own level. Make the title clearly bigger or smaller. While you're at it, check whether the small labels (about 15, 14, and 12 px by eye) really need three different sizes.
- Names: the page is called "Listens", but every number on it is a download, follower, or finish rate. Use one word, or say how they relate. "Average listen 31 min" and "Finished an episode 62%" also leave out who or what they measure (per listener? per download?). Restore the missing noun so a stranger reads the figure the way you mean it.

**What's working.**
- Every KPI card puts last month's figure under this month's ("44,310 in October"), so each number comes with its comparison. Keep that pattern.
- The color is disciplined: neutral grays everywhere, with one accent used only for highlighted data. That restraint is why the eye knows where to go. It just needs to be pointed at true things.
- The table is well set: numbers right-aligned in tabular figures, light rules, and headings aligned with their columns. It's easy to scan down the Downloads and Finished columns.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a control for changing the period. It looks like static text, and if it is a control it needs to look like one.
- Whether "Top episodes" can show more than three rows.
