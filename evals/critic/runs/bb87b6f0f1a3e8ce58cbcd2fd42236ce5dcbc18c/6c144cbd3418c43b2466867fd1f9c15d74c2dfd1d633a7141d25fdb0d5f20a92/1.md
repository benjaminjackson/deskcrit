**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Crossing the river" in a big brush script, then across to the second script heading, "Good to know", then down to the blue button. It reads as a friendly local ferry's passenger guide. The mood is split. The script feels like a café chalkboard or a party invite, and the rest of the page is plain, sober serif and sans text.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an information page that tells passengers on one ferry route when boats run and how to bring a bike, and sends them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** The page is clear and easy to scan, but it doesn't look like one thing. It uses three unrelated typefaces, and paragraph text is set two different ways. That makes it look assembled rather than designed. The copy also avoids the one fact passengers came for: the times.

**What would move it most**
1. **Three typefaces with no plan, and two different looks for paragraph text** (defect and convention). There's a script for the two big headings, a serif for the subtitle, the section headings and the sidebar paragraph, and a sans for the main paragraphs and the nav. Dair says faces should either match closely or contrast plainly. Here the script contrasts with everything, while the serif and sans compete for the same job. The left column's paragraphs are sans, but the "Good to know" paragraph is serif and slightly larger. A reader sees two kinds of paragraph and wonders whether the sidebar is a different kind of content. Gerstner's point is that things of one kind should look alike. On mood, the script's party tone fights a page about timetables and the last boat before dusk (my taste, not a rule). Fix: give all paragraphs one face, and either drop the script or keep it only for the page title as a deliberate one-off. Don't use it for a sidebar label.
2. **The copy avoids the times** (defect). "Between early morning and late evening", "thins to one boat each hour" and "the last crossing leaves before dusk" are vague exactly where people will act. A passenger can't plan from "before dusk", and each person will guess a different hour. The page then sends them to printed timetables "in the shelter" or to a button. Fix: name the first and last departure times and the winter dates here, or say plainly that the timetable has them. Only you have those facts.
3. **Too many type sizes, with neighbors that collide** (convention). I count about eight sizes: H1 ~64, sidebar heading ~44, section headings ~28, subtitle ~24, logo ~22, sidebar paragraph ~20, body ~17, nav ~16. The subtitle and section headings are only a few pixels apart, and so are the two paragraph sizes, so they read as accidents rather than levels (Bringhurst 3.1.1). Fix: keep four levels (title, section heading, body, small) and let weight and color handle the rest. Also give the subtitle a size clearly between the title and the headings.
4. **"Good to know" doesn't say what the box holds** (convention, WCAG 2.4.6). The heading is the most visible thing on the right but describes nothing. The box is really about where to find printed timetables and notices of changes. A heading that names that topic would also let a quick scanner find it.

**Smaller things.**
- The card's right edge (~1150 px) stops about 34 px short of the nav's right edge (~1184 px), and its top (~147 px) sits just below the title's top (~130 px). Both look like near misses: line them up or offset them clearly.
- The card paragraph runs only about 25–30 characters a line with loose line spacing. It breaks into short pieces and ends on a lone "week." Widen the card or shrink the text to match the body, and tighten the line spacing for the short measure.

**What's working.**
- The left column is well chunked: each section heading sits close to its own paragraph with clear space before the next. A scanner can find "bicycles" in a second.
- One blue, used only on the single real action. The button is unmistakably the next step, and nothing competes with it.
- Main body lines run around 65–70 characters, and the content shares a clear left edge with the logo at 96 px.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the button and the nav links, and how the sidebar reflows on a phone (whether "Good to know" lands above or below the guide).
