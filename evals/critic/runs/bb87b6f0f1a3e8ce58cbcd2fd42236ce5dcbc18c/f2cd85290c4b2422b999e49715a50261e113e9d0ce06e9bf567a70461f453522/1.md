**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Know which rack every boat is on", then jumps right to the one navy tile, M4, and drops to its caption, "Rack M4, single scull · $95 paid". Within two seconds it reads as software for a rowing club's boathouse, aimed at the person who runs it. The feel is calm, plain and believable, like a club notice board done well, not a startup pitch.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a marketing homepage. It has to convince a rowing-club captain or committee member that Slipway will replace how they track boats on racks and collect rack fees, and get them to start mapping their own boathouse.

**Verdict.** It's a strong, specific page that mostly does its job. The one thing really holding it back is that the product picture doesn't show what the headline promises: you're told you'll know which boat is on every rack, but the plan only shows which racks are full.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plan doesn't show what the headline promises.** The headline says "which rack every boat is on". The panel on the right shows 18 rack codes (U1–L6), each either sage (taken) or white with a dashed border (free), and no boat names or owners. Only the selected M4 gets any detail, and even that is a boat type ("single scull"), not a boat. The first two features have the same gap. "Club boats and private boats" isn't visible on the plan: every taken rack is the same sage. "A plan for the boathouse wall… with each boat name on its rack" describes a picture the page doesn't show. So the viewer takes the main claim on trust when the mockup could prove it at a glance, and the captain's real question ("whose boat is on L3?") goes unanswered. The fix: make the plan show its contents. Put a boat or owner name on the taken racks, or at least on several, and separate club racks from private ones by something more than color. Keep the rack codes as small secondary labels. Use the maker's real or sample data; don't invent it. *Defect* (the picture undercuts the claim). Pairing a visual with its headline is my judgment here, not a cited rule.

2. **The ask doesn't compare against what the club uses now.** "Map your boathouse" is a good, exact ask, and "See a sample boathouse" lets the viewer judge for themselves. Neither sets Slipway next to what a captain already has, whether that's the paper list on the boathouse wall, a spreadsheet, or chasing fees one by one. "A plan for the boathouse wall" comes close. A comparison against that current tool, in the maker's own terms, would show the value on ground the viewer already trusts. Name the current tool somewhere in the hero (the subhead is the natural place) rather than only listing what Slipway does. *Taste*, argued from how the ask lands.

**Smaller things.**
- The hero paragraph ends with "glance." alone on the last line. Rebreak it, or trim a word, so the last line carries at least two words (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- The free racks U3, M6 and L5 have dashed borders that look faint, and their white fill is close to the off-white panel. These are the racks a member would claim, so their edge should be a meaningful boundary. Measure it and darken it to at least 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11).

**What's working.**
- **The "Doors to the slipway" band across the plan.** It turns a grid of boxes into a real place the viewer recognizes, and it shows Slipway thinks the way a boathouse is laid out. Keep that kind of physical landmark.
- **One navy carries both the selected rack (M4) and the primary button.** The accent means "the thing you act on" everywhere. Below that, the scale is tight and clear: a big headline, one body size, a medium-weight feature heading, and a small gray detail size. Nothing competes with the headline.
- **The copy is specific and concrete.** "Members claim a free rack themselves, and the captain sees every boat at a glance" says who does what. "Held until 31 March 2027" and "$95 paid" are exact. The feature headings name what the user does, not abstract categories. None of it could sit on anyone else's page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the rack tiles respond to hover or tap in the live sample, and the focus states on the two hero actions.
