**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold sans headline, which is the right place to start, and then drops into three justified serif columns. Before you read a word, the columns look moth-eaten: there are white holes between words all down each column, and a crooked white channel runs through the first. It reads as a short article or info page about a local ferry. It's calm and bookish in intent, but the text looks broken.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web article or info page telling riders how the river ferry runs, read on a desktop screen.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but the text setting fails. Justified columns this narrow, with no hyphenation, can't keep even word spacing, so the body copy (almost the whole page) is full of gaps that slow reading and look careless. A smaller problem: the headline promises "the seasons," and the copy covers only winter.

**What would move it most**
1. **Justified text on a very narrow measure leaves holes and rivers in every column (defect).** Each column holds only about 22 to 32 characters per line, against Bringhurst's 40 to 50 for text in several columns. Hochuli says that under about 50 characters, even word spacing in justified text "becomes difficult, if not impossible." That is what happens here:
   - In column 1, "minutes between early" and "weather, and a little" are stretched to the right edge, and the word spaces stacked down lines 6 to 12 join into a crooked white channel (a river).
   - In column 2, "Riders wheel them on" and "bicycle during the" have gaps wider than a word.
   - Column 3 opens with "In winter the timetable", which is mostly air.

   The reader's eye trips at each hole, and the even gray that justification is supposed to give turns into a blotchy texture. The fix: set the text flush left (ragged right), which costs nothing on a web page. Or, if you keep justification, turn on hyphenation and widen the measure toward 45 characters or more. The space is there: the block stops about 270 px short of the right side.

2. **The headline promises more than the copy delivers (defect, in the copy).** "The river ferry through the seasons" tells the reader to expect how the service changes over the year. Column 1 is the everyday timetable, column 2 is bicycle rules, and only column 3 mentions a season (winter). A reader looking for summer hours or holiday changes won't find them and will wonder what's missing. Either the headline names what the page actually covers (how the ferry runs: timetable, bicycles, winter changes), or the page needs the other seasons' information, which only you have.

**Smaller things.**
- **Balance (taste):** The three columns end at about x 1012, leaving a ~270 px empty band on the right against a 120 px left margin. It looks like a fourth column that never arrived. Widening the columns to use that space also fixes the measure in finding 1. If the band is meant to stay empty, give it a job, such as a timetable or landing details.
- **Unlabeled columns (common sense):** The three columns are three topics (crossings, bicycles, winter and notices) with nothing to signal that. Short subheads would let a rider jump straight to, say, the bicycle rules.

**What's working.**
- **Headline/body contrast:** The heavy sans headline against the bookish serif body is a clear contrast of form, weight, and size. There are only two type levels, so there's no doubt about where to start, and the pairing doesn't clash.
- **The grid:** Three equal columns, equal gutters, a shared top edge, and baselines that line up across all three columns. Keep this when you change the setting.
- **Value and color:** Dark gray text on white, nothing competing, comfortable leading for the body size.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the three columns stack into one on narrow screens, and how the measure behaves at other widths. On a phone, a single justified column would have the same holes.
