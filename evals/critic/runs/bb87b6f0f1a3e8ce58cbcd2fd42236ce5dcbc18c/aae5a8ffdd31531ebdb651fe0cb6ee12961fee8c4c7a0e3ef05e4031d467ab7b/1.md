**First read.** The eye lands on "Changing your payment card", drops to the gray intro, then runs down the three bold subheads: Add, Remove, If a payment fails. It reads at once as a help-center article for a paying customer. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, with nothing selling. Below the last paragraph and to the right of the column, the page is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a support article for an existing customer who needs to swap cards or who has just had a payment fail, read on desktop, mostly by scanning for their own case.

**Verdict.** This is a sound, quiet article that mostly does its job. The structure is clear and the type is well set. What holds it back is the copy in the section with real stakes, "If a payment fails", which leaves the anxious reader to guess what happens to them and what to do. Next are subheads that float between sections, and a path the reader has to go and find alone.

**What would move it most**

1. **The payment-failure and removal copy leaves gaps exactly where the reader will act.** This is a defect.
   - "We try again after three days" never says what is tried. The reader has to work out that it means charging the card again.
   - "After a third failed try the account moves to read-only" is missing its comma after "try", so it stumbles on first reading. It also stops at the system's state. It never says what read-only means for the reader: what they can no longer do. The fix is to finish the sentence in their terms, with "you".
   - "until a payment goes through" does not say what gets a payment through. Do they add a new card? Wait for the next retry? Someone with a failing card needs that next step most. Name the action from the page's own "Add a new card" section rather than leaving it implied.
   - In "Remove an old card", "once another card is set as the default" assumes the reader knows how to set a default, and the page never tells them. They hit a dead end in the middle of the task. Say where that is done; only you know the step.
   - All of this is editorial: restore the missing subject and verb, and land each claim on the reader. No new facts are needed beyond the steps you already know.
2. **The subheads float halfway between sections.** This is a convention.
   - "Add a new card", "Remove an old card" and "If a payment fails" each sit about as far below the paragraph before them as above their own text. Both gaps look roughly 30 px.
   - The heading doesn't visibly belong to what follows it, so a reader scanning for their case gets weaker chunks, and that scanning is the main way people use a help page.
   - Proximity is how a viewer reads groups (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on Wertheimer's rule). Bringhurst (2.2.2) sets subheads with more space above than below.
   - Fix: add space above each subhead and take it from below, so each one clearly sits on top of its own text.
3. **The path to the setting is written out but not linked.** This is taste, or common sense.
   - "Open Settings, choose Billing, and select Add card" makes the reader leave the article and retrace the path from memory.
   - Every reader here wants to go and do this, so a direct way into the billing screen is the next step to put in front of them.
   - Fix: make that path, or the words "Add card", go straight to the billing screen. If it has to stay as text, set the menu names apart, for example in bold, so they stand out from the sentence.

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: the paragraphs look like about 78–85 characters per line, past Bringhurst's 45–75. Narrow the column by roughly a tenth.
- A lone word, "days.", sits on the last line of the hold paragraph under "Add a new card" (Bringhurst 2.4.2). This will mostly go away once the column is narrower; otherwise rewrap or tighten the line.

**What's working.**
- **The intro paragraph** answers the two worries people bring to this page: whether they can change the card now, and what happens to money already paid. It does this before the reader has to scan anything. Keep it.
- **The type scale is clean.** There is a bold title, bold subheads at one clear step down, one body size and a gray breadcrumb. Each level has an obvious job, and weight carries the difference along with size.
- **The headings are written as the reader's own cases** ("Remove an old card", "If a payment fails") rather than as category labels, so a scanning reader can find their situation by its name.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't check whether the breadcrumb links show focus and hover states, or how the column reflows on a phone.
