**First read.** The eye goes straight to the white headline, "See where every dollar goes this month", then down to the subhead and the white "Try it free" button. It reads as a landing page for a personal budgeting app. It feels calm and trustworthy, but plain. Then the eye drifts right into the empty half of the navy band, looking for a picture of the product that isn't there.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the home page of a consumer budgeting app, and its job is to get people managing their own or a household's money to start a free trial.

**Verdict.** The words and the type are solid and the page reads in seconds, but the viewer never sees the product. The empty right half of the hero is the biggest problem: it leaves the page unbalanced and asks people to link their bank accounts to an app they've only been told about.

**What would move it most**
1. **The right half of the hero is empty.** About 40% of the navy band, to the right of the headline and buttons, holds nothing. All the weight sits on the left, so the band tips (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). The empty area also isn't shaped space doing a job; it's just left over (Tschichold; Müller-Brockmann). Worse, this is the one spot where the viewer would expect proof. The headline promises "see where every dollar goes", and nothing shows what "seeing" looks like. A blank hero visual is also a stock template move (unslop, "Empty box or generic picture as the hero visual"). It makes the page look like an unfinished template, not this product. Fix: put a real view of the product there, such as the category limits and the heads-up warning the subhead describes, so the picture finishes the headline's promise instead of repeating it. If an image is supposed to load there, check a live browser; if it is, this finding is just about load reliability. *Defect.*
2. **"Try it free" leaves the main question open.** For a finance app, the viewer's first questions are free for how long, whether a card is needed, and what happens when it ends. The button text and the area around it answer none of these. In this spot the viewer reads the ask as a commitment: someone who reads "free forever" and later gets charged will remember it. Fix: say the term of the offer next to the button, using the facts only you have. Neither the button nor the page sets the app against how people track money today, such as their bank's app or a spreadsheet. That kind of comparison would let the viewer judge the value on their own terms; supply it if you have one. *Defect* (unclear terms on a commitment).
3. **The trust point is the third item in a quiet row.** "Read-only access … can never move money" answers the biggest objection to "Connect your accounts" in the subhead. Right now it's the last of three equal columns below the fold line of the hero. Fix: move that reassurance up so it sits near the subhead or the button, where the worry comes up. *Taste*, argued on how this audience thinks: nobody tries a money app until they believe it's safe.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline looks to be set at an extra-bold weight (about 800). That's a common template move (unslop, "Headings at weight 800 or 900"). At this size, a regular or semibold weight would still lead the page and look less stock.

**What's working.**
- The headline is specific and spoken to the reader ("every dollar", "this month"). The subhead lists three concrete steps and ends on what the viewer gets: "a heads-up before you go over." Keep this copy as it is.
- The type scale is clear: headline, subhead, feature headings, and body are each a plain step apart, all on one left edge at 64 px that runs from the logo through the feature columns.
- Exactly one primary action: the filled white button clearly outranks the outlined "Watch the tour".

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The bottom 220 px or so under the feature row is blank white. That could be the end of the viewport, or a section that hadn't loaded yet. Check in a live browser.
- Hit areas of the nav links (Features, Pricing, Security, Sign in) can't be measured from the image.
