I ran the full checklist on this page. The layout and type hold up; the biggest problem is that the article never tells the reader how to change the card.

**First read.** The eye goes straight to the large black headline, "Update the card that pays for your subscription." Then it drops into an even, calm block of body text with a few blue links. It reads at once as a help-center article for a billing product: quiet, trustworthy, and well spaced. Nothing competes with the headline. After a few seconds you notice that the article is all about what happens around the task, and it never says how to do the task.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help-center article for a customer or account admin who has come to replace the card on their subscription, and who mainly wants to get it done and know what happens next.

**Verdict.** The typography and layout are clean and do their job. The article does not. The headline promises a how-to, and the body gives the rules around the task but no steps and no link to where it's done. That gap, plus one unclear deadline in the "declined" section, is what's in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The article never shows how to update the card.** The headline is an instruction, and the first sentence says you can replace the card "from the billing page." After that, the page covers invoices, roles, bank transfer and declines. It never gives the steps to get there, and "the billing page" is the one destination in the article that isn't a link. "check your billing history," "roles and permissions" and "paying by invoice" all are. A reader who came to do the task has to leave and hunt through the product for a page the article only names. That is exactly what Nielsen's "recognition rather than recall" warns against: making the user carry information from one place to another. It's also plain common sense for a how-to. *Defect.* Fix direction: put what the reader came for first. Make "the billing page" a link, and add the short sequence of steps (or the menu path) before the paragraphs about open invoices and roles. Those details are useful, but they're secondary.

2. **The downgrade deadline can be read two ways.** In "When the new card is declined": "We try a declined card again after three days and after seven days … If the card is still declined after the second attempt, the subscription moves to the free plan." Is the "second attempt" the day-three retry (the original charge being the first attempt) or the day-seven retry (the second *retry*)? It's also unclear what the three and seven days count from: the first decline, or the invoice date. This is the one date on the page a customer will act on and hold the company to. If someone reads it as day seven when it's day three, their team loses paid features earlier than they planned. *Defect.* Fix direction: an editorial edit, not new content. Name the retry by its day instead of its count (e.g. "If the card is still declined after the seven-day retry…") and say what the days count from. Only the maker knows which reading is true.

**Smaller things**
- Sidebar and top nav don't mark where you are. "Update a payment card" in "In this section" and "Billing" in the header look exactly like the items around them. Mark the current item with one cue, such as bolder weight, a side bar or a background (Nielsen 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Inline links are marked by color alone: blue, no underline. Readers with weaker color vision may miss them. Underline them by default (WCAG technique G183/F73).
- Body lines look about 80 to 85 characters long. For example, "You can replace the card on file at any time from the billing page. The new card is" is about 83. That's just past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. Narrowing the text column by about 10% would bring it into range, and the right side has plenty of room to spare.
- There's no search anywhere on a help site. People arrive here with a problem in their own words, and a search field in the header is the standard way in (Nielsen 7).
- The sidebar label "Update a payment card" and the headline "Update the card that pays for your subscription" name the same page differently. That's minor, but matching them helps people confirm they landed in the right place. (Common sense.)

**What's working**
- **Clean hierarchy.** There are four type levels, each clearly different: the large bold headline, the bold section head, the body text, and the small gray breadcrumb and sidebar. Keep this scale; it's why the page reads calmly at a glance.
- **Headings say what follows.** "When the new card is declined" names a situation the reader may be in, not a category like "Troubleshooting." It sits clearly closer to its own paragraph than to the one above.
- **The copy talks to the reader.** Lines like "Your workspace stays open during this period" say what a fact means for the person reading. Keep that voice when you add the steps.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the links show a visible focus ring and a hover underline for keyboard users.
