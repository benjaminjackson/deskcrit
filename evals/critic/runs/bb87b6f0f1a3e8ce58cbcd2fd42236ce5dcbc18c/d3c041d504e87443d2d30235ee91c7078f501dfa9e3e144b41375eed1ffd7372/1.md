**First read.** The eye lands on "Send invoices in minutes, not hours," drops to the blue "Start free trial" button, then moves down to the three gray cards. It reads in about two seconds as a web app for invoicing, sold to small creative studios. It feels calm, tidy and competent, but also anonymous: nothing on the screen looks like this product rather than any other SaaS landing page, and the top-right quarter of the hero is blank.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the desktop homepage hero of an invoicing tool. Its job is to get owners of small design and photo studios to start a trial or book a demo.

**Verdict.** It's clean and readable, and the hierarchy works. It doesn't sell, though: it asks a visual audience to trust that the invoices are "clear" without ever showing one, and the section below the fold line just repeats the hero paragraph. The biggest problem is that the product is missing from its own homepage.

**What would move it most**

1. **The product never appears, and the hero's right half is empty.** To the right of the paragraph and buttons, from about x=720 to the right edge and from the headline down to the buttons, there is nothing. All the weight sits in the left column, so the hero looks lopsided and unfinished, as if an image failed to load (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance": a heavy side needs a counterweight). That empty area is also where the page could make its case. The audience is designers and photographers, people who judge by looking. The paragraph promises "clear invoices" and "see what each client owes," but the viewer has to take both on trust. Put a real view of the product there, such as a sent invoice or the "who owes what" page, so viewers can compare it with the invoices they send now and judge "clear" for themselves. If the space stays empty on purpose, then narrow the measure or center the block so the emptiness looks chosen. Defect (balance), plus taste on the missing preview.

2. **The feature cards say the same things a second time, and in places a third.** The hero paragraph already makes three claims: send clear invoices, follow up on late payments, see what each client owes. The three cards (y≈570–745) repeat those same three claims in the same order. Inside each card, the body then rewords its own title: "Chase late payments politely" / "Send a polite reminder when a payment is late." Someone who read the paragraph learns almost nothing new below it, and the one block that should build the case (titled "Why studios use Quillfern") just restates it. This point is common sense, not a cited rule. Fix: either cut the hero paragraph down to the setup and accountant points the cards don't cover, or have each card body carry the one detail its title leaves out. The "on a schedule you choose" and "link or a PDF" details are already there; lead with them instead of the restated title. Defect (wasted space and reading).

3. **The headline and the call to action could belong to anyone.** "Send invoices in minutes, not hours" is the time-saving line every invoicing tool uses. It doesn't mention studios, the audience the eyebrow just named. "Start free trial" doesn't say how long the trial lasts or what it costs to start, and those are the terms a viewer will later hold the company to. The page also never compares itself with what the studio uses today. Direction: tie the headline to the studio's own situation, using a fact only the maker has (for example, what they invoice for or what they use now). Next to the button, state the trial terms in plain words. Don't add new claims. Defect (copy).

**Smaller things**
- The eyebrow "FOR SMALL DESIGN AND PHOTO STUDIOS" is small, uppercase and letterspaced above the headline. That's a stock landing-page move, and it makes the hero look like a template (template tell, unslop). Set it in sentence case at normal tracking, or put "for small studios" into the headline itself.

**What's working**
- The hierarchy is clear: the headline is the one dominant element, and there is exactly one filled primary button with "Book a demo" visibly lower as an underlined text link. The viewer knows what to do next.
- The body copy is plain and specific in a good way. "Nothing to install" and "your accountant can see every invoice from one shared link" are concrete benefits that answer real objections. Keep these.
- It's calm and well built: one blue accent on neutrals, an even three-column grid with equal 24 px gutters, a comfortable paragraph measure (about 60 characters), and "Home" bolded to show the current page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- The hover and focus states of the button, nav links and "Book a demo," and how the three cards reflow at narrow widths.
