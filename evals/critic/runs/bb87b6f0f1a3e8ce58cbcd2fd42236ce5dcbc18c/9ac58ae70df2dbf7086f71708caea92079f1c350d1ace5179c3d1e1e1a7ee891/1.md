Nothing below the feature lists tells it where to go next, and the only colored thing on the page is "Start free", up in the corner of the header. It reads as a clean, calm, generic SaaS pricing page for some kind of scheduling or calendar tool. It looks finished but not convincing.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a pricing page for a scheduling product, and its job is to get a visitor to pick a plan and sign up for it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do that job yet. The page shows prices but gives no way to act on them, and no way to compare them. There's no button on any plan, and the three feature lists don't line up, so the visitor can't tell which plan they need or how to get it.

**What would move it most**

1. **No plan has a way to choose it.** None of the three cards ends in a button, so the eye path runs out at "Email reminders / Reports / Priority support" and stops at the empty lower third. The only action is "Start free" in the header, far from the prices and tied to no plan. A visitor who has just settled on Team has to scroll back up and click something that may not start Team at all. Controls belong next to the thing they act on (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, natural mapping). A button on each plan is also the pattern visitors expect from every other pricing page they've used (Nielsen, Jakob's law). Direction: give each card its own action at the bottom, in the same place on all three. **Defect.**

2. **The plans can't be compared, and the Team price can be read two ways.** Each card lists three different features: Solo has "Booking page" and "Email reminders", Team has "Shared availability" and "Reports". The visitor can't tell whether Team includes the booking page and reminders, or whether Business includes reports. They have to guess, or hold one card in their head while reading the next (Nielsen, recognition rather than recall). On top of that, "$29 / month" for a plan called Team doesn't say whether that's per person or for the whole team of up to 10. A buyer will remember a price and hold you to it, so this is a defect, not a style note. If they read it as a flat team price and the bill comes per seat, you've lost their trust at checkout. Direction: make the step-up between plans explicit (each plan says what it adds to the one before it, or one shared feature list), and state the billing unit next to each price. Only you know which unit is true. **Defect.**

3. **"Start free" collides with three paid plans.** The page names no free plan and no trial, yet the only button says "Start free". A visitor could read it as "there's a free tier" (where is it?) or "there's a free trial" (how long, of which plan, card required?). Either way the page doesn't answer it. The subtitle, "Simple plans for teams of every size. Change or cancel whenever you like.", could sit on any SaaS pricing page. Its second sentence is the only reassurance about commitment, and it's the vaguest line on the page. Direction: if there's a trial, the page needs to say what it is and attach it to the plans, and the button text should name what actually happens. The trial's length and terms are facts only you have. **Defect.**

4. **No plan stands out, so the page doesn't help anyone decide.** The three cards are identical in size, border, weight, and color, so the eye gives all three the same attention and the visitor does all the choosing alone (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance by contrast). If one plan is the one most visitors should pick, make it clearly the first thing seen, for example with a stronger border, a fill, or its own label, and set the other two back. **Convention.**

- Subtitle: "you like." sits alone on the second line, a runt (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Rebalance the break or narrow the block so the two lines are closer in length.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is disciplined: page title, plan name, price, and feature text are four clearly separate levels in one family, with weight doing the work between them. Keep this when you add buttons and a recommended plan, and don't add new in-between sizes.
- The prices are set big and dark, with "/ month" small and gray beside them. The number reads instantly, which is right, as long as the unit gets fixed as in finding 2.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav and the button, and how the three cards stack on a phone (whether the recommended plan, once there is one, still comes first).
