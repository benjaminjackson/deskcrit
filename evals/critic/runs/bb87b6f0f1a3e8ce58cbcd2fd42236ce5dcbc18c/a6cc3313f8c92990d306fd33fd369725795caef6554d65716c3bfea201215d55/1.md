**First read.** The eye goes to "Overview" and then runs along the four big numbers in the top row, which are all the same size, so none of them leads. Then it drops to the solid blue Saturday bar on the right, the only strong color on the screen. It reads as a calm, tidy sales dashboard for a small farm shop. It's well made but has nothing to say: it shows the month without telling you how the month went.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a monthly overview for the farm shop's owner or manager, who wants to know at a glance how November went and where to look.

**Verdict.** As a piece of craft it's competent, but it leaves the point of the screen to the reader. Revenue and orders both fell from October while visitor numbers rose, and the owner has to work that out by subtracting and comparing two charts. Nothing on the screen says it, and the loudest mark is the one fact that probably isn't news.

**What would move it most**
1. **The screen shows the figures but not what changed.** Each KPI card puts October's figure under November's ("$34,105 in October" under "$31,270"), so the owner has to subtract to learn that revenue fell about 8% and orders about 7% (2,390 to 2,214). Nothing shows direction: no change figure, no up or down mark, no color. At the same time, the "Shop visitors" chart has November above October across the whole month. More visitors and fewer orders is the most useful thing on this screen, and it's split between a card and a chart that nothing connects. Defect: the owner can glance at this, see nothing wrong, and miss a falling conversion rate. Direction: show the change and its direction next to each KPI, so a fall reads as a fall. Then let the visitor chart, or a line near it, say in words what it shows. The chart titles are topics ("Shop visitors", "Orders by weekday"). Titles that state the finding are common sense here, not a cited rule. The maker has to supply the actual finding.

2. **Nothing leads, and the one thing that does is the wrong one.** "Overview" is set at about the same size as the four KPI figures, so there are five equal claims at the top and no first stop. The only saturated mark on the page is the blue Saturday bar, so that's where attention goes. But "Saturday is busiest" is the fact a farm-shop owner already knows. The same blue also means "November" in the visitor chart next to it, so one color carries two unrelated meanings side by side. Convention (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance by differences of size). The color point is taste. Direction: make the page title smaller than the KPI figures, so the numbers carry the top. Save the accent for what needs attention this month, such as a metric that moved, instead of a weekday that's always busy.

3. **Several numbers can be read two ways.** These are the figures the owner will act on, so ambiguity here costs something. Defect (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, and Few: the reader should never have to guess what a number measures).
   - **"148"** on the Saturday bar has no unit. It could be the average per Saturday or the month's Saturday total. The other six bars have no labels and no y-axis, so they can only be compared by eye, never read.
   - **The "Shop visitors" chart** has no unit on the y-axis, and its roughly 13 points across 30 days don't say what each one covers: a day, or a few days.
   - **"Farm box subscribers 18%"** labels a count but shows a percentage. It could be 18% of customers or 18% of orders.
   - **"Best-selling produce"** is sorted by revenue, so apple cider, with the most units sold (231), comes last. "Best-selling" usually means units, so a reader can take the ranking backwards.

   Direction: give every figure its unit and its base ("per Saturday", "visitors per day", "of customers"). Then either sort the table by units sold or retitle it to match the revenue sort.

**Smaller things.**
- Sidebar: the nav labels start at different x positions (about 60, 66, 62, 68 and 64 px), and the icons drift with them. Put all the icons on one column and all the labels on one left edge.
- The Settings icon looks like a sun or crosshair rather than a gear, and Produce uses a document icon. Use the gear people expect, and an icon that suggests produce.
- In the chart row, the gap between the two cards falls under neither KPI divider (around x 891 to 908, against dividers at 757 and 1002). Line the split up with the KPI columns, or move it clearly away from them.
- The visitor chart uses a legend at the top right. Label the two lines at their ends instead.
- The light gray weekday bars and the gray October line look under the 3:1 contrast that chart marks need (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure them and darken them if so.

**What's working.**
- **The palette is restrained.** It's all gray with one blue, and there are no template effects (gradients, glows and so on), so once the accent is pointed at the right thing it will land hard.
- **The chart basics are right.** Bars stand on a baseline in natural Mon to Sun order, and the line chart starts at zero with light gridlines and nothing extra.
- **Every KPI carries last month's figure, and everything fits on one screen.** The comparison data is already there; it only needs to be stated as a change. The table's number columns are right-aligned and line up cleanly.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" is a control that changes the period. It looks like plain text. If it can be clicked, it needs to look clickable.
- Focus states in the sidebar.
