**First read.** My eye lands first on "Before you board", the only bold, large thing on the page. Then it drops into three paragraphs set entirely in capitals. They read as a wall: every line has the same rectangular outline, the reading slows right down, and the page sounds like it's shouting a notice instead of informing a passenger. It looks like a plain, calm passenger-information page for a small river ferry, and the capitals pull against that calm.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a short web page that tells ferry passengers how the service runs (frequency, bicycles, winter changes) before they travel.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound: one clear heading, short paragraphs, a sensible measure. But it is badly let down by setting all the body copy in capitals, and by copy that leaves out the times a passenger actually needs. Fix the case first; it's the one change that most alters how the page reads.

**What would move it most**
1. **All three body paragraphs are set in capitals.** This affects every line from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING." Capitals take away word shapes, so the reader has to decode letter by letter. Over eight lines of running text that is slow and tiring. It also reads as raised-voice signage, which sits oddly under a friendly heading like "Before you board". The rule this breaks is Butterick, *Practical Typography*, "All caps": capitals suit headings shorter than one line, never whole paragraphs. Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, p. 23, notes text in capitals is particularly hard to read. Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for the short label at most. *Defect.*
2. **The page gives a frequency but no times, so a passenger can't plan from it.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat leaves. Without a starting time, "every forty minutes" doesn't tell you when to arrive. "In winter" doesn't say which months or dates, and "one boat each hour" doesn't say at what minute past. The last sentence then sends the reader to a printed timetable at the landing, which is the place they were trying to plan their way to. These are the facts a passenger acts on and holds the operator to, so leaving them vague is a defect, not a style note. Fix: give the first and last departure, the departure minutes, and the dates the winter timetable starts and ends. If the timetable lives elsewhere online, link it here. Only you have these facts. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.**
- I counted two lines that could be clearer, though neither is wrong. "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" works. "Each crossing takes about twelve minutes in calm weather" leaves open what happens in rough weather. If the answer matters to passengers (longer crossings, cancellations), say so in a clause. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- **The hierarchy.** It is unmistakable: one bold heading, a much smaller muted label above it, and body text clearly below both. That's three type sizes with big steps between them, so each level has an obvious job. Keep this scale when you change the body to mixed case.
- **The paragraphs.** Each one holds a single topic (the route and timing, bicycles, winter) with even space between them, so a reader looking for "can I bring my bike?" finds it at a glance.
- **The measure and line spacing.** Lines run roughly 65 to 70 characters with comfortable leading. That's within the range Bringhurst gives, so the body will read well once it's in mixed case.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The block sits in the left two-thirds, leaving a large empty area at the right and bottom. That's fine at this width. Check how it reflows on a phone.
