**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board," then runs into three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, which read as a solid gray block. It looks like a ferry's passenger-information page. The heading and the small label feel calm and orderly. The body text undoes that and makes the page feel like a posted warning notice. Nothing sits below the text, and the lower half of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that gives ferry passengers the practical facts they need before they travel: when the boats run, how bikes work, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but the body text, which carries all the content, is set in all capitals, so it's the hardest part of the page to read. That one decision is the main thing in the way. After it, the copy leaves out the times a passenger actually needs to plan a trip.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in all capitals.** All three paragraphs, from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" down to "…AT EACH LANDING.", are two to three lines each in capitals. Capitals remove the word shapes that readers use to read quickly. Every word becomes a rectangle of the same height, so the reader has to decode letter by letter. On a page whose whole job is to be read, the content is now the slowest part. It also clashes with the tone of the heading: a friendly "Before you board" is followed by text that looks like shouting. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says not to capitalize whole paragraphs and to keep capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels, and captions. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) notes that text in capitals is especially hard to read. **Fix:** set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case, and keep capitals only for the short "PASSENGER NOTES" label. In mixed case the same column will hold more characters per line. The capital lines now run about 70 characters, so check that the mixed-case lines stay within about 45 to 75 characters (Bringhurst). If they run longer, narrow the column. *Defect.*
2. **The copy gives a feel for the schedule but not the times.** "Between early morning and late evening" and "every forty minutes" don't tell a passenger when the first and last boats leave or which minutes past the hour to aim for. "In winter" doesn't say which months. These are exactly the facts a passenger acts on and later holds the operator to. "Late evening" can mean 8 pm to one reader and 11 pm to another, and the reader who guesses late gets stranded at the quay. The page then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing," which they only reach once they're already standing at the landing. **Fix:** give the actual first and last departure times, the winter date range, and where to find the timetable before leaving home. Only the operator has those facts. *Defect.*

**What's working.**
- **The heading stands clearly apart from the label and body.** "Before you board" is large, bold, and dark. The label above it is small, gray, and spaced out. The jump in size, weight, and color between the three levels is big enough that you know where to start without thinking. Keep that scale when you reset the body text.
- **The page is quiet and well organized.** It uses one left edge for the label, heading, and paragraphs, has no decoration, and puts clear space between paragraphs. Each paragraph covers one topic (schedule, bikes, winter), so a passenger can scan to the one they need once the text is in mixed case.
- **The bike paragraph tells riders what to do.** "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail" says who does what and when. That's the standard the schedule paragraph should meet.
