**First read.** My eye goes straight to "Before you board" and then hits three paragraphs set entirely in capitals. They read as one gray block, and the tone shifts from a calm heading to something like a posted regulation. I can tell within a second that this is practical information for ferry passengers, but the body makes me work to read it, and it sounds like it's shouting rules at me rather than helping.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells first-time river-ferry passengers what they need to know before they travel, read on a laptop or phone just before the trip.

**Verdict.** The structure is clean and sound, but the piece fails at its main job, which is easy reading. The body is all capitals, and the copy leaves out the facts a passenger needs to plan a trip. Fix those two things and this is a good page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in all capitals.** All three paragraphs (lines 187–416 px down the page) are full caps. Every word is a rectangle of the same height, so the eye loses the word shapes it uses to read quickly. The passenger slows down, rereads, and the page feels like a warning notice instead of help. It also works against your own hierarchy: the small capitals label "PASSENGER NOTES" is meant to stand out by being in caps, but the body is in caps too. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") keeps capitals for headings shorter than one line and says not to capitalize whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls running text in capitals particularly hard to read. Direction: set the paragraphs in normal upper and lower case and keep caps for the one-line label only. The size, measure, and leading can stay as they are. *Defect.*
2. **The copy is vaguest exactly where a passenger has to act.** "Between early morning and late evening" leaves out the first and last sailings, which is the one fact someone needs to avoid being stranded. "Early" can mean 5 a.m. to one reader and 8 a.m. to another, and the reader who guesses later misses the boat. "Every forty minutes" doesn't say what time the sequence starts from, so nobody can work out a departure. "In winter" doesn't give dates, so a reader in early November can't tell whether boats run every forty minutes or every hour. Anything with no departure time to anchor it ("about twelve minutes") is also hard to act on. Direction: replace each vague span with the real times and dates, which only you have. Then point readers to where the full timetable lives online, not only "in the shelter at each landing", since they're reading this before they get there. A page called "Before you board" should give them what they need to plan before they leave home. *Defect.*

**Smaller things.** None. Once the case and the copy are fixed, the rest holds up.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear: a small gray label, then a bold heading, then body text. Each size is clearly different from the next, and the heading sits close to the text it introduces. Keep the three-level scale.
- The measure and leading are right. Lines run about 70 characters at comfortable spacing, and the paragraph gaps are clearly bigger than the line gaps. Once the text is in mixed case, the lines will get a little shorter and land well inside the 45–75 range Bringhurst gives.
- Each paragraph covers one topic (the service, bicycles, winter), which makes the page easy to scan. Keep that split.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column reflows on a phone, which is likely where people read this at the landing.
