**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board" and then hits a gray wall: three paragraphs set entirely in capitals, each word the same rectangle as the next, so nothing in them catches the eye on a skim. It reads as a ferry operator's passenger information page: calm, plain, a little bureaucratic, like a notice board.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells first-time ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long it takes and what to do with a bicycle, so they can turn up at the right time.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound: a clear title, short paragraphs and a sensible column. Two things hold it back. Setting the body text in all capitals makes the page much slower to read than it needs to be. And the copy gives vague times exactly where a passenger needs exact ones.

**What would move it most**

1. **All three paragraphs are set in capitals** (defect). Every line of the body, from "THE RIVER FERRY RUNS…" to "…AT EACH LANDING.", is uppercase. Capitals have no ascenders or descenders, so every word becomes the same plain block. The reader has to spell their way through instead of recognizing word shapes. Skimming for one fact, like "bicycles" or "winter", becomes a line-by-line hunt, and the page reads as if it's shouting. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") keeps capitals for headings shorter than one line, labels and captions, and says not to capitalize whole paragraphs. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read". The fix is to set the paragraphs in upper and lower case and keep capitals for the short "PASSENGER NOTES" label. If this is a CSS `text-transform: uppercase` left on the body style, it's a one-line change.

2. **The times are vague, and times are what a passenger acts on** (defect, copy). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" leaves out three things:
   - the first and last boats
   - which minutes the boats leave at
   - when "winter" begins and ends for the hourly service

   A reader can take "late evening" as 9 pm or as 11 pm and miss the last boat. The last line then sends them to a printed timetable in a shelter, so they only find out once they're already at the landing. The direction of the fix is to restore the missing facts (the first and last departure times, the winter dates) or point to where the timetable can be seen before leaving home. Only the operator has those facts. "About twelve minutes in calm weather" is fine as written.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing separately.

**What's working.**
- **A clear type scale.** There are three sizes with big steps between them: a small gray tracked label, a large bold title, and body text at a comfortable reading size. You can tell each level at a glance; keep that.
- **The measure and spacing.** Lines run about 65 to 72 characters, inside Bringhurst's 45 to 75 range. The space between paragraphs is clearly larger than the space between lines, so the three topics separate without needing headings.
- **Restraint.** There's one typeface, one neutral palette, and no boxes or ornament. That suits a practical notice, and the title "Before you board" puts the page in the reader's own moment.
