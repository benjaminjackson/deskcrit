**First read.** My eye goes to the headline first, then across to the one red seat (B4) in the plan, then down to the red "Put on a show" button. The red ties the seat to the button, which works well. It reads as a calm, plain booking tool for village halls and amateur theaters. It feels specific and friendly. My one stumble: the plan is mostly green, and for a second I read it as a mostly empty hall. "18 seats, 3 free" in the corner corrected me.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a landing page for people who run community shows, and that its job is to get them to start a show or try the sample plan.

**Verdict.** This is a strong, honest page: the headline, product picture and lede all make the same specific case. The biggest thing in the way is the color code in the seat plan. Green means "sold", which is the opposite of what seat maps usually mean, and there is no key. So the picture that should prove "sell every seat" first reads as an empty hall.

**What would move it most**
1. **The seat-plan colors run backward, with no key** (right panel). Defect. Most booking sites a viewer has used show green for "available". Here green is sold, white with a dashed edge is free, and red is the seat just bought. A viewer first sees a hall that looks open, which is the opposite of the claim beside it. They only fix that by finding "18 seats, 3 free" in small gray type in the corner. Using red for the bought seat adds to this, because red on a seat map usually means "taken" or "unavailable" (Apple HIG and Material on status colors). Here it means "just sold to you". The fix: make the sold seats read as full and the free ones as gaps. One way is a neutral or muted fill for sold seats with the free seats clearly empty. Keep the brand red for the one seat being bought, since it carries the story. Then add a short key ("Sold / Free / Your seat"), so no state relies on color alone (WCAG 1.4.1).
2. **Three buttons in the hero** ("Put on a show", "Try a sample plan", "Talk to us"). Template tell (unslop). Three buttons in a row is the stock template hero; it makes this hero look like anyone's and weakens the one choice that matters. "Try a sample plan" is the best ask on the page: it lets a show organizer see the tool working on a hall before they commit. "Talk to us" is the weakest, because it could sit on any site and doesn't say what a conversation gets them. The fix: keep one primary button and one secondary. Move "Talk to us" to the nav or footer. "For theatres" already gives larger venues a way in.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "row" sits alone on the third line, and the break splits "the front / row" (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Change the measure or the break so the last line holds a phrase, for example "the front row" together.
- The dashed edges of the free seats (A3, B6, C5) look much fainter than 3:1 against the white and beige. They carry meaning, so make them darker (WCAG 1.4.11).
- "Seat B4, row B" says the row twice. "Seat B4" alone is enough.
- "Concessions at checkout": some readers will think "concessions" means the snack stand until they read the next line. Starting the heading with the meaning, such as "Child and senior prices", would remove the doubt. This is taste.
- The logo (about 20 px), the feature headings (about 19 px) and the lede (about 18 px) look only a step or two apart in size. Weight is what separates them now, which works, but the sizes look chosen one at a time. Measure them and bring them onto one scale.

**What's working.**
- The copy is concrete and talks in the viewer's own world: "prints the door list", "families of the cast", "the box office stops taking orders by phone". That last line compares the tool with what the organizer does today, which is the strongest kind of argument. Keep the copy this plain.
- The hero picture is the product doing the job the headline promises: a named hall, a named play, a real price. It is not a generic screenshot or decoration. Using red only for the one booked seat and the primary button gives the eye a clear path.
- The restraint: one family of type, a warm neutral palette, plain feature columns with no icons or emoji. The page looks like it belongs to its audience.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I couldn't see whether the seat plan is interactive on the live page. If visitors can click it, the free seats need visible hover and focus states.
