**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline, "Booking links that fill your calendar", then drops to the "Create your link" button. From there it slides into a row of four blue-outlined cards that all pull equally, with four blue titles, four blue badges and four blue icons. It reads clearly as a scheduling SaaS homepage for small service businesses. It feels clean and competent but generic: the right half of the hero is blank white, and everything below the headline is the same blue. Once the eye leaves the headline, nothing on the page stands out.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you didn't say: the hero of a marketing homepage for a booking-link tool. Its job is to get small practices (clinics, tutors, coaches) to create a link or watch a live example.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job: the offer is clear in one sentence and the next step is obvious. What holds it back is that it never shows the product. The hero's right half is empty, so the visitor has to take "fill your calendar" on trust. Below the fold, the blue accent is on everything, so the page flattens into a stock template.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero never shows the product, and half of it is empty.** The headline and subhead fill the left ~650 px. From x≈660 to the right margin there is nothing, so the hero tips hard to the left and the blank space looks like a missing image rather than a choice. More importantly, a visitor who has never used a booking link gets no picture of what their guests would see: a calendar of open slots, a confirmation. The page asks them to "Create your link" before showing what a link looks like. The only proof is the "See a live example" text link, which is a click away. This is a defect: it slows the decision and weakens the claim. It is also a template tell, the empty hero visual (unslop). Arnheim's *Art and Visual Perception* on balance explains why the page feels lopsided. Fix: put a real preview of the booking page in that space, the thing your guests would see. Ideally make it the same thing "See a live example" opens, so the headline's promise has a picture next to it.

2. **The blue accent is on almost everything, so nothing below the headline stands out.** Blue is used for the headline, the four nav links, both filled buttons, the four 2 px card borders, the four card titles, the four icons, the four badges and the "Learn more" links. With the accent everywhere, the cards are as loud as the call to action, and your main button loses its pull once the eye moves down. The saturated borders are also the stock "bright colored border" look. This is a convention break with a real cost (Apple HIG and Material Design 3 on accent color). Fix (approved direction): keep the accent for the main action and at most one key highlight. Draw the headings, icons, borders and other elements in neutral colors: near-black titles, gray icons, hairline gray borders.

3. **Every feature card wears a badge, and most of the badges aren't status.** "New", "Auto", "Email" and "Beta" all use the same filled blue pill. Only "New" and "Beta" mark a state. "Auto" and "Email" just repeat the card's title in another word. Four matching pills read as decoration, so the viewer stops reading them, and the one that matters ("Beta", which changes what a buyer can rely on) gets lost. The badge also pushes "Shared calendars" onto two lines (see Smaller things). This is a template tell ("PRO, New, and Beta badges everywhere", unslop). Fix: remove "Auto" and "Email". Keep at most one quiet text badge, and only where the status changes the visitor's decision.

4. **The feature row is the stock four-card pattern, and the copy beneath it is generic.** Each card has the same parts: icon, bold blue title, pill, three lines of text and a "Learn more" link. This pattern is on thousands of SaaS pages, so the row looks like anyone's. Inside the cards, the copy is actually concrete ("the day before and an hour before", "nobody has to do the math"), and that's the part worth keeping. The four identical "Learn more" links don't say where they go (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.4), and each one is a fourth link competing with the button. This is a template tell, with a convention break on the link text. Fix: drop the boxes and let the four items sit as a plain row of headings and text on the grid. Either cut the "Learn more" links or give each one text that names its destination.

**Smaller things.**
- Card 1: "Shared calendars" wraps to two lines because the "New" badge takes the space. Its body text and "Learn more" end up ~30 px lower than in the other three cards, so the lines no longer form rows across the row of cards (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). The badge is also centered on the two-line title instead of sitting on a baseline. Fixing finding 3 fixes this. Otherwise, keep every title on one line.
- The customer strip at the bottom ("Harbor Dental … Orchard Vets") is plain gray text with no label. It reads like a footer nav, not proof that these practices use the product. Give it a short label from your own copy, or set the names as logos, so it reads as customers.

**What's working.**
- The headline and subhead. "Booking links that fill your calendar" names the product and the outcome in seven words. The subhead then says how in three verbs: share, let people pick, keep. The visitor knows what this is within a second.
- The type system. One family, about four sizes with clear steps between them (headline, subhead, card title, body/nav), weight doing the fine distinctions, and a body measure well under 75 characters. Keep this when you rework the color.
- The pair of actions in the hero. There is one filled button ("Create your link") and one text link that lets the visitor check the claim first ("See a live example"). That second option is the right instinct, so make it visible (finding 1).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the buttons and links. Whether the cards wrap sensibly at tablet and phone widths. Whether the "Learn more" links have distinguishing accessible names.
