**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold title, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then into a plain block of gray-black prose on white. It reads as an information page for a small local river ferry, written for people about to ride it. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, like a notice in a parish newsletter. After the title, though, nothing tells the eye where to go. To find out when the boat runs, you have to read every sentence.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how long the crossing takes, and the rules for bicycles, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound. The page still falls short of its job, because the facts a passenger acts on (when the boats leave and when the last one goes) are vague and buried in running sentences. The main problem is the copy, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable facts are vague, and you have to dig for them.** Paragraph 1 says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". Paragraph 3 says the winter "last crossing leaves the quay before dusk". A passenger will plan around these lines and hold the operator to them, yet neither gives a clock time. "Before dusk" can mean the last boat leaves just before dark, or well before it. Read it the first way and you may arrive to find it already gone. The winter change is also left to the last paragraph, so someone who skims the first paragraph takes away the summer frequency. And the page sends people to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which they can only read once they are already standing at the landing. This is a **defect**. It slows the reader and can leave them stranded, and the content is the kind Krug says should be self-evident and scannable. Fix direction: state the first and last departures and the winter dates and times as times. Pull the schedule out of the prose so it can be scanned on its own, for example as a short list or a two-season block near the top. The actual times are facts only the maker has.
2. **The first paragraph describes the boat, not what the passenger gets.** One sentence of about 50 words runs from the crossing time through the tide to "the crew must hold a wider course around the sandbank by the old harbour wall". That tells the reader what the crew does. It leaves them to work out that their own crossing takes longer at low tide, and by how much "a little longer" is meant. This is a **defect**, mild, in the copy. Fix direction: split the sentence and end it on the reader, for example "…twelve minutes in calm weather. At low tide, most mornings, allow longer: the boat swings wide around the sandbank by the old harbour wall." If there is a figure for "longer", give it.

**Smaller things.**
- The line breaks look set by hand rather than by the column width. "quay" would fit on the first line, and "and" would fit after "boarded,". So paragraph 2 has a short-line notch in the middle, and the last line of paragraph 1 ("sandbank… low tide.") sticks out past every line above it, which makes the block look unfinished (Bringhurst 2.1.3; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, "The line"). Remove the forced breaks and let the text wrap to a set column width.

**What's working.**
- The text is easy to read. The body type is a comfortable size at about 55–65 characters per line, the line spacing is generous, and the dark text on white has strong contrast. Keep this measure and spacing when you restructure the content.
- There is a clear two-level hierarchy. One bold title, distinct in both size and weight, sits over plain body text, with no extra sizes. Paragraphs are separated by space only and stay flush left. If you add a schedule block, give it one subheading level, not a new set of sizes.
- It is honest and plain, with no stock template effects or decoration. That suits a public service notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** If the line breaks are hard-coded (`<br>`), the text will break badly at narrower widths, such as on a phone, where most people at a landing will read it. Check it at a phone width.
