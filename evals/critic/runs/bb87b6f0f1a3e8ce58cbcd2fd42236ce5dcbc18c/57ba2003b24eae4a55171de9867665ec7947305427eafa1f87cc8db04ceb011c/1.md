**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, "Rainfall at the reservoir", then falls straight into the big framed chart. There it stops, because three blue bars with no words, numbers or axis give it nothing to read. Next it drifts right to "Getting there", which turns out to be bus and parking directions. It reads as a visitor page for a reservoir or garden. The page feels clean and calm, but the main thing it promises tells you nothing.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public information page for visitors to a reservoir site. It should show recent rainfall and tell people how to get there.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The chart is the largest thing on the page and the title is about it, but it has no labels, no values and no unit, so a visitor can't learn anything about rainfall from it. The directions are the only part that informs anyone.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart has no labels, so it says nothing.** This is a defect, and the main one. The three bars in the left panel have no category labels (which months, years or gauges?), no values, no axis and no unit (mm? inches? per day or per month?). The viewer can see that the middle bar is tallest and nothing more. They can't tell when it rained, how much, or whether that's normal. The same gap means you can't judge whether the bars are in a sensible order or cover a fair time span. Tufte and Few both hold that a reader should never have to guess what a number measures. Fix: put a label under each bar, show a value scale or the value on each bar, and state the unit. Add the period and the source if the data has them.
2. **Neither the title nor the chart says what the data shows.** This is a defect, argued as common sense, since the entry's sources aren't on the approved list. "Rainfall at the reservoir" names a topic, not a finding, and the chart has no caption, so the viewer has to work out the point themselves. Right now they can't, because of finding 1. Fix: once the chart is labeled, have the title or a one-line caption state what the visitor should take from it. That finding is a fact only the maker has (for example, whether rainfall is up or down and against what), so supply it rather than leaving it to the bars.
3. **The heavy frame is louder than the data.** This is a convention. The chart sits in a dark gray border about 3 px thick on a pale blue tint. The frame is the darkest line on the page and boxes the bars in, so the panel reads as an object rather than as data. Wilke's approved fix: remove the frame around the plot and keep the baseline thin and pale, so the bars are the strongest marks in the chart. The tint can go too. White space already sets the chart apart from the text column.

**What's working.**
- The type hierarchy is clean. The bold title, the bold smaller "Getting there" heading and the regular body text each have an obvious job, and the steps between them are wide enough to read at a glance. Keep this scale as you add chart labels. Labels should be a smaller, lighter fourth level, not a new mid size.
- The "Getting there" copy is concrete and complete. It names the bus number, the stop, how often it runs, when parking is free and where the cycle racks are. A visitor can act on every sentence without filling a gap.
- The two-column layout has a consistent left margin and a gutter wide enough to separate the two topics without any rules.
