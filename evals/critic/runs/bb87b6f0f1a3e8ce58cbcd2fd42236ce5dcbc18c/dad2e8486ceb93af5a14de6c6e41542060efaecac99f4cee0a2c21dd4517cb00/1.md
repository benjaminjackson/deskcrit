**First read.** The eye goes to the bold question, "Save changes to the shared calendar?", then to the solid blue "Save changes" button. Then it slides left along a row of four actions and has to stop to sort them. This is a confirmation dialog in a calendar app, for someone who has just edited a shared schedule. It feels calm and tidy, and the title and the primary button are right. The row of buttons is where it slows down.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web confirmation dialog shown before edits to a shared calendar go live. Its job is to let the editor publish, keep working, or throw away the edits without making a mistake.

**Verdict.** The dialog almost works. The question is clear, the consequence is stated, and there's exactly one primary action. What gets in the way is the button row. "Discard changes" is the one action that can't be taken back, but it's styled as a mild blue link, placed between two safe actions, and paired with an unrelated "Export as file." The riskiest choice is the easiest one to hit by accident.

**What would move it most**
1. **The destructive action looks like the harmless ones (defect).** "Discard changes" is the second item in the row. It uses the same blue text-button style as "Export as file," and it sits right next to "Keep editing." The two link-styled actions read as a pair of lesser options. Nothing tells the viewer that one throws away the new rehearsal times and the other just saves a copy. Someone who reaches for "Keep editing" or the export can lose their work with one misplaced click. Apple HIG ("Alerts," "Buttons") and Material Design ("Dialogs") both call for destructive actions to be set apart from the safe ones and styled as destructive. Usually that means separating it by position, such as alone at the far left away from "Keep editing," and marking it with a destructive color (often red) as well as position. Make sure it is never the default action. If the product can offer undo after discarding, that's even safer than relying on the styling.
2. **"Export as file" doesn't belong in this decision (convention).** The dialog asks one question: publish, keep editing, or throw away? Export answers none of these. It's an occasional side task, but it takes the first spot in the row and makes the choice four actions long. That makes every user read and reject an option they almost never want, and it hides how the real choice is structured. Nielsen's progressive disclosure says rare options belong behind a control or somewhere else, not in the main row. Platform dialog conventions (Apple HIG, Material) also expect two or three actions in a confirmation. Move export to the editor's menu or toolbar. Then the dialog has three actions, and the destructive one can be clearly separated.

**Smaller things.**
- The button row's left edge almost lines up with the text, but not quite. The "Export as file" label starts about 34 px to the right of the left edge of the heading and body, so it looks like a misalignment rather than an indent. Once the row is down to three actions, either align the first label with the text column or right-align the whole group so the offset is obviously intentional (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*).

**What's working.**
- The body line names who is affected and what they'll see: "will be shown to everyone who follows this calendar." The editor knows exactly what pressing Save commits them to. Keep that sentence.
- There's a clean hierarchy of action weights. The solid blue "Save changes," the outlined "Keep editing" and the text buttons step down clearly, so there is no doubt which action is the main one.
- The type is restrained. There are two sizes with clear jobs (a bold question and a regular body line) and plenty of space inside the card, so the decision isn't crowded.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Which button has focus when the dialog opens, and whether Escape closes it. For a dialog with a destructive option, check that default focus lands on "Save changes" or "Keep editing," never on "Discard changes."
- There's no scrim, and the page behind the dialog is blank white. That may just be how it was captured. In a live browser, check that the calendar shows dimmed behind the modal so the editor still has the context of what they're saving.
