**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Shipments peak each third quarter," drops into the bars and goes to the tallest one, Q3 2026 at 705. It reads as a calm, well-made board slide. The chart doesn't point to the peaks the headline is about, though, and it draws the forecast bar exactly like the real ones. You only find out Q4 2026 is a forecast from a note in parentheses in the subtitle.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is page 3 of a sales review for the October board meeting. Its job is to show directors that shipments are seasonal and peak in Q3.

**Verdict.** This is solid, restrained chart work: an honest zero baseline, values labeled on the bars, a takeaway headline and a source line. The biggest problem is that the Q4 2026 forecast looks like an actual result. A board will remember 560 as a number the company hit, not a projection. The second problem is that the chart doesn't show the headline's point; the viewer has to find it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The forecast is drawn as fact.** The last bar, Q4 2026 at 560, is the same solid blue as the seven actual quarters, and its label is just as definite. The only signal is "(Q4 2026 is the forecast)" in the gray subtitle and "Q4 2026 from the September forecast" in the source line, and a director looking at the bars will see neither. At a board meeting people quote numbers back. If 560 is remembered as a result and the quarter lands lower, the slide will look like it overstated. Projections should look different from observed data (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). Fix direction: draw that bar so it plainly differs, for example as an outline, a lighter tint or hatching. Put "forecast" right under or on the bar, and add a range if the September forecast has one. *Defect.*
2. **The headline makes a claim the chart doesn't point to.** "Peak each third quarter" is the finding, but all eight bars carry the same weight. The viewer has to find Q3 on the axis twice and check that each is the local high. Fix direction: make the two Q3 bars the accent color and the other six a quiet gray or a pale tint, so the pattern is visible before anyone reads the labels. Once a solid fill means "actual," pick the forecast treatment from item 1 so it doesn't clash with the highlight. *Taste, common sense:* the chart should show the headline's claim, not just support it on inspection.
3. **The headline stops at the fact and misses the bigger story.** The bars also show every 2026 quarter above the same quarter of 2025 (455 vs 410, 590 vs 520, 705 vs 610). A board will likely care about that as much as the seasonality, and the headline doesn't say it. The headline also doesn't say what the Q3 peak means for the people in the room, such as what it calls for in planning. Fix direction: decide which point this slide is meant to make. Then finish the headline with what it means for the board, in their terms. Only you know the business consequence, so I won't write it. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- The subtitle says "Q4 2026" twice: "eight quarters to Q4 2026 (Q4 2026 is the forecast)." An editorial trim like "eight quarters to Q4 2026, the last one forecast" reads more cleanly, and the forecast label moves onto the bar anyway (item 1).

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and stripped down. Bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, values sit directly on the bars, and there's no legend, frame or effects. Nearly all the ink is data. Keep that when you add the highlight.
- The type is clear. A bold serif headline sits over a plain sans for everything else, and there are four clear levels (headline, subtitle, chart title, labels/source) with no near-duplicate sizes.
- Every element in the frame is placed with care: one left margin at about 80 px runs from the wordmark to the source line, and the footer rule and page number close the frame.
