**First read.** The eye goes straight to the huge black headline, and then it stalls. The four lines are stacked so tightly that they run into each other: the descenders of "Quayside" sink into "crossings", and "spring through early" overprints "summer". That makes the heading a dark knot you have to pick apart word by word. Next the eye drops to a calm, readable paragraph, and after that there is nothing. It reads as the top of a ferry timetable page, and it feels confident but broken.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the opening of a web page that tells people who use the river ferry when it runs this spring.

**Verdict.** As it stands this doesn't work. The headline, which is the first and largest thing on the page, can't be read cleanly because its lines collide. And a page labeled "Spring timetable" shows no times at all.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline lines collide** (defect). Across all four lines of the heading (rows ~150–370), the line spacing is set below the type size. The descenders of "Quayside", "crossings", "spring" and "through" run into the letters of the next line. The tittles (the dots on the i's and the j), and in places the tops of the letters, sit on top of glyphs from the line above. A reader has to separate the words before they can read them, so the one line meant to be grasped at a glance takes the longest to read on the page. It also looks like a bug rather than a choice. Bringhurst allows negative leading on display type only "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide." Hochuli, p. 28, faults headings set "too tightly… with too little interlinear space." Fix: open the line spacing until no letter on one line touches a letter on the next. The heading can still be tighter than the body text, just not this tight.
2. **The page promises a timetable and doesn't give one** (defect, possibly a capture issue). The label "Spring timetable" and the heading set up departure times. The paragraph gives the frequency ("every forty minutes") and the length of a crossing, but not when the first and last boats leave. For "by daylight", those two times are the whole point. Below the paragraph, the lower 40% of the frame is empty, so the eye path just ends. The viewer still can't answer "when can I catch it?" If the timetable is further down the page, this is fine, as long as it starts close enough under the paragraph to be seen on the first screen. If it isn't there, the page needs at least the first and last sailing times, and those are facts only you have. Bear in mind that those times are what people will hold the ferry to, so they must be unambiguous.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline looks like it's set in an extra-bold or black weight. That's a stock template move (unslop), and at this size it's part of why the colliding lines turn into one solid black mass. A semibold or bold weight would carry the size just as well.
- The paragraph's last line is the single word "weather." Rewrap it, or adjust the measure slightly, so the line doesn't end on one short word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The scale is clear: a small gray label, a very large heading, and readable body text. That makes three distinct levels with big steps between them, and nothing competes. Keep this scale when you fix the leading.
- The body paragraph runs about 65 characters a line with comfortable line spacing, and it shares a crisp left edge with the label and the heading. It's the best-set thing on the page.
- The copy is concrete and talks to the reader in their own terms: "north landing to the market quay", "every forty minutes", "about twelve minutes in calm weather". Units and places are named, and nothing is generic.
