**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers," and then reads straight down three short paragraphs. It reads as a calm, plain information page for people who ride a river ferry. It's quiet and easy to read. The last line is set noticeably smaller, so it comes across as a footnote, but it's the one line that tells you to do something.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a desktop web page that tells a passenger when the ferry runs, how bikes work on board, and what to check before they travel.

**Verdict.** The page reads cleanly and its type is well set. But it has the importance of its parts backwards. The one warning a passenger has to act on is the smallest thing on the page. The schedule, the one fact they came to plan around, is given only in vague words.

**What would move it most**

1. **The winter warning is set as fine print.** "Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel" is the only instruction on the page. It's also the line most likely to strand someone. Yet it looks about 16 px, under body text that looks about 19 px. So the eye files it as a caption and skims past it. The size gap is also too small to read as a separate level. Body, footnote, and heading make three sizes, and the two smaller ones sit close enough together to look like an accident rather than a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: a contrast must be obvious; Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1: sizes from a deliberate scale). This is a **defect**: readers who skim will miss the warning. The fix: give this line at least the weight of the body text and move it up to where a passenger checks first, near the schedule. Make the change with weight or placement rather than yet another size.

2. **The schedule can't be planned around.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" leaves out the first and last crossings and the times a run departs. A passenger can't tell whether they can still catch a crossing at, say, the end of the day, which is exactly what they'll later hold the operator to. "Timetables change in winter" can also be read two ways: when does winter start, and what changes, the times or the frequency? The cost of reading it wrong is a missed last boat. This is a **defect** in the copy. The fix: name the first and last departure times. If the page lists times, set them as a short list rather than prose so they can be scanned. Say when the winter timetable begins. Only you have those facts, so I'm not supplying them.
