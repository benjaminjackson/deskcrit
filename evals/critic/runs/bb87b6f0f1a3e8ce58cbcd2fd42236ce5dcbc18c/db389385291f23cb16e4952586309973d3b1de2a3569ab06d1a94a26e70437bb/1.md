**First read.** The headline grabs the eye right away. It is huge, black and heavy, and it fills the upper left. From there the eye drops to the gray subhead, then to the blue button, and then has nowhere left to go: the right side and the bottom third are empty white. It reads as a calm, competent SaaS hero for a team weekly-planning tool. It feels clean but bare, as if the page stops before it shows anything.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the homepage hero of a team weekly-planning product, meant to make a team lead or manager start a trial.

**Verdict.** The message is clear and the layout is disciplined. But the page promises "one honest picture of the next five days" and never shows it, so the viewer has only the words to go on and no reason to click. That missing proof is the single biggest thing in the way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The promise is never shown** *(defect)*. The subhead's whole pitch is a picture: one view of the week built from tickets, meetings and leave. Nothing on the page shows that view. The viewer has to imagine the product and take "honest" on trust, and a word like "honest" persuades only when you can check it. The empty space also unbalances the screen. All the weight sits in the top-left block, and nothing at the right or bottom offsets it, so the hero looks unfinished rather than spare (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Fix: put the product's actual week view in that empty area, at a size where a viewer can read real rows. Showing the claim is the strongest proof this page can offer. The subhead also stops at the artifact ("one honest picture"). It doesn't say what the viewer can then see or decide that they can't today. Supplying that consequence is the maker's call, so I won't write it.
2. **The ask is generic** *(defect)*. "Start a free trial" could sit on any SaaS page. It doesn't say what the viewer gets or how it compares with how they plan now. Your own copy already holds the hook: the gap between the week a team plans and the week it "actually works." The strongest ask lets the viewer test that gap against something they already trust, like their own team's tickets and calendar or their current planning view. If the product really builds a first week from the viewer's own data, the button and a line near it should say so in those terms. If it doesn't, say what the first minute of the trial gives them. Don't add a claim the product can't back.
3. **The headline is set like a stock template** *(template tell, plus convention)*. The headline looks like extra-bold (about weight 800) at default tracking. That heavy-weight hero is a stock move a viewer has seen on many other products (unslop: "Headings at weight 800 or 900"), so a headline with a real point of view looks like anyone's. At about 88 px the default spacing also looks loose, especially across "Plan the week your." Large type needs tighter letter and word spacing than text (Bringhurst 2.1.1; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*). Fix: drop to a bold or semibold weight and let size carry the rank, then tighten the tracking and word spaces until the two lines read as a solid block. The headline will still dominate: it is about four times the size of anything else.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline line break: "Plan the week your / team actually works" splits "your team" across lines. Break at the phrase instead ("Plan the week / your team actually works") or rebalance the measure (Bringhurst 2.4; the source for heading breaks is unconfirmed, so treat this as common sense: the phrase is the unit of meaning).

**What's working.**
- **The headline copy has a point of view.** "Actually works" names a real gap between the planned week and the real one, and that is what a team lead feels. Keep that line.
- **The subhead is concrete.** "Tickets, meetings, and leave" names the inputs in the viewer's own words, and "next five days" is precise where "this week" would be vague.
- **The structure is disciplined.** The wordmark, headline, subhead and button share one left edge at x≈120. The nav closes at the same right margin. There's one primary action, and the type scale is clear (headline about 88 px, subhead about 20 px, nav and button about 16 px). Keep that restraint when you add the product view.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I can't tell whether the empty right side and bottom are the design or a hero image that didn't load. Check in a live browser. If an image is meant to sit there, finding 1 becomes "make sure it loads and shows the real week view."
