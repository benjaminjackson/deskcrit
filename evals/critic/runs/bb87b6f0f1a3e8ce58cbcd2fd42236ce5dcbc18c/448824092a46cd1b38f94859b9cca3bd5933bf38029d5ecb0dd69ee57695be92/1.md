**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Share the kiln without double-booked shelves," then goes across to the shelf map on the right, where it's caught by the one brown square (M4). From there it drops to the brown "Add your studio kiln" button, which is the same color. Within two seconds it reads as booking software for a shared pottery studio. It feels calm and warm, made by someone who knows the trade, and doesn't look like a template.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the homepage of a SaaS product for community pottery studios. It has to get a studio owner or kiln technician to add their kiln or look at a sample firing.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The point is clear, the hierarchy is clean, there is one ask, and the look belongs to this product. The biggest gap is that the shelf map doesn't show either thing the copy promises: who is on each shelf, and that a shelf can't be booked twice.

**What would move it most**
1. **The product picture doesn't prove the claim.** The subhead says Kilnshare shows "who has work on them," and the headline promises no double-booking. In the panel on the right, though, every booked space is an anonymous green block. Even the detail row for M4 ("Six mugs, cone 6 … $18 paid") doesn't say whose mugs they are. A studio owner looks at the picture for proof and finds a seat map with no people on it, so the main pain point (two members fighting over one shelf) is something they have to take on trust. Make the picture show what the headline promises: put member names or initials on the booked spaces and the owner's name in the M4 detail row, and if you can, show what happens when someone tries to take a space that's already booked. The maker has to supply the real names and details. *Taste:* the image and the headline should make one argument together, and right now the image only illustrates the topic.
2. **The ask doesn't compare against what the studio does now.** "Add your studio kiln" is a good, specific ask, and "See a sample firing" lets the viewer judge for themselves. But the page never sets Kilnshare next to the way the studio books shelves today (a sign-up sheet, a group chat, a spreadsheet). So "one list for the technician" and "split fairly" read as claims, not as relief from a mess the viewer already has. Say which current method this replaces, in the subhead or the feature row, and let the viewer see the difference on their own terms. The maker should name the method their audience actually uses. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- The "Kiln posts" band between the middle and bottom rows isn't explained. Potters know posts go between every shelf, so one labeled bar reads like either a mistake or a separate bookable slot. Either label what it means or cut it (common sense).
- The free spaces (T3, M6, B5) have dashed borders that look very pale against the cream panel. Right now the white fill does all the work. If the dashed edge is meant to say "free," darken it to about 3:1 against the panel (WCAG 1.4.11), and check the value with a color picker.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is disciplined. One large headline, one solid button with a plain text link beside it, and the current page ("Product") marked in the nav by weight alone. Keep the restraint and keep exactly one solid button.
- The palette means something. Sage for booked, white with a dashed edge for free, and brown for both the selected space and the call to action, all on a warm off-white that feels like clay and kiln. The free spaces have a non-color cue, and M4 is named in words below the grid, so color is never the only signal.
- The copy talks like the trade: "glaze firing," "cone 6," "bisque and glaze apart," "loads the kiln from one list." Each feature heading says what happens and who it helps, without stock product language.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the shelf map is interactive and what the hover and focus states look like; how the two-column hero reflows on a phone.
