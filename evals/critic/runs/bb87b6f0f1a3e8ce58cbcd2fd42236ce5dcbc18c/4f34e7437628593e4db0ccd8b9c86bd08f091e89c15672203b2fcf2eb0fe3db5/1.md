**First read.** My eye lands on the three big figures in the top row, $4.82M first, then drops to the blue bars climbing left to right, then moves across to the small plan table. Within a second it reads as a revenue dashboard for a subscription business that's growing steadily. It feels calm, plain, and trustworthy, close to a finance tool done right.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the overview screen a founder or finance lead opens to check how revenue did this year and what's driving it.

**Verdict.** This is a well-built, sober dashboard that mostly does its job. The one thing in the way is serious for a finance screen: two numbers that both claim to be "2025 revenue" don't agree, and the heaviest lines in the chart are its frame and gridlines rather than the data.

**What would move it most**
1. **The plan table's total contradicts the chart.** *Defect.* "Revenue by plan, 2025" (right column) adds Starter, Team and Business to a Total of $4.82M. That's exactly the "Annual recurring revenue, Dec 2025" card, so the table looks like a Dec ARR split. But its heading says 2025 revenue. Reading by eye, the twelve monthly bars add up to roughly $4.48M of recognized revenue for 2025, and December alone (~$447K) works out to about $5.4M a year, so neither matches $4.82M either. A finance reader who adds up the bars, or just compares the table to the card, gets three answers to "what was revenue?" and stops trusting every number on the screen. The heading has to name what the table actually holds (WCAG 2.4.6, headings describe their content). If it's ARR by plan as of December, the heading should say so, in the same words as the card. If it really is 2025 recognized revenue, the total has to match the chart, and the plan figures need checking. Check the bar totals against the source data. My sum is by eye.
2. **The chart's frame and gridlines are as dark as the data.** *Convention.* The plot sits in a black box, with black gridlines at 100, 200, 300 and 400 drawn at full strength. They cut across every bar, so the bars look sliced into bands, and the box is the hardest edge on the page. On a chart whose only message is "steady growth", that scaffolding competes with the trend. Remove the frame, and make the gridlines thin and pale gray (or keep just a light 0 baseline) so the blue bars are the darkest marks in the chart (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 23.2; Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 5).

**Smaller things.**
- The "Jan to Dec 2025" pill (top right) could be a label or a date filter. If it's a picker, give it a chevron or calendar icon so it looks clickable (Norman, signifiers). If it isn't, drop the button-like fill.
- The lower row doesn't sit on the KPI cards' columns: the chart ends at about x 960, and the table starts at about x 1012, while the card edges are at about 907 and 925. Take the chart/table split from the card columns (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).
- The "Monthly revenue, 2025" and "Revenue by plan, 2025" headings sit a few pixels apart vertically. Put them on one baseline (Müller-Brockmann).

**What's working.**
- The KPI cards do this right: each has a label that names the metric and its period, one big figure, and a comparison to last year. Keep that three-part pattern on every card.
- The chart is honest and well labeled: bars start at zero, the units ("Thousands of US dollars, recognised revenue") are stated, and there's a source line ("closed invoices only"). That's exactly what makes a finance reader trust it, which is why the mismatch in finding 1 matters so much.
- The color is restrained: one blue for data, green only for positive change (and every change also has a "+" sign), and gray for everything else.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the date pill opens a picker, and how hover and focus states look on the navigation.
