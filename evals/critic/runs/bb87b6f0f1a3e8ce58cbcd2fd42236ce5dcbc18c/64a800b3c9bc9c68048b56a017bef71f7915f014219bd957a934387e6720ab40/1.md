**First read.** The eye lands on "The Wexley Gazette," drops past the double rule, and then moves left to right across three bold headlines of the same size, with nothing to say which one to read first. It reads as the front page of a small local paper. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy, but also unfinished, because the whole bottom half of the frame is blank.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the front page of a local news site or paper, where residents scan for what is happening in town and pick a story to read.

**Verdict.** The type and grid are well made, but this doesn't work yet as a front page. It doesn't rank the news: three stories of equal weight sit in a band across the top, and the empty half below makes the page look abandoned rather than finished.

**What would move it most**
1. **No lead story.** The three columns are identical: same heading size, same weight, same length, same position. The reader gets no signal about which story matters most, so they have to read all three headlines to decide. Choosing what leads is the main job of a newspaper front, and the page skips it. Lupton and Phillips call a uniform row of identical items with no accent a fault, and Tschichold asks that the order of importance show through differences of size. The masthead does lead the page, but among the news nothing does. Fix: make one story the lead with a larger headline, more width or more text, and set the other two back. *Defect.*
2. **The bottom half is empty, so the page tips upward.** All the content sits above about y = 415 on an 800 px frame, and the ~380 px band below it is blank. This isn't shaped white space giving the stories room. It's leftover space, and it makes the page look top-heavy and like something failed to load or was never filled (Tschichold and Müller-Brockmann on deliberate negative space; Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, on balance). This is fine if the page really continues below the fold in a longer layout, or if the frame was simply cropped too tall. Otherwise either give the content more of the frame (a larger lead story, as in finding 1, would do much of this) or end the page closer to where the content ends. *Defect.*
3. **The masthead has no date.** Every story depends on time: "reopen… in April," "from March," "from next month," "ending forty years," "through the summer term." With no date or issue line under "The Wexley Gazette," the reader can't tell which month "next month" is, or whether April is still ahead. These are the dates readers will act on, such as when they can drive or cycle over the bridge and when the market moves. Fix: add the issue date to the masthead area. That date has to come from you. *Defect (copy).*

**What's working.**
- **The grid is disciplined.** There are three equal columns with equal gutters, their outer edges line up with the ends of the double rule, and all three headings and first lines of text share baselines. Keep this when you add a lead story; vary the column span instead of breaking the alignment.
- **The type scale is clear.** There are three sizes (masthead, story headings, body) with real steps between them, and the headings get bold weight as well as size, so the levels read at a glance. The body is set in a readable serif at a comfortable size, flush left with an even rag.
- **The copy is good.** Each headline names its story in plain words with a verb ("Bridge to reopen in spring"), and each paragraph opens with the news and ends on what it means for residents (cyclists can cross from March, children sell produce on Fridays).
