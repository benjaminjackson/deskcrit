**First read.** The eye lands on the big black headline, "The allotment at the end of the bus line", drops to the italic standfirst, and reads straight into the body. It reads as a long-form feature on a small, independent city magazine: calm, literate, and confident in its writing. Below the fold line it feels oddly bare. The byline promises "Photographs by the residents" and there isn't one picture in sight.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the opening screen of an online magazine feature, meant to pull a general reader into a long article and, over time, toward subscribing.

**Verdict.** The page reads well and the writing does most of the work. The biggest gap is that a story credited to the residents' own photographs opens with no photograph, so its most specific and human material is missing from the first screen. Next come a measure that runs too long and a headline that leaves one word on its own line.

**What would move it most**
1. **No picture where the piece promises one (defect, or taste if it's intentional).** The byline credits "Photographs by the residents", but the first screen is all type, with an empty band under the third paragraph. If a lead photo is meant to sit there and didn't load, this is a capture problem: check it in a live browser. If the page really opens this way, the reader never sees the allotment, the bath-tub beds, or the people, and those are what make this story unlike any other feature on community gardens. The headline sets up a place ("at the end of the bus line"), and a picture of that place is the other half of the idea. Direction: put one resident photograph at or near the top of the column. Ideally it shows the thing the headline names, so the image and the headline make one point together and neither just repeats the other.
2. **The text column is too wide (convention).** Lines in the standfirst and body look like they run about 83 to 87 characters ("It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to" is one line). Bringhurst's comfortable range is 45 to 75, with 66 as the ideal. In a long read this is what tires people: the eye has to travel farther back to find the next line, over thousands of words. Direction: narrow the column to around 65 to 70 characters at this text size, or bump the body size up slightly within the same width. Measure the real character count to confirm.
3. **The headline leaves "line" alone on line 2 (convention).** "The allotment at the end of the bus / line" splits "bus line" and leaves a one-word runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). The biggest thing on the page then looks unbalanced, and the phrase the title turns on breaks in the middle. Direction: break it at a phrase boundary, such as "The allotment at the end / of the bus line", or tie "bus line" together with a non-breaking space so the two words wrap as one.

**Smaller things.**
- The navigation doesn't mark "Features" as the current section. Give it one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Nielsen 1; Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist").
- "Subscribe" is styled exactly like the section links, so the publication's one real ask disappears into the menu. Give it its own, still modest, emphasis (Material Design, button emphasis levels).
- The nav links are small text with tight gaps between them. Measure them against the 24 px target size or spacing rule in WCAG 2.5.8.

**What's working.**
- **The copy.** The standfirst lands on the reader, as in "the best place in the district to hear the news", and the first paragraph opens on a person, a date, and a concrete condition. It's specific enough that no other magazine could run it. Keep it.
- **A clear, short type scale.** There's one dominant headline, the standfirst is set apart by italic rather than by another size, the body is set roomy, and the meta line and kicker are small. Every level is obvious at a glance.
- **Restraint.** The small green "Feature" kicker is the only color, and the column is held back from the nav edges, so nothing competes with the reading.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the empty band below "Most of them are still in use." is the end of the content, the space for a photo that didn't load, or a lazy-load gap. Check in a live browser.
- Hover and focus states on the nav links.
