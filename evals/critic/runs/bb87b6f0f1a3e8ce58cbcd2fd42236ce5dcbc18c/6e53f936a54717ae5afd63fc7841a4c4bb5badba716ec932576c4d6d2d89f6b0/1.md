**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Help around the house, on your schedule", drops to the one-line subhead, then gets pulled straight to the first card. That card holds the only photo on the page: a dark, ornate wood-paneled hall with leather sofas. The other three cards are bright flat illustrations, so the row reads as three of a kind plus one stranger. It feels calm, warm, tidy and trustworthy, like a local home-services site. The eye then stops at the prices and has nowhere to go: the empty band at the bottom and the button far up in the top right leave the scan with no ending.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a local home-services company. It should let a homeowner see which services exist, what each costs, and how to book one.

**Verdict.** It is a clean, competent page that mostly does its job. Three things hold it back: the photo in the first card belongs to a different design and the wrong kind of customer, the prices give no unit, and the page offers no next step after the cards.

**What would move it most**

1. **The window-cleaning photo breaks the set and tells the wrong story.** It sits in card 1, top left of the row. Three cards use flat vector drawings on a pale mint tile. The first uses a dim, busy photo of a grand paneled room with leaded windows and red leather sofas. It looks more like a country-house library than the small home with a small yard that "Garden care" describes. It is the darkest, busiest thing on the page, so it takes attention from the headline. It also makes the row look assembled from two sources (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind follow one program). The photo is also hard to read: the windows are the point, but they melt into the dark woodwork and furniture, and nothing says "clean glass" at a glance (taste). *Defect.* Fix: give window cleaning a flat illustration that matches the other three, in the same mint tile and palette. Or, if photos are the plan, switch the whole set to photos with one lighting and crop style, and show ordinary homes.

2. **The prices don't say what they buy.** These are the "From $89 / $189 / $24 / $65" lines at the foot of each card. People hold you to prices, so they are where ambiguity costs most. "From $24" for laundry pickup could mean per load, per bag, per pound, or per week. "From $89" for windows could mean per visit or per window. A homeowner who reads "per visit" and then gets billed per window feels misled, and that is the trust this page is selling. *Defect* (common sense). Fix: add the unit after each price ("per visit", "per load", whatever is actually true). Only you know the unit; the point is that each price needs one.

3. **The eye path ends at the prices.** After the cards there is a band of empty cream about 150 px deep. The cards show no action of their own: no link, no arrow, no "Book" cue. The only way to act is "Book a visit" in the far top right, which the eye passed before it understood the offer. A visitor who has picked "Deep cleaning, from $189" has to backtrack and wonder whether that button books the service they chose (Arnheim on the eye's path. The "Clear eye path" entry cites Arnheim's *The Power of the Center*, which is not on the approved list, so treat this as common sense). *Defect.* Fix: give the path an ending below or inside the cards. Either make each card plainly lead to booking that service, or put one booking action after the row. Use the space at the bottom for it; it is doing nothing now. A precise ask such as booking this exact service beats a general "Book a visit".

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks after "on your", leaving "schedule" alone on line 2, because the heading box is held to about half the width with plenty of room beside it (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Widen the box to fit one line, or break after "house," so each line is a phrase.
- The headline copy could sit on any cleaning, handyman, or dog-walking site. The subhead is the specific, convincing line ("a vetted crew arrives with their own supplies"). Consider letting the headline carry some of that specific claim rather than the generic "on your schedule". This is an editorial point; don't add claims you can't back.

**What's working.**
- The subhead tells the whole process in one sentence with real verbs ("Pick a service, choose a day, and a vetted crew arrives"). A stranger knows exactly what happens. Keep that voice.
- The type plan is clean: a sturdy serif for the logo and headline and a plain sans for everything else. There are three or four clearly separate sizes, and the card titles are set apart by weight, not by an extra size. Every card follows the same structure: picture, bold title, two lines of description, price in green.
- The palette is restrained: warm cream ground, deep slate for text and the one filled button, and teal, coral and mustard kept inside the illustrations. "Book a visit" is the only dark filled shape, so it is plainly the primary action.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are clickable and what hover or focus shows on them. This matters for finding 3: if the cards do link to booking, they need a visible cue that they do.
