**Verdict.** It mostly works. There is one clear message, clean type, a zero baseline and labelled values. But it shows a forecast as if it were already shipped, and it draws every bar in fake 3D. For a board, the first is a real defect: directors will remember 560 as a fact.

**What would move it most**
1. **The Q4 2026 forecast looks exactly like the seven actual quarters.** It's the far-right bar: same blue, same fill, same bold label. The only signs it's a forecast are the parenthesis in the subtitle and the footnote at the bottom left. Anyone reading the bars, which is what the eye does first, takes 560 as shipped. They also read the drop from 705 as something that already happened. Numbers on a board slide get quoted back and held against the maker, so this is a **defect**. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says projections should look different from observed data. Fix: make that bar look plainly different, for example as an outline or a lighter or hatched fill. Put "forecast" right at the bar or its axis label. Show a range if the forecast has one. Then the subtitle doesn't need to carry this.
2. **Every bar is drawn in 3D, with a dark side face and a pale top cap.** The extrusion adds ink that shows no data. It also blurs where each bar ends, because the eye can't tell whether the value sits at the front edge or the back of the cap. On top of that it makes the slide look like an office chart preset, which clashes with the careful type. The labels save the exact values, but comparing bar heights at a glance is what the chart is for, and that gets harder. This is a **convention**, and breaking it costs something here: Wilke, ch. 26.1, "Avoid gratuitous 3D"; Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design," pitfall 7; Tufte. Fix: draw the bars flat, so each value is read from one length.
3. **The headline leaves out the bigger story and claims more than two points show.** "Peak each third quarter" rests on two Q3s. The chart also shows every 2026 quarter above the same quarter of 2025, but neither the headline nor the layout helps a viewer see that. The matching quarters sit four bars apart, so a director has to do the year-over-year comparison in their head, and that comparison is probably what the board cares about. This is **taste**. If growth is part of the point, say it in the headline, using the chart's own numbers. Consider putting the matching quarters next to each other (2025 and 2026 side by side per quarter) so the comparison is made for the viewer. If seasonality really is the only point, soften "each" to match two years of evidence.
