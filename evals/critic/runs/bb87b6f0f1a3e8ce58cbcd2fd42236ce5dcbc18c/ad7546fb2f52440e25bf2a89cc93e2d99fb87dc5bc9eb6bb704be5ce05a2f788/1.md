**First read.** The eye goes to the big cream serif headline, then across to the booking card on the right, then down to the amber "Set up your studio" button. Within a few seconds it reads as a calm, well-made product page for pottery studios: dark kiln-room green, warm cream type, and one amber accent. It feels made by people who have stood in a kiln room. Below the hero, the sections get quieter and more alike, and the page stops at the prices.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing landing page that gets owners or managers of pottery studios to sign up for a shelf-booking tool.

**Verdict.** It does its job. It's clear, specific and restrained, well above the usual landing page. The biggest thing in the way is the end: a viewer convinced by the prices has nowhere to act, and the pricing copy leaves two questions open at the exact moment they're deciding.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page ends at the prices with no ask.** The "One price per studio" band is the last real content. Under "$12 a month" and "$29 a month" there's no button, only the footer. The pricing section is where a convinced viewer decides, and to act they have to scroll back to the hero. The only primary button sits at the top of the page, so the eye path ends in a dead end exactly where intent is highest. *Defect* (common sense: no approved source states it). Fix: put the primary action under the plans, ideally one per plan so the click also records the choice, styled like the hero button.
2. **The pricing copy can be read two ways, and the prices are the line people hold you to.** "One price per studio" sits over *two* prices. A stranger can read it as "there's a single plan", which the two columns then contradict. Or they can read it as "you pay per studio, not per member", which is probably the point, but the page never says so. Separately, the prices are in "$" while the footer says "made in Stoke-on-Trent". A UK studio can't tell whether it would be billed in dollars, or what that comes to in their own currency. *Defect.* Fix: say the one fact the heading is hinting at, in the viewer's terms. If billing isn't per member, say what that means for the viewer. And show the currency your target studios actually pay in. Both facts are yours to supply.
3. **The copy never sets Kilnroom next to how the studio books shelves today.** The lines are concrete and good ("a number to write on the foot", "without asking the teacher"). But each one only describes what Kilnroom does, and the viewer has to work out what they'd stop doing. "See a sample firing" is the strongest ask on the page, because it lets the viewer judge for themselves. Nothing compares the product to the system they already know and trust. *Taste.* Direction: somewhere near the hero or the steps, name how studios handle this now and what that costs them, in their words. You know the real current practice; I won't write it.

**Smaller things.**
- Pricing: "$12 a month" and "$29 a month" look the same size and serif as the section heading "One price per studio" above them, so the prices read as headings of equal rank. Make the heading and the prices clearly different sizes, or set the prices in the sans in bold (Bringhurst on sizes from a scale).
- Hero headline: line 2 ends on "the" and "firing" sits alone on line 3. That splits the phrase "the firing" and leaves a one-word runt (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rebreak it so "the firing" stays together, for example by narrowing or widening the measure.
- Line drawings: a vase, stacked bowls and a kiln fill the right column of every section below the hero. "A visual on every section" (Sokolovsky, unslop) is a stock move. The drawings are specific and consistent, but they decorate rather than inform, and the product itself appears only once. Keep one where it earns its place, or replace them with more glimpses of the real product, like the hero card.

**What's working.**
- **The hero card.** "Glaze firing, cone 6 / Loads Friday 16:00 / Beginners class, 14 bowls / Half free — Book" shows the product with real-looking data. The headline's promise becomes concrete at a glance, and image and headline make one idea. Keep it, and keep it this specific.
- **Color restraint.** The deep green, cream and muted gray carry everything. Amber is kept for the primary button, the one free shelf, and accents in the drawings, so it always means "here". It doesn't look like a stock dark template.
- **Plain, specific copy.** "Collect when told", "write on the foot", and "name the students later" sound like a person who knows the work. The hero body sentence says who does what, and to what.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether "Book" in the hero card and "See a sample firing" are live, and what they show. How the two-column hero and the right-column drawings reflow on a phone.
