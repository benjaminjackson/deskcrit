**First read.** The eye goes to the three-line headline, then to the dark card on the right, then down to the amber "Add your beach" button. Within seconds it reads as a tide-and-current app for sea swimmers, and it feels quiet, calm and capable. Then the eye returns to the card's bar chart to work out what it shows, and the card doesn't tell it.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing landing page for a UK (Cornish) sea-swimming app. It needs to get swimmers to trust it with a safety decision and add their first beach.

**Verdict.** This is a clear, well-built page with a sharp headline. The hero card is the product's proof, but it doesn't show the one thing the product promises, the safe window. The look is also stock enough that the page could belong to any app.

**What would move it most**

1. **The hero card never shows a safe window, and its chart is unlabeled.** The bar chart in the top-right card has no time axis, no unit and no label. A viewer has to guess that the 24 bars are hourly tide height and when they start. The page promises a marked "safe window", but nothing on the bars marks one. Every bar is the same gray, so the hours around 06:10 and 18:40 look no different from 09:30, "Ebb at its strongest". The chart and the table below it sit in the same card but don't refer to each other. The viewer gets a decorative wave and a list, not the "know before you leave the car" moment. *Defect.* Every chart needs labeled axes with units, so the reader never has to guess what a number measures (Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2; Few). Showing the main finding on the chart itself is common sense here, because it is the product's entire pitch. Direction: put a few hour marks under the bars, say what the bars measure, and mark the swim windows on the chart itself. Tying the four table rows to their points on the chart lets the card show the product working instead of describing it.

2. **The verdict column can be read two ways, and on a safety product that matters.** In the card's right-hand column, "Swim", "Avoid" and "Next" are set as equals. The two verdicts are the same gray, so "Avoid" is no more prominent than "Swim". "Next", the only bold word, takes the place of a verdict on the 12:25 "Low water, slack" row. Is low water swimmable or not? One reading: it's the next swim. The other: it's just the next event, verdict unknown. A swimmer who picks the wrong one is acting on a safety call the page never made. *Defect* (copy and hierarchy; no checklist line covers it). Direction: keep the verdict and the "next" pointer as separate signals, and give "Avoid" more weight than "Swim" through weight, value, or the amber the page already uses.

3. **Prices are in dollars on a page made in Penzance.** The pricing reads "$0" and "$4 a month". Everything else points to UK swimmers: "car park", °C, Porthgwidden, "made in Penzance". A UK viewer reads $ as either a mistake or a product that isn't really for them, right at the moment they're deciding to pay. Price is the line people hold you to, so a wrong currency is a defect, not a style note. *Defect* (common sense, since unfinished copy has no catalog entry). Direction: show the currency your actual customers pay in. If you sell in more than one market, that's a fact only you have.

4. **The color scheme is the stock dark template.** The page is a near-black gray ground with white text in a neutral sans, and its only color is the amber on one button. Viewers have seen exactly this on many other product pages, so nothing in the look says sea, Cornwall or cold water. The copy has plenty of character ("before you leave the car", "Dipper", "Year-rounder"), and the look doesn't match it. *Template tell* (Damir Sokolovsky, unslop, "Neutral dark template look"). Direction: tint the gray ramp toward the product's own world, and take an accent from that world too. The safe-window marks from finding 1 are a natural first home for that color.

**Smaller things.**
- The pricing columns start about 5 px left of the feature columns above them (x≈464 vs ≈469). That's a near-miss, not a choice. Put both rows on one column grid (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*; Arnheim on ambiguous placement).
- The pricing section has no action, and the only way to sign up is the hero button far above. Taste: a plain "Add your beach" under the plans would close the page.
- The headline promises a decision, but nothing says why this beats what swimmers already use, such as a printed tide table or a tide app. A short comparison of that kind would let them see the value for themselves. You'd need to supply the comparison.

**What's working.**
- The headline names a specific moment from the swimmer's own life ("before you leave the car"), and "Add your beach" asks for one concrete, easy step. Keep both.
- The hero card shows a real beach, a real day and real times instead of a generic picture. That's the right idea, and it only needs to finish the job (finding 1).
- Type and spacing are disciplined. There are four or five sizes with clear steps, the measure is comfortable, and the features and pricing sit in clear groups with generous gaps between sections.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether "See a sample morning" gives a fuller demo that answers finding 1. Also how the hero card and its 24 bars hold up on a phone.
