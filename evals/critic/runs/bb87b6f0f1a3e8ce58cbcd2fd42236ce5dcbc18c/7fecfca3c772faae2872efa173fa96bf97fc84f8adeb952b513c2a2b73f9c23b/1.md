**First read.** The eye lands on the light-blue strip of four numbers and moves across them without stopping on any one, because all four are the same size and the same blue. Then it jumps to the dark Saturday bar, the only solid block of color on the page. This reads as the monthly bookings screen for a small pottery studio, made for the owner. It feels calm, tidy and organized, but it looks like a lot of other admin templates.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is for the studio owner, to see at a glance how November went compared with October and where the bookings came from.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The figures are there, the comparisons are there, and it fits on one screen. What holds it back is that the same blue is on almost everything, so the color stops pointing at anything, and none of the titles say what the numbers mean. The owner has to find the story on their own, when the screen should hand it to them.

**What would move it most**
1. **One blue on every heading, number and label, so nothing stands out (defect, and a template tell).** The blue appears on the page title "Bookings", the date range, all four KPI numbers, every card title, the table headers, the Revenue column, the legend text and the nav. It costs the viewer three things:
   - The four KPI numbers and the page title look equally important. They're about the same size and the same color, so nothing is clearly first (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance by size).
   - The Saturday bar is the one place where blue really means "look here", and it has to compete with about twenty other blue things.
   - In a web app, blue text usually means a link. The Revenue figures ($5,760…) and "1 to 30 November 2026" look clickable, and nothing tells the viewer whether they are. A screen that uses its accent everywhere also looks like a stock dashboard (unslop: one accent, once or twice per screen).

   Fix: set headings, headers and figures in the neutral text color. Keep blue for the current nav item, real links or controls, and the data you want to stand out (the November line, the Saturday bar). If the date range is a picker, make it look like a control. Then decide which single figure the owner should see first (revenue, if this is about money) and make it clearly bigger than the other three.

2. **The titles name what the chart shows, not what it found (taste; I can't cite an approved source for this).** All four KPIs went up compared with October, Saturday is by far the busiest day, and November ran above October across the whole month. None of the text says this. "352 in October" leaves the owner to do the subtraction four times, and "Seats booked by weekday" leaves them to notice Saturday on their own. Fix: have the titles and the KPI sub-lines state what changed, using numbers the piece already has. For example, turn the October line into a change with a direction, and use a chart title that names the busiest day. This is the main job of a monthly review screen, and right now the viewer has to do it.

3. **The line chart's unit and scale are unclear (defect).** "Daily bookings" has no unit on the y-axis, and the KPI next to it counts *seats*. The viewer can't tell whether a booking means one seat or a group. The two also don't seem to add up: daily values of about 13–23 over 30 days come to well over 386. The chart is also called "daily" but looks like it plots only about 12 points between 1 and 30 Nov. Read as days, those points would be roughly every 2–3 days, which doesn't match the title. One of these is wrong: the title, the sampling, or the unit. Fix: label the y-axis with its unit (Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*), use the same unit as the KPI, and either plot every day or retitle the chart to match what's plotted. Also check that the numbers add up across the KPI, the line chart and the weekday chart.

4. **"Fullest classes" sorts by seats sold, not by how full each class is (defect in the copy).** "Fullest" suggests a fill rate, like the 88% at the top. The table actually ranks by the number of seats, which favors classes that run more often or have more seats. An owner deciding which class to add sessions for could pick the wrong one. Fix: either rename the table so it says it ranks by seats booked, or add a fill-rate column and sort by that.

**Smaller things.**
- The legend in the top right of the line chart makes the eye go back and forth. Label "November" and "October" at the right-hand ends of the lines instead (Tufte; Wilke).
- The October line is a pale gray that looks under 3:1 against white. Since it's the comparison line, darken it a step while keeping it gray (WCAG 1.4.11).
- Only Saturday has a value on the weekday chart. The other six bars have no scale or labels, so you can't read Sunday's or Thursday's number. Add small value labels at the bar tops in the same gray.
- In the Fullest classes table, the Class column is about 500 px wide for short names, which leaves a big gap before Teacher. Narrow it so each row reads as one line of information.

**What's working.**
- The KPI strip gives every figure its October comparison, right under the number. That context is the hardest part to get right, and it's already there.
- Both charts keep the past in gray and the present in blue, and the weekday chart highlights one bar instead of coloring all seven. Keep this logic when you take blue off everything else.
- Everything fits on one screen, with even margins and a nav that clearly shows the current page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "1 to 30 November 2026" and the Revenue figures are clickable. Check in the live app; it changes finding 1 from "looks like a link" to "is a link that needs to look like one".
