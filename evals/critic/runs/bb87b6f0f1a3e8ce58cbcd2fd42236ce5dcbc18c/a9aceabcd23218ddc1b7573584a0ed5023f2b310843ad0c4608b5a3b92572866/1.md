**First read.** My eye lands on "Changing your pickup time" and then runs straight down the four bold headings. It reads as the help section of a small bakery's ordering site, for someone who has already placed an order. The tone is calm, plain and trustworthy. The typography is clean and the headings make it easy to scan. Nothing on the page says what the set is, though: it opens on the first question.

**What it's for.** My assumption: answering a customer's practical questions about a bread order they've already placed (changing it, collecting it, allergies, cancelling), on a web page.

**Verdict.** The layout mostly works: the four answers are grouped clearly, easy to scan and comfortable to read. The weak spot is the words. Two of the answers deal with commitments a customer will act on, and both can be read two ways. That will send people to the counter or the cancel button with the wrong idea.

**What would move it most**
1. **The cancelling deadline and the collection rule are ambiguous** (defect). These are the lines people will hold the bakery to.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say a day ahead of what, or how a day is measured. It could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time on the previous day. A customer who cancels the evening before a morning pickup may think they're in time and find they aren't, then argue about it at the till.
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name" doesn't say whose name. It could be the name on the order or the collector's own name. A friend who gives their own name when the order is under yours gets turned away.
   - Fix direction: make each rule exact, using facts only the bakery has. The deadline needs its reference point (before the pickup time) and its unit (hours, or a clock time on a given day). The collection line needs to say whose name it means. Write it as a plain instruction, the way the "two hours after the chosen time" line already does well.
   - "Open your order" also doesn't say where the order lives (the account page, the confirmation email). Name the place. Don't make the reader work it out.
2. **The answers run too wide** (convention, Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2: 45 to 75 characters). The one-line answers, such as "Give the person your order number…" and "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead…", stretch across about 820 px and look like roughly 90 to 95 characters per line. That's past the comfortable range, so the eye has a long trip back to the start of each line. With one-line answers this costs little now, but it will get worse once any answer grows to a real paragraph. Fix direction: cap the text column at about 65 to 70 characters. The right-hand white space (about 300 px of it) can absorb the change.
3. **Nothing names the set, and four equal headings compete to be first** (defect if this really is the top of the page; fine if a title sits above the screenshot). The four headings are the same size and weight. That is right for parallel questions. But with no title above them, the eye starts at "Changing your pickup time" without knowing this is a help or FAQ section, or how far the list goes (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the logical order through size). Fix direction: put one clearly dominant title above the questions that says what the set covers. Keep the four questions equal below it.

**Smaller things.**
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the Allergies answer, and the short ending looks like a stray. Narrowing the column will reflow it. Otherwise rebreak it so the last line carries more than one word.

**What's working.**
- **Grouping by space.** Each heading sits about 8 px above its answer and about 40 px below the answer before it, so each question and answer reads as one unit with no rules or boxes. Keep that ratio.
- **The type scale.** There are two clear sizes (headings around 32 px bold, body around 18 px regular) and nothing in between. That is all this content needs. The dark, near-black text on white reads easily.
- **The headings are written as the reader's task** ("Collecting for someone else", "Cancelling an order"), so people can find their question at a glance. The best answers ("holds orders for two hours after the chosen time", "the bread has already been baked for you") are specific and give the reason, which is the standard the ambiguous lines should meet.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "Open your order" links to the order. Check in the live page that it does, since that's the action this answer is about.
