**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Help for ferry passengers", drops to the two bold subheads, then runs down the left column. It reads as a plain help page for a small local ferry service. It feels calm, tidy and practical, if a little bare: the lower third of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web help page that tells ferry passengers how to get ready for a crossing and how to report a problem afterward.

**Verdict.** The layout and type are sound and easy to read. The page falls short on content: on the two things a passenger most needs to act on, the timetable and where to send a complaint, it sends them somewhere else instead of telling them.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page points away from itself for the facts it exists to give.** Bullet 1 says to "Check the timetable board at your landing," and step 3 says to send your note "to the address on the noticeboard in the shelter." Someone reading this at home, which is when people read a help page, cannot do either. They would have to travel to the quay to find out when the boat runs or where to write. "The last boat leaves before dusk" is the one deadline on the page, and it can be read two ways. Dusk moves by the day, so one passenger reads it as "be there well before dark" and another arrives at dusk thinking they're on time and misses the boat. *Defect.* The fix: put on this page the address, the last sailing as a clock time (or where to find the current one), and a link to the timetable. Only you have those facts. If this is actually a printed notice posted in the shelter, the address pointer is fine, but "before dusk" still needs a time.
2. **"If something goes wrong" names a situation, not what the section holds.** The three steps are a complaint process: write down the details, describe it, leave a note. A passenger with a problem right now, like a missed last boat or trouble on board, reads the heading as immediate help and finds paperwork instead. The section also never says what happens after you leave the note, such as who replies and roughly when. That reassurance is what makes someone bother to write at all. *Defect* (WCAG 2.4.6 asks that a heading describe its section). The fix is a heading built from what the steps already do, such as "To report a problem after your crossing." If there is a way to get help on the spot, name it here or say where to find it. If there is a reply time, add it. That is your fact to supply.
3. **Neither list hangs.** In both columns the wrapped lines go back under the bullet or the number instead of lining up with the first word. The bullets also have a wide gap of about 20 px before the text. So each item's first line starts indented and the rest don't, the left edge zigzags, and the small bullet dots are nearly the only thing showing where an item starts. Scanning the four tips takes more effort than it should. *Convention*, partly supported: Bringhurst §4.4.2 says to make lists clear by spacing and by hanging the markers. Wrapped lines lining up with the first word is standard practice rather than a rule he states. The fix: indent the wrapped lines to the text start (a hanging indent) and close the bullet gap to a normal space.

**Smaller things.**
- The last line of bullet 4 is the single word "clear.". Rebreak the line or adjust the measure slightly so it carries two or more words (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The columns share baselines for the first two lines, then drift apart. The right column's lines at about y 271 and 339 sit between the left column's lines because the gaps between list items aren't a multiple of the line spacing. Setting the item gaps to whole line steps would keep rows straight across the gutter (Müller-Brockmann). This is a refinement, not urgent.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is clean.** There are three sizes with big steps (title, subhead, body), and the subheads also differ by weight. You always know what level you're reading. Keep it this tight when you add the missing facts.
- **The copy speaks to the passenger and gives reasons.** "Because winter sailings run once an hour," "since the machine is slow to give change," "so that the gangway is kept clear." Each tip says why, so people are more likely to follow it.
- **The list types match the content.** The tips are bulleted because their order doesn't matter, and the complaint steps are numbered because it does. Lines of about 60 characters with generous leading make both columns easy to read.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the two columns stack on a phone. That matters here because passengers will check this on the quay.
