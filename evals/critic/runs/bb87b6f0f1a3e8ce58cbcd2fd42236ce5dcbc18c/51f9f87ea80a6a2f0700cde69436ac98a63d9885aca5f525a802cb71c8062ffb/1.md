**First read.** The eye goes straight to "Account settings", then down four identical white cards, each with a bold title, one gray line and an outlined button on the right. Then the blue "Billing" highlight in the sidebar pulls it left, and that's where it trips: the sidebar says you're on Billing, but the page says you're on an overview of everything. It reads as a calm, competent SaaS settings screen that is unsure which page it is.

**What it's for.** My assumption: an account-settings screen in a web app, where a workspace member finds one setting (profile, notifications, billing, sessions) and goes to change it.

**Verdict.** The craft is clean and the page is easy to scan, but the navigation contradicts the content. The sidebar marks "Billing" while the page shows a four-card overview, and the sidebar and cards list different sections. A user can't easily tell where they are or how the settings are organized. Fix the structure. Leave the styling alone.

**What would move it most**
1. **The sidebar says "Billing" but the page is "Account settings."** The highlighted item at the top left (tinted background, bold blue) tells the user they're on Billing. The heading and the four cards say this is a general overview, with Billing only third in the list. A user who clicked Billing expecting invoices lands on a summary and has to click "View invoices" again. A user who arrived some other way thinks they're in the wrong place. The current-location marker has to match the page it's on (Nielsen heuristic 1, visibility of system status; Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist"). Direction: either this is the Billing page, so the heading and content become billing, or it's an overview, so it gets its own nav item (or none is highlighted) and Billing stops being selected. If this capture caught a half-finished click, check it live. As shown, it's a **defect**.

2. **The sidebar and the cards are two different maps of the same settings.** The sidebar lists Profile, Notifications, Billing, Members, Integrations and Security. The cards list Profile, Notifications, Billing and Sessions. Sessions isn't in the sidebar (presumably it lives under Security). Members and Integrations have no card. Three cards repeat sidebar items one for one, so the screen offers two routes to the same three places, but not to the rest. A first-time user can't build a correct picture of what's in settings and where it lives (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). The duplicates just add reading (Nielsen heuristic 8). Direction: pick one job for the cards. Either they become a complete index that matches the sidebar names exactly, or the overview shows only what the sidebar can't, such as status and things that need attention, and stops repeating the nav. In both cases, "Sessions" and "Security" need to share a name if they're the same place. **Defect.**

3. **The billing line leaves out what the user actually holds the product to.** "Your plan renews on 1 March. The invoice goes to the address on file." gives a date with no year and no amount or plan name. It also points to an address the user has to remember or go look up. Renewal date, charge and billing email are the facts people act on and dispute later. "1 March" could read as next March or a past one in a long-lived account, and "the address on file" asks the user to recall something the screen could show (Nielsen heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Direction: add the year, and name the plan or amount and the address the invoice goes to, using facts only you have. **Defect**, minor in size but on the line that carries money.

**Smaller things.** None beyond the above.

**What's working.**
- The alignment is disciplined. The title and cards share one left edge at the content column, the workspace label's right edge lines up with the cards' right edge, and the first sidebar item sits on the same line as the page title. Keep this grid when you restructure.
- Each card works the same way: bold title, one plain sentence that says what the setting does for *you* ("Your name and photo appear on comments and in the member list"), and one verb-labeled button. The copy speaks to the user and names consequences. The Sessions line ("You are signed in on three now") gives a live fact, which is exactly what an overview card should do.
- All four buttons share one secondary style. On a settings index where no action outranks the others, that's the right call, so don't promote one to a filled primary.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus indicators on the four buttons and the sidebar links, and whether "Workspace: Harbour Studio" is meant to be a switcher (it doesn't look clickable now, so if it is, it needs a cue).
