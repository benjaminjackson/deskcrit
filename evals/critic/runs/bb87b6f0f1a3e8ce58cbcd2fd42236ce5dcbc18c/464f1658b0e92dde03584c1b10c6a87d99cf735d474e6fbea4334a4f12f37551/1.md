**First read.** The eye lands first on the robot's dark face screen at right. It's the heaviest, highest-contrast mass on the page. Then it jumps left to the headline, then down to the blue button. Within two seconds this reads as "an AI meeting-notes bot for teams." The feel is calm, tidy and trustworthy, until the robot makes it look like a generic AI startup.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the landing-page hero for a meeting-notes tool. It has to get someone who runs or attends a lot of video calls to connect their calendar, or at least open the sample notes.

**Verdict.** The words and type are strong. The copy is specific, plain and convincing. The picture is the weak point: a stock cartoon robot on circuit traces takes the most visual weight on the page, says nothing the headline doesn't, and shows none of what the product actually makes. Replace it with the product's output and this hero works.

**What would move it most**
1. **The robot is a stock AI picture, and it takes the first look away from the headline** (template tell, plus a defect). The right half shows a smiling robot head wired to circuit traces with dot ends. Readers have seen this on countless AI products, so the page looks like anyone's. Its dark face screen is also the strongest contrast on the page, so it wins the first glance over the headline. That glance tells the viewer nothing: "a bot" is the least interesting thing about Minutehand. The headline promises notes ready before you hang up, and the image doesn't help with that promise. Image and headline should make one idea together, and here they don't. The fix direction (unslop): take the robot out and show what the product produces. Here that means the notes themselves, with the decision at the top and tasks carrying a name and a day, as the three columns below describe. Use the maker's real sample, the same one the "Read a sample set of notes" link opens. That way the hero picture is the proof, and the eye lands on the headline first and then on the evidence.
2. **The circuit traces lead the eye nowhere** (defect; this part is common sense, since the eye-path rule has no confirmed source beyond Arnheim). Eight gray lines run out from the robot and stop at empty blue circles near the edges of the right column. They're pure ornament: removing them loses nothing. They also pull the eye outward and off toward the edges, away from the button. They go when the robot goes. Don't keep them as a backdrop for whatever replaces it.

**Smaller things.**
- The ask, "Add it to your calendar," names one concrete action, which is good. The secondary link, "Read a sample set of notes," is the stronger persuader, because it lets viewers judge the output for themselves. Neither one sets the notes against something the viewer already has, such as notes from a call they attended or what they write down themselves today. If the maker has that comparison, the sample is the place for it.

**What's working.**
- **The copy.** "Notes from every call, written before you hang up" is a concrete promise with a time in it. The paragraph says who does what, to what, and when, and it heads off the main worry with "You correct a line in one click." The three feature headings name real behavior ("Tasks with a name and a day," "Nothing kept you did not ask for"), not adjectives. Keep this voice in every edit.
- **A tight type scale and one accent.** I count about four sizes: headline, lead paragraph, feature headings, small body. The steps between them are clear. A single dark blue marks the one primary button, and the secondary action is a plain underlined link. The order of actions is clear.
- **Clean structure.** The 80 px left edge is shared by the logo, headline, paragraph, button, rule and first column. The three feature columns hang on an even grid with a lot of open space, which suits a product that promises less clutter.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states and the hover state on the button and nav links.
