I've finished checking the image against the checklist; here's the critique.

**First read.** My eye lands on the headline "Hand out parking bays without the arguments", jumps right to the dark Q4 tile in the bay plan, then comes back down to "Map your car park". Within a few seconds it reads as software that helps a residential estate run its car park. It feels calm, plain and sure of itself. It doesn't look like a template. The one place I stalled was the plan itself: it took me a moment to work out which bays are taken and which are free.

**What it's for.** Assumption: this is the top of a marketing landing page, for estate managers or residents' associations deciding whether to try Bayline for allocating parking.

**Verdict.** It works. The hierarchy is clear, the picture shows the actual product, and the copy is specific. The biggest problem is the product picture. Its colors say the opposite of what they mean, and its "$15 a month" can be read as Bayline's price.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bay plan's colors are backwards and there's no key.** In the right-hand panel, the sage-green tiles are the taken bays. The white dashed tiles (P3, Q6, R5) are the free ones, and navy Q4 is the selected one. Most viewers read green as "available", so they'll see 14 open bays and three empty slots. Only "18 bays, 3 free" in the corner lets you work it out, and that takes counting. This picture is the page's proof that the product makes allocation clear, so a plan that needs decoding works against the headline. *Convention:* status colors shouldn't be reversed (Apple HIG: Color; Material Design), and meaning shown by color needs a second cue (WCAG 1.4.1). *Fix:* make the occupied state neutral, or give the free state the "go" color. Then add a second cue for each state, such as a short legend or a "free" label in the empty bays.
2. **"$15 a month" can be read two ways.** It sits bottom-right of the panel, right-aligned like a price, on a sales page whose nav has "Pricing" in it. One reading is the resident's fee for Bay Q4. The other is what Bayline costs. A manager who leaves thinking it's $15 a month and later sees a different price feels misled. If they think it's the resident fee, they may wonder whether Bayline handles billing. *Defect* (a price line with two readings). *Fix:* tie the number to the bay in words, for example by putting the fee in the "Flat 7, permit until April" line or labeling it as the resident's permit fee. Keep it out of the price-tag position.
3. **The headline promises "without the arguments", but nothing shows how.** The subhead lists what Bayline stores and who sees free bays. That's record-keeping. It doesn't say how the arguments stop, which is the claim the viewer will actually act on. The subhead also never sets Bayline against what an estate office uses now, so the viewer has to take the value on trust. *Copy, taste:* end the subhead on what the viewer gets, for example what they no longer have to settle by hand. Name the kind of comparison that would help (their current permit list or sign-up sheet) and let the maker supply the specifics. The secondary link "Look at a sample car park" partly does this already. It would be stronger if the viewer could compare the sample against their own setup.

**What's working.**
- The hero picture is the product, not decoration. It shows real bay codes, a named car park, a one-way lane and a specific permit, so a manager can see their own car park in it. Keep it specific like this.
- The copy is concrete everywhere. "Map your car park" is an exact first step. The three feature lines each say who does what ("A resident sends a visitor a pass for one day, and the barrier lets them in").
- The type and spacing are restrained. There's one clear headline, body text at a comfortable line length, one navy primary button with an underlined text link below it, and the nav marks the current page. Nothing fights the headline.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bay tiles are interactive, and if so their hover and focus states. If they are, the color-only coding in finding 1 matters even more.
