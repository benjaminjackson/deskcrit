**First read.** My eye lands on the headline "Notes from every call, written before you hang up." Then it goes across to the pale card on the right and sees real meeting notes: a decision with a date and three tasks with names and days. Then it drops to the dark blue button. **What it's for.** My assumption: a landing page that gets people at small companies who sit in lots of video calls to connect Minutehand to their calendar.

**Verdict.** This page does its job. The headline and the sample card make one clear promise and back it up on the spot. The weakest part is the primary button: its words don't say what clicking commits you to, and that is where a careful viewer will stop.

**What would move it most**
1. **The main button doesn't say what you're agreeing to.** "Add it to your calendar" (the dark blue button, left side) can be read two ways. It could mean adding an event, or it could mean giving a bot access to your calendar so it joins every call you have. The paragraph above says it "joins your video calls," so the second reading is the real one, and that's the one that makes people hesitate. The page tells you nothing about scope (every call or only the ones you pick), cost, or what screen comes next. The viewer has to guess at the exact moment you're asking them to commit. *Defect.* Fix: start by putting the noun back ("Add Minutehand to your calendar"). Then add the facts only you have, right next to the button: which calls it joins, what it costs or whether it's free to start, and what happens after the click. Don't add new claims you can't support.
2. **The ask makes people grade your sample instead of testing it on their own call.** "Read a sample set of notes" offers a sample written by you. The card already shows one, so the link adds little. The strongest proof for this product is a comparison the viewer already trusts: notes from a call they were actually on, next to what they or a colleague wrote by hand, or next to what they remember was decided. Right now the page asks for trust where it could offer a test. *Taste, argued from the job:* a note-taker is judged on accuracy, and only the viewer knows what really happened on their own call. Fix: point the secondary ask at the viewer's own next call, in the terms of what you actually offer. You supply the specifics.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks inside its verb phrase: "written before you / hang up" leaves "hang up" alone on a short third line. If you break after "call," it reads in two clean phrases (Bringhurst, section 2.4). This is a convention, and the cost is small.
- The feature columns end on runts: "and knows." under "Decisions at the top" and "who." under "Tasks with a name and a day." Rewrap them or adjust the column width slightly so no short word sits alone on the last line (Bringhurst, section 2.4.2).
- The third feature heading, "Nothing kept you did not ask for," takes two tries to parse. A reorder or added words would fix it without changing the claim, for example "Nothing is kept unless you ask."
- *Taste:* the styling is the default SaaS starter look: one sans serif (looks like Inter), slate grays, one blue button. It costs little here because the card's content carries the identity. But nothing in the type or color would tell you this is Minutehand if the copy were removed.

**What's working.**
- **The sample card as the hero image.** It shows the product's actual output, with dates, names, days and even a correction ("14 March, not 7 March"). So the headline's promise is proven before anyone reads the paragraph. Keep it real and specific. Don't swap it for an illustration.
- **Copy that ends on what the viewer gets.** "Someone who missed the call reads two lines and knows" and "You correct a line in one click" say who does what. Protect that when you edit.
- **Restraint.** There's one primary button, one quiet text link, and a type scale with clear jumps: headline, lead paragraph, feature headings, body text, small labels. The eye never has to choose between competing elements.
