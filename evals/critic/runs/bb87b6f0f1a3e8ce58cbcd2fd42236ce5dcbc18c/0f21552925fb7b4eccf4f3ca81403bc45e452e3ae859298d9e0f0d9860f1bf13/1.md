**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Plan the whole trip in one place", moves to the subhead, then hits two green buttons and stalls there, because they seem to say the same thing. Then it drifts right into a large empty beige area and finds nothing. It reads as a group trip-planning app's landing page: calm, tidy, and unfinished, as if the main picture never loaded.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a consumer web landing page, and its job is to get people who plan group trips to understand the product and start using it.

**Verdict.** The type and copy are solid, but the hero doesn't do its job yet. The right half and the bottom third are empty, so the page never shows what Wayfold is, and the two buttons offer the same action twice. The empty space is the biggest problem.

**What would move it most**
1. **The hero has no picture, and half the screen is empty space.** Everything sits in the left half and upper two-thirds. The right half (roughly x 720–1200) and everything below the buttons are blank beige. The page tips hard to the upper left with nothing on the right to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). The emptiness doesn't read as a planned rest area. It reads as a missing image. Ruder's point that empty space works only when it stands in tension with full space doesn't hold here, because nothing pushes back against it. The visitor gets told "flights, stays, and day plans together" and never sees it, so they have to take the claim on trust. **Defect.** It's fine only if a hero image is meant to sit there and simply failed to load in this capture. If so, check it in a live browser. If not, fill the right side with something that shows the product doing its job, such as a real trip view where a group's plans have come together. Don't use a stock travel photo.
2. **Two buttons for one action.** "Get started" (filled) and "Start now" (outlined) mean the same thing. The visitor stops to work out the difference, finds none, and starts wondering if they're missing something. The second button also weakens the main one (Nielsen heuristic 8; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*; WCAG 2.4.4, since you can't tell the links apart by their text). **Defect.** Keep one. If there's room for a second button, give it a different job, like "How it works" or "See an example trip". The page already has a "How it works" nav link that could do this.
3. **The button doesn't say what the visitor gets.** "Get started" is a generic label that could be on any page. The subhead has a sharp, specific pain point, "without a dozen chat threads", but the button doesn't follow through on it. It doesn't say what you do first (create a trip? invite your group?) and doesn't set the product against the chat thread the visitor uses today. **Defect** (copy). Name the first concrete action in the button, using a verb the product actually supports. Let the hero visual from finding 1 carry the before-and-after against the group chat. The maker needs to supply the actual action.

**Smaller things.** None.

**What's working.**
- The headline really does dominate. It's large, heavy, tightly leaded, and dark against warm beige, so the eye has exactly one place to start. Keep this scale and weight when adding the visual.
- The subhead lands the claim on the reader. "So a group can agree on a trip without a dozen chat threads" names a pain the visitor recognizes. It runs about 70 characters per line in a clear, mid-gray face, a comfortable measure.
- The palette is restrained: one deep green for the logo and actions, near-black for the headline, gray for secondary text. That keeps the actions easy to find. When you add the visual, don't let it bring in competing colors.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the right half is a hero image that didn't load. Check that in a live browser before acting on finding 1. Also the focus states of the nav and buttons, and what's below the fold.
