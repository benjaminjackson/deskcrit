**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three evenly set paragraphs and reads them in order. It reads as a calm, plain information page from a small local service, and it is honest and easy to read. The second thing you notice is that the whole right half of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers how the service runs, meaning how often it goes, what bikes cost and how to board with them, and what changes in winter. They should be able to plan a trip from it.

**Verdict.** As typesetting it's clean and readable. As a passenger page it falls short, because the facts someone would act on are vague and buried in running prose. "Between early morning and late evening", "before dusk" and "in winter" don't let anyone catch a boat. The biggest problem is the words, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times you would plan around are never given** (defect, copy). The first paragraph says the boat runs "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening". The third says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk", and that the winter timetable starts "in winter". A passenger wants to know when the first and last boats leave, and these lines can't tell them. "Before dusk" in particular can be read two ways: the boat leaves at a fixed time, or it leaves whenever it gets dark that day. If the reader guesses wrong, they miss the last boat. The page then sends them to printed timetables "kept in the shelter at each landing", so they can only find the real answer once they're already at the quay. Give the actual times: first and last departure from each side, the dates the winter timetable starts and ends, and the clock time of the last winter crossing. If the times really do change, link to the timetable or put it on the page, rather than only pointing to a noticeboard.
2. **The useful facts are written as a story, so you can't scan them** (defect; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: pages built for scanning, with short blocks). Every 40 minutes, about 12 minutes per crossing, bikes free, one boat an hour in winter: these are the four things a passenger comes for. Each is in the middle of a sentence, with the same weight as the sandbank and the crew's course. Someone looking for "can I bring my bike, and what does it cost" has to read the whole second paragraph. The fix is to pull the key facts out so the eye can find them: a short fact block or table above the prose (frequency, crossing time, hours, bikes, winter), or bold lead-ins at the start of each paragraph ("Bicycles:", "Winter:"). The prose can stay underneath as the explanation. The heading has the same issue: "Notes for…" says the page is notes, not what's in them. A heading like "Crossing times, bikes and the winter timetable" tells people they've come to the right place.

**Smaller things.**
- Balance: the text column takes about 620 of 1280 px, flush left, with nothing to its right, so the page looks unfinished and tips to the left (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). Keep the readable measure, and either centre the column in the frame or use the right side for the timetable or fact block from finding 2.

**What's working.**
- The body text is set well: a plain sans at a comfortable size, near-black on white, flush left with an even rag, and comfortable line spacing. Lines run about 70–75 characters, which is at the top of the comfortable range but still inside it. Keep this when you add structure.
- The heading and body are clearly different in both size and weight. There are two levels and no near-misses, so there's no confusion about where to start.
- Paragraphs are marked by space alone, and the space between paragraphs is clearly bigger than the space between lines. Each topic reads as its own block.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone, where most passengers standing at a landing would read this. Check that the measure and text size hold up at narrow widths.
