It reads as the top of a landing page for a small local ferry, aimed at day-trippers and locals. It feels calm, clean and a little bare. Then the eye hits the last line, "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge." That line has nothing to do with ferries, and it is wrong: nothing on this page is left-aligned.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the hero of a passenger ferry's website, meant to get a visitor to plan a crossing (open the timetable).

**Verdict.** The hierarchy is sound and the headline is good, but this isn't ready to ship. A designer's note about the page itself has been left in as body copy, and the page holds back the facts a ferry visitor came for (when the boats go and what a ticket costs), so they have to click before they learn anything they can use.

**What would move it most**
1. **The stray closing line has to go.** It sits under the button: "The strong left alignment of this page gives it a calm, clear edge." This is a **defect** (unfinished copy; there's no catalog entry, so this is common sense). A passenger reads a sentence about the page's own layout, which tells them nothing about the ferry. The sentence is also false, because every block is centred, so the page looks like a template nobody checked. That costs trust exactly where a transport service needs it. It's also the last thing on the page, so it's what the reader leaves with. Delete it. If the space below the button needs something, use a fact that helps someone travel, such as where the north landing is.

2. **The page describes the timetable instead of giving it.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" makes the visitor rebuild the useful facts. When is the first boat? When is the last? When is the next one? Is it the same at weekends? Nothing anywhere says what a crossing costs, either. This is a **defect** (common sense, not a cited rule). Those are the facts people plan around and later hold the operator to, and the page answers them with vague words, so getting anything usable takes a click. Put the real figures on the page: "First boat 6:40, last boat 22:20, every 40 minutes," plus the adult fare. Then "See the timetable" becomes a way to check details, not the only way to find out anything. The headline already works because it's concrete ("twelve calm minutes"). Give the details below it the same treatment.

**Smaller things.**
- **Everything is centred.** The headline, paragraph, list and button all sit on one centre line by default (Tschichold, *The New Typography*, on central-axis setting). For a short hero this is fine, but the three benefit lines have no bullets and no shared left edge, so they read as three loose captions rather than a list. Either align the paragraph and list to a common left edge, or set the three benefits in a row so they read as a group. This is **taste** at this length.
- **There's no picture of the bay.** The page sells a view ("watch the harbour slide past") using type alone, so it could belong to any service. One photo from the open deck would do the selling that the paragraph is trying to do. This is **taste**.

**What's working.**
- **One clear entry point.** The headline is far larger and heavier than anything else, so the eye knows where to start. Bold dark headline, regular body, small blue wordmark: three clearly different levels with no near-duplicate sizes. Keep the scale this tight.
- **The headline makes a concrete promise.** "Twelve calm minutes" names a time and a feeling, which beats the usual "Explore the bay".
- **One primary action, named exactly.** "See the timetable" says what the button does, it's the only filled colour on the page, and it's comfortably big enough to tap. Keep it the only button.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus and hover states on the button. This matters little here.
