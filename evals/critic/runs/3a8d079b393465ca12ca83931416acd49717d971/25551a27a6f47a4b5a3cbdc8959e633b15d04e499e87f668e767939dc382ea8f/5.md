**First read.** The eye lands on "Before you board", which is clearly the most important thing, then drops into a solid grey-black slab of capitals. That slab feels like a notice shouting at you rather than notes for passengers. It reads as a page of ferry rules. It is calm and well spaced until the body copy starts, and then reading becomes work.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web info page that tells first-time river-ferry passengers how the service runs before they turn up at the landing.

**Verdict.** The layout is sound, but the piece fails at its one job, which is to be read quickly. Every sentence of the body is set in capitals, so these three short paragraphs are slow and tiring. That is also where the facts a passenger needs are buried. The second problem is the copy: it gives no actual times.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body text is set in all capitals (defect, and it breaks a convention).** All three paragraphs (y ≈ 180–430) are uppercase. Capitals all have the same height and shape, so words lose the outlines the eye recognises them by, and the reader is pushed to spell letter by letter. This is a dense block of about ten 70-character lines, not a one-line label. It also reads as shouting, which is the wrong tone for "notes" meant to help. Butterick (*Practical Typography*, "All caps") says don't capitalize whole paragraphs, and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*) calls text in capitals "particularly difficult to read". Fix: set the paragraphs in upper and lower case, and keep capitals for the one-line "PASSENGER NOTES" label, which is already letterspaced and works.
2. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on (defect).** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat leaves, so the reader can't plan a trip from this page. "In winter… one boat each hour" doesn't say which months count as winter or when the hourly boats leave. The page then sends the reader to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which they can only reach after they've travelled there. Someone reading this at home can't tell whether they'll catch the last boat back, and a wrong guess strands them on the far bank. Fix: give the first and last departures (for example, "first boat 6:40, last boat 22:00"), name the winter months, and link to the timetable or put it on this page.

**Smaller things.**
- "Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded" describes what happens instead of telling the reader. "Wait until foot passengers have boarded, then wheel your bike on" fits the heading's "you" and reads as an instruction.
- "Twelve minutes in calm weather" hints at delays without saying what happens in rough weather. Say whether crossings are slowed or cancelled, and where to find out.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy at the top: a small, tracked grey label, then a large bold heading, then the body. These are three clearly different levels in one family, and the label-to-heading spacing ties them together. Keep this.
- "Before you board" names exactly what the section holds and speaks to the reader. It's the best line on the page.
- The text column is a sensible width and starts on one clean left edge at x = 120, with even spacing between paragraphs. Once the text is in mixed case, the measure and leading will read comfortably.
