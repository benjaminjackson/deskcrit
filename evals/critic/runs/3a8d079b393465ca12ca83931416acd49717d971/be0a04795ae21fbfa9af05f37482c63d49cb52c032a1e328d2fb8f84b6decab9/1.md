**First read.** The eye lands on "Bring your bike in for a service", reads down the subline and price list, then crosses to the filled button in the right-hand panel. Within seconds it reads as a local bike shop's booking page for walk-in servicing. It feels calm, clear and plain. The two versions are the same pixel for pixel except for the "Book drop-off" button: deep teal in version 1, saturated royal blue in version 2.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a repair-booking page that gets a local cyclist to pick a drop-off day and commit to it.

**Verdict.** Neither version does the job better. The button colour is the only change, and both colours make the button the clear primary action with readable white text. What holds both versions back is the same in each: the date rows don't look selectable, and the booking never asks which service you want.

**Which version and why.** Both fills reach well past 4.5:1 against the white label. Both stand out as the only strong colour on a neutral page, and both read as "press here". The blue is the more conventional action colour. The teal looks more like a choice the shop made and less like a framework default (my taste). That is a matter of brand, not of whether the page works. Neither colour carries a status meaning that would mislead, so the change makes no difference to the job.

**What would move it most** (applies to both)
1. **The date options don't look like choices, and nothing shows which one is picked** (defect). The three rows in the right panel are white boxes with pale borders. They look like filled-in text fields or static rows. They have no radio circle, no check, no chevron, and no selected state, so a visitor can't tell whether to click one first or whether pressing "Book drop-off" books Tuesday by default. Give the rows an obvious choice cue, such as a radio or a selectable-card treatment, and show the chosen state clearly (Nielsen, heuristic 1, visibility of system status). The row borders also look below 3:1 against the grey panel, and they are the only thing that marks each row as a control (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.11). Darken them, or let the choice cue do that work.
2. **The booking never asks which service** (defect). The prices on the left are a static list, and the panel on the right only asks for a day. Someone who wants the $160 rebuild books the same way as someone who wants the $25 check, and the shop finds out at the counter. The subline promises "we call you before doing any work that costs more than the quote", but the page never produces a quote. Make the service a choice in the booking, either by making the price rows selectable or by adding a service step to the panel. Then the button commits to a specific, priced job (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). This also makes the "quote" line mean something.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "…for a / service", leaving one word alone on line two and splitting "a" from its noun. Rebreak it, or widen the measure so it breaks after "in" or sits on one line (common sense).

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean: one big headline, a readable subline at a comfortable line length, and a quiet price list with right-aligned figures that are easy to scan.
- The copy is specific and concrete: "before 10 am", "the same evening", and a promise to call before extra cost. That earns trust that generic copy wouldn't.
- The two-column split puts the information on the left and the action on the right, with the one filled button as the only strong colour. Keep that restraint whichever button colour you choose.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether clicking a date row selects it, and what the button does if no day is chosen.
- Focus indicators on the rows and the button.
