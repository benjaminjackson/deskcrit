**First read.** My eye lands on the red-and-white lighthouse first, the only colour on the page, and then moves right to the bold serif headline. The two work as a pair. It reads as a small, calm page about a local lighthouse, maybe a heritage site or a tourist-board entry. It feels tidy, quiet and well set, and it is also over quickly: the page ends two-thirds of the way down with nothing to do.

**What it's for.** You didn't tell me, so I'm assuming it's a page that should get people to visit the lighthouse. If it's only a history note, finding 1 matters much less.

**Verdict.** The craft is good, but the page doesn't do its job. It tells a nice story and then stops without giving a visitor what they need to come: when it's open, what it costs, where it is. That gap is the main problem. The layout and type are not.

**What would move it most**
1. **The visitor information is buried in the prose and only half there (defect).** The two facts a visitor acts on are tucked into paragraphs 2 and 3: "open to visitors on summer weekends" and "Tickets are sold at the cottage by the car park". Neither gives a reader enough to plan a visit:
   - "Summer weekends" can be read as June to August or as the school holidays, and it gives no hours. Someone who reads it the wrong way turns up to a locked tower.
   - There is no price, no address and no way to get there.
   - The page ends on scones.

   A reader scanning for "can I go, and when?" has to read all three paragraphs and still can't answer. Pull the practical facts out of the story into a short block of their own (dates, hours, price, where to park), set apart by space or a small label under the text. Then end on one exact ask, such as "Climb the tower: open Saturdays and Sundays, 10–4, May to September". Leave the history to do what it already does well, which is make people want to go.
2. **The illustration is a stock lighthouse, so it only repeats the headline (taste).** The flat red-and-white tower on a wave says "lighthouse" and nothing more. The headline already says that. The copy has specific, visual things the picture could show instead: the estuary mouth, the ferry crossing the bay, the gallery you climb to. As it stands, the left third of the page could belong to any lighthouse anywhere. If the image showed something only Gull Point has, the pairing would earn its space and the page would stop looking like a template.

**Smaller things.**
- Paragraph 1 ends with "room." alone on its last line. That's a runt (Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.4.2). Tighten the paragraph by a word, or let the measure take a few more characters, so the last line carries at least two words.

**What's working.**
- **The pairing is aligned precisely.** The top of the illustration lines up with the headline's cap height, and its bottom lines up with the last baseline, so image and text read as one locked block. Keep that when you add the visitor block.
- **The text is comfortable to read.** Body lines run about 65–70 characters with generous leading, and the type has just two sizes (bold display serif over a regular text serif). The hierarchy is clear without any fuss.
- **Colour is restrained.** The warm red and yellow are all on the lighthouse, so the subject comes forward against the cool sky and sea, and the rest of the page stays neutral.
