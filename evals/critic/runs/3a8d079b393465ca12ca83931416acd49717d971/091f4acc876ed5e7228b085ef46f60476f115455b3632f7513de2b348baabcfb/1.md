**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the opening screen of a long-read feature on a small community or local-life magazine. Its job is to pull a casual reader into the story and keep them reading.

**Verdict.** It's readable and orderly, and the words are good. As a feature opener it is flat. The headline breaks badly, the text column runs too wide for comfortable reading, and nothing visual makes the case for a story about people and a garden. The writing is doing all the work.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline's line break.** "The allotment at the end of the bus" fills the first line and "line" sits alone on the second. This is the first thing anyone sees, and it reads as a mistake. The lone word looks like an afterthought, and splitting "bus line" weakens the phrase that carries the place. Bringhurst (2.4.2) rules out a lone short word on a paragraph's last line. That he says the same of headings is my own judgment, but the reason holds just as well here. Fix: make the headline break at a phrase boundary with a fuller second line, for example "The allotment at the end / of the bus line". Get there with a narrower headline measure, balanced wrapping, or a manual break. *Defect.*
2. **Body lines are too long for a long read.** The body paragraphs run about 85–90 characters per line across a column of roughly 720 px (e.g. "It started with a letter. Halima Osei, who has lived on the estate since 1998, wrote to"). Bringhurst gives 45–75 as comfortable and 66 as ideal. Over thousands of words, the eye tires and loses its place on the way back to each line start, and that is exactly the reader this page has to keep. Fix: narrow the column, or raise the body size a step, so lines land in the mid-60s. Count the characters on the live page to confirm. *Convention, and it costs something here.*
3. **The opening screen has no picture and no warmth.** This is a story about forty households, beans and squash, and a Saturday queue at a water tap, with photographs by the residents themselves. Yet the first screen is cool grey type on white that could open any company blog. The one green label is the only nod to the subject. A reader skimming the homepage gets no image of the place or its people to make them stop. The credited photographs aren't visible: maybe they come further down, maybe they failed to load. Either way, the opening sells the story on words alone. Fix: lead with one resident photograph, such as the queue at the tap or the bath-tub beds, at or above the headline, and let the green do a little more work. Itten, *The Art of Color*, supports matching colour mood to the subject. Placing the photograph is my own *taste*, but I'd argue for it hard: a resident's picture is the strongest asset this piece has, and it's missing from the screen that decides whether anyone scrolls.

**Smaller things.**
- The top navigation doesn't mark the current section. "Features" looks the same as "Food", "Places" and "Subscribe". Give the current item one cue, such as a bolder weight or an underline (Nielsen 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").

**What's working.**
- **The copy.** The headline is specific and makes you curious. The standfirst is concrete ("the queue for the shared water tap is the best place in the district to hear the news") and ends on a clear promise ("We spent a season with the people who built it"). The opening ("It started with a letter") pulls you straight in. Keep all of it.
- **The order of the top of the page is clear.** The small green "Feature" label, the heavy headline, the grey byline, the italic standfirst and then the upright body each look different, and each step is obvious without extra rules or boxes.
- **The text is set generously.** Body line spacing (about 1.6) and the tight headline leading suit the type. The only problem is how wide the column is.
