**First read.** My eye goes straight to the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into the body and gets stuck. The lines of each paragraph sit on top of each other: descenders hit the ascenders of the next line, so each paragraph looks like a dark smear. It reads as a plain information page for passengers on a river ferry. It feels broken, not plain.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page or notice that tells ferry passengers when the boat runs, how bikes travel, and where to find timetables.

**Verdict.** It fails its job. The body text is set with line spacing below its own type size, so the lines collide and the three paragraphs, which hold everything a passenger needs, are hard to read at all. A second problem sits behind that one: the schedule gives no actual times, so even once the text is readable a passenger can't plan a crossing from it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The body lines overlap (defect).** This happens in all three paragraphs, at about x 120–750 and y 140–360. In the crop, the g's, y's and q's of each line run into the ascenders of the line below: "forty minutes" collides with "takes about", and "on after foot passengers" with "the side of the deck". The reader has to work out each line from the jumble, keeps losing their place going back to the left edge, and skips. The line spacing looks to be about 0.8 times the roughly 19 px type, which probably means a unit was lost (a `line-height` of 15 px or 0.8 where 1.5 was meant). Bringhurst §2.2.1 (p. 37) and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 47) both say longer lines need more space between them, and these lines run 65–75 characters. Direction: set the body leading to about 1.45–1.6 of the type size (around 28–30 px here). Then check that the gap between paragraphs is still clearly bigger than the gap between lines.
2. **The timetable copy has no times in it (defect).** This sits in paragraphs 1 and 3. "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "the timetable thins… in winter" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are exactly the lines a passenger acts on, and each can be read more than one way. Does "early morning" mean 6:00 or 8:00? Which months count as winter? Is dusk 16:00 or 17:30? If a passenger guesses wrong, they miss the last boat. The text then sends them to the printed timetable at the shelter, so the page fails at the one thing someone would open it for. Direction: give the first and last departure times, the winter dates, and the winter last-crossing time, or link to the timetable itself.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Notes for ferry passengers" names a category, not what's inside. A passenger scanning for times or bike rules gets no help from it, and the three paragraphs have no subheadings. Consider short heads like "Times", "Bicycles" and "Winter service" (taste).

**What's working.**
- There's one clear entry point. The bold headline is about twice the body size and much heavier, so there's no doubt where to start.
- The measure is about right, at roughly 65–75 characters per line, set flush left with an even rag. Once the leading is fixed, these paragraphs will read comfortably, so keep the column width.
- The palette is restrained: near-black on white with a single sans-serif family. That suits a utility notice.
