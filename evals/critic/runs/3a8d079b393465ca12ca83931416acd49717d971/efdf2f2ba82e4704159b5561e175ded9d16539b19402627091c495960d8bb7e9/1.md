**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold headline, "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into a solid grey block of about 16 lines. After that it has nowhere to go: no break, no bold word, no number that stands out. It reads as a plain, honest information page for people catching a river ferry. It feels calm and tidy, but it's work to get anything out of it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that ferry passengers check, often in a hurry or at the landing, to find out when the boat runs, how to board (with or without a bike), and how to pay.

**Verdict.** The type is clean, but the page doesn't do its job. Passengers come to look things up, and everything they might need is in one unbroken paragraph, so every answer means reading it all from the top.

**What would move it most**
1. **The whole page is one block of text** (defect). The paragraph under the headline covers at least five separate topics: when it runs and how long a crossing takes, boarding order, bicycles, tickets and passes, and winter hours and where to find notices. A cyclist who wants to know where to lock the bike, or someone checking the last winter crossing, has to read from the start, because nothing marks where each topic begins. Nielsen ("How Users Read on the Web", NN/g) is the source: people skim screen text, and a long unbroken block fails them. The fix is to split it into short sections under subheadings that say what's in them ("When the ferry runs", "Boarding", "Bringing a bicycle", "Tickets and passes", "Winter service"). Turn the bicycle steps into a short numbered list, since they're a sequence: wait at the gate, wheel to the lower deck, lock to the rail, keep bags on the bike.
2. **The facts people act on are buried mid-sentence and left vague** (defect). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" are the lines a passenger plans around and will hold the operator to, but neither gives a time. Someone reading "late evening" could turn up at 22:30 and find the last boat has already gone. "Before dusk" changes through the winter, so the reader can't work out when to be there. Give actual first and last departure times, or point to the timetable with the times on it, and put them at the top where they stand out. Right now the most useful fact on the page looks no different from the note about keeping the stairs clear.
3. **The page only uses two levels of text** (taste). Right now there's a headline and body text, nothing in between. A single subheading level, in the same family at a bolder weight, would give the eye places to stop as it scans, without adding another size. This mostly comes with fixing #1, but it's worth naming so the subheadings come out as a clear level of their own and don't end up as bold body text.

**What's working.**
- The text itself is easy to read. The lines run about 60 to 70 characters, the line spacing is comfortable, the text is dark grey on white with plenty of contrast, and it's set flush left in a plain sans serif. Keep all of that when you split the content up.
- The headline is clearly the most important thing on the page because it's both bigger and heavier, and it sits close to the text it introduces.
- The copy speaks plainly and in the reader's own terms ("the crew waves them on", "the ramp on the upstream side"). Keep that voice in the new sections.
