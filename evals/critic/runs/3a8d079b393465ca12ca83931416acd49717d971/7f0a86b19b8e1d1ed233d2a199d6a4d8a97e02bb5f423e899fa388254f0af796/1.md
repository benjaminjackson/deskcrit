**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the hero of a web page for a local pool or swim school. Its main job is to get nervous adult beginners to sign up for the Tuesday class, and its second job is to mention the pool's other sessions.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job. The headline makes a clear promise and the subtitle is well written, but a viewer who is convinced has nothing to do next. The page also undercuts its own headline by giving the rest of its space to two offers that have nothing to do with learning to swim.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's no ask, so the page ends where it should be converting.** After the subtitle the viewer is persuaded and has nowhere to go: no "Book a place", no start date, no price, no venue. The eye goes headline → subtitle → cards → blank space, which is the dead end Arnheim's account of a clear eye path rules out. The page also fails Krug's test that a page should show within seconds what you can *do* on it. This is a **defect**: anyone ready to act has to hunt for a next step or leave. Fix: put one exact ask directly under the subtitle, naming the class itself. Something like "Book your first Tuesday class: 7pm, [date], £X", rather than a generic "Get in touch". For this audience, an ask that lowers the stakes ("Try one class first, no commitment") will do more than one that asks them to trust a claim.
2. **The headline promises one thing and the page then talks about two others.** "Learn to swim" is aimed at adult beginners. "Join the lanes" is for people who can already swim, and "Family hour" is for parents with children. Shown at the same level directly under the headline, they turn a page with one message into a timetable, and a nervous beginner is left wondering whether this place is really for them. This is **taste / common sense** (the one-idea rule has no approved source, so I'm arguing it on its merits): either make the lessons the whole page and move the other sessions lower down as "Also at the pool", or change the headline to describe the pool as a whole.
3. **The type below the headline has two levels where it needs three.** I count four sizes: the headline (about 96 px), the subtitle (about 22 px), the card headings (about 20 px) and the card body (about 16 px). The subtitle and the card headings are only a couple of pixels apart, so they look like the same level. That makes the subtitle as large as, or larger than, the headings of the sections below it, which is backwards. The card headings are also regular weight in near-black, so they differ from the body text only by a small step in size and a slightly darker grey. That's the weak contrast Bringhurst warns against with "don't compose without a scale" and Dair with multiplying the contrasts. This is a **convention** broken at a real cost: the cards read as one grey mass rather than two choices. Fix: drop to three sizes plus the headline, and make the card headings different by weight (semibold) rather than by another near-identical size.

**Smaller things.**
- Colour mood: the page is entirely neutral grey on white, with no water, warmth or reassurance. For an offer whose main barrier is nerves, it feels clinical and could belong to any business (taste). One calm hue, or a photo of the coach in the water, would say "this is safe and human" before a word is read.
