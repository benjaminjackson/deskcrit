**Verdict.** The headline and the first paragraph work. But the two text columns are set at two different body sizes that almost match, so the page looks unresolved. The worse problem is that this sizing pushes the one thing the reader can act on, the volunteer invitation, into the weaker column, and that invitation doesn't tell the reader how to show up.

**What would move it most**

1. **Two body sizes side by side, with lines that don't line up** (convention, and a defect in effect). The left paragraph looks about 22 px with loose leading. The right one looks about 17 px with tighter leading. Both are plain paragraph text in the same face and weight, and neither is a heading. The gap between them is too big to look like the same size and too small to look like a clear step. So the reader sees a mistake, not a hierarchy (Bringhurst, §3.1.1 "Don't compose without a scale"; Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: weak contrast is worse than none). Because the sizes and line spacing differ, the lines across the gutter never sit on the same rows (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*, "Construction of the grid"). The first line on the right also sits a few pixels higher than the first line on the left, which reads as a near-miss. **Fix:** pick one of two directions. Either set both columns at one body size on a shared baseline, or make the difference so big that the right column becomes a clearly separate kind of element, such as a labelled panel. Don't keep both columns as near-equal body text.

2. **The size order is backwards for what the page is for** (defect, if my assumption about its purpose is right). The scenery paragraph gets the big type. The paragraph that asks people to join gets the small type. A reader who skims, which is most readers, takes in the mood and misses the ask. **Fix:** decide which paragraph does the page's job and give it the weight. If recruiting volunteers is the point, the invitation should be at least as prominent as the description. Better still, make it its own clearly marked element.

3. **The invitation leaves the reader to work out how to come** (defect, from the copy). "Volunteers meet on Thursday evenings" gives no time and no address, and says nothing about whether to just turn up or get in touch first. "Anyone who wants to help" ends the page without telling the reader what to do. These are exactly the details people act on. Someone who arrives at 6 when work starts at 7, or who doesn't know whether to just turn up, probably won't try again. **Fix:** give one concrete step in the reader's terms. For example: "Come by any Thursday from 6:30 pm — the shed is at the end of Mill Lane; just walk in." Add a contact for anyone who wants to ask first. The warm details you already have (the kettle, the spare apron) do the persuading well. Keep them, and pin the logistics down next to them.
