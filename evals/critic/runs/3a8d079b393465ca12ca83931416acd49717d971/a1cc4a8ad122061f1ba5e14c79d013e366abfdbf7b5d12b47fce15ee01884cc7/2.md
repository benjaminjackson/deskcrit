**First read.** The eye lands on "How can we help?", the white serif headline on the navy band, and drops straight to the white search bar under it. Then it moves to the grid of eight equal topic cards and scans them row by row. In two seconds it reads as a software company's help centre. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also familiar: this layout could belong to almost any SaaS product.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a product's help centre, where users who are stuck either search or pick a topic and get to an answer quickly.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The hierarchy is clear, the grid is disciplined and the search box is the obvious place to start. What stops it doing better is the topic set, which mixes two audiences and two products. Beyond that, the page shows no sign of knowing why people actually come to it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The topics describe two different products (defect).** Six cards speak to someone running a software workspace: "Set up your workspace and invite your team", "Connect calendars, chat tools, and storage", sign-in and notifications. "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" speaks to a shopper waiting for a parcel. A first-time visitor can't build a picture of what Harbor is or which part of it is theirs (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). The admin wonders why returns are here, and the shopper wonders whether they're on the right site at all. "Harbor Help Center" in the header doesn't settle it either. The fix: if Harbor really does both, make the split visible, either as two labelled groups ("For your team" / "For your orders") or with a line under the headline saying who the centre serves. If it doesn't, the card is wrong and should go.
2. **The page doesn't anticipate anyone's question (taste).** Everyone gets the same eight equal cards and a search box that says "Search for articles". Most people arrive with one of a handful of problems, like a password reset, a failed payment or a sync error. Right now they have to put that problem into words or guess which category it's in. Put the likely needs in front of them: example queries in the placeholder or under the field ("Try 'reset password' or 'refund'") and a short "Popular articles" row of real titles. That is my own judgement, but on a page whose whole job is routing, it is the cheapest way to shorten the trip.

**Smaller things.**
- Card descriptions: most end on a single word on the last line ("recovery.", "receipts.", "data.", "where.", "messages.", "your team."). Across eight cards this makes a choppy, half-empty second line (Bringhurst, 2.4.2). Fix it by tightening the copy so each description fits one line or breaks into two even lines.

**What's working.**
- **The navy band and the serif headline.** The headline is the only serif and the only large type on the page, and it sits on the only dark area. One element clearly comes first, and the search box under it is the obvious next step. Keep that one-voice contrast and don't add a second serif or a second dark band.
- **A strict shared grid.** The logo, "Browse by topic" and the cards' left edge all line up at the same margin, and the nav's right edge lines up with the last card column. The gutters are even and the icons share one stroke weight. That's why the page feels calm, so keep it when you add sections.
- **Plain, task-shaped card copy.** "Track a delivery or start a return" and "Fix sync problems and error messages" name things people actually do, not internal categories.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I couldn't see the focus ring on the search field or on the cards, or whether the whole card is clickable. Check both in a live browser, since keyboard users will mostly be working through this grid.
