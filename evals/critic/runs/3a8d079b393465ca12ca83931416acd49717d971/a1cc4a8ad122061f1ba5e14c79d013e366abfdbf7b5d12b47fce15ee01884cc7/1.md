**First read.** The eye goes straight to "How can we help?" in white serif on the navy band, drops to the search field, then scans down the 4×2 grid of topic cards. It reads at once as a product's help center home. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, but it could be any company's: nothing on the screen tells you whose help center it is or what product it supports.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the landing page of a support site, where a customer who has a specific problem should reach the answer, or a person, in as few steps as possible.

**Verdict.** The page is well built and gets the basics right, but it only offers to help you look; it doesn't help you yet. The biggest problem is that the first screen holds only a search box and eight broad categories. It has no common questions, no service status and no visible way to reach a person, so every visitor has to hunt, even for the five or so problems that bring most people here.

**What would move it most**
1. **The first screen gives the visitor nothing they can act on right away** (defect; common sense, since the anticipation principle has no approved source). Someone arrives with one problem: "I can't sign in", "where's my order", "why was I charged twice". Here they have to either put the problem into search words or guess which of eight categories it belongs to. Both paths cost at least two more steps before they read an answer. There's room for one more band: either the off-white space below the grid, or a row under the search field. Use it for the five or six articles people actually open most, written as the questions they'd ask ("Reset your password", "Track my order"). Pull the "Status" nav link into a one-line live status message so an outage explains itself, and end the page with a plain fallback such as "Still stuck? Chat with us, we reply in about 2 hours". At the moment "Contact us" is a 15 px grey link in the corner, so anyone the articles don't help has to go back up and find it.
2. **The topic list doesn't say what the product is** (defect; Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). "Set up your workspace and invite your team", "Integrations" and "Connect calendars, chat tools" describe collaboration software. "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" describes a shop. A visitor who reads both will wonder whether they're in the right help center, or whether "orders" means something else in this product. The name "Harbor" doesn't settle it either. If Harbor really sells both, add a line under the headline that says so ("Help for Harbor workspaces and the Harbor store"), or group the topics under the two products. If it doesn't, the shipping card is a leftover from a template.

**Smaller things.**
- The search field relies on its placeholder. It has no magnifier icon and no submit button, so "Search for articles" disappears once typing starts and the only way to submit is Enter. Add a search icon at the left, plus either a button or a visible label (NN/g on placeholder-only fields; Norman on signifiers).
- In the Privacy and security card, "data." sits alone on the last line, and so does "where." in Notifications. Tighten the copy or adjust the card width so each sentence ends with at least two words (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- The headline "How can we help?" is the line every help center uses. It works, but it says nothing about Harbor. This is taste: a subtitle that names the product would do more than "Browse a topic or search the guides.", which only describes the page.

**What's working.**
- **Hierarchy.** It's clear at every level: one serif headline in a dark band, then a bold section label, then bold card titles over grey descriptions. Keep the serif for the headline only, because that contrast with the sans text is what makes the top of the page feel deliberate.
- **The topic grid.** Four even columns share the header's left and right edges, the icons have one stroke weight, and each title has a one-line description that names actual tasks ("Passwords, two-step sign-in, and recovery"). Those descriptions help people pick the right card, so keep them concrete.
- **Restraint.** One navy, a neutral grey range and no stray accents. The page feels calm, which is the right mood for someone who arrives frustrated.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards and nav links have hover and focus states, and whether the whole card is clickable or only its title.
- How the 4-column grid reflows on a phone, where most "where's my order" visits are likely to come from.
