I couldn't measure anything because there was no facts file, so all sizes and contrast below are judged by eye. I assumed the page's job, since you told me nothing about the piece.

**First read.** The eye lands on the serif "How can we help?" on the navy band, drops to the white search field, then spreads across the eight topic cards. It reads at once as a software company's help-centre home page. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, and also like every other help centre.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a customer with a problem arrives here and should reach the right article as fast as possible, by searching or by picking a topic.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job. The hierarchy is clean, search comes first, and the topics are easy to scan. What holds it back is that it stops at sorting. Every visitor has to either type a query or guess a category, and nothing on the page shows the answers people most often come for.

**What would move it most**
1. **No direct route to common answers.** Below the search there are only eight categories. There are no popular articles, top questions or "known issues" links. Someone who can't sign in has to pick "Account and sign-in", open another page, and scan again. That is two extra steps before they see anything useful. The large empty band under the grid (about y 750–800 and beyond) has room for it. Add a short "Popular articles" list of five or six actual article titles, or put the top two or three article links inside each card. This is my own judgement, not a published rule, but it is the cheapest way to make the page answer questions instead of only routing them. *Defect: it slows the visitor.*
2. **"Orders and shipping" blurs what Harbor is.** The other seven cards describe a workspace tool: inviting your team, integrations with calendars and chat tools, sync problems. The upper-right card says "Track a delivery or start a return". A first-time reader can't tell whether Harbor ships physical goods or whether this card came from a template. If Harbor really does both, word the card so it says what gets shipped (for example "Hardware orders: track a delivery or start a return"). If it doesn't, remove the card. *Defect, from reading the copy: it confuses the visitor about what the product is.*

**Smaller things.**
- Several card descriptions end with one word alone on the last line: "receipts.", "recovery.", "data.", "where.", "messages." (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 2.4.2). Tighten the wording or rebalance the line breaks (for example with `text-wrap: pretty`) so the last lines carry two or more words.
- Taste: the page is clean but anonymous. The navy band, the 4×2 grid of outline-icon cards and "How can we help?" could belong to any product. If the help centre should feel like Harbor's, the most likely place for that is real content, such as the popular articles in point 1, rather than decoration.

**What's working.**
- **Search comes first.** The white field on the dark band has the strongest contrast on the page and sits right under the question, so a visitor who knows what they want acts within a second. Keep that contrast and placement.
- **The topic grid is easy to scan.** Each card has a single job and the same build (icon, bold name, a gray line in plain words), and it sits on a strict four-column grid with even gutters. Card descriptions like "Passwords, two-step sign-in, and recovery" say what is inside in the customer's own terms, not internal jargon.
- **The typography plan is clear.** The serif is kept to the one display line and a single sans face does everything else, with about four distinct levels (headline, section heading, card title, body).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the whole card is clickable and has a hover or focus state (there's no arrow or chevron, so the hover cue matters). Whether search suggests results as you type. How the four-column grid reflows on a phone.
