**First read.** The eye lands on the serif "How can we help?", drops straight to the white search field, then down to the eight evenly weighted topic cards. It reads at once as a support home page. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and it also feels like a stock help-centre template: nothing on the screen tells you what Harbor is or what its users usually get stuck on.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about the piece, so I'm assuming this is the home page of a software product's help centre. It's for existing customers who have a problem and want to find the right article fast, either by searching or by browsing.

**Verdict.** It mostly does its job: the layout is clean, well ordered and easy to scan. Its weakness is that it is generic. The topics mix two different kinds of product, and the page gives the visitor no shortcut to the problems people actually come with. Every visitor has to start from scratch, either by typing a search or by guessing a category.

**What would move it most**
1. **The topics don't add up to one product.** The cards talk about a workspace, inviting your team, integrations, calendars and sync errors, which sounds like a SaaS tool. "Orders and shipping: Track a delivery or start a return" sounds like a shop. The header says only "Harbor Help Center", and the hero copy ("How can we help? / Browse a topic or search the guides") could sit on any company's site. A first-time visitor can't build a clear picture of what Harbor is or which of these areas applies to them. Someone with a delivery problem may wonder whether they're even on the right site (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2, conceptual model). *Defect.* Fix: make the topic set describe one product, or if Harbor really does both, group the cards under two labelled headings. Also say what Harbor is in the subhead, in the user's terms, instead of describing how the page works.
2. **Nothing anticipates why people came.** Above the fold there are only eight broad categories of equal weight. There are no popular articles, no suggested searches, and no notice of current issues. "Status", the one link that answers "is it broken, or is it me?", is a quiet nav item, and "Troubleshooting" is the last card. Most visitors arrive with one specific problem, so making them pick a category or think up a search term adds a step that a short list of top answers would remove (Nielsen, heuristic 7: Flexibility and efficiency of use; the rest is common sense). *Defect.* Fix: under the search field, add three to five suggested searches or a "Popular articles" row naming real tasks, such as "Reset two-step sign-in" or "Download an invoice". Show a status banner when there is an incident.

**Smaller things.**
- "Browse by topic" (looks about 18 px bold) and the card titles (looks about 16 px bold) are almost the same size and weight, so the section heading doesn't read as a level above the cards. Make it clearly larger or set it apart by colour or case (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8).
- Single-word last lines: "data." in Privacy and security and "where." in Notifications. Rewrite or rewrap those descriptions so each ends on more than one word (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).
- The search field is the page's main action, but it's a bare white box with no magnifying-glass icon and no submit button. Add the icon so it reads as search at a glance, not just as a text box (Norman, signifiers).

**What's working.**
- The hero is search-first, with one dominant headline and the field directly under it. The centred layout is right here: there is exactly one thing to do, and the eye finds it in about a second. Keep that.
- The serif display headline against an otherwise all-sans page gives the brand a voice without competing for attention. The contrast is clear and not a near-miss.
- The topic grid is disciplined: four even columns, one icon style with matching strokes, a bold title and one plain line describing what's inside. The descriptions name tasks ("Track a delivery or start a return"), not just categories. Keep that habit when you rewrite them.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** I couldn't check the focus ring on the search field and cards, the hover states that show the cards are clickable, or how the four-column grid reflows on a phone. The image stops at about 800 px, so if popular articles already sit below the fold, finding 2 becomes a question of where they're placed rather than whether they exist.
