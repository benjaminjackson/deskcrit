**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the big serif headline, "Small-batch ice cream, made on Mill Street", then drops to the three plum script headings and the hours bar. It reads as the home page of a small, local ice-cream shop: warm, handmade, a bit sweet. In version 2 the second look goes wrong. The three paragraphs are a heavy brush script, so the page stops reading like a page and starts to look like a row of chalkboard signs. Reading them takes work.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the home page of a local ice-cream shop, there to tell people what makes it different and when they can come.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The only change between them is that version 2 sets the three paragraphs in the script face, and that is a clear step back. Version 1 works. Its main weakness is that it doesn't give a visitor the two things they came for: where the shop is and what flavours they can get today.

**What changed, and why version 1 wins**
1. **Version 2 sets the paragraphs in a script face (defect, convention).** The three paragraphs under "Our milk", "Our fruit" and "Our tubs" are about 40 words each. In version 2 they are in the same bold brush script as the logo and headings. Joined, slanted letters slow every word, and this copy is the case for buying here (one dairy eleven miles away, fruit from the Saturday market, 50p off when you bring a tub back). People will skim it or skip it. Bringhurst (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1) says a text face should not carry extra ornament, so keep script and other decorative faces for a few words of display. Version 1 does exactly that.
2. **Version 2 breaks the type levels (defect).** The headings and the body text are now the same face and colour family, only about 20% apart in size. "Our milk" no longer stands clearly above its paragraph, so the three-column structure turns into three blocks of similar texture. In version 1 the change from script to sans does that job instantly.
3. **Version 2 drops the type plan (convention).** In version 1 each face has one job: serif for the headline, script for the brand and short labels, sans for reading. In version 2 the script does all three, while the hours bar stays in sans. Two faces now carry body text, so the hours bar looks like it came from another design (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*; Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8).

**Remaining findings for version 1**
1. **The page never says where the shop is or what's on today (common sense).** The headline says "Mill Street" and the bar gives the hours. But there is no street number, no map link, and no flavour of the day. Those are the two things a visitor to a scoop shop most wants. Right now they have to go through "Flavours" or "Our shop" to find them. A line in the hours bar like "12 Mill Street · today: plum & rhubarb" would make the page answer "can I go now, and what will I get?"

**Smaller things.**
- The headline is specific ("Mill Street") but the three script headings are only labels. "Our tubs" could say what the reader gets, for example "Bring your tub back, get 50p off."
- There is no current-page marker in the nav. That's minor on a home page.

**What's working.**
- In version 1 each face has one job. Script is kept to the logo and three two-word headings, so it brings warmth without costing readability.
- The palette is restrained: blush header, cream ground, plum and near-black type. It fits a small food shop, and the hours bar is set off with a tint, not a border.
- The copy is concrete and local ("eleven miles up the valley", "Closed in January while we clean the machines"). It sounds like a real shop, not a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav links.
