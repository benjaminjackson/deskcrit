**First read.** The eye lands on the brush-script "Crossing the river" and then goes straight across to the second script heading, "Good to know", and its blue button. Only after that does it come back to the body copy. It reads as a small ferry operator's page for passengers. It feels friendly and casual, but the loopy script doesn't fit the plain sans paragraphs under it. The page looks put together from three different kits.

**What it's for.** You gave me no brief, so I'm assuming this is a passenger info page for a local ferry route. Its job is to tell people when the boats run and what they can bring, and to send them to the timetable.

**Verdict.** It's tidy, but it doesn't yet do its job. The one thing a passenger comes for, when the boat leaves, is answered with "early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk". The type also uses three faces, with two of them doing body text, so the page has no single voice.

**What would move it most**
1. **The schedule copy gives no times** (defect). Under "When the boats run", the copy says "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening… the last crossing leaves before dusk." Times are what a passenger acts on and will later hold the operator to, and this copy leaves them guessing. Does "early morning" mean 6:00 or 7:30? Does "before dusk" in winter mean 15:30 or 16:30? If they guess wrong they miss the last boat home. The first departure, the last departure and the winter cut-off are each a single fact. Put the real clock times in the sentence, for example "First boat 6:40, then every 40 minutes; last boat 21:20. November–February: hourly, last boat 15:50", and keep the timetable button for the full detail. This is common sense rather than a cited rule: put the information people came for in front of them.
2. **Three typefaces with no plan, and body text set two ways** (convention plus defect). The page uses a brush script for the two big headings, a serif for the subtitle, the section headings and the card's body text, and a sans for the nav, the main body text and the button. Dair's rule is that faces should be in concord or in clear contrast. Here the serif and the sans share one job, body text: the main column is set in the sans at about 18px, and the card in the serif at about 20px. The reader sees two kinds of paragraph and wonders whether the card is a different sort of content. Gerstner's point is that elements of one kind follow one program. Set all body text in one face and one size.
   The script is a separate problem, and a matter of taste. A brush script says surf café or wedding favour. A transit page should say reliable and plain. The script also appears twice at near-headline size, so "Good to know" pulls the eye away from the H1 and across the page. Either drop the script, or keep it for the H1 alone and set "Good to know" in the same face as the section headings.
3. **Muddled type scale** (convention). I count about eight sizes: script H1 about 64px, script card heading about 44px, section headings about 28px, subtitle about 26px, logo about 22px, card body about 20px, main body about 18px, nav and button about 16px. The subtitle and the section headings are only about 2px apart. The two body sizes are about 2px apart too. Neither pair reads as two levels; they read as slips (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Cut it to one display size, one heading size, one body size and one small size. Let weight and colour separate the subtitle from the headings.

**Smaller things.**
- The "Good to know" heading names no topic. The card is about where to find printed timetables and notices, so a label like "Printed timetables and notices" would say what it holds (WCAG 2.4.6).
- The card text runs about 25–30 characters a line at the larger serif size, well under Bringhurst's 45–75. It's choppy to read. Fixing item 2 (smaller body type) helps, or the card could be a bit wider.
- "week." sits alone on the last line of the card paragraph. Rewrap it or edit the line so the last line carries more than one word.
- The card's right edge stops about 34px short of the nav's right edge ("Landings"). It reads as a near-miss rather than a choice. Align it to the nav's right edge or pull it clearly in.

**What's working.**
- The page is broken into short sections. The headings "When the boats run" and "Bicycles and luggage" name exactly what a passenger is looking for, and each sits closer to its own paragraph than to the one above. Keep that structure.
- The palette is restrained: near-black, grey and one blue. The single blue button is the only primary action, so "See the timetable" is clearly the next step.
- The bicycle paragraph is good passenger copy. It's concrete about cost, boarding order and where to lock the bike.
