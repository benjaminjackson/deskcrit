**First read.** The eye lands on "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes", drops through the paragraph and the three short lines, and stops on the blue "See the timetable" button. It reads at once as the top of a local ferry's website. It feels calm, clean and orderly, but also bare: it is all text on white, with nothing about the bay, the boat or the place. It could be a template with the ferry's words pasted in.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the first screen of a small passenger ferry's website, for locals and visitors deciding whether and when to take the boat.

**Verdict.** The page is tidy and its order of reading is right, but it doesn't yet do its job. It promises a crossing, then holds back every fact a rider needs to act on, and it sells a view without showing one. The biggest problem is that someone who wants to catch a boat learns almost nothing here except that a timetable exists.

**What would move it most**

1. **The facts a rider needs are missing (defect).** The paragraph says "every forty minutes from early morning until late evening". Nowhere does it say:
   - where the boat goes (the headline says "cross the bay", but to where?)
   - when the next boat leaves
   - what a ticket costs

   "Early morning" and "late evening" can't be planned around. Someone deciding whether to make the 5:40 has to click through to find out. "North landing" also assumes the reader already knows where that is.

   The button text "See the timetable" is a good ask: it names one exact thing. But the page should answer the commonest question before the click. Direction of the fix:
   - put the next departure, the fare and both landing names on this screen, in the space now taken by the vague hours
   - keep the button for the full timetable

   No approved source covers this. It's common sense: a transport page is judged on whether you can catch the boat.

2. **The page sells a view without showing one (taste, argued).** The copy asks the reader to "watch the harbour slide past from the open deck". The screen only has black and blue Inter type on white. That is the thing a stranger can't picture, and it's what makes this ferry different from a bus. As it stands, nothing tells you this is a particular bay, and the page could belong to any service.
   - Direction: one strong photo of the boat or the harbour, sitting with the headline.
   - The bottom third of the screen is already empty and could hold it.

**Smaller things.**
- **Type scale:** the paragraph (about 20 px) and the list of three features (about 18 px) look only a step apart, so they read as a slip, not as two levels (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1, "Don't compose without a scale"). Either set them the same size, or tell the list apart by weight or colour.
- **Headline line break:** "twelve" ends line one and "calm minutes" starts line two, which splits the number from its unit, the very promise of the headline. Break after "bay": "Cross the bay / in twelve calm minutes". This is common sense; the source for headline breaks is unconfirmed.
- **Repeated copy:** two of the three features repeat the paragraph ("Bring a bicycle" / "Bicycles travel free", "open deck" twice). Use the list for facts the paragraph doesn't give, such as the fare, journey time or accessibility.

**What's working.**
- **Clear order of reading:** one heavy headline, one body text, one saturated button. The eye never has to choose where to go next, so keep that order when you add the facts and the picture.
- **The headline:** it promises a concrete, measurable benefit (twelve minutes) and a feeling (calm) in six words. That is far better than a slogan.
- **Restraint:** one typeface, one blue, and nothing that doesn't do a job. This suits a calm crossing.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- **Below the fold:** whether the empty band at the bottom is followed by more content when you scroll. Check it in a live browser before treating the empty space as a fault.
- **Keyboard focus:** the button's keyboard focus indicator.
