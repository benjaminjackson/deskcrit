**First read.** My eye goes straight to the bold sans heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", and then into a calm serif column of four short paragraphs. It reads as an information page from a small local ferry service, and it feels quiet, plain and trustworthy. The second thing I notice is the big indent at the start of every paragraph. It catches the eye before the words do.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page that tells current or would-be ferry passengers how the service works: the schedule, bicycles, winter hours and facilities.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is good and the hierarchy is clean, so nothing here confuses the reader. The biggest weakness is the copy: a passenger opening this page wants departure times, fares and the last boat, and the text gives only "early morning", "late evening" and "before dusk". On the typesetting side, the paragraphs are marked twice, by an indent and by space, and the first paragraph is indented right under the heading.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times passengers act on are vague (defect).** Paragraph 1 says the ferry runs "between early morning and late evening", and paragraph 3 says the last winter crossing "leaves the quay before dusk". These are the lines people plan around and later hold the operator to. "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00. "Before dusk" moves by an hour across the winter. A reader who guesses wrong misses the last boat, and the page then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which means they have to be at the quay to find out. The fix is to give the first and last departure times for summer and winter, or a link to the timetable on the page itself. If fares exist, state them; at the moment the only price on the page is "no extra charge" for bicycles.
2. **Paragraphs are marked by both an indent and space, and the opening paragraph is indented (convention).** All four paragraphs start with a first-line indent that looks about three ems wide, and a blank-line gap also separates them. Either signal alone tells the reader a new paragraph has started. Using both is redundant, and the indent is wide enough to leave a hole at the left edge of every block. That breaks up the crisp left edge the heading sets up. Indenting the first paragraph is a convention break: Bringhurst (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.3.1) says a paragraph that follows a heading should start flush left. The "never both" part is common sense rather than a sourced rule: two marks for one thing is noise. Pick one. On the web, keep the space and drop the indent. If you keep the indent instead, remove the space, cut the indent to about one em, and set the first paragraph flush.

**What's working.**
- **The pairing of heading and body type.** A bold sans heading over a serif text face makes the two levels obvious through weight and form as well as size. Keep it to two levels.
- **The reading column.** Lines look to be around 70 characters, with generous leading and a soft, even right edge. The text is easy to read; don't widen the column.
- **Short paragraphs, one topic each** (schedule, bicycles, winter, facilities). A passenger can find the part they need. If the page grows, give each topic a small subhead.
