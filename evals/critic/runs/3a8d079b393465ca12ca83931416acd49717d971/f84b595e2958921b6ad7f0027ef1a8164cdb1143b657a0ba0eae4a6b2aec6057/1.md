**First read.** My eye goes straight past the page title to the dark middle card. It's the only dark area, the only serif, and the only amber on the page. Then I look across to the two white cards and they seem to come from a different site. It reads as a short list of three walks. It feels clean but unfinished, like a component from another design system was dropped into the middle.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a section of a local walking or tourism site where a visitor compares three routes and picks one to open.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well yet. The middle card breaks every pattern its neighbours set, without a word explaining why. Visitors can't tell whether Woodland loop is recommended or just styled differently, and the row stops reading as three comparable options.

**What would move it most**
1. **The middle card looks like it belongs to a different design** (defect). "Woodland loop" differs from the other two cards in almost every way:
   - Its title is a serif; the others use the sans.
   - Its button is an amber outlined pill; the others are solid blue with small corner radii.
   - Its corners are square; the others are rounded.
   - Its inner padding looks about half theirs, roughly 16 px against 34 px.
   - Its title, text and button all sit higher than the matching lines next door, so nothing lines up across the row.
   - Its serif title, at roughly 30 px, is almost the size of the page title "Walking routes", so two levels collide.

   Visitors see it as either a highlighted pick or a mistake, and nothing tells them which. Either way, the three routes no longer compare side by side. Elements of one kind should follow one set of rules, with a variant only where those rules call for it (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*), and items in one row should share edges and baselines (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*).

   Fix: decide what the dark card means. If it's just a style, bring it back into line. If it's a featured route, keep the dark fill but use the same type, padding, corners and grid as the others. Then add a label that says why, such as "Easiest walk" or "Our pick", so colour isn't the only sign (WCAG 1.4.1). Its button should also be solid, not outlined. As it stands, the featured card has the weakest-looking button on the page.

2. **The cards are about twice as tall as what's in them** (taste, argued). Each card's content ends around y 390, but the card runs to about y 600. That leaves the bottom 40% of all three boxes empty. Visitors read the empty area as missing content, like a map, photo or details that didn't load. Meanwhile the facts they'd compare are buried in prose: distance, terrain and facilities.

   Fix: either shrink the cards to fit their content, or use that space for what walkers decide on. That means a map thumbnail or photo, plus distance, rough time and difficulty as short labels in the same place on every card. Then a visitor can compare the three routes at a glance.

**Smaller things.**
- Widows in the descriptions: "end." in Cliff path and "metres." in Woodland loop sit alone on their last lines. Adjust the wording or the measure so each last line has more than one short word (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- "View route" appears three times. In context it's fine, but "See the cliff path map" would say what visitors get.

**What's working.**
- The descriptions are well written. Each one gives distance, terrain and one memorable detail (a cafe, beech trees, benches, bicycles) in a sentence a walker would actually say. Keep that voice.
- Underneath the odd card, the layout is sound. Three equal columns, even gutters, generous outer margins and a clear title make a calm frame. The fix is to make the middle card follow it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus styling on the buttons, especially whether the amber outline stays visible on focus.
- How the row reflows on a phone.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 5 yes, 3 no (every element does a job; parts read as one whole; same element looks the same)
What matters first: 3 lines, 1 yes, 2 no (one element dominates; text levels distinct)
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 10 yes, 3 no (exact alignment; baselines across columns; white space gathered)
