**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline, drops to the four illustrated cards, moves across the pictures, and stops on the green prices. The page reads as a local home-services company, maybe one serving older or busy homeowners. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and also very familiar: it looks like a lot of service-business templates. Once the eye reaches the prices it has nowhere to go. The only button is back up in the top-right corner.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the homepage hero for a local home-services business. Its job is to show a homeowner what is offered and roughly what it costs, then get them to book a visit.

**Verdict.** It's clean and competent, but it doesn't finish its job. It tells people what's offered and what it costs, then leaves them at the prices with no way to act on what they picked. The prices themselves can't be trusted yet, because none of them says what you get for the money.

**What would move it most**
1. **Picking a service leads nowhere (defect).** The headline, the subhead ("Pick a service, choose a day…") and the four cards all build up to a decision. That decision lands on "From $89" and similar lines, and none of them comes with a next step. The only way to act is "Book a visit" in the nav, about 600 px up and across from the card the visitor just chose. It also books a general visit, not the service they picked. The eye path ends at a dead end (Arnheim on a clear path from focal point to action). The control also sits far from the thing it acts on (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, on natural mapping). The subhead promises a flow of "pick, choose a day, crew arrives", and the page never starts it. Put the action where the decision happens. Give each card a clear booking action that carries the service through, such as "Book window cleaning" or a "Choose a day" link on the card. Or put one primary button right under the cards, as well as the one in the nav.

2. **The prices have no unit, so they can't be compared or held to (defect, copy).** "From $89" for window cleaning could mean per visit, per window or per house. "From $24" for laundry could be per load, per bag or per pound. "From $65" for garden care could be per visit or per month. Prices are the numbers people remember and later hold the company to. Each one can be read two ways, and the wrong reading costs either a lost lead ("$189 just for a kitchen?") or an angry customer ("$24 was per bag, not per week"). Put the unit and a reference point on each price: "From $89 per visit, up to 10 windows" or "$24 per bag, up to 15 lb".

3. **The headline makes a promise the page contradicts, and it could belong to anyone (copy).** "Help around the house, on your schedule" would fit any cleaning, handyman or home-care company, so it gives the visitor no reason to choose this one. "On your schedule" is the only specific claim, and the Laundry pickup card breaks it: "We collect on Monday and bring it back folded on Wednesday" is a fixed schedule. A careful reader will notice. Lead with what is actually different here: the vetted crew that brings its own supplies (now buried in the subhead), the service area, or how soon you can get a slot. Then either make the laundry days a choice or soften the headline's promise.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks as "on your / schedule", leaving one word on line 2 and splitting the phrase. Either widen the text box so it sets on one line, or break it as "Help around the house, / on your schedule."

**What's working.**
- The cards are disciplined. They share one grid and equal gutters. The title, description and price are in the same order in every card, and the prices line up on one baseline whatever the description length. This makes the four services quick to compare. Keep that structure when you add the actions.
- The palette is held well. A warm off-white page, one dark slate for type and the button, and coral, teal and gold kept to the illustrations. The illustrations carry the colour, and the price green and button slate stay distinct. All four illustrations share one flat style and level of detail, so they read as a set.
- The type plan is clear. A serif for the wordmark and headline and a plain sans for everything else, with three or four sizes that each have a job. The headline clearly leads.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the cards are clickable. If they are, nothing on them signals it, and Finding 1 still applies. Check in a browser whether the cards are links and how hover and focus look on them.
- The empty band below the cards is probably just where the screen ends. Check that the next section starts close enough to show the page continues.
