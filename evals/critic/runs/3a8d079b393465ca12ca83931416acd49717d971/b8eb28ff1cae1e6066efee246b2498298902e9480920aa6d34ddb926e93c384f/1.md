**First read.** The eye goes straight to the bold serif title, "Winter timetable at the harbour", then drops into two calm columns of running text. It reads as a ferry operator's information page: quiet, tidy and bookish, with a pleasant feel. Then the visitor starts looking for the timetable the title promised, and there isn't one. The bold "Fares and cards" catches the eye next, and it doesn't lead to any fares either.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a web page telling winter passengers when the island boat runs, what it costs and what to expect on board.

**Verdict.** The typography is competent, but the page doesn't do its job. Both headings promise content that isn't there, and the one thing a passenger came for, the departure times, appears only as "every ninety minutes" in the middle of a paragraph.

**What would move it most**
1. **The title promises a timetable and the page gives none.** *Defect.* Someone who arrives at "Winter timetable" expects to look up a time. What they get is three paragraphs of prose, and the only scheduling facts are buried in them: "every ninety minutes", "opens at seven", "an hour later than in summer". They can't find the first or last sailing, and they can't plan a trip. Headings must describe what their section holds (WCAG 2.4.6, Headings and labels). Krug's point applies too: if the visitor has to read every sentence to find the answer, the page has failed. Fix it one of two ways. Either add the actual departure times, set as a short table with tabular figures right under the title, which is the strongest option. Or rename the page for what it really is, something like "Travelling to the island in winter".
2. **"Fares and cards" sits over crossings, bicycles and dogs, and there is not one fare.** *Defect.* This is the only section heading on the page, so it's the second thing the eye lands on. It sends the visitor looking for prices that don't exist. The one card mentioned (the season card) is in the left column, under no heading. Anyone checking what a ticket costs will either assume they missed it or leave without an answer. Prices are exactly what people act on and later hold the operator to, so a misleading label here costs more than it would elsewhere. Fix: either give the section its fares, or relabel it for what it holds ("On board", "Crossing times and luggage").
3. **The page has only half a heading structure.** *Convention* (Gerstner, *Designing Programmes*: elements of one kind follow one program). The right column has a heading and the left doesn't, so the left column reads as an untitled introduction even though it covers office hours, sailings and the waiting room. Because the heading pushes the right column's text down, its lines also sit about 12 px off the left column's lines. Across the gutter, lines face the gaps between lines (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Fix: give each column a heading at the same level, as in "Office and sailings" / "On board". That fixes the consistency and puts the baselines back in step.

**Smaller things.**
- The two grey rules at y≈585 do no job. They start about 80 px below the left column's text and about 130 px below the right's, so they neither close the text nor divide anything. Remove them, or tie them to something that follows. (This one is common sense; the catalogue entry covers heavy borders, not stray light rules.)
- Single-word last lines in the right column: "thirty-five." and "bags." (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Rewrap or edit so the last lines carry more words.

**What's working.**
- The type is well chosen and well set. A text serif at a comfortable size, lines of about 58 to 62 characters, generous but not loose leading, and paragraphs separated by space alone. Keep all of this when you add a table.
- The hierarchy is clear: one dominant title, a bold heading one clear step down, then body text. When you add headings, keep them to this one level.
- The two-column grid with a wide gutter and a generous left margin gives the page an easy, unhurried calm that suits a small harbour.
