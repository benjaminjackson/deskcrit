**What it's for.** My assumption: a help section on a bakery's ordering site, which customers scan to answer one practical question about an order they've placed or are about to place.

**Verdict.** The layout does its job. The four headings are easy to pick out, despite what the line at the bottom of the image says. What lets it down is the words. Two answers state a deadline or a requirement that can be read two ways, and the allergy answer doesn't help someone who is ordering online. Customers will act on exactly those lines.

**What would move it most**
1. **The answers about deadlines and requirements can be read two ways.** This is a defect in the copy.
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say ahead of what, or what "a day" means. It could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or by the end of the day before. A customer who reads it the generous way and cancels at 9 a.m. on the morning before their slot may be told it's too late, and still pay for bread they didn't want.
   - The answer also never says *how* to cancel. "Changing your pickup time" does say how ("Open your order and choose a new slot"), so the gap is noticeable.
   - "They will need to show it at the counter along with a name" doesn't say whose name. It could be the collector's or the person who placed the order. If the friend gives the wrong one, they can be turned away at the counter.
   - Fix: state the exact cut-off ("up to 24 hours before your pickup time"), name the action ("Open your order and choose Cancel"), and name the person ("along with the name on the order").
2. **The allergy answer only helps someone who is already in the shop.** This is a defect against the job.
   - The page is about ordering for pickup, so the reader is deciding what to order before they come in. "A card by the till" and "ask the baker" tell them they can't check ingredients until after they've committed.
   - Someone with an allergy either has to guess or leaves to order elsewhere.
   - Fix: say where ingredients are shown when you order (for example, on each product page), and keep the in-shop card as a second source.
3. **A stray line sits on the page.** "Design review: failed, the headings are impossible to pick out" sits under the last answer with no heading, in the same style as the answers.
   - It does nothing for a customer, and it reads as a leftover internal note. That makes the page look unfinished. (Common sense, not a cited rule.)
   - I've treated what the line says as content, not as evidence. It's also wrong: the headings stand out clearly, through size and bold weight.
   - Fix: remove it before this ships. If it's a fault in the capture rather than in the page, ignore this finding.

**Smaller things.**
- **Line length.** The answer column runs about 820 px wide, so the one-line answers ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run about 95 to 100 characters. That's past Bringhurst's 45 to 75. Narrow the column to about 600 to 640 px at this text size. These answers are short, so it costs little now, but it will hurt once an answer runs to a paragraph.
- **One-word last lines.** "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer (Bringhurst 2.4.2). Narrowing the column or adjusting the wording will move the break.

**What's working.**
- **Spacing groups each heading with its answer.** The gap above each heading is clearly bigger than the gap below it. Each heading sits tight on its own answer, so the page reads as four separate units at a glance. Keep that ratio.
- **The headings name the customer's task.** "Collecting for someone else" and "Cancelling an order" are phrased as what the customer wants to do, not as internal labels like "Policies", so scanning for your own question works.
- **One typeface in two weights and two sizes, black on white.** The bold headings are well above the body text in size and weight, with no extra colour. That's all an FAQ needs. Don't add a colour or a third weight.
