**First read.** The eye lands on "Low-light houseplants", moves across the four pot drawings, and comes to rest on the row of four teal "Add to cart" buttons, which are the heaviest things on the page. It reads as a category page in a small online plant shop. It feels calm and tidy, and could be anyone's shop.

**What it's for.** You didn't say, so I'm assuming this is a shop category page meant to help someone with a dim room pick one low-light plant and buy it.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound: it's clean, on a grid, and easy to scan. But it doesn't yet help anyone choose. The pictures are one generic plant drawn four times, so the thing a shopper most needs to compare (what each plant looks like) is missing. After that, the name and price fight for second place in every card.

**What would move it most**
1. **The pictures don't show the products. (Defect.)** All four cards use the same terracotta pot with a generic leaf shape. You can't tell the snake plant from the ZZ or the cast iron plant. The pothos is drawn upright although the copy says it "trails to 6 ft". The cast iron plant's 8 in pot is drawn the same size as the 6 in ones. A plant is bought on its looks and its shape in the room, so as it stands the only real way to tell them apart is the price. No approved source covers this; it's common sense for a product grid. If these drawings are stand-ins for photos that haven't been added yet, this goes away once real photos of each plant at a consistent scale and angle are in. If the drawings are the intended art, each one has to draw its own plant: the snake plant's upright blades, the ZZ's glossy leaflets on a stem, the cast iron's broad strap leaves, the pothos spilling over the rim. The pot also needs to show the 6 in and 8 in difference.
2. **The name and the price are the same level. (Convention.)** In every card, "Snake plant" (about 18 px bold) and "$24" (about 20 px bold) are near-equal in size, weight and colour. The eye can't tell which to read first, and the card has no clear order. A difference this small looks like an accident rather than a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8; Bringhurst, §3.1.1, on working from a scale). Pick one to lead and make the difference obvious. On a page where people are choosing a plant, I'd let the name lead and set the price smaller, or keep the same size but make it regular weight. Bringhurst's point applies one level down too: the subtitle under the headline (about 15 px) and the spec lines (about 14 px) also sit a step apart without meaning anything. Use one size for both.

**Smaller things.**
- **Current page not marked.** The nav item "Houseplants" looks exactly like "Pots" and "Care guides", so nothing shows where you are. Mark it with one cue, such as bolder weight or an underline (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- **The buttons are the loudest thing on the page.** This is taste. Four solid teal blocks outweigh the plants, so the eye goes to "buy" before "which". It eases once the pictures carry real content. If it still dominates, a lighter or outlined button would let the plants lead.
- **The copy stops at size.** Also taste. The subtitle, "do well more than six feet from a window", is good: it's specific and speaks to the reader's own room. The cards don't carry that through. A short line on what each plant needs or tolerates (watering, pet safety, how hard it is to kill) would give the reader a reason to choose one over another, not just a price to compare.

**What's working.**
- **The subtitle.** "Four plants that do well more than six feet from a window" puts the category in the reader's own room, so they can judge whether it applies to them without trusting a label. Keep that kind of line.
- **The grid.** Cards, headline, logo and nav share clean left and right edges, the gutters are even, and every card stacks in the same order (picture, name, spec, price, button). Comparing across the row costs no effort.
- **The palette.** It's restrained: one neutral range, a muted leaf green, terracotta and one teal accent. The warm pots sit forward on cool grey-green tiles, so each picture reads clearly.
