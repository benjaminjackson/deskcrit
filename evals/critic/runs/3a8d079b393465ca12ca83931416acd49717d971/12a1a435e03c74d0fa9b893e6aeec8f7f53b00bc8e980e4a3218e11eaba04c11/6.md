**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, then drops to the orange bars and settles on the Saturday peak and the Sunday dip. It reads as the home screen of a small bakery's online shop, calm and tidy. The first question I had was "3.8 what?", and nothing on the page answers it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's at-a-glance overview: is the shop doing well this period, and when do the orders come in?

**Verdict.** The layout, type and chart are clean and well judged, but the three headline numbers can't be read. They have no units, no period and no comparison, so the most prominent part of the page tells the owner nothing they can act on. Fix the numbers before anything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three headline numbers have no units, so each can be read more than one way** (top row of cards). Is "3.8" a percentage or a ratio? "64.20" has no currency sign. "Active subscribers 12.4" is the worst: you can't have 12.4 subscribers, so it must be thousands or hundreds, and the viewer has to guess which. These are the numbers the owner remembers and repeats to a partner or a bank. If they're misread by a factor of 100 or 1,000, the owner makes wrong decisions with confidence. The cards also give no period. The chart caption says September 2026, but nothing says the cards cover the same month. That leaves the user to work out context from elsewhere (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall). Direction: put the unit on each value (3.8%, £64.20, 12.4k or the full count 12,400) and the period on the row ("September 2026" beside "Shop overview", or under each label). *Defect.*
2. **No number comes with a comparison** (same cards). Even once 3.8% is labelled, the owner can't tell whether it's good, bad or the same as last month. A bare figure on a dashboard can't be judged. Few names this in "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (pitfall 2). Direction: add a small line under each value giving the change from the previous period, such as "+0.4 pts vs August", or the target, in a quieter weight so the big figure still leads. *Convention.*
3. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding** ("Orders by delivery day"). The chart has a clear message: Friday and Saturday carry the week, and Sunday is about a third of Saturday. As it stands, the owner has to work that out from the bars. Stating it in a subtitle or the title ("Friday and Saturday bring 40% of orders; Sunday is the quietest day", using the real share) turns the chart into something to act on for staffing and baking. *Taste:* this is my judgment, not a rule from an approved source. It matters here because the chart takes up most of the screen.

**What's working.**
- **The type scale is tight.** I count about five sizes, each with a clear job: wordmark and nav, page title, card label, big figure, and chart text. The big figures are heavy and dark and the labels light, so the order of reading is obvious. Keep that when you add units and comparisons, and set the new lines in the label style, not as another size.
- **The chart is honest and quiet.** Bars start at zero, the days run in weekday order rather than by value, the gridlines are faint, and it uses one brand colour with no legend. It also gives its source and month. That restraint is why the Saturday peak reads instantly.
- **The grid is consistent.** The three cards and the chart panel share the same outer edges, gutters, corner radius and inner padding, so the page reads as one system.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether there's a period selector anywhere (if the cards cover a fixed month, that needs saying; if they can change, the control needs to be visible). I also couldn't check keyboard focus on the nav links.
