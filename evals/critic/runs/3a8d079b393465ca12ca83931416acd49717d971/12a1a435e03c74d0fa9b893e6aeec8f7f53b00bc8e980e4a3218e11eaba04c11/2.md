**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, then drops to the orange bars, where Saturday stands out as the peak and Sunday as the dip. It reads as a bakery's shop admin overview: tidy, calm, familiar. But on that first glance I can't tell what any of the three headline numbers means. 3.8 of what? 64.20 in which currency? 12.4 subscribers can't be right, since you can't have 0.4 of a person.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the internal overview screen where the bakery's owner or staff check at a glance how the shop is doing and when orders pile up.

**Verdict.** The layout and chart are clean and do their job. But the top row, which is the first thing anyone reads, gives three numbers with no units, no time period and nothing to compare them against. So the screen shows figures without telling the owner whether the shop is doing well. Fixing that row matters far more than anything else here.

**What would move it most**
1. **The three KPI cards have no units, no period, and no comparison.** This is a defect. "3.8" under "Checkout conversion rate" is almost certainly 3.8 %, but it could be a ratio. "64.20" has no currency. "12.4" active subscribers only makes sense as 12.4k, and someone reading it cold could take it as twelve. The time frame for all three exists only in the chart caption at the bottom ("September 2026"), and it's unclear whether that caption covers the cards too. Nothing says whether 3.8 % is up or down, or above or below target. So the owner has to supply all of that from memory, and these are the figures they'll act on and repeat to others. Few names the bare headline number as a classic dashboard pitfall ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). The fix: put the unit in the value (3.8 %, £64.20, 12.4k), the period on each card or once in the page header ("September 2026"), and a comparison under each number, such as "▲ 0.4 pts vs August" or "target 4 %". Use a small secondary line so the big numbers still lead.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title "Orders by delivery day" names the topic but leaves the finding to the reader. Something like "Friday and Saturday carry the week; Sunday is about a third of Saturday" would tell the owner what to plan for. This is common sense; no approved source covers it.
- The leftover band of empty grey under the chart card reads as unused space, not a chosen margin. It's minor at this viewport.

**What's working.**
- **The chart is honest and quiet.** It uses one colour, starts the bars at zero, has light gridlines and keeps the natural Monday-to-Sunday order. The weekly shape reads instantly. Keep all of that.
- **The source line under the chart** ("Crumbwell order system, orders delivered in September 2026") is exactly the kind of context the KPI cards lack. Carry that habit up the page.
- **The hierarchy is clear and the palette is restrained.** The page title, card labels, big values and chart title are distinct levels. The brand orange is used only on the wordmark and the bars, so the screen feels like one product.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover tooltips on the bars (exact counts per day). Whether the KPI cards link through to Orders or Subscribers.
