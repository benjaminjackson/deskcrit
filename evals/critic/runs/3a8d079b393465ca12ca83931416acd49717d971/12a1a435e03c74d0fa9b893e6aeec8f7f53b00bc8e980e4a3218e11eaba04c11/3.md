**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers first, then falls to the orange bars, where Saturday towers and Sunday drops. It reads as a calm, tidy admin overview for a small bakery that delivers. Then the numbers stop me. "3.8" what? "64.20" in which currency? And "12.4" active subscribers can't be a headcount, so is it thousands, a percent, or a rate?

**What it's for.** My assumption: the owner's at-a-glance check on how the shop is doing, read daily or weekly.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are sound, but the three headline numbers, the most prominent thing on the page, can't be read. They have no units, no period and nothing to compare them with, so the owner can't tell from them whether the shop is doing well.

**What would move it most**
1. **The KPI numbers have no units, no period and no comparison** (the three cards across the top). This is a defect. "3.8" is presumably a percent, but the viewer has to supply the %. "64.20" has no currency sign. "Active subscribers 12.4" breaks outright: subscribers come in whole numbers, so the viewer has to guess whether it means 12.4 thousand, 12.4%, or something else. There's also no period. The chart says September 2026, but nothing says whether these cards cover September, today or all time. And even with units, a bare 3.8% doesn't tell the owner whether it's good or bad. These cards are the largest type on the page and the first thing anyone reads, so the page's main message is the part that doesn't work. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") names exactly this: put the unit, the time period and a comparison next to each headline number. That means "3.8%", "£64.20" (or whatever the currency is), "12,400" or "12.4k", a period line such as "September 2026", and a small change line under each, such as "▲ 0.4 pts vs August". The bold numbers already hold their attention; they just need to say something.

2. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding** ("Orders by delivery day"). This is taste; the books that argue for it aren't on my approved list. The chart's real message is that Friday and Saturday carry the week, at about 1,050 and 1,130 orders, while Sunday collapses to about 380. As it stands, the owner has to work that out from the bars. A title such as "Weekend demand peaks Saturday; Sunday is a third of it" tells them what to plan around. You could also mark Sunday if it's a reduced-service day, because otherwise a low Sunday looks like a problem when it may be a choice.

**What's working.**
- The structure is clear: a page title, then a row of three equal cards, then one wide chart, on a single grid with gutters that match. The big bold numbers clearly dominate, so the order of reading is never in doubt. Keep that scale contrast when you add units and change lines. Set those in the small grey label size, not a new middle size.
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, there's one series in the brand orange, the weekdays run in their natural order, and the source line states what the data is and when it's from. Nothing in it needs removing.
- Restraint with colour: the orange appears only in the wordmark and the data, so it reads as the brand rather than decoration.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards update live or are fixed to September. If they're live, the mismatch with the September chart matters even more, which is another reason to label the period.
