**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers, 3.8, 64.20 and 12.4, and stops there trying to work out what they are. Then it drops to the orange bars, where Saturday is the tallest and Sunday the shortest. It reads as a small bakery's shop dashboard. It feels calm, tidy and on-brand, with a warm orange that suits a bakery. That reaction comes before any analysis: three numbers you have to decode before they tell you anything.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the owner's or staff's landing screen for checking how the online shop is doing at a glance: sales, subscriptions, and which days are busy.

**Verdict.** The layout and the chart are well made, but the most prominent part of the screen doesn't do its job. The three headline numbers have no units, no time period and nothing to compare them against, so the owner can't tell what they mean or whether they're good.

**What would move it most**

1. **The three KPI cards don't say what their numbers measure (defect).** "Checkout conversion rate 3.8" is probably a percentage. "Average order value 64.20" is in an unnamed currency. "Active subscribers 12.4" can't be read as written, since you can't have 12.4 people. It's probably thousands, but it could be hundreds or a ratio. These are the largest things on the screen, the first thing anyone reads, and each one makes the viewer guess. A wrong guess here, such as 12.4 versus 12,400 subscribers, is a wrong picture of the business. The unit is the word that was cut to keep the number short, and it's the word that held the meaning. Fix: put the unit right on the number ("3.8%", "£64.20", "12.4k" or simply "12,400"). A unit can sit smaller and in grey next to the figure so it doesn't spoil the big number. (Tufte, *Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2; Few: the reader should never have to guess what a number measures.)

2. **No KPI has a period or a comparison, so none of them shows good or bad (convention, and it costs here).** Even with units, "3.8%" alone doesn't tell the owner whether to be pleased or worried. There's no previous period, target or trend. The screen also doesn't say which period the cards cover. The chart's source line says September 2026, so the viewer can't tell whether the cards are this month, all-time, or September too. Fix: next to each number, show the period and the change since the last period, such as "This month · ▲0.4 pts vs October", or a small sparkline. (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2.) This is the change that turns the row from labels into a health check.

3. **The chart title names the topic, not what it shows (taste).** "Orders by delivery day" makes the viewer work out the finding: Friday and Saturday carry the week, at roughly 1,040 and 1,130 orders, and Sunday drops to about 380. That finding is probably why the chart is there, since it drives baking and staffing. A title such as "Friday and Saturday bring in a third of the week's orders; Sunday is the quietest" hands it over. I'm arguing this from what the owner needs to act on. I'm not citing a rule for it.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart's y-axis has no unit. "Orders" is implied by the title, but a small "orders" label at the top of the axis would remove all doubt.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear: one title, a row of KPI cards, and one chart, with a few well-separated type sizes. Labels are regular weight and grey, and values are heavy black, so you're never unsure which is which. Keep that scale when you add units and deltas.
- The chart is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are faint, the days are in their natural order, there's only one colour, and a source line gives the dataset and month. Nothing on it is decoration.
- The palette is restrained: neutrals plus one bakery orange, shared by the wordmark and the bars, so the screen feels like it belongs to this brand and not to a template.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the top navigation, and whether the chart gives exact values on hover. If it doesn't, the reader is left estimating bar heights against 300-unit gridlines.
