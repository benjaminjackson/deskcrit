**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers in the top row, starting with 184,220, then drops to the three coloured lines below. It reads as a weekly growth dashboard for a SaaS marketing team. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy. That trust lasts about three seconds: you then try to read the chart and find nothing on it to read.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a growth or marketing lead opens this to see how traffic, sign-ups and conversion moved over the last 12 weeks, and which channel drove the change.

**Verdict.** The structure is sound, but it doesn't do its job yet. The chart takes up two-thirds of the screen and says nothing you can use. On top of that, the conversion card shows a gain that the other two cards prove is really a drop.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart has no labels at all, so it tells you nothing about channels.** This is a defect. "Visits by channel" has three lines (blue, orange and green) but no legend, no names on the lines, no y-axis values and no week labels. A viewer can see that blue is highest and everything rises gently. They can't say which channel blue is, how many visits any line stands for, or which week had the dip around the middle. That was the reason to look at the chart. Without y values you also can't tell whether the axis starts at zero, so the gaps between the lines, and how steep they look, can't be trusted. Tufte (*Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2) and Few both treat axis labels and units as the minimum. Tufte and Wilke prefer names written on the data over a legend. The orange and green lines have only colour to tell them apart, which fails WCAG 1.4.1 (Use of Color). The empty band about 70 px tall under the baseline, and the blank left margin, look like space set aside for tick labels that never appeared. If they failed to render, check that in a live browser. Either way, the chart as shown can't be read. **Fix:** write the channel name at the right-hand end of each line (for example "Organic 92k"), add a few y-axis values with units, and label the weeks. Then you don't need a legend, and the empty band has a job.

2. **The conversion delta contradicts the other two cards.** This is a defect, and the worst kind on a dashboard. Visits rose 6.1% and sign-ups rose 2.4%, so the previous period was roughly 173.6k visits and 3,820 sign-ups. That's a conversion rate of about **2.20%**. The current 2.12% is therefore **down about 0.08 pts**, yet the card shows "+0.1 pts" in green. Anyone who does the sum stops trusting every number on the page. Anyone who doesn't comes away believing the funnel improved when it got worse, and that's a decision-making error. **Fix:** check the calculation or the source of the comparison figure. If the drop is real, show "−0.08 pts" in your negative colour. Show the same number of decimals as the rate itself, because rounding to "0.1" hides the size of the change.

3. **The chart title names a topic, not a finding.** This is taste: the source for this rule isn't on the approved list, so I'm arguing it on common sense. "Visits by channel" makes the viewer work out the story. Once the lines are labelled, a title like "Paid search drove most of the 6% rise in visits" tells a busy lead in one glance why this panel matters. A dashboard someone checks weekly gets the most from a line that changes with the data.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI row.** It has three equal cards, each with a plain label, one large number, and a comparison to the previous period in its own small line. That's the right structure for a glance dashboard: each number has context, and none fights the others for attention. Keep it.
- **A restrained type scale.** I count four clear sizes: the product name, "Overview", the large figures, and labels and deltas. Weight does the rest. Nothing collides.
- **A light chart.** The gridlines are faint, there's no 3D or shading, and nearly all the ink is data. When you add labels, keep them as quiet as the gridlines.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the chart's axis labels are missing from the design or just didn't render when the screenshot was taken. Check in a live browser.
- Whether "Last 12 weeks" (top right) is a date-range control. If it is, it has no shape, border or chevron to show it can be clicked. If it's only a label, it's fine.
- Hover tooltips on the chart. Tooltips can add detail, but they can't stand in for labels you can see.
