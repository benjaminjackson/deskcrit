**First read.** The eye goes straight to the three big numbers (184,220 / 3,912 / 2.12%) and reads them left to right. That part works. It then drops into the chart, which takes up two thirds of the screen, and stalls: three coloured lines climb gently, with no names, values or dates. It reads as a clean, calm SaaS analytics overview whose main chart tells you nothing.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a marketing or growth lead checks traffic, sign-ups and conversion for the last 12 weeks, and wants to see which channels are driving visits.

**Verdict.** The top half does its job. The chart, the biggest thing on the screen, doesn't: the viewer can't tell which line is which channel, how many visits any line stands for, or which week is which. As shipped, "Visits by channel" shows only that three unnamed things went up.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart has no series names, no axis values and no dates (defect).** In the "Visits by channel" panel the blue, orange and green lines have no legend and no labels. The y-axis has four grid lines but no numbers, and the x-axis has 12 points but no week labels. The viewer can't answer the question the title asks: which channel is the blue one, is it 10k a week or 100k, and when did orange peak? Tufte and Few both require every chart to say what its marks measure, with readable scale values and units. Wilke and Tufte favour labels placed at the data over a legend. The fix:
   - Put the channel name at the right end of each line (e.g. "Organic search 21.4k"). That beats a legend you have to decode by colour.
   - Add values to the y-axis (visits per week) and dates to the x-axis (week starting, every 2–4 weeks).
   - The empty band under the x-axis (about y 690–755) and the wide left gutter are where those labels belong, so this costs no extra space.
2. **Without values, the scale can't be trusted (convention).** With no tick labels, the viewer can't tell whether the y-axis starts at zero or is cropped. The gaps between the three lines could be large or tiny, and the "steady climb" could be +2% or +40%. Cairo and Tufte say a scale has to be readable and even before a chart can be read honestly. Label the ticks. If the axis doesn't start at zero, a line chart is fine (Wilke, Few), as long as the values make that plain.
3. **The series are told apart by colour alone (defect).** Once the lines have no names, hue is the only thing separating them. The orange and green are close in lightness, so a viewer with a red-green colour deficiency loses even that. WCAG 1.4.1 requires colour-coded meaning to also be shown another way. The direct labels from finding 1 fix this.
4. **The title names a topic, not a finding (taste).** This is my view, not a rule from an approved source: on a dashboard, a title like "Organic drove most of the +6.1% in visits" tells the reader what to look for, and "Visits by channel" leaves that to them. It is a smaller gain than labelling, but cheap once the labels exist.

**Smaller things.**
- The chart title's left edge (about x 71) sits a couple of pixels off the card labels above it (about x 69). Put them on one edge so it reads as intended rather than a near-miss.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards.** Each number gets one clear size, a muted label above it and a comparison line below it ("+6.1% vs previous 12 weeks"). That's exactly what a headline metric needs, and "+0.1 pts" for a rate is the precise unit. Keep this structure.
- **The figures agree with each other.** 3,912 sign-ups ÷ 184,220 visits ≈ 2.12%, so the cards back each other up and a reader who checks will trust them.
- **The restraint.** One neutral grey ground, white panels, one typeface, about four type sizes with clear steps, and light grid lines. The data, once it's labelled, will have no competition.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether "Last 12 weeks" (top right) is a date-range picker. If it is, it needs a control's look (border or chevron), because as plain text no one will know they can change it.
- Whether the chart has hover tooltips with values. Even if it does, the static labels are still needed, because a dashboard has to read at a glance.
