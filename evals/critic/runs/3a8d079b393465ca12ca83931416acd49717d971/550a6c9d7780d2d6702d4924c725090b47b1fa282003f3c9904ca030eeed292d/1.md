I recommend version 2's layout and copy with version 1's bold entry headings: version 1 does the job better.

**First read.** In both, the eye lands on the large serif "Release notes", then moves down the column. In version 1 it then stops on each bold "Version 4.x, date" line, so you see three separate releases before you read a word. In version 2 the eye goes from the title straight into a grey block of text. The version lines don't stand out, and you have to read to find where one release ends and the next begins. Both feel calm, bookish and well made, a small app's changelog done with care.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since none was given: a release-notes page for a desktop photo editor. Existing users scan it to find their version and see what changed.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. The two are the same except for one change: version 2 sets the entry headings in regular weight at about the body size. That removes the one cue that lets you scan the page by release. Version 1 is a sound page with only small things left to fix.

**What would move it most**
1. **The version headings (the one change between the two).** In version 2, "Version 4.2, 20 October" and the other two version lines use the body text's face, weight and colour, and are only a pixel or two larger. They read as the first line of each paragraph, not as labels. A reader looking for "what changed in 4.1" has to read every line instead of jumping to it. On a changelog, jumping to your version is the main thing people do. Version 1 sets the same lines in a bold serif about 4 px larger and a shade darker. That step is small, but the extra weight makes it obvious at a glance, which is exactly what Dair asks for. **Convention** (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: a contrast that is not obvious is worse than none; and ch. 8a–8g and 9: build contrast from more than size). Fix for version 2: put the weight back, as version 1 does. Keep version 1's treatment.

**Smaller things** (these apply to version 1, and equally to version 2)
- The top-right navigation draws "Release notes" exactly like "Download" and "Support", so it doesn't mark the page you are on. Give it one cue, such as bold weight or an underline (Nielsen Norman Group, Laubheimer, "Menu-Design Checklist", guideline 5). **Convention.**
- The dates ("20 October", "15 July") have no year. Once the list runs past a year, a reader can't tell how old a release is. Add the year. **Taste / common sense.**
- In the 4.0 entry, "version" and "3" break across lines ("version / 3"). A non-breaking space would keep "version 3" together. **Taste.**

**What's working** (version 1)
- **The hierarchy has clear steps.** A large bold title, bold version headings, then regular body text. That is three sizes plus weight, each with an obvious job.
- **Grouping by space.** Each heading sits right on top of its paragraph, with a much larger gap before the next release, so each entry holds together as a unit.
- **The copy.** Each note says what the user gets in plain words, often with "you": "you can still drag the line by hand", "converted the first time you open them". The text column is about 75–80 characters wide, at the upper edge of a comfortable line length.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether the navigation links are big enough to click. They look like text about 17 px tall, separated by dots. Check that each has a clickable area of 24 px or more, or enough spacing to meet WCAG 2.5.8.
