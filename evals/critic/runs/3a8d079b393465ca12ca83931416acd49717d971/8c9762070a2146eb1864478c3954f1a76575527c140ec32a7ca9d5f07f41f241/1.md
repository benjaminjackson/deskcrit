**First read.** The eye goes straight to the headline: big, black, three lines, impossible to miss. It then drops to the subhead and the dark green button, and after that drifts right to the white invoice card. It reads as invoicing software for freelancers and small studios. It feels calm, warm and trustworthy, and also familiar: cream background, a grotesque typeface, rounded cards, three feature tiles. It is the standard SaaS landing page, done neatly.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: the hero of a marketing page for an invoicing tool aimed at freelancers and small businesses, meant to get them to start the 14-day trial.

**Verdict.** The page does its job. Purpose, offer and next step are all clear in about three seconds, and the build is careful. The biggest thing in the way is the invoice card. It is the only picture on the page and it proves nothing the copy claims. Together with the templated look, that leaves the page with no moment where the viewer sees why this is better than what they use now.

**What would move it most**
1. **The invoice card shows the result but none of the promises** (taste, argued as common sense; the usual source for this point isn't on the approved list). The headline promises speed ("the time it takes to pour coffee"). The subhead promises two things: the tool fills in clients, rates and tax, and it chases late payments. The card on the right is a finished invoice for Harbor Print Co. Nothing in it shows anything filled in for you, any time saved, or any reminder going out. The picture and the headline sit side by side without adding up to one idea, so the claim rests on trust. That is the weakest kind of proof for a viewer who already sends invoices with Word, a spreadsheet or a competitor. The fix should make the card show the claim: the invoice half-built with client, rate and tax visibly auto-completed, or a small "Reminder 1 sent · Paid 2 days later" state attached to it. Then the viewer can compare it with their own process on terms they already accept. This is also the one spot that could stop the page looking like anyone's product.

2. **The ask and the claim are generic in the places that count** (copy). "Start free for 14 days" is a clear button. But it asks the viewer to commit to a trial, not to do one specific thing they would recognise. Something like "Send your first invoice free" fits the headline's promise and lets them judge the speed claim against their own experience. The three feature tiles have the same problem in a quieter way. "Reports for tax time" and "Pay by card or transfer" could sit on any invoicing site. "Reminders that sound human … written in your own words" is the only tile that says something specific to this product, and it deserves more weight than an equal third.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "coffee." sits alone on the third line. Re-break the headline or tighten its width so the last line carries more than one word ("…time it takes / to pour coffee." reads better and lands the image).
- Too many sizes below the headline. I count roughly six: subhead about 19 px, card titles about 18 px, nav and button about 15–16 px, card body about 15 px, invoice rows about 14 px. The subhead and the card titles, and the card body and the invoice rows, are only a step apart. Bringhurst: work from a short scale. Merge into about three text sizes and separate the rest with weight and colour.

**What's working.**
- The headline. It names a real job ("send an invoice") with a concrete, human measure of time, at a size and weight that owns the page. Keep it dominant.
- The subhead is specific: who does what (Tallyfold fills in clients, rates, tax; then chases payments for you). It lands on the viewer with "for you". Don't let it get trimmed.
- The craft: one grid (120 px margins, three equal tiles, the card's right edge on the margin), one green used only for the single primary action, and invoice figures right-aligned in tabular numerals that add up correctly ($685 + 8% = $739.80). That correctness quietly builds trust in a money product.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Button hover and focus states, and how the two-column hero reflows on a phone (the card should not land above the headline).

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 7 yes, 1 no (main image and headline make one idea)
What matters first: 3 lines, 2 yes, 1 no (distinct text levels with no near-equal sizes)
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 13 yes
Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (no lone short word on a last line)
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: 12 lines, 12 yes
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 33 yes
