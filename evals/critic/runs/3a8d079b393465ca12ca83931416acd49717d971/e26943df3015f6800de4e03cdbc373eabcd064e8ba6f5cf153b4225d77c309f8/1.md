**First read.** The eye lands on the headline "Update the card that pays for your subscription", reads straight down into the body text, and stops at the "When the new card is declined" subhead. This is a billing help article for a SaaS product called Ledgerly. It feels calm and trustworthy: one typeface, blue underlined links, plenty of space. There's no visual noise. You notice what's missing only once you go looking for the steps.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a help-centre article for an account owner or billing admin who came here to change the card on file and wants to get it done fast.

**Verdict.** The page is well built, but it doesn't do its main job. It's a how-to article with no "how": it explains what happens around a card change and never tells the reader where to click. The retry rules, which are the part people will hold the company to, can be read more than one way.

**What would move it most**
1. **The article never says how to update the card** (defect). The first paragraph says you can replace the card "from the billing page", and that's the only pointer to the actual task. "Billing page" isn't a link and comes with no path. There are no numbered steps, no button names and no screenshot. The reader came here to do one thing and leaves knowing the rules but not the route, so they have to go hunting in the product or contact support. Nielsen's sixth heuristic (recognition rather than recall) applies: don't make the user find from memory something you could have shown them. The fix: put a short numbered sequence near the top, such as "Go to Settings → Billing → Payment method → Replace card", with "billing page" linked straight to it. Move the paragraph about who has permission ahead of the steps, because a reader without the billing role needs to learn that before trying.
2. **The decline and retry rules are ambiguous, and this is where the money is** (defect, copy). "We try a declined card again after three days and after seven days" leaves two questions open. Three and seven days counted from what: the first decline, or seven days after the first retry? And "if the card is still declined after the second attempt" could mean the day-3 retry (if the original charge counts as attempt one) or the day-7 retry. Read it one way and the reader thinks they have a week before being downgraded; read it the other way and it's three days. Both are plausible readings. The cost of the wrong one is an unexpected move to the free plan, and the reader will hold the company to whichever version they believed. A related gap: "any invoice that is already open keeps the old card until it is paid". Many people change cards because the old one expired, and the page doesn't say what happens to an open invoice charged to a dead card. The fix: state the timeline in exact terms the reader can count, such as "We retry 3 days and 7 days after the first decline. If the day-7 retry fails, you move to the free plan." Then say what the move to the free plan costs them in their own terms (what stops working) and what happens to an open invoice on an expired card.
3. **Nothing shows where the reader is** (convention). In the left sidebar, "Update a payment card" looks exactly like its siblings, and "Billing" in the top nav isn't marked either. Someone arriving from search can't see which item they're on or tell that the sidebar is this section's table of contents. Nielsen's first heuristic covers this, and Laubheimer's "Menu-Design Checklist" (NN/g) names an unmarked current page as the most common menu mistake. The fix: mark the current item in the sidebar and in the top nav with one clear cue, such as a bolder weight, a left bar or a tint.

**Smaller things.**
- Body lines run about 82–86 characters ("You can replace the card on file at any time from the billing page. The new card is"), which is past Bringhurst's 45–75 range. There's empty space to the right, so the fix is to narrow the text column to about 66 characters rather than to shrink the type.
- The same task has three names: "Update a payment card" in the sidebar, "Payment methods" in the breadcrumb, and "Update the card that pays for your subscription" in the headline. A reader can't be sure they're all the same page. Pick one name for the sidebar and the headline (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 2).
- The help-centre header has no search box. On a help site, search is the main way readers get in, so add one to the header (Nielsen's seventh heuristic, flexibility and efficiency of use).

**What's working.**
- The structure is calm and disciplined. One typeface, body text at a comfortable size (larger than the nav), clear steps from headline to subhead to body, and space that groups each subhead with its own paragraph. Keep all of this when you add the steps.
- The links are blue and underlined, and their text says where they go ("roles and permissions", "paying by invoice", "restore your previous plan"). They work out of context and don't rely on colour alone.
- The copy speaks to the reader and calms them where it counts: "Your workspace stays open during this period" is exactly the line an anxious admin needs.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether focus indicators show on the links and nav, and whether anything sticky covers them when focused.
