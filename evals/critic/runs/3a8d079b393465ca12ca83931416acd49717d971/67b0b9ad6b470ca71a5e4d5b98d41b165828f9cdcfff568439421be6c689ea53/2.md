**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, drops to the tallest bar (Growth, 4.0%), and then moves right to the table. It reads as one page of a SaaS analytics dashboard for a growth or revenue team. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, and a little generic.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is an internal analytics page that tells a team which plan's trials convert best in Q3, so they can decide where to put effort.

**Verdict.** The craft is good, but the chart doesn't do its job yet. It presents a 0.9-point spread as a clean ranking with no sense of how much of it is noise, against no prior quarter or target. It also defines "converted" two different ways on the same screen. A reader will come away saying "Growth converts best, Enterprise is middling", and the data only partly supports that.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bars look more certain than the data is (defect).** Enterprise's 3.4% rests on 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials, which the table on the right shows. By my rough arithmetic its plausible range is about 2.3–4.4%, wide enough to cover every other bar. Starter (286 paid) is tight, at about ±0.35 points. The four bars are the same colour and solidity, each with a crisp one-decimal label, so the eye reads them as equally firm and ranks Enterprise third with confidence. This is the decision the page exists to support, and the chart invites the wrong call. Show the uncertainty (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16): add an interval or error bar to each bar, or at least flag the small sample on Enterprise.
2. **No yardstick, so nothing reads as good or bad (defect).** Every number is a bare Q3 value. There is no Q2, no same quarter last year, and no target. A reader can't tell whether 3.1% for Starter is a problem or normal. The 5.0% top gridline is the only reference on the chart, and it's arbitrary. Put a comparison next to each value, such as the change from last quarter or a target line (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte, *Visual Display*, ch. 2).
3. **The definition contradicts the subtitle (defect, copy).** The subtitle says trials "became paid accounts within 30 days". The Definition says a trial counts as converted "when the first invoice is paid" and that trials "still open on 30 October" count as not converted. Take a trial that started 1 July and paid on day 50. Under the subtitle it isn't converted; under the Definition it is. These are two different metrics, the reader can't tell which one is plotted, and anyone who recomputes it will get a different number. Pick one rule and state it once, in both places: whose 30 days, and what counts as paid.
4. **The title names the topic, not the finding (common sense; my judgment, since the only source for this rule is off the approved list).** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" makes the reader work out the story from four similar bars. Once points 1 and 2 are settled, state what holds up. For example: "Growth trials convert best (4.0%); Starter lags at 3.1%; Enterprise is too small a sample to rank." The title then carries the message the reader would otherwise have to build for themselves.

**Smaller things.**
- **Y-axis labels and gridlines:** they repeat values already printed on every bar, so this is ink that carries nothing (Tufte's data-ink ratio). Drop the axis labels, or keep one faint baseline.
- **Text sizes:** the subtitle (about 15 px) and the Definition text (about 14 px) are both plain grey or dark running text, a size apart. There are four sizes between 12 and 15 px in all. Collapse these to one body size and one small size, and use weight or colour for the rest (Bringhurst, 3.1.1).
- **Table:** it gives Trials and Paid but not the rate, so it can't be checked against the bars at a glance. "Paid" alone could mean paid accounts or revenue. Add a "Conversion" column and rename "Paid" to "Paid accounts".

**What's working.**
- The bars start at zero, the categories are in natural tier order, there's one restrained purple, and values are labelled directly on the bars. The chart doesn't exaggerate the differences, so keep that.
- The table sits next to the chart and gives the raw counts. That honesty is what makes the sample-size problem visible at all, so keep it there and build on it.
- The current tab (Conversion) is clearly marked, and the source line names the dataset and its exact dates.
