**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, then on the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%, and moves left to right along the four labelled bars. After that it goes over to the table on the right. It reads as a calm, clean page from an internal analytics product for a growth or product team. It feels trustworthy, maybe more than the numbers can back up.

**What it's for.** My assumption: one page of an internal analytics tool, built so that product, marketing or finance staff can compare how each plan's trials convert to paid and decide where to act.

**Verdict.** It is competent and tidy, and the construction is sound. Its weakness is that it presents differences the data can't support as if they were firm. It also leaves the viewer to work out what the numbers mean, with no takeaway and nothing to compare them against.

**What would move it most**
1. **The bars show differences the sample can't back up (defect).** The four bars rise and fall by tenths of a point, from 3.1% to 4.0%. The labels to one decimal place and the four distinct heights tell the viewer there is a clear ranking: Growth is best, Starter is worst, Enterprise is behind Team. The table next to the chart shows how thin the counts are. Enterprise is 38 conversions out of 1,120 trials. Rough 95% ranges by eye:
   - Enterprise: about 2.4–4.5%
   - Growth: about 3.3–4.7%
   - Team: about 3.1–4.1%
   - Starter: about 2.8–3.5%

   Only the Starter–Growth gap is clearly real. Everything else overlaps. Someone who moves budget toward Growth over Enterprise because of this chart is reacting to noise. Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16) says rates estimated from samples should show their uncertainty. **Fix:** add error bars or a shaded range on each bar, or at least an "n = 38" style note under each label. Grey out or note any gap that isn't significant.
2. **The title names the topic but says nothing about the finding, and no number has a baseline (taste + convention).** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells the viewer what the chart is. It doesn't tell them what happened. With no previous quarter, target or trend next to any bar, they can't tell whether 3.4% is good, bad or unchanged. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") flags metrics shown without a comparison. A page like this exists to show changes, and right now it shows one snapshot. **Fix:** give each plan a comparison, such as a Q2 marker on each bar or a "+0.3 pt vs Q2" line. Then rewrite the title or subtitle to state what the viewer should take away, worded within the uncertainty from point 1. For example: "Growth converts best; the other plans are within noise of each other." That title is my taste. The comparison is the convention.
3. **The definition and the source line contradict the subtitle (defect).** The subtitle says trials count if they convert "within 30 days". The Definition says trials "still open on 30 October are counted as not converted", which reads like a fixed cutoff date, not a 30-day window. This leaves two readings of the same rule:
   - A July trial that pays on day 40 counts, because it paid before 30 October.
   - It doesn't count, because it paid after day 30.

   These readings produce different rates. Separately, the source line says the data covers "1 July to 30 September 2025". Conversions up to 30 October need payment data from after that date. People will quote this number and be held to it, so the rule has to read only one way. **Fix:** make the window rule and the cutoff one sentence, for example "Each trial has 30 days to convert; we stopped counting on 30 October, when the last window closed." Make the source line cover the payment data too.

**Smaller things.** None worth listing beyond the points above.

**What's working.**
- Each bar has its value printed directly above it. The gridlines are faint, and the bars start at zero. The chart is honest and uses almost no ink that isn't data. Keep that restraint when you add error bars.
- The table of counts next to the chart is the right call. It's the only reason the sample-size problem can be spotted at all. The figures are right-aligned and line up cleanly.
- Hierarchy and space are well handled. There is one clear title, the right column sits a level below it, and the empty lower right is one deliberate open area rather than gaps spread everywhere.
