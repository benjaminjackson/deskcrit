**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Trial to paid conversion by plan", drops to the four violet bars, and goes straight to the tallest one, Growth at 4.0%. Then it moves right to the table and the definition. It reads as an internal analytics report page for a SaaS product team: calm, tidy, trustworthy. What's missing after five seconds is the answer to "so is that good?"

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard page for product and growth people. It should let them compare how each plan's trials convert to paid this quarter and decide where to act.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, honest chart, and its numbers check out against the table. All four rates recompute exactly from Trials and Paid. But it shows the reader numbers without telling them what the numbers mean. There is no earlier period or target to judge them against, no takeaway in words, and no sign that some of the gaps between bars are within noise.

**What would move it most**
1. **No comparison, so the reader can't tell good from bad.** Each bar is a bare rate for one quarter. A product lead looking at "3.1%" for Starter can't tell whether it's up, down, or below target, which is the question they came with. This is a convention: Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2. The fix is to put a comparison next to each bar: Q2 2025 as a grey bar beside each violet one, a target line, or a change label such as "+0.4 pt vs Q2" under each value.
2. **The bars invite conclusions the sample can't carry.** Enterprise's 3.4% rests on 38 conversions out of 1,120 trials. By my rough calculation, that's about ±1.1 points at 95%, so it could be anywhere from about 2.3% to 4.5%. Growth (118 of 2,950) is about ±0.7. The chart draws Growth as clearly ahead of Enterprise, but this data can't separate them. Only Growth over Starter is likely to be a real difference. The equal, solid bars and the bold value labels make all four gaps look equally firm, so someone will act on noise. This is a defect: the reader is misled about how sure the comparison is. Add error bars or a light range on each bar, or at least a note that names the Enterprise sample size (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16, on showing uncertainty).
3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" tells the reader what the axes are. It leaves them to work out the point, which they'll each do differently. This is taste, and I'd argue it hard for a report page: make the headline the conclusion, worded only as strongly as the data allows. For example: "Growth trials convert best; Starter converts least, at 3.1%". Keep the current line as the subtitle.
4. **The definition can be read two ways, and the method depends on it.** The subtitle says "became paid accounts within 30 days". The definition says "Trials still open on 30 October are counted as not converted". So a trial started on 1 July that pays on day 45 (mid-August) has two possible outcomes. Under the 30-day reading it isn't counted. Under the 30 October cutoff reading it is. If the cutoff is what's actually applied, July trials get up to three months to convert while September trials get one. That would inflate the rates and change the meaning of every bar. This is a defect, because it's the rule the numbers are held to. State one rule: "A trial counts as converted if its first invoice is paid within 30 days of the trial starting."

**Smaller things.**
- The right column ends at about y=415, so the lower-right quadrant beside the chart is empty and the page feels left-heavy. Either let the column carry the comparison or notes (Q2 figures, sample-size caveat), or narrow the page so the gap closes.

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest: bars start at zero, the ticks are even at 1%, and every bar has its value as a direct label, so there's no legend and no guessing. Keep that.
- The table beside the chart gives the raw counts, so anyone can check a rate. That's why the numbers are trustworthy. Keep the counts visible when you add a comparison.
- It uses one hue and a quiet neutral palette, with a clear three-level type scale (page title, section heads, body/labels). Nothing competes with the data.
