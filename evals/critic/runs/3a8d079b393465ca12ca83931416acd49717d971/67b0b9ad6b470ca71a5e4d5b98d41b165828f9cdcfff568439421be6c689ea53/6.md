**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title, drops into the chart and stops on the tallest bar, Growth at 4.0%, then crosses to the table. It reads as one page of an internal analytics product: calm, tidy and correct. It doesn't tell you what to take from it, so you finish with "so what?"

**What it's for.** My assumption: an internal analytics page for product, growth or finance staff, comparing how often trials on each plan become paid accounts so they can decide where to put effort.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid, and the numbers match the table exactly (286 of 9,210 is 3.1%, and so on). But the page shows four rates as if their differences were real and meaningful, without the sample sizes, ranges or comparison a reader needs to act on them. The biggest problem is that it invites a conclusion the data may not support.

**What would move it most**

1. **The bars look more certain than the data is.** This is a defect. Each bar is shown as an exact figure with no range. Enterprise's 3.4% rests on just 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. Its plausible range runs from roughly 2.3% to 4.4%, which covers every other plan. Starter, Team and Enterprise (3.1 / 3.6 / 3.4) are probably within noise of each other; only Growth against Starter looks like a real gap. A reader will rank the plans anyway and may act on that ranking. The sample sizes that would warn them are in a separate table across the page, not next to the bars (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16). **Fix:** add an interval to each bar, or at least print "n = 1,120 trials" under each plan label. Add a rate column to the table so each count and its rate can be read together.

2. **There is nothing to judge 4.0% against.** This is a convention, and it costs something here. There is no previous quarter, no target and no company-wide average, so the reader can't tell whether 3–4% is healthy, slipping or improving (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", pitfall 2). **Fix:** add the Q2 2025 rate for each plan, for example as a faint paired bar or a tick mark. Alternatively, add a reference line for the target or the all-plans average (about 3.4%).

3. **The title names the metric, not the finding.** This is taste. "Trial to paid conversion by plan" is a label, so the reader has to work out the message, and they will read the tallest bar as the message. Once points 1 and 2 are settled, say what the data does support in the title or a one-line note. For example: "Growth trials convert best; the other plans are too close to call", or "Conversion rose on every plan since Q2". That turns a chart into an answer.

**Smaller things**
- **Right column:** it ends at about y 415 and leaves a large dead block in the lower right while the chart fills the left. The page feels unfinished and leans left. Either fill the space with the comparison from point 2 (such as a Q2 vs Q3 table), or let the chart take more width.
- **Definition vs subtitle (copy):** the subtitle says "became paid within 30 days", but the definition sets the cutoff by calendar date: "still open on 30 October are counted as not converted". This can be read two ways. Either a July trial is judged at day 30, or it gets until 30 October. Under the second reading, early-quarter trials had up to three months to convert while late-quarter ones had 30 days, which skews the rates. State the single rule, for example "judged 30 days after the trial started; data complete as of 30 October".

**What's working**
- **Honest bar chart:** the axis starts at zero, there is one colour, values are labelled directly on the bars and gridlines are faint. Nothing exaggerates the differences visually. Keep this restraint when you add ranges.
- **Clear scope:** the subtitle, definition and source line together spell out what was counted, when and from where. Most dashboards skip this, and it is what lets a reader trust the numbers.
- **Restrained type:** one family with a clear scale (title, then subtitle and body, then small labels) and right-aligned, tabular figures in the table. It reads at a glance.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the bars or table rows reveal counts or intervals on hover, and whether the nav tabs keep this layout across the other sections.
