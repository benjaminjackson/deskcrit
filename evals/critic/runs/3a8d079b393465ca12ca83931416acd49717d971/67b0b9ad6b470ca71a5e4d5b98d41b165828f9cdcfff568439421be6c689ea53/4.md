**First read.** The eye lands on the bold title "Trial to paid conversion by plan", drops to the four purple bars, and stops at the tallest one, Growth at 4.0%. It then moves right to the table and the definition. It reads as a clean, calm analytics report page for a SaaS team. It is quiet and well made, and it doesn't tell you what to conclude.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a product or growth team uses this page to compare how well each plan's trials convert to paid in Q3 2025, and to decide where to act.

**Verdict.** The craft is solid and the numbers check out (286/9,210 = 3.1%, 38/1,120 = 3.4%, and so on). The page invites a conclusion its data can't support, though. The bars make Growth look like the clear winner, but the plans differ by less than a point, and Enterprise rests on only 38 conversions. The chart also gives no benchmark, so the reader can't tell whether any of these rates is good.

**What would move it most**

1. **The chart shows differences that may be noise as if they were real.** In the bar chart, Growth's 4.0% towers over Enterprise's 3.4% and Starter's 3.1%, and the bold value labels make the gaps feel exact. A reader will take away "Growth converts best." But the table on the right shows Enterprise has 38 paid accounts out of 1,120 trials. A rough 95% range on that rate is about ±1.1 points, which covers Growth's 4.0% entirely. Growth itself (118 of 2,950) is about ±0.7 points. Only the Starter-to-Growth gap is likely to be real. The page hides that, and a team could shift pricing or sales effort because of a gap that isn't there. *Defect* (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 16: show samples with ranges or sample-size notes). Fix: put a thin interval mark on each bar, or at least "n = 1,120" under each plan name. Then make the title say only what the data supports.

2. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** "Trial to paid conversion by plan" says what the chart is, so each viewer works out the message alone, and most will reach the overstated one above. *Taste* (no approved source; common sense for a report page): the one line everyone reads should carry the conclusion you stand behind. For example, "Growth and Team trials convert more often than Starter; Enterprise is too small a sample to rank." The subtitle already does its job well, so keep it.

3. **No benchmark, so the reader can't tell good from bad.** Every rate stands alone, with no Q2 figure, no target, and no trend. A reader can't tell whether 3.1% for Starter is a problem or a record. On a page whose job is to prompt action, that is the missing half of the story. *Convention* (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": put a comparison such as the change from last period or the target next to each number). Fix: add last quarter as a pale bar or tick beside each bar, or a target line, and a change column in the table.

**Smaller things.**
- The table heading "Trials by plan, Q3 2025" covers only half of what the table holds, since it also lists paid accounts. "Trials and paid accounts by plan, Q3 2025" names it fully.
- The table has no conversion-rate column, so a reader who checks the bars against the counts has to do the division. A right-aligned "Rate" column would join the two halves of the page. *Taste.*

**What's working.**
- The chart is honest and clean. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are pale and sit behind the data, the values are labelled on the bars, and the single purple adds no fake categories. Keep this restraint when you add intervals or a benchmark.
- The subtitle, definition, and source line are well-written copy. "Share of trials started in Q3 2025 that became paid accounts within 30 days", together with the 30 October cutoff, tells a stranger exactly what is being counted. Many dashboards skip this.
- The hierarchy is clear: one dominant title, the chart, then the secondary column. The open space in the lower right gives the eye somewhere to rest.
