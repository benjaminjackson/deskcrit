**First read.** The eye goes first to the big blue headline, then to the "Create your link" button, then across the four outlined cards. It reads as a scheduling tool for small service businesses. It feels clean, competent and very familiar. After the headline, everything is the same blue, so the eye has no second place to settle and drifts across the cards. The right half of the hero is empty, so you never see the product.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the hero of a marketing landing page, meant to get owners of small appointment-based businesses (dental, tutoring, physio, vets) to make a booking link.

**Verdict.** It works and it's tidy, but it's forgettable. You're told what the product does but never shown it. The blue is spread over almost everything, so the main action has to compete with about twenty other blue things.

**What would move it most**
1. **The product never appears, and the right half of the hero is empty.** The headline and subhead take the left ~640px. To their right, above the cards, is a blank block as big as the headline area. The visual weight all sits on the left, so the hero looks unfinished rather than calm (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). It also wastes the strongest argument on the page. "See a live example" is the best ask here because it lets visitors judge the product for themselves, but it's hidden behind a small underlined link. Put the live example in that space: a real booking page with open slots, ideally one of the listed customers', like Harbor Dental. Then the visitor can compare it with how their own clients book today, and the headline and the picture make one point together. *Defect* (balance), plus a *taste* point on the missing image.

2. **The accent blue is on almost everything, so nothing stands out by color.** It's on the headline, the nav links, both buttons, every card's 2px border, icon, title and badge, the four "Learn more" links, and the rule above the customer names. "Create your link" is the one thing this page needs clicked, but its color doesn't set it apart; only its fill does. Keep the full-strength blue for the main action, the links and at most one highlight. Make the card borders light gray or remove them, and make the card titles and icons dark neutral (Apple HIG, *Color*; Material Design 3, color roles; Wathan and Schoger, *Refactoring UI*). *Convention*, and here it costs you emphasis.

3. **The headline could be anyone's.** "Booking links that fill your calendar" could be the headline of any scheduling product. The subhead describes the mechanism ("share one link, let people pick a time") but doesn't say what changes for the reader. "Keep every meeting in the right place" doesn't say what the right place is. Your best copy is in the cards: "cut down on no-shows," "so nobody has to do the math." For a clinic or tutor, no-shows and back-and-forth phone calls are the real pain. Lead with that, in their terms, using "you," and say what's different about Slotwise. *Taste*, but it's what decides whether a visitor stays.

**Smaller things**
- Card 1, "Shared calendars": the "New" badge pushes the title onto two lines. Its description and "Learn more" then sit about 30px lower than the other three cards, so the rows don't line up across the cards. Shorten the title, or put the badges above the titles so they never compete for width.
- The badges mix two kinds of information in one pill style. "New" and "Beta" are status; "Auto" and "Email" describe features and repeat what the card copy already says. Drop "Auto" and "Email" and keep the status pills.
- The headline breaks "fill your / calendar", leaving one word alone on the last line and splitting the phrase. Rebreak it so the last line carries more than one word (e.g. "Booking links that / fill your calendar"). Similarly, the subhead splits "every / meeting".
- The customer-name strip has no label, and in small gray text it reads as footer links. Give it a short label (e.g. "Used by clinics, tutors and vets") or show the actual logos, so it works as proof.

**What's working**
- The headline is clearly the dominant element: large, bold and set tight. There are four or five clearly different type sizes, each with an obvious job, and the card text is well sized and comfortable to read.
- The grid is solid. The hero, the cards and the rule all share the same left and right edges (64px to 1216px), with equal gutters between the cards.
- The card copy is concrete and lands on the reader: "Send a note the day before and an hour before to cut down on no-shows." Use this voice in the hero.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Hover and focus states on the two primary buttons and the four "Learn more" links.
- How the four cards reflow on narrower screens.

**Checklist**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 5 yes, 3 no (headline and image make one idea; every element does a job; each kind of element looks the same everywhere)
- What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 10 yes, 3 no (baselines align across columns; only related things look alike; visual weight balances)
- Text: 30 lines, 29 yes, 1 no (headings break at phrase boundaries)
- Color: 9 lines, 8 yes, 1 no (strong color kept to a small share)
- Pictures: 12 lines, 12 yes
- Charts and data: skipped, no chart
- Interface: 33 lines, 33 yes
