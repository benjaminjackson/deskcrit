**First read.** The eye lands on the big blue headline, then drops straight to the four cards with heavy blue borders. Those cards pull harder than the "Create your link" button between them. It reads as a scheduling SaaS homepage for small service businesses. It feels tidy, competent and generic: almost everything is the same blue, and the right half of the hero is empty white.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a marketing homepage that gets small practices (dentists, tutors, physios, coaches, vets, judging by the customer row) to sign up and make their first booking link.

**Verdict.** It's clean and readable, but it doesn't sell. One blue is used on nearly everything, so the main action doesn't stand out, and the hero has no picture of the product and no line that sets it apart from every other booking tool. A visitor learns the category but not why this one.

**What would move it most**
1. **The brand blue is on everything, so the button stops being the thing to look at.** The blue appears on the headline, the four nav links, both filled buttons, all four card borders, the icons, the titles, the badges and the "Learn more" links. Since everything is blue, being blue means nothing. The cards hold the most blue on the page, so they out-pull "Create your link", and the eye goes past the ask to the features. It also groups unrelated things: the headline looks like the same kind of object as the links. Fix direction (approved wording): keep the blue for the main action and at most one key highlight, and draw the headings, icons, borders and other elements in neutral colours. Concretely: dark headline, grey or no card borders, neutral card titles. Apple HIG and Material Design 3 both say to save the accent for action and interactive state. *Convention, costing a defect.*
2. **The hero has no picture of the product, and its right half is dead space.** About 570 px of empty white sits to the right of the headline (x≈660–1216, y≈100–380). The page is weighted to the left with nothing on the right to balance it (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). It also wastes the one spot where you could show the product. For a booking tool, the product is easy to show: the booking page a guest sees, with slots in their own time zone. Put that there, ideally as the page a dentist's patient would actually see. Then the headline and the picture make the case together instead of the headline working alone. *Defect (the page is lopsided, and the viewer has to imagine the product); the specific picture is taste.*
3. **The headline could be on anyone's site.** "Booking links that fill your calendar" describes the whole category, and it promises something a link can't do: links don't create demand. The customer row shows a real niche (small clinics and practices), but no line says so. The viewer can't tell what is different here, so nothing pushes them to act over the tool they already know. Fix direction: say who it's for and what changes for them, e.g. something about patients or clients booking themselves and showing up. The secondary ask "See a live example" is the strongest line on the page because it lets the viewer judge for themselves. Make it point at a real customer's page ("See Harbor Dental's booking page") so the comparison is on terms they already trust. *Taste, argued: copy is part of the design.*

**Smaller things.**
- Feature badges: "New" and "Beta" are statuses, but "Auto" and "Email" are descriptions styled exactly like them. The viewer can't tell what a badge means. Keep badges for status only, or drop them.
- First card: the "New" badge squeezes "Shared calendars" onto two lines. That card's text and link then sit about 30 px lower than in the other three cards, so the rows don't line up across them (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*). Put the badge above the title or let the title run full width.
- Four "Learn more" links give no clue where each one goes, and read as noise when skimmed (WCAG 2.4.4). Name the destination, or make each card the link.
- Customer row (y≈770): grey names with no label and no logos. It reads as a footer or placeholder text, not as proof. Label it ("Used by 2,000 practices like…"), or show real logos with a number.
- Card borders: a 2 px saturated outline round each card adds weight but nothing else. Spacing or a light tint would hold the group.
- The nav "Start free" and the hero "Create your link" are the same action with two names. Use one name so the viewer knows they're the same step.

**What's working.**
- The headline is clearly dominant: big, bold and tightly set, with an obvious place to start reading.
- The card body copy is concrete and lands on the reader: "so nobody has to do the math", "to cut down on no-shows", "so the first minutes are useful". Each line ends on the benefit, said to the person. Keep that voice and bring it up into the hero.
- The main ask is filled and the secondary one is a plain link, so their order is clear. "See a live example" is the right kind of second ask.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards are fully clickable or only their links; hover and focus states on the cards and links.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 5 yes, 3 no (visual and headline together; every element does a job; same element looks the same)
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 9 yes, 4 no (exact alignment; baselines across columns; no accidental groups; balance)
Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
Color: 9 lines, 8 yes, 1 no (strong colour kept to a small share)
Pictures: 12 lines, 12 yes
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 32 yes, 1 no (link purpose from its text)
