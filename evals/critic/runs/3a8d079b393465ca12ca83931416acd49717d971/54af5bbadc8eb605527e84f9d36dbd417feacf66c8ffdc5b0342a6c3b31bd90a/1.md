**First read.** The eye lands on the bold sans heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into a single column of four serif paragraphs on white, with the right 40% and bottom third left empty. It reads as a quiet, plain information page for a local river ferry. It's calm and well set, and it feels like a leaflet that has been typed in rather than designed around what a passenger needs.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about it, so I'm assuming it's a web page that lets someone who's about to take the ferry find out when it runs, how long it takes, and what they can bring.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is sound, but the page doesn't do its job. A passenger can't plan a crossing from it because every time is vague and buried mid-paragraph. The biggest problem is the copy, not the layout.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts a passenger acts on are stated in ways no one can act on (defect).** Paragraph 1 says the ferry runs "between early morning and late evening" and the crossing takes "a little longer when the tide turns". Paragraph 3 says the winter last crossing "leaves the quay before dusk", and "In winter" names no months. Departure times, last boats and seasons are exactly what a passenger remembers and holds the operator to. "Before dusk" in December could mean 15:30 or 16:15, and someone who reads it the later way misses the last boat and is stranded on the wrong bank. Give clock times for the first and last boats in each season, the date the winter timetable starts, and an actual figure for the tidal delay ("up to 20 minutes").
2. **The timetable is somewhere else (defect).** Paragraph 3 sends people to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing", which means you have to be standing at the river to learn when the boat leaves. A page like this exists to save that trip. Put the timetable here, as a short table of departures by season. That would also give the page its natural focal point: the information people came for, ahead of the prose.
3. **Four topics, no signposts (convention: Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web").** The paragraphs cover four separate things: schedule, bicycles, winter service and the landing stages. They're short, but nothing labels them, so someone looking for "can I bring my bike?" or "when's the last boat?" has to read all of them. Put the schedule first and give the other topics short subheads that name what's in them ("Bicycles", "Winter service", "At the landings"). The heading and body faces already contrast well enough to carry a second heading level.

**Smaller things.**
- Fares are missing. The page says bicycles ride "at no extra charge" but never says what the base fare is or how to pay, so a passenger is left wondering. Add one line on fare and payment.
- "Notes for ferry passengers" names the audience, not the content. A heading like "Riding the river ferry: times, bikes and landings" tells a stranger they're in the right place (taste).

**What's working.**
- The type. A serif body at roughly 68 characters a line with comfortable leading, under a bold sans heading that differs in form and weight as well as size. It reads easily and makes a clean two-level hierarchy. Keep it when you add subheads and a table.
- The restraint. There's one neutral colour, nothing decorative, and the empty space is gathered into one open area rather than scattered. That suits a public-service notice. Add structure, not ornament.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the column reflows cleanly on a phone, which is where most passengers will read this.
