**First read.** The eye lands on "The Wexley Gazette", drops past the double rule, and then moves left to right across three bold headlines that all carry the same weight. Nothing tells you which story matters. Next you notice the gappy justified columns, and then the blank lower half of the page. It reads as a small-town paper's front page: quiet, trustworthy, old-fashioned, and unfinished.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the front page of a local news site or paper, where a resident checks quickly what is happening in town.

**Verdict.** It's tidy and readable, but it doesn't work as a front page yet. No story leads, and the stories fill only the top half of the frame, so it looks like three wire items on a blank page, not an edition. The biggest problem is the missing lead story.

**What would move it most**
1. **No lead story (defect).** "Bridge to reopen in spring", "Market moves indoors" and "School wins garden prize" share one size, one weight and one column width. The reader gets no signal about which matters most, so they scan all three and nothing reads as news. This is a basic front-page job and the page doesn't do it. Tschichold asks that the order of importance be shown by differences of size (*The New Typography*). Fix: pick one lead, probably the bridge, since it affects the most people. Give it a clearly larger headline and a wider measure, for example two columns, or a full-width top slot with the other two below it. Then set the other two back.
2. **Top-heavy page with a dead lower half (defect).** All the text sits between y≈40 and y≈415 of an 800 px frame. Below that is nothing. That doesn't read as chosen white space. It reads as a page that ran out of content or failed to load. Arnheim's point on balance applies: a heavy region needs a counterweight. Fix: once there is a lead story, its larger setting will use more of the depth. Then fill the rest with something a reader needs, such as more stories, briefs or a "What's on" box. Or, if three stories is truly the whole edition, shorten the frame so the page ends where the content does.
3. **Justified text in narrow columns opens holes (convention).** At roughly 40–45 characters a line, justification without hyphenation stretches word spaces badly: "months of repairs to its arches." and "Engineers found that two of the five" in column 1, "the old corn exchange from next" and "many shoppers away. The hall has" in column 2, "a regional prize for the vegetable" in column 3. The grey of the text breaks up, and the eye stumbles across the gaps. Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, "The line", p. 34) says that under about 50 characters, even word spacing becomes difficult, if not impossible. Bringhurst warns of "white acne" in short justified lines. The measure itself is fine for multi-column text, since Bringhurst gives 40–50. Fix: set the columns flush left, ragged right, or turn hyphenation on if you keep justification.

**Smaller things.**
- There's no date or issue line under the masthead (common sense). A news front page that doesn't say when it's from leaves the reader unsure whether "next month" and "in April" are current. Add a dateline under or beside the masthead.

**What's working.**
- The type plan is disciplined. One serif family is used throughout: a large bold masthead, bold headlines and a regular text weight. That gives three clear levels with no near-duplicate sizes. Keep it as you add a lead headline size.
- The double rule under the masthead is a fitting newspaper detail. It cleanly separates the title from the content, so keep it.
- The copy is good local reporting. Each opening sentence says who does what and when ("will reopen to cars in April, after eighteen months of repairs"). The bridge story heads off an obvious reader question by noting that cyclists and people on foot can cross from March.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the blank lower half is the design or a capture that cut off before more content loaded. Check the live page. If more content exists, finding 2 shrinks to a question of where the fold falls.
