**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. It starts at the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time", and reads down as eight lines of near-identical text. The headings look like slightly smaller body text, so for a moment they read as captions or stray lines, not headings. It reads as the help or FAQ section of a small bakery's ordering site. It's calm and plain, but flat, like a draft that hasn't been styled yet.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an FAQ for a bakery's pre-order customers, who come to it with one specific question about pickup, collection, allergies or cancelling and want that answer fast.

**Verdict.** The copy is mostly clear and the spacing groups it correctly. But the typography gives the scanning reader nothing to scan by. Headings, body and the page itself all carry the same weight, and there's no title. The second problem is the cancellation line: it's the one place the customer is held to a deadline, and it can be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings don't read as headings (defect).** Each question ("Changing your pickup time", "Allergies and ingredients" and so on) looks about 18 px against body text of about 19 px, in the same regular weight and the same near-black. So a heading is a hair *smaller* than the text it introduces. Someone hunting for "can I cancel?" can't run their eye down the headings. They have to read every line, and the four topics blur into one block. A difference of a pixel or two in size only, with nothing else changing, looks like an accident, not a level (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8). Fix: make the headings differ obviously from the body. Use a semibold or bold weight at the body size or one clear step up, so weight does the work rather than size (Dair, ch. 8a–8g and 9; Refactoring UI, "bolder, not bigger"). Count the sizes afterwards: you want two clearly different levels, not two that nearly match.
2. **No page title, so nothing is seen first (defect).** Four headings and four paragraphs sit at equal strength, and nothing says what the page is: "Your order: questions", "Pickup and orders" or similar. A customer who arrives from a link or a search result can't confirm at a glance that they're in the right place. Fix: add one title clearly above everything else, so the order of importance is plain (Tschichold, *The New Typography*). This also gives the headings something to sit under, which makes their level easier to read.
3. **The deadlines and the steps leave gaps the customer has to fill (defect, copy).**
   - "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" can mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time up to the day before (for example until midnight, or until closing). A customer who reads it the generous way and cancels at 9 pm the evening before may be refused. Give the exact cut-off: "You can cancel until [time] the day before your pickup."
   - The section also never says *how* to cancel.
   - "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" stops at the fact. Say what it means for the customer: what happens after two hours, and whether they still pay.
   - In "show it at the counter along with a name", whose name is meant: yours or theirs? Say "your name". This one decides whether a friend leaves with the bread.
   - "Open your order" doesn't say where. Point to it ("in your confirmation email" or "under My orders"), or make it a link.

**Smaller things.**
- **Line length:** the single-line paragraphs ("Give the person your order number…", "Orders can be cancelled…") run about 90–95 characters, well over the 45–75 range (Bringhurst 2.1.2). Narrow the text column to about 600–640 px.
- **Runt:** "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies answer. A narrower column will change the breaks; check that no one-word last line remains (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- **The spacing between groups:** about 10 px from heading to answer against about 35 px between topics. Each question clearly belongs to its answer. Keep that ratio when you restyle the headings.
- **The headings name what each section holds**, in the customer's own words ("Collecting for someone else"), not system terms. "After that the bread has already been baked for you" explains the rule kindly and in a human voice. Keep that tone.
- **One plain sans-serif, dark on white, all flush left:** readable, with nothing extra to strip out.
