**First read.** The eye has nowhere to land. Eight lines of grey-black text in one weight start at the top-left and run down. Only the gaps tell you there are four groups. You read the first line, "Changing your pickup time", and only then work out that it was a heading. It reads as help text for collecting bread orders from a small bakery. It feels calm and plain, but more like a draft than a finished page.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a help or FAQ section where bakery customers find quick answers about collecting, changing, or cancelling an order, mostly by scanning for their question.

**Verdict.** The copy is mostly good and the spacing is sound, but the page doesn't do its main job. The headings look the same as the answers, so you can't scan for your question. One cancellation line can also be read two ways, and it's the line people will hold the bakery to.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings can't be told from the answers** (defect). "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" are set in the same face, weight and colour as the paragraphs below them. If anything they look a pixel or two *smaller* than the body text. On a help page the headings are the only thing people read before choosing where to stop. Here they have to read every line to find their question, and the page looks like one long block cut at random. Dair, *Design with Type* (ch. 8): when two things are meant to differ, the difference has to be obvious at a glance, and a near-match looks like a mistake. Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show the order of importance through differences of size. Fix: make the headings clearly different from the text. A semibold or bold weight at the same size or slightly larger is usually enough here (Refactoring UI: bolder, not bigger). You could also set the answers in a slightly lighter grey. Don't make the headings one size up in the same weight.
2. **"Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" has two readings** (defect, copy). It could mean 24 hours before the pickup slot, or any time before the day of pickup. Someone who picks the second reading and tries to cancel the night before an 8 am pickup will be refused and feel misled. It's also passive, so it doesn't say who cancels or how. Fix: name the cutoff exactly and say how to do it, for example: "You can cancel in your order until 6 pm the day before pickup. After that, your bread is already in the oven." The second sentence then also says what the cutoff means for the customer.
3. **There's no title saying what this page is** (defect, if this is the whole page). The page opens straight on the first question. Someone who lands here from a link can't tell at once whether this is the bakery's help page, their order page, or something else (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*: a page should explain itself). If a page title sits above this crop, ignore this point. If not, add one, such as "Collecting your order", in a clearly larger size so it becomes where the eye starts.

**Smaller things**
- Line length: the paragraphs run about 85–95 characters a line ("Give the person your order number… along with a name."), above Bringhurst's 45–75. With only two lines per answer the cost is small, but limit the column to roughly 600–680 px at this size.
- "Collecting for someone else": "along with a name" leaves it unclear whose name is needed, theirs or yours. Say which: "They'll need to show it at the counter and give your name."
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergies paragraph (Bringhurst 2.4.2). It will go away once the column is narrower, or you can trim the sentence: "Ask the baker if you're unsure."

**What's working**
- The spacing groups the content correctly. Each heading sits about 10 px above its answer and about 26 px below the previous one, so the four groups hold together even without weight contrast. Keep these spacings when you change the heading weight.
- Most of the copy is plain and speaks to the reader: "Open your order and choose a new slot from the list", "Ask the baker". The line about the bread already being baked for you gives a reason for the cancellation rule, not just the rule itself. Keep that tone.
- The text contrast is high (near-black on white), and the body size is comfortable.

**Couldn't judge from a still**
- Whether "Open your order" points to a real link or button elsewhere on the page. If it can be linked from here, it should be.
