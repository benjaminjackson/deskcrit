**First read.** There's nowhere for the eye to land. It falls on the top-left line, "Changing your pickup time", and then reads down the column like one long paragraph, because the headings and the answers look alike. After a few seconds it reads as a help or FAQ panel for a bakery's order-ahead service. It feels calm, plain and unfinished, like a draft in a text editor rather than a designed page.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since I wasn't told: a help section where a customer who has ordered bread online finds the one answer they need (change, hand off, check allergens, cancel) and acts on it.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job well. The four questions aren't set apart from their answers, so the reader can't scan for their question and has to read everything. Behind that, the two lines people will hold the bakery to, about cancelling and about the hold time, can each be read two ways.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headings don't read as headings.** "Changing your pickup time", "Collecting for someone else", "Allergies and ingredients" and "Cancelling an order" look the same size as the answers below them, or a touch smaller (around 18 px against 19 px). They share the same weight and the same near-black colour. The eye sees eight lines of equal rank, so someone who came only to cancel has to read the first three answers to find theirs. Not seeing which way the sizes go is a defect in itself: sizes this close look like a mistake, not a level (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 3.1.1). Tschichold asks that the order of importance be shown by differences of size. The fix: make the headings clearly the first level. Change more than size: pair a modest size step with a heavier weight, or keep the size and change the weight and colour of the answers (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8–9). The page also has no title, so nothing says what this is or whose it is ("Your order: questions" or the bakery's name). Add one above the four questions as the single dominant element (Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). **Defect.**
2. **The commitment lines can be read two ways.** "Orders can be cancelled up to a day ahead" doesn't say a day ahead of what. It could mean the day before collection or 24 hours before the slot. A customer with a 9 am pickup who cancels at 8 pm the night before could believe either. Under one reading they get a refund and under the other a dispute. It also never says *how* to cancel, which every other answer does. "The bakery holds orders for two hours after the chosen time" leaves out what happens after that: whether the bread is lost and whether they're still charged. "Along with a name" doesn't say whose name, the collector's or the buyer's, which is exactly what someone collecting for a friend needs to know. Fix: state the cutoff exactly ("until 6 pm the day before your pickup"), name the action ("Open your order and tap Cancel"), and finish each claim with what it means for the reader ("after two hours we can't keep it, and it isn't refunded" or whatever the policy is). **Defect.**
3. **The allergy answer points to the wrong place.** "Every loaf has a card by the till" helps someone already standing in the shop. Someone ordering ahead needs to know before they pay. For people with an allergy, this is the answer that matters most. Fix: point to ingredients on each product page, or say plainly that they're only shown in the shop and how to ask before ordering. **Defect, common sense.**

**Smaller things.**
- Line length: "Give the person your order number…" and "Orders can be cancelled…" run about 95–100 characters, above the 45–75 range (Bringhurst, 2.1.2). Narrow the column to about 640 px.
- "anything." sits alone on the last line of the allergy answer. A narrower measure or a small rewrite will fix it (Bringhurst, 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- The spacing groups things correctly. The gap between a heading and its answer (about 10 px) is clearly smaller than the gap between questions (about 25 px), so each pair already holds together. Keep that ratio when you change the heading styles.
- The answers are short and mostly start with something to do ("Open your order…", "Give the person…"). That's the right voice for help text.
- One plain sans serif face, dark text on white, one left edge. Nothing competes with the words.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether these items expand or link anywhere, and whether "Open your order" points to a real link on the live page.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 7 yes, 1 no (says in one sentence what it's for)
What matters first: 3 lines, 0 yes, 3 no (one element dominates; distinct text levels; contrast beyond size)
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 13 yes
Text: 30 lines, 28 yes, 2 no (line length; widows)
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes

Charts and data: skipped, none
