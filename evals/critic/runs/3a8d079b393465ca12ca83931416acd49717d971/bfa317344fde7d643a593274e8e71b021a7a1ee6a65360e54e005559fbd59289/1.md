**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline, drops to the big blue 38% slice, then has to jump right to the legend to learn what blue means. The second thing you notice in the pie is the grey 25% "Other" slice at top left, which is bigger than every named category except salaries. It reads as a tidy, quiet annual-report page. It feels competent and calm, and it doesn't say much.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is one page of an annual report. It shows shareholders, staff or the board how operating costs split in fiscal 2026, so they can see where the money goes and whether that's changing.

**Verdict.** It's clean and well made, but it shows a breakdown without telling the reader anything about it. There's no total, no comparison and no stated finding, and a quarter of costs sit in an unexplained "Other". The biggest problem is that a reader leaves knowing the proportions but not whether they are good, bad, new or expected.

**What would move it most**
1. **There's nothing to judge the shares against.** The page gives percentages only. There's no total operating cost in currency and no fiscal 2025 figures. A board member can't tell whether 38% on salaries or 12% on cloud hosting is up, down or normal, or whether 9% on marketing is a lot of money. For an annual report, that comparison is what the reader came for. **Defect.** Few calls a bare figure with no comparison a dashboard pitfall ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"). **Fix:** put total operating costs and the period next to the chart, and show last year's share beside each category, at least in the legend rows. The change is the story.
2. **The title names a topic, not a finding.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" and the subtitle both describe the chart. Neither says what it shows. The reader has to work out the point alone, and different readers will take away different points. **Taste**, argued on its merits: a report page earns its place by saying something. **Fix:** make the headline the finding, such as "Salaries took 38% of operating costs; cloud hosting grew to 12%", whatever is actually true. Keep the current line as the subtitle.
3. **"Other" at 25% weakens the chart.** It is the second-largest slice, and the reader can't learn anything about it. On a cost breakdown, an unexplained quarter reads as either sloppy or evasive. **Defect** (copy and content): it hides the information the page exists to give. **Fix:** split "Other" into its two or three largest parts until no catch-all is bigger than the smallest named category. If that can't be done, add a note that says what "Other" contains.
4. **Names and values are separated, and only colour links them.** The percentages sit on the slices, but the category names sit in a legend about 300 px away. Each slice means a look across and a colour match. Blue and purple, and teal and grey, are the pairs most at risk for a colour-blind reader. It still works because each percentage appears once and can be matched by number, but that's a workaround, not the design. **Convention** (Tufte and Wilke: label at the data; WCAG 1.4.1, use of colour). **Fix:** put the names on or beside the slices and drop the legend. You could also turn the legend's table into a sorted horizontal bar chart. It already has the rows, the order and the right-aligned figures, and bars would make 16, 12 and 9 easy to compare.

**Smaller things.**
- **Slice labels on orange and teal.** White bold "12%" on orange looks like about 2.5:1, and "16%" on teal about 3.3:1. At roughly 17 px bold these count as normal text, which needs 4.5:1 (WCAG 1.4.3). Measure them, then use dark labels on those two slices or darken the fills.

**What's working.**
- The type plan: a serif headline and subtitle over a plain sans for the data and furniture. The levels are distinct, and the page reads as a report rather than a dashboard.
- The legend table: values right-aligned, rows ordered by size with "Other" last, and fine row dividers. Keep that structure whatever replaces the pie.
- The slices are ordered the same way, largest first, running clockwise from 12 o'clock with "Other" last, so the pie and the table match. The pie itself is a fair choice for five parts of a whole.
