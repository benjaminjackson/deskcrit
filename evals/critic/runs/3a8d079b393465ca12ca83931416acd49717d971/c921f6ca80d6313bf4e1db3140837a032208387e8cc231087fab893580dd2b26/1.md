**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the big black serif headline, drops to the standfirst about the boat being for sale, and then to the byline. It reads as the top of a long-form magazine feature: quiet, literary and confident. In version 2 the first impression is snagged before reading starts. The headline looks like one dark block, and the eye catches on letters running into each other.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the opening screen of a long-read feature on an editorial site. It has to draw a browsing reader into a slow, human story.

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job and version 2 does not. The pages are identical except for the headline's line spacing. In version 2 it is so tight that the lines collide, which damages the one element the page relies on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The headline's lines collide in version 2 (defect).** The descenders of "people" and "keeping" touch the "l", "h" and "t" of "lighthouse lit". The "g"s of "forgotten" and "lighthouse" touch the "F", "S" and "k" of "Fair Skerry". The reader has to separate the letters before reading them, and the headline turns from three lines into a dark smudge. On a page whose whole case is careful craft, it looks like a CSS slip. Bringhurst (p. 37) allows a display line to be set tighter than its type size, but only "so long as the ascenders and descenders don't collide." Version 2 crosses that line. Fix: open the line spacing until nothing touches. Version 1's setting, about 1.1, already does this. It still reads as one tight unit, so keep it.

**Smaller things** (these apply to version 1 as well)
- The headline ends "lit on / Fair Skerry", which leaves "on" dangling at the end of line 2. That is taste: a break before "on" would keep the place name with its preposition. It isn't a defect, because the break still falls at a phrase boundary.

**What's working** (keep these in version 1)
- The type scale is clean, with four clear levels: a headline of about 64 px, a standfirst of about 21 px, and small sans for the kicker, nav and byline. Pairing a serif for reading with a sans for metadata separates the two roles at a glance.
- The standfirst does real work. "Forty years", "rowed out each week" and "Now the last boat is for sale" give a concrete stake and a turn in two sentences, and the measure is about 75 characters.
- There is one accent: a red "Long read" kicker that labels the format without competing with the headline. Everything hangs on a single left edge at x=120.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** The large empty area on the right and lower half is probably where the lead photograph or body text sits below. Check in a live browser that the hero image loads and is not missing.
