It reads as the contact page of a small design studio, written for prospective clients. **What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is the studio's contact page, and its job is to turn an interested visitor into a lead with enough detail to quote on.

**Verdict.** It's a clean, readable form that works. The biggest thing in the way is that the form doesn't ask for what the page says the studio needs. The intro asks for a budget and a timeline, then hands the visitor one blank Message box. Most will skip those details, and the first reply becomes a round of questions.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form ignores its own instructions.** This is a defect. The left column says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form on the right has only a bare, unprompted Message box, about 430 px away from that sentence. To include them, the visitor has to remember an instruction from another column while typing into an empty field (Nielsen, heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall; heuristic 10, help sits near the step it concerns). In practice most people write "Hi, we need a website" and the studio spends its two-day reply asking for the basics. The fix is to put the request where the typing happens. The usual way is two short optional fields, Budget (a select with a few ranges, including "Not sure yet") and Timeline (a select or short text). At the least, add hint text under Message that names both. Then the intro paragraph can drop its second sentence.
2. **The words could be on any studio's page.** This is taste. "Tell us about your project" and "Send message" are what every agency contact form says. A visitor comparing three studios learns nothing here about why to pick this one, or what happens after they click. The one specific, valuable line is the promise, "reply within two working days", and it's set as grey body text. Two changes would help. First, make the ask say what the visitor gets: a button like "Send — we'll reply within 2 working days", or a headline that names the outcome (a call, a quote, a first idea). Second, put one piece of proof from the visitor's own world next to the form, such as a recent client or project link, so they judge the studio against work they can see rather than taking it on trust.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Contact" looks exactly like Work, Services and About, so the current page isn't marked. Give the current item one cue, such as bold, no underline plus a bar, or the text colour (Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Form fields: none is marked required or optional. If Message is optional, or you add Budget and Timeline as optional, say so after those labels; otherwise asterisk the required ones and explain the asterisk once (Budiu, NN/g "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- Input borders: the mid-grey edges on white look close to 3:1, maybe under it. Measure them, and darken to at least 3:1 if needed (WCAG 1.4.11).
- Grid: the card's right edge (about x 1068) lines up with nothing. The nav and page margin end at about 1224, which leaves a 212 px dead strip on the right and an arbitrary 56 / 400 / 560 split. Either run the form to the 1224 margin, or set both columns on a stated grid so the right side looks chosen rather than left over (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clear: a heavy two-line headline, plain body text, small bold field labels and one solid blue primary button. You can tell each level at a glance, and there are only about four sizes. Keep this scale.
- The labels sit above the fields, closer to their own field than to the one above, and there's one action. The form is quick to read and hard to get wrong.
- "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a concrete promise the visitor can hold you to. Keep it, and make it more visible.
