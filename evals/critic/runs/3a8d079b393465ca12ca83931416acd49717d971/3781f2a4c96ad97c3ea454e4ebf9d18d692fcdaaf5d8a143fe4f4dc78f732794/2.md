**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Tell us about your project", then crosses to the white form card and drops to the blue "Send message" button. It reads at once as a small studio's contact page. It's calm and orderly and quick to grasp, and it's also anonymous: nothing on it tells you what Fernwick Studio makes or why you'd pick them over another studio.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a design studio's contact page, meant to get a prospective client to send a project inquiry the studio can act on.

**Verdict.** It does its job. The path from headline to form to button is clear and the form is honest and short. The main weakness is that the page asks for information it gives the visitor no place to put. Second, it looks so generic that it adds nothing to the visitor's reason to write in.

**What would move it most**
1. **The intro asks for budget and timeline, but the form has no place for them.** The paragraph on the left says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form on the right offers only Name, Email and a short Message box. The visitor has to remember the instruction and type it into free text, and most won't. The studio then gets inquiries without the two facts it said it needs, and has to spend its first reply asking for them. Put the ask into the form where the visitor acts on it: an optional budget-range picker and an optional timeline field, or at least placeholder or helper text inside the Message box that names both. This is a defect (common sense; no approved source states it).
2. **Nothing on the page is specific to this studio.** The system font, the grey page with a white card, blue underlined links and "Tell us about your project" could belong to any agency, SaaS or freelancer. For a design studio, the contact page is a piece of its own work. A visitor deciding whether to write in sees no sign of the studio's taste, and the generic copy gives them no reason to act now. You don't need decoration to fix this. Give it one thing only this studio would say: a line naming the kind of projects you take on, or one recognisable type choice or colour. This is taste.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Contact" looks the same as Work, Services and About, so nothing marks the current page. Give the current item one cue, such as a bolder weight or a bar under it (Nielsen 1; Laubheimer, NN/g "Menu-Design Checklist").
- Form labels: no field is marked required or optional, so the visitor can't tell whether they can skip their name. Add an asterisk to required labels with a one-line legend, or "(optional)" after optional ones (Budiu, NN/g "Marking Required Fields in Forms").
- Input borders: the grey field outlines on white look about 3:1 at best. Check the border colour against #fff, and darken it if it falls under 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11).
- Headline: "Tell us about your / project" leaves one word on the second line and splits the noun phrase. Rebreak it as "Tell us about / your project", or widen the column so it fits on one line. This is taste.
- Grid: the form card ends at about x=1068 and lines up with nothing, while the nav runs to about x=1224. That leaves a dead strip on the right and suggests the card was sized by eye. Take the card's right edge from the nav's right margin, or from a column that recurs on the page (Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*).

**What's working.**
- The headline-to-button path. One heading, one card and one filled button, with nothing competing. Keep exactly one filled primary action.
- The intro copy makes a concrete promise ("reply within two working days") instead of "we'll be in touch". That's the line that earns the message, so keep it word for word.
- Labels sit above each field, closer to their own input than to the one above, in a single column. It's the right structure; don't swap them for placeholder-only fields when restyling.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus indicators on the fields and the button, and whether the email field uses an email input type and validates when the visitor leaves it. Check in a browser by tabbing through and typing a bad address.
