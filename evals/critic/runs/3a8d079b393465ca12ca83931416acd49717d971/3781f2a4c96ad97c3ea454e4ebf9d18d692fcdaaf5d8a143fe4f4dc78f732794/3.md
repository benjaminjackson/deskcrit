**First read.** The eye lands on "Tell us about your project", the only large dark type, then moves right into the white card and down the three fields to the blue "Send message" button. Within two seconds it reads as the contact page of a small agency. It feels calm, tidy and competent, and also anonymous: apart from the name in the corner, nothing on the screen says this studio and not some other one.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the contact/enquiry page of a design studio. It should get a prospective client to send a brief the studio can act on, and give them some sense of the studio while they do it.

**Verdict.** As a form it works. Anyone can tell what to do and do it. As a studio's contact page it is weak. It asks for a budget and a timeline but gives them nowhere to go. It looks like a default template, which is a poor showing for a company that sells design. Those two points are the gap between a working form and one that brings in good briefs.

**What would move it most**
1. **The form doesn't collect what the intro asks for.** The paragraph on the left says "Include a budget range and a rough timeline if you have them." The form on the right has only Name, Email and a short, empty Message box. The visitor has to remember that request from another column while facing a blank box, and many won't, so the studio gets briefs with no budget and has to email back to ask. This is a defect: Nielsen's heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall, says context should sit where the user acts. Put the request where people answer it. Either add two optional fields (a budget select with a few ranges and a timeline field) or put a hint under the Message label ("What you need, your budget range, and when"). The Message box is also only about five lines tall, which is small for "tell us about your project". Make it taller so it invites a real brief.
2. **It looks like anyone's page, on the one site where that costs most.** The system-blue button, gray background, white card with a thin border, default sans and underlined blue links are the stock look of a framework starter. A prospective client judges a design studio partly on its own site. This page tells them nothing about the studio's taste, so it gives them no reason to pick this studio over the next tab. This is taste, and I'm arguing it for this audience only: on a bank's support form, neutral would be right. Give the page at least one deliberate choice of the studio's own, such as the type, a brand colour for the action, or one image or line of work beside the headline. The fix is not more decoration. The copy is the stronger part and can carry some of this: "We read every message and reply within two working days" is a concrete promise. Keep it, and put it close enough to the button that it reassures people at the moment they commit.
3. **The layout doesn't sit on the page's own grid.** The header and footer run from x≈56 to x≈1224. The content stops at the card's right edge, x≈1068, which lines up with nothing. That leaves a 156 px dead strip on the right, plus a large empty band below the card. Together they make the block look dropped in rather than set. This is a convention: Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems*, says every block takes its edges from one set of columns. Either run the two columns to the full 56–1224 measure, or set a narrower content width and use it for the header too.

**Smaller things.**
- Nav, top right: "Contact" looks exactly like Work, Services and About, so nothing shows which page you're on. Mark the current page with one cue, such as a bolder weight or a bar.
- Form labels: no field is marked required or optional. If all three are required, say so once. If you add budget and timeline, put "(optional)" after those labels.
- Input borders: the pale gray outlines on white look below 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure them, and darken them if they fall short so the fields still read as fields.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean. One large bold headline, a gray intro, small bold labels and one solid primary button. Each of the four levels has an obvious job, and there is a single path from headline to button.
- The labels sit above the fields, close to their own field and well clear of the next one, in a single column. Keep that through any redesign.
- The intro copy is specific and talks to the visitor: a reply time they can hold the studio to, and exactly what to include. Copy that does real work is the hard part, and it's already done.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Focus states on the fields and button, and whether a focus ring is visible on the light card. Error and success states after "Send message". How the two columns stack on a phone.

**Checklist.**
The whole piece: 8 lines, 8 yes
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 11 yes, 2 no (one grid governs the page; divisions follow one proportion)
Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: skipped, no images
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 27 yes, 4 no (input borders 3:1; shows current page; nothing to recall from elsewhere; required fields marked), 2 unsure (focus indicator; focus not obscured)
