**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops straight into three even paragraphs, and there it stalls. Nothing in the body stands out, so to find out when the boat runs you have to read every sentence. It reads as a calm, tidy information page from a small local ferry. It feels pleasant and unhurried, which is the wrong pace for someone checking whether they'll make the last boat.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page for river-ferry passengers, meant to tell them quickly when boats run and what the rules are, including the bicycle rules.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and competent, but the page doesn't do its main job. The facts a passenger acts on are vague, and they sit in the middle of sentences, so nobody can plan a trip from this page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable gives no actual times, and what it does give is hidden in prose (defect).** Paragraphs 1 and 3 say "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "about twelve minutes… a little longer when the tide turns", and in winter "one boat each hour" with the last "before dusk". A passenger plans around those numbers and will hold the service to them. Yet each phrase can be read more than one way:
   - "Early morning" could mean 6:00 or 8:00.
   - "Before dusk" could mean 15:30 or 16:45 in December.
   - "Winter" has no dates, so you can't tell whether next Tuesday counts.

   Read it the wrong way and you miss the last boat, which is what this page exists to prevent. The page then points you to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", so the screen sends people to paper to learn what it should have told them. The fix:
   - Give the first and last departure from each landing as clock times, for summer and for winter, with the dates winter starts and ends.
   - Put them in a small table or list, or at least in their own block above the prose, so the eye finds them before the narrative.

   The copy then only needs to cover the exceptions (tide, weekly changes).
2. **The page has no way to scan it, so every visit means a full read (taste, though with a clear cost).** Each paragraph covers one topic: the service, bicycles, winter. Nothing on the page names those topics, and there is no second level below the headline. A rider who only wants the bike rule has to read the timetable first. To fix this, give each paragraph a short subhead that names what it holds ("Times", "Bringing a bicycle", "Winter service"), or start each paragraph with its key fact in bold. The paragraphs are already short and already one topic each, so this is a small change with a big payoff.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "Notes for ferry passengers" names the audience, not what the page gives them. Something like "Ferry times and bicycle rules" tells a visitor in one look that they're in the right place.
- Paragraph 2: "The crew asks that bags stay on the bicycle" is a rule dressed up as a request. If it's a requirement, say so plainly, and say why if there's a reason.
- Paragraph 3: "lists any changes for the coming week" sends people to a physical noticeboard. If changes are posted anywhere online, link to them here.

**What's working.**
- The type is well set and worth keeping. One family, a bold headline at roughly twice the body size, and dark text on white make a clear, distinct two-level hierarchy. The measure looks like about 65 to 72 characters with comfortable leading, and the ragged-right edge is even. It reads easily.
- Each paragraph holds one topic. That makes adding subheads trivial, because the content is already chunked correctly.
- The page carries no ornament at all. There are no boxes, rules or decoration competing with the words, so once real times are added they will be the most prominent thing on the page.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone. Passengers will most likely check this on the landing, from a phone, so check it there.
