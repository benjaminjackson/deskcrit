**First read.** The eye lands on the three big numbers (3.8%, €64.20, 12.4k), moves across the row, then drops into the orange bars, where Saturday's peak and Sunday's drop stand out. It reads at once as the owner's dashboard for a small bakery's online shop. It feels calm, clean and well built, though it could be any shop's template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a monthly check for the shop owner on whether the business is healthy, and on which days orders land so they can plan baking and delivery.

**Verdict.** The KPI row works well. The chart, which takes most of the page, gives a misleading picture: it adds up raw September totals across weekdays that occur a different number of times. It also never says what the owner should take from it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart compares weekdays that don't occur equally often (defect).** September 2026 starts on a Tuesday, so Tuesday and Wednesday each occur five times and every other day four. Their bars are inflated by about 25% for that reason alone. Divided per occurrence, Monday is about 160 orders, Tuesday about 142, Wednesday about 152 and Thursday about 205. The smooth climb from Monday to Thursday that the chart shows doesn't exist: Tuesday is actually the weakest weekday. An owner who plans staff or baking from this chart would plan for the wrong days. In Tufte's terms (*Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, ch. 2, graphical integrity), the bars' sizes don't match the effect in the data. The fix is to plot average orders per delivery day, and say so in the title or on the axis. Alternatively, use a window of whole weeks (for example, the last 4 weeks) so every day counts the same number of times.
2. **The chart title names the topic, not the finding (common sense; no approved source).** "Orders by delivery day" leaves the owner to work out the story, and the story is the reason the chart is there. Once fix 1 is made, the title should state the result, for example "Saturday is your busiest delivery day; Sunday under half of it". If Sunday is low because there are fewer delivery slots, not less demand, add a note on that bar. Otherwise the owner may read a capacity limit as weak demand.

**Smaller things.**
- **Time windows don't match:** the KPIs say "Last 30 days", the chart says "September 2026", and subscribers say "as of 30 September". If this is opened on any day other than 30 September, they describe different periods. Name one period everywhere.
- **The three changes use three different measures:** points, euros and percent. The owner can't tell at a glance which number moved most. Show each change as a percentage, or add the percentage next to the absolute change.
- **The period is fixed and can't be changed:** there is no control to switch the window or compare months (Nielsen, heuristic 7). The empty right side of the top bar is the natural place for a date-range picker.

**What's working.**
- **The KPI cards:** each has a plain-language label, one large number, the period, and a change vs the previous period. The unit (%, k, €) is set lighter and smaller so the figure itself dominates. Keep this structure.
- **The chart itself is honest about its form:** bars start at zero, gridlines are faint, there is one colour, labels sit directly under the bars, weekdays run in their natural order, and a source line is given. Keep all of that when you change the data behind it.
- **The changes don't rely on colour alone:** the ▲ and the + sign carry them as well as the green, so they still read for colour-blind viewers and in greyscale.
