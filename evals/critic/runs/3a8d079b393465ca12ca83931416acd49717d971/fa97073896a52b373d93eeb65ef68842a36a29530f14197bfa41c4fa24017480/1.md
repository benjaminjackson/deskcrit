**First read.** The bold heading top left lands first and reads cleanly. Then the eye drops to where the paragraph should start, below the heading's left edge, and finds nothing there. Each centred line starts somewhere different, so the eye has to look for the start of every line. The right 40% of the screen is empty. It reads as a plain information page for people taking a local river ferry. It feels calm but unfinished, like a document with the wrong alignment button pressed.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a web page telling ferry passengers how the service runs (frequency, crossing time, bicycles, winter changes), read on a laptop by people planning a trip.

**Verdict.** This doesn't do its job well yet. The type and colour are sound, but the centred paragraphs under a flush-left heading make three short paragraphs harder to read than they need to be. The copy also leaves out the details a passenger actually needs to plan around.

**What would move it most**
1. **Centred paragraphs under a left-aligned heading** (defect and convention). All three paragraphs are 4-line blocks centred on an axis near x≈440, while "Notes for ferry passengers" sits flush left at x≈120. Every line starts at a different point (roughly x 124 to 157), so the eye searches for the start of each line. On text people scan for facts, that costs time on every line. The heading and the body don't share an edge, so they read as two pieces placed separately. The near-miss between the heading's edge at 120 and the first paragraph's widest line at 124 looks like a mistake rather than a choice. Tschichold (*The New Typography*) and Bringhurst (2.1.3) keep centring for a few short lines. Fix: set the paragraphs flush left on the heading's edge so the whole page hangs from one line. This one change fixes the ragged left edge, the alignment near-miss and the mixed axes.
2. **The copy leaves out the details passengers act on** (defect). "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "one boat each hour" give no clock times, and winter has no dates. A passenger can't tell whether 7:15 pm is safe, or when "winter" starts. Readers plan around times and later hold the operator to them, so vague times will cause missed boats. The page then sends readers to printed timetables in the shelters, which they can only see once they're already at the landing. Fix: give the first and last departures, the winter dates and the winter last departure as times. Better still, put a short timetable or link on this page rather than in the shelter.
3. **Lopsided frame** (taste, with support from Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). All the content sits in the left 60% of a 1280 px screen. Nothing balances the right side, so the page looks unfinished rather than airy. Fix: centre the left-aligned column as a block in the window, or put something useful in the right side, such as the timetable from finding 2.

**Smaller things.**
- Heading spacing: the gap under the heading (~30 px) looks about the same as the gaps between paragraphs, so the heading doesn't tie to the text below it. Add space above each paragraph group or take some out under the heading (Arnheim, proximity).
- Findability (common sense): the bicycle and winter information each sit in their own paragraph without a label. A bold lead-in or a small subhead ("Bicycles", "Winter service") would let a rider go straight to their part.

**What's working.**
- One sans family in two weights, with a large bold heading over regular body text: a clear two-level scale with no sizes that nearly collide. Keep it.
- Lines run about 65–72 characters with comfortable line spacing, and the near-black text on white has strong contrast. Once the paragraphs are flush left, the text will read easily.
- The sentences are plain and concrete ("lock them to the rail along the side of the deck"). That's the right voice for passenger notices.
