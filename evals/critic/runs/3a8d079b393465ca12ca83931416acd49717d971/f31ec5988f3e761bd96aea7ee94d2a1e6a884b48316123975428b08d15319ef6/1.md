**First read.** The eye goes to the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" and then hits three wide blocks of text that run almost from edge to edge. The bottom half of the screen is empty. It reads as a plain, default information page: calm, but tiring, with nothing to scan. A passenger looking for "when does the boat go?" has no time or label to find.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page, read on a laptop, that tells ferry passengers how the service runs: how often, how long a crossing takes, the rules for bicycles, and the winter changes. People will check it while planning a trip.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do the job well. The facts a passenger needs are buried in long, very wide paragraphs, and the copy leaves out the actual times. The type is clean, but the page makes the reader dig for what they came for.

**What would move it most**

1. **The lines are about twice as long as they should be.** All three paragraphs span the full content area, roughly 1,120 px. That looks like about 140 to 150 characters per line. The eye has a long way back to the start of each next line and loses its place. You can feel it in the first paragraph, at "Each / crossing takes…". Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 characters as the comfortable range for one column (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). The fix is to cap the text column at about 65 characters, roughly 600 to 680 px at this size, and let the empty space sit to the right. *Convention, with a real reading cost (defect).*

2. **The page has prose where a passenger needs answers to scan.** Three topics are run together as paragraphs with no subheads: when the ferry runs, bicycles, and winter. Someone who only wants to know whether they can take a bike, or when the last boat leaves, has to read everything. Nielsen ("How Users Read on the Web") is clear that people skim screens rather than read them. The fix is to give each topic a short subhead that names it, such as "Times", "Bicycles" and "Winter". Then pull the hard facts (frequency, crossing time, last boat) out of the sentences into a short list or a small table. *Defect.*

3. **The copy leaves out the numbers people will plan around.** "Between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "a little longer when the tide turns" can't be planned against. Someone reading "before dusk" in December may turn up at 16:30 and miss a boat that left at 15:45. Each of these is a timing a passenger will act on, so each one being vague is a defect, not a style note. The fix is to give the first and last sailings as clock times for each season, and to say "up to N minutes longer" for the tide. If times change, say where the current timetable is online, not only "in the shelter at each landing". *Defect (copy).*

**Smaller things.**
- Line spacing looks like about 1.5. That's fine for a normal-width column but tight for lines this long (Bringhurst 2.2.1; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*). Fixing the line length fixes this too, so don't add more leading as a workaround.

**What's working.**
- The type scale is clean: two sizes, clearly apart, and the heading is set apart by weight as well as size. Keep the scale when you add subheads, and make them bold body-size or one step up, not a new in-between size.
- The text is near-black on white at a comfortable body size (about 18 px), set flush left with an even rag. That's easy to read and doesn't need changing.
- The 80 px left margin and the plain, single-family palette suit a public-service notice. Nothing fights for attention.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the text reflows on a phone. Many passengers will check this at the landing, so confirm the column and any timetable table work at 320 px wide.
