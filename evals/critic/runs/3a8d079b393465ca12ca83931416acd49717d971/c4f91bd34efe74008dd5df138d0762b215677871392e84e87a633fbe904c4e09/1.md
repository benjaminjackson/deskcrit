**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big serif quote and reads all three lines. Then it drops to the bold attribution, and only then, last and faintest, to the grey line with the news. It reads as a warm, quiet feature from a local library newsletter: calm, literate, a little old-fashioned in a good way. After reading the quote I still didn't know why "old" is in quote marks or what the item was about until I reached the last line.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a newsletter header or email card telling library patrons that the refurbished reading room reopens, and making them want to visit.

**Verdict.** It is well made and nearly does its job. Two things hold it back. The quote spends the biggest type on a line that doesn't land without context the page never gives. And the actual news, when the room reopens and what's new, is the weakest thing on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The quote doesn't land for a stranger.** It is the headline, at about 96 px across three lines. The scare quotes around ‘old’ and "nobody calls it anything else" only make sense if the reader knows the room has been renamed or rebuilt, and the page never says so. A patron reads the largest text twice and is left guessing whether the room is new, renamed, or just being called old affectionately. The joke belongs to the volunteer and the editor, not the reader. Either make the body copy supply the missing fact ("It has a new name on the door, but…") so the quote pays off, or choose a quote that works on its own. *Defect: copy.*
2. **The news ranks last.** The bold, near-black attribution ("said a volunteer…", about 20 px) outranks the grey paragraph (about 18 px, mid-grey) that holds the only actionable information: it reopens Monday, with lamps, shelves and a jigsaw table. The two sizes are only a couple of pixels apart, so the difference comes from weight and colour, and both point the wrong way. Swap their weights. Set the attribution in regular weight or grey as a quiet credit, and give the reopening line the second level: darker, and a clear size step up from the credit (Bringhurst, *Elements*, 3.1.1: "a modest set of distinct and related intervals"). *Convention.*
3. **"Monday" is a date that can be read two ways.** In a newsletter that arrives on some day and gets read on another, "Monday" could mean this coming Monday or the one after. It is the one fact the reader will act on. Give the date ("Monday 1 December") and ideally the opening hours. *Defect: copy.*

**Smaller things.**
- The opening “ sits on the left margin, so "It's" starts indented compared with "room" and "anything" below it. At this size the jog is plain to see. Hang the quote mark into the margin so the letters line up. This is common display practice; the catalog's source for it is unconfirmed.

**What's working.**
- The pairing: a warm text serif for the quote with a plain sans for everything else, on a cream ground with one rust accent for the label. It sets a library mood without a single ornament. Keep it.
- One flush-left edge at 110 px carries every block, and the empty right side and lower area are gathered rather than scattered, so the page feels calm and deliberate.
- The typographic detail is right: curly quotes, nested single quotes, a true em dash, and a properly letterspaced all-caps label.
