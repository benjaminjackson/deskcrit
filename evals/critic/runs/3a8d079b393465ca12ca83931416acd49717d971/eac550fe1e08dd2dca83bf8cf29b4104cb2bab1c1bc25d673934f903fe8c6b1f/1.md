**First read.** My eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", then reads down two even paragraphs and slows at a last line set in smaller type. It reads as a plain, tidy information page for people taking a local river ferry. It feels calm and competent, and a little bare: one column at upper left, with the right side and bottom of the frame empty.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page that tells a first-time passenger when and how to take the ferry, so they can plan a trip.

**Verdict.** It's well set but doesn't do its job. A passenger can't plan a trip from it because it gives no times, and the one warning that could stop a wasted journey is the smallest text on the page.

**What would move it most**
1. **The winter warning is demoted by a size that looks like a mistake.** The last line ("Timetables change in winter. Check the noticeboard…") looks about 16 px, against body text at about 19 px. That gap is too small to read as a separate level, so it looks like a slip rather than a choice (Dair, *Design with Type*, ch. 8: weak contrast is worse than none; Bringhurst, §3.1.1 "Don't compose without a scale"). It also turns the page's order of importance upside down. This is the only line that tells the reader to do something, and it's the line that keeps them from turning up to no ferry, yet it's styled like a footnote. **Defect.** Fix: drop the in-between size. Either set it at body size and mark it with weight or a short label ("Winter:"), or move it up next to the schedule paragraph it qualifies. Three sizes (heading, body, nothing smaller) are all this page needs.
2. **The copy describes the service but never gives the passenger a time.** "Every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" doesn't say when the first or last boat leaves, and those are the two facts a passenger plans around. "Every forty minutes" with no starting time doesn't tell them whether they've just missed one. The fallback, "check the noticeboard at the landing before you travel", only helps once they're already standing at the landing. **Defect.** Times are exactly what a reader acts on and later holds the operator to, and right now they have to fill those gaps themselves. Fix: give the first and last departure times and the minutes past the hour (or a small timetable), say when the winter timetable starts, and point to where it can be checked before leaving home.

**Smaller things.**
- The heading "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any transit page. A heading that says what the reader gets ("Crossing times and bikes on the river ferry") would let them know at a glance that the answer is here. This is my taste.
- "About twelve minutes in calm weather" leaves the reader wondering what happens in rough weather. Either say it or cut the qualifier.
- Body lines run about 70–75 characters, at the top of the comfortable range. That's fine as it is, but don't widen the column.

**What's working.**
- The bicycle paragraph is good copy. It says who does what, in what order, and where ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail along the side of the deck"), so a rider knows exactly what to expect. Write the schedule paragraph to the same standard.
- The setting is clean: one plain sans family, one strong heading, generous leading, tidy rag, a single left edge, and dark text on white with plenty of contrast. Keep all of it when you add the times.
