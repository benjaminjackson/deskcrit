**First read.** I look first at the bold title "Rainfall at the reservoir", then at three blue bars in a heavy grey frame, and I try to read rainfall from them. I can't: there are no numbers, no dates and no labels. Then the eye slides right into a paragraph about bus times and parking, which sits against the chart's border and so reads as its caption. It looks like an unfinished wireframe parked in the top-left corner of the page.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a visitor-information web page for a reservoir or park. It should tell visitors about recent rainfall and how to get there.

**Verdict.** It doesn't work. The only chart says nothing, because it has no axis, units or category labels. And the travel text is attached to it as if it explained the chart, so the page's two pieces of information read as one confused one.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart can't be read** (defect). The three bars in the framed box have no value axis, no units (mm? inches?), no category labels (which months, years or gauges?) and no date. The title promises rainfall, but the viewer can only see that the middle bar is tallest, and can't tell what any bar measures. Tufte and Few both require labelled axes or direct labels, with units and a date, so the reader never has to guess what a number measures. Fix: label each bar under its base with its period, and put the value with its unit on or above each bar. Then rewrite the heading or add a one-line caption that states the finding, such as "May was the wettest month this spring: 84 mm." That last part is taste, not a published rule, but a visitor wants the conclusion, not just the topic.
2. **The travel text is attached to the chart, so it reads as the chart's caption** (defect). "Getting there" starts right at the chart's right border with no gap. Meanwhile the title sits about 50 px above the chart. So the closest thing to the chart is unrelated text, and the eye groups them: proximity decides grouping (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*). Pairing "Rainfall" with "bus stops at the east gate" also leaves the viewer unsure whether the page is about weather or about visiting. Fix: separate the two topics clearly. Give "Getting there" its own section with a gutter or a full row of space, and give the chart a section heading of its own so the page title isn't doing that job. Better still, decide whether these belong on the same page at all, and if they do, title the page for the visit, not for the rainfall.
3. **The chart's frame is heavier than its data** (convention). The dark 2–3 px border and blue-grey tint around the plot are the strongest lines on the page after the bars. They box the chart in, and their right edge is the line the text runs into. Fix: remove the frame and tint so the bars are the darkest marks, and keep only the thin baseline (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*). Use space, not a box, to set the chart apart.

**Smaller things.**
- Layout: everything is packed into the top-left. The right third and the bottom 40% of the screen are leftover space, not a deliberate open area, so the page looks unfinished and tips to the left (Arnheim on balance; Ruder on gathered space). Once the sections are separated, set the content on a grid that fills the width it claims.
- Grid: the text column starts exactly on the chart's border, with no gutter, so no column structure repeats. Give the columns a consistent gutter.

**What's working.**
- Bars are the right form for comparing a few amounts, and they rise from a shared baseline, so their lengths can be compared honestly once there's a scale.
- The type is plain, readable and well set. The title, section heading and body are three clearly separate levels. The paragraph runs at about 45 characters per line with comfortable line spacing and a gentle rag. Keep that.
- The "Getting there" copy is concrete and useful to a visitor: the route number, which gate, how often, when parking is free, and where the racks are.

**Checklist.**
- The whole piece: 8 lines, 3 yes, 5 no (one-sentence purpose; image and headline make one idea; every element does a job; copy finished; headings name their section)
- What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
- Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 7 yes, 6 no (one grid; space shows the groups; no accidental groups; elements stay clear of each other; white space gathered; balance)
- Text: 30 lines, 30 yes
- Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
- Pictures: 12 lines, 12 yes
- Charts and data: 16 lines, 12 yes, 4 no (data-ink; direct labels; axis values, units, and labels; title states the finding)
- Interface: skipped, no controls
