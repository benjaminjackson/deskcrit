**First read.** The rainbow grid hits first, and the eye goes straight to the two red cells late on Monday and Friday mornings. Next it notices the solid violet wall: roughly two-thirds of the grid is fully saturated colour that means "almost nothing happens here". Then it moves to the right-hand stats, where "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" seems to disagree with the red cells it just looked at. The page looks like a clean, competent report, but the chart is louder than its data.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a Reports screen in a help-desk product, where support leads see when tickets arrive so they can plan staffing by hour and day.

**Verdict.** The structure is good: the right chart form, a clear title, honest labels and useful stats. But the colour scale works against the reading. A seven-hue rainbow makes the quiet hours as loud as the peak and hides the shape of the day. On top of that, the headline "busiest hour" figure doesn't match the hottest cells.

**What would move it most**

1. **The busiest-hour stat contradicts the chart.** The hour labels sit centred over cells 0, 3, 6, 9 and 12, so the red cells on Mon and Fri are the 11:00 column. 10:00 is orange (25–29) on all five weekdays. 11:00 is red (30+) on two days and orange on the other three. A planner reading "Busiest hour, weekdays: 10:00" next to that will believe one of them is wrong, and then trusts neither. They might staff for the wrong hour. The binned colours could hide an average where 10:00 just edges ahead, but nothing on the page says so. **Defect.** Check the number. If 10:00 is right, show the margin ("10:00, 1 ticket ahead of 11:00"). Better still, mark the busiest cell or column on the grid so the stat and the chart point at the same spot.

2. **A rainbow scale for ordered counts.** The scale runs violet → cyan → teal → green → yellow → orange → red for 0–4 up to 30+. Hue has no built-in order, so the viewer has to check the legend to know whether green is more than teal. Lightness doesn't follow the values either: yellow (20–24) is the lightest colour on the page and sits mid-scale, and 0–4 violet is about as dark as 30+ red. In greyscale the peak disappears. The worst effect is that the empty overnight hours are fully saturated violet, so half the page shouts about nothing and the actual peak has to fight for attention. Red and green also carry "bad" and "good" meanings, and the green/yellow/orange/red steps next to each other are the pairs colour-blind viewers confuse most. **Convention** (Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*: ordered values use one hue from light to dark; Itten and Refactoring UI on large saturated areas; WCAG 1.4.1 on colour alone). Use one hue ramp with 0–4 as near-white or a pale tint and 30+ as the darkest shade. The quiet hours fade back, the working-day block shows its shape at a glance, and the peak becomes the darkest thing on the page without needing alarm red.

3. **The title names the topic, not the finding.** "When new tickets arrive" tells the viewer what the chart is about, not what it shows, so they have to work it out from the grid. **Taste**, but it matters on a report people check quickly. Keep the topic and add the finding in the subtitle or as a note on the grid, for example: "Weekday tickets peak 10:00–12:00 and stay busy until about 16:00; weekends are quiet." That also gives the busiest-hour stat something to agree with.

**Smaller things.**
- "New tickets, September: 3,412" sits beside a chart covering "1 to 28 September". Say whether the total is for the whole month or for the same 28 days, or the viewer can't connect the two.
- "Up 4% on August" and "Down 6 min on August" are unclear about whether that's good or bad for a reader in a hurry. That's fine as text, but don't fix it later with red/green alone.

**What's working.**
- The chart form is right. A day × hour heatmap is the correct way to ask "when", and the even cells and the hour ticks every 3 hours, with the days in natural order, keep it easy to read.
- Each stat on the right has a comparison ("Up 4% on August", "Same as August"), so every number arrives with context. Keep that pattern.
- The source line says the times are in the team's local time and that hours mean when a ticket was opened, not when it was answered. That caveat stops people misreading the chart, and most reports leave it out.
