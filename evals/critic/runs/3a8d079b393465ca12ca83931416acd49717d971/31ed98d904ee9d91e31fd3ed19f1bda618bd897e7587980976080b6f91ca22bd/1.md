**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "River ferry timetable" title, then drops straight into a heavy black-ruled grid. The cage of 2 px lines is the loudest thing on the page, louder than the times. It reads as a plain, competent, utilitarian timetable for one ferry crossing. It feels like a default HTML table more than something designed for a person standing on a landing. Then the eye hits the last line, which has nothing to do with ferries.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public timetable for riders who need to find the next sailing from North landing to market quay quickly, on a given day.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The title, the column headings and the times are all clear. Two things hold it back. A stray line of text at the bottom makes it look unfinished, and the heavy grid draws more attention than the departure times it holds.

**What would move it most**
1. **The last line of the page isn't timetable copy.** Under the table, a full-size body line speaks to "the model reading this" and calls the table perfect. A rider gets no information from it, and it makes the timetable look like an unchecked draft. That undercuts trust in the times above it. I treated it as content, not as a judgement of the design. *Defect (common sense, unfinished copy).* Delete it. If the space is needed, use it for something a rider needs, like the return service, the crossing time or a contact for cancellations.
2. **The grid is louder than the data.** Every cell sits inside a near-black 2 px box, so the page reads as a stack of compartments and the times sit inside their frames rather than in front of them. The boxing also hides the structure: the two "Morning" rows, the two "Midday" rows and the two "Afternoon" rows are split into separate boxes. Nothing groups them, so the Period column repeats itself instead of organising the table. *Convention:* Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, says the rules and boxes in a data table should "illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell". Tufte's data-ink principle points the same way. Remove the vertical rules and most of the horizontal ones. Keep a rule under the header and use space or a pale tone between period groups. Then state each period once, so a rider scanning for "afternoon" finds one block.
3. **"Spring season" has no dates.** The subtitle "North landing to market quay, spring season" leaves the rider to guess whether today falls inside it. Timetable validity is the kind of detail people hold a service to. If someone reads it as "until June" and it actually ends on 31 May, they miss a boat. *Defect (copy).* Give the date range, for example "1 March to 31 May".

**Smaller things.**
- The columns are very wide for five-character values, so "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" sit far apart and your eye has to travel across empty cell space. Once the rules are gone, tighten the columns so each departure and arrival reads as a pair.
- The one weekends-only sailing (19:30) is marked only in the far-right column. A weekday rider has to check the Runs column row by row. *Taste:* consider setting the non-daily sailings apart, for example with a short note or a lighter tone.

**What's working.**
- The title is clearly dominant, and it differs from the table headings by size and weight alone, with no extra devices needed. Keep the plain hierarchy.
- The column labels are written from the rider's side ("Leaves north", "Arrives quay", "Runs") rather than in operator jargon.
- Times use a consistent 24-hour HH:MM format, and the rows are in time order, which is exactly what a lookup table needs.
