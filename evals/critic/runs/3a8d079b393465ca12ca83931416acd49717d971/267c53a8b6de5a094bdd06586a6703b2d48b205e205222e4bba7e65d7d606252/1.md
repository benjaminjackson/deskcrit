**First read.** My eye goes to "Landing stage rebuild" first, then down the left column through the paragraph and into the flat blue-and-grey illustration. From there it jumps right to the blue Sign up button, because it's the only strong colour on the page apart from a matching blue bar inside the illustration. This reads as a local ferry service's news page with a signup panel for timetable alerts. It feels calm, plain and civic, but a little unfinished, mostly because the picture looks like a wireframe.

**What it's for.** My assumption, since you told me nothing: this tells ferry passengers about the rebuilt landing stage and gets them to sign up for timetable-change notices.

**Verdict.** The article half does its job cleanly. The signup panel doesn't, and that's the half that asks the reader to do something. It never asks for an email or phone number, so the reader can't tell how the "short note" will reach them, and the form as drawn can't deliver one.

**What would move it most**
1. **The signup form has no way to reach the reader** (defect). In the right-hand panel the fields are "Your name" and "Home landing". The helper text promises "one short note when the timetable changes", but nothing asks where to send it. A reader filling it in will either stop and wonder whether this is an email, a text or an app notification, or press Sign up and get nothing. The form doesn't show how the service actually works (Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, conceptual model). The fix is to make the delivery channel the one field the form can't do without, labelled as what it is ("Email for timetable notes" or "Mobile number for texts"), and say in the helper text how the note arrives. If the name isn't used for anything, drop it: it's the one field a reader would happily skip.
2. **The illustration doesn't show what the article is about, and part of it looks like a button** (defect, common sense; the figure-ground entry has no approved source). The figure is four flat rectangles: pale sky, a light grey block, a dark grey slab and pale blue water, plus a saturated blue rounded bar. The caption says "the new north landing, seen from the water". The article's actual news is the wider ramps, the covered waiting area and the step-free boarding, but none of those can be found in the picture, so it reads as a placeholder that never got replaced. The blue rounded bar is also the same hue and corner shape as the Sign up button, so it pulls the eye and briefly looks clickable. Similar elements are read as a group (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*). If this is the real image, either replace it with a photo or make a drawing that shows the ramp meeting the deck. Either way, take the button blue and button shape out of the picture so the panel's call to action is the only thing that colour means. If a photo failed to load, see "Couldn't judge from a still".
3. **The Sign up ask is generic** (taste). "Get timetable changes / Sign up" could sit on any transport site, and it doesn't use the one thing that would make it feel worth doing: the reader's own crossing. The "Home landing" field already points that way. Make it a picker of the real landings and let the button name the result ("Tell me when my sailings change"), so the reader can see they'll get notes only for the boat they take, not a newsletter.

**Smaller things.**
- **Light weight on all the small text.** The field labels, the helper text under the button, the figure caption and the footer line are set in a light cut of the face at about 12–13 px, so they look spindly next to the regular body text. Set them in the regular weight; light weights are for large sizes (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.3).
- **"Home landing" is an open text field.** People will type spellings and abbreviations that won't match a route. Use a select or radio list of the actual landings (Nielsen, heuristic 5: error prevention).
- **No field is marked required or optional.** Once the contact field goes in, mark it, and mark the name as optional if it stays (Budiu, NN/g, "Marking Required Fields in Forms").

**What's working.**
- **The copy speaks in the reader's terms.** "So prams and wheelchairs roll straight on" turns a ramp spec into what the reader gets out of it. Keep every line written like that.
- **The type scale is clean.** There are four clear levels (headline, panel heading, body, small text), each heading is bold against regular body text, and the body text runs at a comfortable 60–70 characters per line.
- **The layout is quiet.** It's an article column plus one boxed side panel, with a single primary button and generous space, which suits a public service notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Check in a live browser whether the illustration is the intended artwork or a fallback for an image that didn't load.
- The focus, hover and error states of the form.
- How the side panel reflows on a phone: whether it lands above or below the article.
