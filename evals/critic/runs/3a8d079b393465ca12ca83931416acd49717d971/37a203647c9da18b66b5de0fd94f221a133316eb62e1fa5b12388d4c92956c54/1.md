**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline "How the river ferry runs in winter", reads down two plain paragraphs, and stops at a pale blue-grey box about fares. It looks like an article or help page for a local ferry service: calm, clean and readable. Then you notice the whole column is jammed against the left edge of the frame with no margin, and that makes it look unfinished, like a component dropped onto a blank canvas.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public information page that tells winter passengers when the ferry runs, what can delay it, and what it costs to bring a bike, car or van.

**Verdict.** The hierarchy is sound and the page is easy to read, but it doesn't fully do its job. The text has no margin at the left edge and the lines are too long. The bigger problem is that the timetable copy leaves gaps a passenger can't fill: they can't work out the actual departure times or what the "standard fare" is.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable doesn't add up, and the key facts are missing (defect, in the copy).** The first paragraph says the ferry leaves "every forty minutes", the first crossing is "at seven in the morning" and the last is "at six in the evening". Forty-minute steps from 7:00 give 5:40 pm and 6:20 pm, not 6:00, so one of these figures is wrong, or the gaps aren't regular. A passenger who plans around that last boat may be standing on the landing at the wrong time. The page also gives no times for the return trip from the other bank, which matters to anyone who has crossed and needs to get back. In the callout, "Cars pay the standard fare" names a price without stating it. Times, fares and the last crossing are exactly what people act on and later hold the operator to. The fix: give the real departure times (or the true interval and the exact last departure), say the return times, and print the car fare. Write times as figures ("7:00 am", "6:00 pm"), because a reader scanning for times finds digits much faster than spelled-out words.
2. **No margin at the left edge (defect).** The headline, both paragraphs and the callout box all start at x = 0, touching the edge of the frame. Text on the edge looks cut off and gives the column no room. It's also why the page feels shoved left, with about 400 px of empty space on the right and nothing to balance it. Müller-Brockmann's type area exists to stop exactly this. The fix: put the column inside a real margin. On a page this wide, centring the column or giving it a generous left margin would fix both the edge and the balance. If this is a crop of a larger page, check that the live page has the margin.
3. **Lines run too long (convention).** The paragraphs span about 880 px, roughly 100 to 105 characters per line. Bringhurst gives 45 to 75 as the comfortable range (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 2.1.2). At this length the eye has a long trip back to the start of each line. That's tolerable for two short paragraphs, but it gets worse as the page grows. The fix: narrow the column to about 600 to 650 px at this type size, and the callout box can narrow with it.

**Smaller things.**
- The callout box has about 46 px of padding below the text and about 24 px above it, so the box looks bottom-heavy. It's probably the paragraph's own bottom margin carried inside the box. Make the padding equal on both sides (common sense).
- "Check the board at the ticket hut" doesn't say what the board shows (holds, delays, the next departure). One clause would tell the passenger what they're checking for.

**What's working.**
- There are two clear type levels: a large bold headline and plain body text. You know where to start without trying. Keep the scale this small.
- The callout tint is pale but separates the fares from the operating notes without shouting. It's one quiet neutral that does one job.
- The second paragraph sets out the condition and then the action ("When ice forms… Check the board… before you queue"). That's the right pattern for service information.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the column reflows on a phone, and whether the left edge gets a margin at narrower widths.
