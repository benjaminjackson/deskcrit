**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", drops to the grey subtitle, then goes straight down the table. It reads at once as a public timetable for a small local ferry. It feels calm, plain and trustworthy. Nothing pulls for attention it doesn't deserve. The second look is where it slows down: to check whether a boat runs today, the eye has to travel the whole width of the page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a timetable screen or printout for riders crossing from North landing to market quay. They come to answer one question: when is the next boat I can actually take, today?

**Verdict.** It's legible and honest, and it mostly does its job. Two things get in the way. The columns are ordered by how easy they were to fill in, not by what the rider needs. And the copy leaves out the dates and direction a rider would rely on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The day rule sits as far as possible from the time it qualifies, and two columns hold redundant information** (defect). Look at the 12:40, 17:10 and 19:30 sailings. To learn that they don't run every day, the rider reads the departure time at x≈360 and then crosses about 600 px of white space to reach "Weekdays" or "Weekends" in the last column. That column is the one that decides whether the boat exists for them. In between are two columns that add little:
   - **Period** repeats what the clock already says ("Morning 06:40"). It also takes the most prominent position, the first column.
   - **Arrives quay** is always departure plus 12 minutes, so it spends a whole column restating one fact seven times.

   This is Nielsen's heuristic 8 (aesthetic and minimalist design): content that doesn't support the main task competes with what does. The fix: lead with the departure time and put the days right next to it. Say "Crossing takes 12 minutes" once in the subtitle, unless arrival times will ever vary. Drop Period, or turn it into a small group label above each block of rows. The table then gets narrow enough that rows stop needing to be tracked across the page.

2. **"Spring season" is something riders act on, and it has no dates** (defect). A rider checking in late May can't tell whether this table still applies. If they guess wrong, they're standing on an empty landing. Name the dates: "Spring season, 1 March – 31 May". Also say what happens on public holidays, if anything changes.

3. **Only one direction is shown, and nothing says so** (defect, if the ferry runs both ways). "North landing to market quay" is in the subtitle, but there are no return times and no pointer to them. Someone at the quay who needs to get back has nothing to go on. Either add the return table or say where to find it.

**Smaller things.**
- Column heads "Leaves north" and "Arrives quay" are clipped versions of "Leaves North landing" and "Arrives market quay". Use the place names in full, as the subtitle does, so the heads match the signs at the landings.
- The capitals are inconsistent: "North landing", but "north" lower-case in the column head. Pick one form for the place names and keep it everywhere.

**What's working.**
- **Restraint.** It uses one neutral palette, one typeface and four clear text levels: bold title, grey subtitle, bold column heads, regular rows. Nothing competes with the times, so keep that discipline when you cut columns.
- **The row rules are pale and do one job.** They hold each row together across the wide gaps without adding noise. If the table narrows, they could get lighter still or go.
- **24-hour times with leading zeros (06:40), sorted by time.** They're unambiguous, scan in order, and line up cleanly in the column.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the table reflows on a phone, where four columns at this spacing would likely crowd or force sideways scrolling. Check it at 360 px wide.
