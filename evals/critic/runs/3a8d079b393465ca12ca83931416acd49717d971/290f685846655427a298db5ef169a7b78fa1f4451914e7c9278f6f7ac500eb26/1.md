# Version 2 does its job better than version 1

**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the bold serif headline, "A restored hay barn for weddings of up to 120 guests", and then drops into the two paragraphs. In version 1 those paragraphs are in the logo's heavy brush script. They read like a greeting card, and I had to slow down word by word to read about the floor, the doors and the kitchen. In version 2 the same copy is in a calm serif and reads in one pass. In both, the large tan panel on the right is an empty gradient, so the page asks couples to book a visit without showing them the barn.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the hero section of a wedding venue's homepage. It's for couples comparing venues, and its goal is to get them to book a site visit.

**Verdict.** Version 2 is better. The only change is the body face: script in version 1, a readable serif in version 2. That change is what lets a couple actually take in the copy, which is the strongest selling point on the page. Both versions still have the same biggest problem: the space for the barn photo is empty.

**What changed, and why version 2 wins**
- **Version 1 sets about 90 words of body copy in a brush script (defect).** This is the logo's display face, used here for full sentences. The connected, heavy letters and tight line spacing make each line slow to read. A couple scanning several venue sites will skip it, and that copy holds the facts that sell the place: the 1870 barn, the 120-guest capacity in practice, caterer access from early morning, two nights in the cottage, and bring your own band and florist. It also puts a third unrelated face on the page (script, serif headline, sans navigation and button) with no clear role, so the page looks like it's trying too hard. Bringhurst (*Elements of Typographic Style*, 6.2.1) says text faces are for reading. Keep script to a few words of display, such as the logo. Version 2 does exactly this: the serif body matches the headline's family, the script stays in the logo, and the line spacing opens up to suit the roughly 60-character lines.

**What would move version 2 most**
1. **The main picture is an empty gradient box (defect).** It fills nearly half the hero on the right. A wedding venue sells on how the place looks, so a couple sees a promise of "a restored hay barn" and then a beige rectangle. That reads as unfinished or hiding something, and the "Book a visit" button is asking for a trip on trust alone. If this is a placeholder or an image that didn't load, it's fine once replaced. Check that in a live browser. Either way, the fix is a real photo that adds what the headline can't: the oak beams and the glass doors open onto the orchard, ideally set up for a wedding. That lets the photo and headline make one case together instead of the photo just illustrating the words. This is common sense, not a cited rule.
2. **The ask leaves the value to trust (copy, taste).** "Book a visit" is concrete, which is good. But nothing on the page says what booking involves (a date picker? a phone call?) or gives a fact couples compare venues on first, such as price or available dates. A line next to the button that answers one of those, for example "See the barn on a weekday evening, about 45 minutes", turns the ask into a specific thing a couple can say yes to.

**Smaller things.**
- **Headline break:** "120 / guests" splits the number from what it counts. Rebreak so "120 guests" stays on one line (Bringhurst 2.4.6).
- **Lone last words:** "morning." sits alone on the last line of the first paragraph in version 2 ("day." does the same in version 1). Rewrap so each last line holds at least two words (Bringhurst 2.4.2).

**What's working.**
- **Type plan in version 2.** There are three clear levels: a heavy serif headline, the same serif in a regular weight for the body, and a small sans for navigation and the button. Each size has an obvious job.
- **Copy.** It's specific, which is unusual for a venue site: a year, a capacity, what's included, what you may bring. It's warm, it speaks to the couple directly ("we will walk you through the site as many times as you need"), and it couldn't be any other venue's.
- **Restraint.** There's one accent brown, shared by the logo and the only button, so the button is unmistakably the primary action.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the gradient panel is a placeholder or an image that failed to load. Check in a live browser.
- The button's hover and focus states.

**Checklist** (final answers for version 2)
The whole piece: 8 lines, 6 yes, 2 no (picture and headline make one idea; every element does a job)
What matters first: 3 lines, 3 yes
Arrangement and space: 13 lines, 13 yes
Text: 30 lines, 28 yes, 2 no (clean paragraph breaks; heading line breaks). Version 1 also answers no to paragraph set in a reading face, typeface plan, and leading
Color: 9 lines, 9 yes
Pictures: 12 lines, 11 yes, 1 no (image shows its content)
Charts and data: skipped, no chart
Interface: 33 lines, 30 yes, 3 unsure (hover/pressed states, focus indicator, focus not obscured)

Better: version 2
