**First read.** Your eye goes straight to the big serif quote and stays there. Then it drops to the bold "said a volunteer…" line, and only last to the small grey sentence that holds the actual news. It reads as a warm, quiet, literary newsletter lead. The mood is right. But the straight `"` marks and the spaced hyphen at headline size look like a typewriter, not like setting. And the quote is a little puzzling out of context: why is 'old' in quotes?

**What it's for.** No brief was given, so I'm assuming this is the lead item of a library newsletter (web or email). Its job is to tell patrons the reading room reopens Monday and make them want to come.

**Verdict.** It's calm and well spaced, but it buries its own news. The biggest problem is that the largest thing on the page is a quote that doesn't tell you anything has happened. The reopening, the date and the reasons to come sit in the smallest, palest text. Second, the headline punctuation is the clearest craft flaw. At this size it looks unfinished.

**What would move it most**
1. **The news is at the bottom of the hierarchy.** "The reading room reopens on Monday with new lamps, new shelves, and a table set aside for jigsaw puzzles" is set in the smallest grey type. The headline is a quote that only makes sense once you know the room was closed and refitted. A reader skimming an inbox or a feed takes in the headline and the bold line and may leave without learning the room reopens, or when. "Monday" is the one detail people will act on, and it's the least visible word that matters. This is a **defect**: it costs the piece its job. The headline doesn't name what the item is about. WCAG 2.4.6 asks this of headings on the web; in print it's common sense. **Fix:** keep the quote, because it's the charm. But get the reopening and "Monday" up to a level people read without trying. Either put a short news line in the kicker slot ("Reading room reopens Monday" instead of the generic "Library newsletter"), or promote the grey sentence to the bold attribution's weight and colour.
2. **Straight quotes and a hyphen in a ~100 px headline.** The opening `"`, both `'` around old, the closing `,"` and the spaced hyphen in "room - nobody" are all typewriter characters. At display size they are the most visible marks on the page, and they make a carefully set serif look rough. Bringhurst (5.2.1) calls for true dashes; the curly-quote point rests on common practice rather than a checked source. This is a **convention** whose cost grows with size, and here the size is maximal. **Fix:** use curly quotes and apostrophes and a true dash: an em dash, or a spaced en dash.

**Smaller things.**
- **Opening quote not hung:** the `"` sits on the x=110 margin, so the "I" of "It's" starts about 30 px right of the kicker and body text. Hang the mark into the left margin so the letters line up with everything below.
- **Headline line break:** "reading / room" splits the name of the place across lines. Rebreak so "reading room" stays together, for example by narrowing the measure so "the 'old'" ends line one.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing and the three-level scale. A large text serif for the voice, a bold sans for the attribution, and a regular grey sans for detail give three clearly different jobs with no near-duplicate sizes. Keep the pairing, whatever you promote.
- Leading with a person's voice. "Stamped returned books for thirty years" is a specific, human detail no template would write, and it makes the reader care. Keep it; just don't let it stand in for the news.
- Space and palette. One flush-left column, generous margins, cream background, near-black type and one rust accent. It feels like a library without any ornament.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- How the headline rewraps at phone width. At this size it will likely run to five or six lines, which pushes the news further down. Check it at 375 px.
