**First read.** The eye lands on three slabs of saturated colour, orange, green and purple, all the same size. It reads the orange "Learn to swim" first only because it's on the left, then moves right along three equal headlines. It looks like a swimming pool's programme page: bright and friendly, but nothing on it tells you which session to pick or what to do next. The eye then drops off the bottom of the cards into blank colour and finds a grey line of small text that reads like a note left on the page.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a section of a public pool's website that should get adults and families to pick a session and sign up or turn up. Correct me if it's something else.

**Verdict.** It doesn't do its job yet. Three equal cards compete for attention, and none of them ends in anything to act on. The whole lower half of the section is empty colour where the times, prices and booking step should be. The line under the cards claims "one offer clearly leads the page", and it plainly doesn't.

**What would move it most**
1. **Nothing leads, so the viewer has to choose with no help.** All three cards are the same size, weight and value. In grayscale they'd be three identical dark blocks, so hue is the only thing telling them apart. The viewer reads all three headlines at the same level and starts on "Learn to swim" only because it's on the left. Neither the layout nor the colour says which offer matters most (Tschichold, *The New Typography*: show order by differences of size; Albers / Refactoring UI: value should carry structure). This is a defect. Fix: decide which offer this page is for, probably the one with spare places or the one aimed at newcomers. Make that card clearly larger or set it apart in value, and set the other two back. If the three really are equal, keep the cards equal but give the section a heading above them that says what the viewer is choosing between.
2. **The cards stop halfway and never ask for anything.** Under each heading are three lines of copy, then about 300 px of empty colour per card, and there's no action anywhere. The viewer learns that classes happen "on Tuesday evenings" and lanes open "every morning from six", but not what time on Tuesday, how much it costs, whether they need to book, or where to click. Krug's test fails: within seconds a visitor can't tell what they can do here. This is a defect. Fix: use that space for the facts people act on, which are exact times, price and how to book. End each card with one specific ask, such as "Book a Tuesday class" or "See today's lane times", rather than a generic "Learn more". Then shorten the cards to fit their content. The empty colour isn't working as breathing room (Ruder: white space should be shaped and gathered). It's just unfinished area.
3. **The page has no owner.** There's no pool name and no section heading, so the copy could belong to any leisure centre. That's acceptable if the brand sits above this section on the live page. If this block ever appears on its own, as a landing section or a shared image, the viewer doesn't know whose pool this is. This one is common sense, not a convention. Fix: add a short section heading that names the place, for example "Swim at [pool name] this week".

**Smaller things.**
- The grey line under the cards ("Design review: approved, one offer clearly leads the page") looks like a leftover review note rather than content. Remove it before anything ships. It's also wrong.
- Three fully saturated hues cover most of the section, so colour decorates instead of pointing (Refactoring UI; Apple HIG accent colours: more than about two competing saturated accents splits attention). If one card leads, give it the strong colour and calm the other two, or keep the hues only as a band or tag on each card.
- The row of cards sits left of centre: the left margin is about 56 px and the right about 96 px. Centre the row or run it to an equal margin on both sides.

**What's working.**
- The headlines are verbs and plain phrases ("Learn to swim", "Join the lanes", "Family hour"). They name what you'd actually do, in the audience's words. Keep them.
- The body copy is concrete and talks to the reader: "a coach in the water beside you", "three speeds to choose from", "floats and toys for the children". Each line lands on what the visitor gets, which is rarer than it should be.
- The type is clean and sure of itself: two sizes far apart, bold and tightly set headings, and white text that looks comfortably readable on all three colours (about 5:1 or better by eye).

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether the cards are clickable as a whole, and what hover or focus shows. If they are links, they need a visible signal that they can be clicked.
