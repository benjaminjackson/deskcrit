**First read.** The eye lands on the headline, "Cross the bay in twelve calm minutes". It drops through the paragraph and the three short lines to the blue button. It reads as the top of a local ferry company's website. It feels clean and calm, but it's thin: half the frame is white, there's no water, no boat and no sense of place, so it could be any company's page with the words swapped.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is the top of a ferry operator's website. Its job is to get locals and visitors to take the crossing, with the timetable as the next step.

**Verdict.** The words and the order of reading work. As a page, though, it does too little: the left-hand column is good, and the right half of the frame is empty. The biggest problem is that the page never shows the "calm" trip it promises, and it leaves out the two facts a ferry passenger actually wants: when the next boat leaves and what it costs.

**What would move it most**

1. **The right half is empty, and the promise has nothing to show it.** All the content sits in a column about 620 px wide, from x=160 to about 780. Everything to the right of that, plus the bottom third, is blank. The page tips hard to the left, and the empty space looks left over rather than chosen. Arnheim's point on balance applies: a heavy side needs a counterweight, and nothing here provides one (*Art and Visual Perception*, ch. I "Balance"). Ruder makes the related point that empty space should be shaped against dense areas, not left as what remains (*Typography: A Manual of Design*). The headline sells a feeling, "calm minutes" and "watch the harbour slide past", but the reader has to take that feeling on trust. Put a picture on the right that does what the words can't: the open deck with the harbour going by, or the boat on flat water. The headline then makes the claim and the picture proves it, without the picture just repeating the words. If there's no photo budget, a simple map of the crossing (north landing to the far shore, "12 min") would also balance the page and answer "from where to where". *Defect (balance) plus taste (the picture pairing).*

2. **The facts that decide whether someone takes the ferry are missing or vague.** "Every forty minutes from early morning until late evening" means the reader still can't plan a trip: what time is "early", and what is "late"? "The north landing" assumes the reader already knows the town. There's no fare anywhere. "Bicycles travel free" sounds like a price promise, but it gives no reference point: free on top of what fare? The only way to find any of this is the button. That breaks Nielsen's recognition-over-recall heuristic: the information needed to act should be on the screen in front of the person. It's also common sense that a ferry page should show the next departure. Put the real span ("06:20 to 22:40"), the place names on both ends of the crossing, and the adult fare in the text. "Next boat 14:40" beside the button would be stronger still. This makes "See the timetable" a check rather than a hunt. The button wording itself is good; keep it. *Defect.*

3. **Plain blue on white doesn't say sea, harbour or calm.** The palette is one stock UI blue, Inter and white. It's tidy and gives no wrong signals, but it's the default of every web template. A colour should carry the mood the message calls for (Itten, *The Art of Color*), and nothing here suggests water or a day out. Fixing finding 1 with a photo would mostly solve this too. Otherwise, take the accent from the harbour itself (a deeper sea blue or a hull colour) and let one area of tint carry it. *Taste.*

**Smaller things.**
- The paragraph (about 20 px) and the three feature lines (about 18 px) look almost the same size. Nobody sees two levels; it reads as a mismatch (Bringhurst, §3.1.1). Set them at the same size, and if the list needs to stand apart, use weight or a small icon.
- The three feature lines have no bullets or icons, so they read as stray short sentences. A tick or small icon would mark them as a list and make them faster to scan.

**What's working.**
- The headline names a concrete benefit with a number ("twelve calm minutes"). It's specific to this product and sounds like a person talking. Keep that voice.
- There's one clear entry point and one primary action, and the button text says exactly what happens when you press it. The reading order is clean from the brand name to the button.
- Everything shares one crisp left edge, and the text is well set: the paragraph runs about 70 characters a line, the spacing between lines is comfortable, contrast is strong, and the two-line headline is tight.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- I can't see focus or hover on the button.
- There's no site header or navigation. If this is a cropped capture of just the top section, check the live page; if the real page has no navigation either, visitors have no way to fares, routes or contact details except the one button.
