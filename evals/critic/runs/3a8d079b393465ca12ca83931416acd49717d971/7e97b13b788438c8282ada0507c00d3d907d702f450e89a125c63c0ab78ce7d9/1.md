**First read.** My eye goes first to the serif headline, then to the large navy slice marked 38%, then to the fan of small coloured slivers at the top left with their tiny labels. After that I end up in the legend on the right, because that is the only place the slices have names. It reads as a calm, well-set annual-report page with a busy chart in the middle of it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a page of a company annual report (print or PDF). Its job is to show shareholders and staff where the operating money went in fiscal 2026 and what stands out.

**Verdict.** The page around the chart is well made, but the chart is the wrong shape for this data. With 14 slices, nine of them 4% or less, the pie only tells you that salaries are a big share. The real reading happens in the legend, which is a sorted table with no bars. The page also never says what the numbers mean or how they compare with last year.

**What would move it most**
1. **Fourteen slices is too many for a pie, and the legend is doing the chart's job.** Nine slivers at the top left (4% down to 1%) are each labelled only with a percentage, so to find out what "3%" is you have to match a colour across the page to the legend. With four 3% slices and four 2% slices, that matching is the whole task. Several colours are close to each other: navy Salaries, mid-blue Software licences and indigo Utilities; teal Rent and pale-teal Equipment repair. Red Travel sits right next to green Insurance, which is the classic pair that colour-blind readers can't tell apart. So for many readers the legend lookup fails. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") and Wilke (*Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 10.1) reserve pies for about five slices. Tufte and Wilke both say to label the data directly rather than through a legend. WCAG 1.4.1 says colour must not be the only carrier of meaning. *Convention, and a defect for colour-blind readers.* **Fix:** the legend on the right is already sorted, with right-aligned figures and hairline rules between rows. Turn it into a horizontal bar chart: put a bar on each row in one colour (or navy for the top category and grey for the rest), and drop the pie. If you must keep a pie, keep Salaries, Rent, Cloud hosting and Marketing, and group the other ten into one "Other (10 categories) 25%" slice with its breakdown in a note.

2. **The title names the topic but doesn't say what you found, and there's nothing to compare against.** "Where operating costs went in fiscal 2026" leaves the reader to work out the point for themselves. Readers of an annual report want to know whether 38% on people is normal, rising or falling, and whether 12% on cloud hosting is new. With no prior year and no target, they can't judge any of these figures as good or bad. Few ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design") asks for a comparison next to every key number. *Convention for the comparison; taste for the title wording, since no approved source covers it.* **Fix:** put last year's share, or the change in percentage points, in a column beside each row. Rewrite the headline to state the finding, e.g. "People and premises took over half of what we spent", or whatever the real change from 2025 is.

**Smaller things**
- The white bold "12%" on the orange slice looks around 2.8:1, below WCAG 1.4.3 (it looks about 17 px, so it needs 4.5:1; even the 3:1 large-text minimum isn't met). "16%" on teal looks borderline too. Measure both, then use dark text on the light slices or darken those colours. This goes away if you switch to bars.

**What's working**
- The typographic frame. The serif headline sets a sober tone against the sans used for everything else. The small tracked-capital "4.2 COSTS" label and the "Section 4 of 9" running head tell you where you are in the report. Keep this system as it is.
- The legend is the strongest part: sorted largest to smallest, bold right-aligned figures in a neat column, thin rules between rows. That is the base the bar chart should be built on.
- The source line is honest and specific. It names the management accounts and says the shares are rounded, and the figures do add up to exactly 100.
