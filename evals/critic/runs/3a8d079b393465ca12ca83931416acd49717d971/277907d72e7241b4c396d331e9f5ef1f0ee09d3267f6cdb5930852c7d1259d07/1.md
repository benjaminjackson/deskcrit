**First read.** The eye lands on the bold serif headline, "Notes for the summer fete", then breaks off reading and jumps between the six underlined italic phrases scattered through the two paragraphs. At a glance it looks like a short web page full of links, not a set of instructions. The feel is calm, bookish and friendly, which suits a village fete.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this tells helpers and stallholders the few rules and times for the day. That means arrival, cake labels, the raffle times and parking, and it has to work when someone skims it once.

**Verdict.** The page itself is clean and well set, but the one device carrying the message, the emphasis, works against it. Every key fact is italic and underlined at once, so the facts look like links, the sentences break into pieces, and nothing ranks above anything else.

**What would move it most**
1. **The emphasis uses two signals at once, and the underline makes it read as links.** All six key phrases, from "arrive before ten" to "on the field only", are italic and underlined. On a screen an underline means "click here", so a reader will try to tap "three o'clock sharp" or wonder what they're missing. Even read as emphasis, stacking italic and underline makes a sentence look restless and hard to read in a straight line. Lupton (*Thinking with Type*, p. 94) says one signal is usually enough, and Hochuli (*Detail in Typography*, p. 44) says that underlining "hinder[s] linear reading". **Convention, plus a defect: the reader can't tell emphasis from links.** Fix: drop the underline and keep italic, or use bold if the facts must stand out when skimmed. Keep underlines for real links only.
2. **Every fact gets the same emphasis, so nothing is most important.** Six phrases in about fifty words are all marked the same way. The times that carry a penalty ("collected by four or the prizes go back in the box") look no different from "labelled with their ingredients". When everything is emphasised, the reader has nothing to scan for. **Defect for a skim-once notice.** Fix: the content is really a list of four rules with times. Pull the times and deadlines into a short list, with each rule on its own line and the time first, and let the prose go. If you keep the paragraphs, emphasise only the deadlines.
3. **The copy hides the consequences and leaves gaps.** "Arrive before ten" doesn't say who it's for: stallholders, or everyone? The raffle penalty is in the middle of a sentence, and "Parking is on the field only" doesn't say which field. Each of these lines is one people will be held to on the day, so a reading that can go two ways means someone parks in the wrong place or loses a prize. **Defect, common sense.** Fix: say who each rule applies to and give the place, for example "Stallholders: arrive before 10 am."

**Smaller things.**
- "only." sits alone on the last line of the second paragraph, split from "on the field". Rewrap the line or rework it so the phrase stays together (Bringhurst 2.4.2).
- "o'clock" uses a straight apostrophe. Use a curly one. It's minor at body size.

**What's working.**
- There's one clear headline, bold and much larger than the text, so you know straight away what the page is.
- The body text is a comfortable reading serif at a good size, with generous line spacing and lines of about 70–75 characters. Keep that setting.
- The palette is quiet: dark grey on white with no added colour, which suits a community notice.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether any of the underlined phrases are actually links. If they are, they need to be told apart from plain emphasis, and their wording needs to say where they go.
