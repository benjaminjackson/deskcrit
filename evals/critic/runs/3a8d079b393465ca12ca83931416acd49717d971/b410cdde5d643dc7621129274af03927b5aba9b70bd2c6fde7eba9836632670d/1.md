**First read.** In both versions the eye lands on the big dark-green headline, "Plan a week of dinners in ten minutes," then drops through two paragraphs and three short claims to the green "Plan my first week" button. It reads as a calm, plain landing page for a meal-planning app aimed at busy home cooks. The only real difference is alignment. Version 1 is set flush left on the logo's left edge and leaves the right side empty. Version 2 centres every block, paragraphs included, on one axis.

**What it's for.** Assumption: the homepage hero of a meal-planning app. Its job is to get a busy home cook to understand the promise and click "Plan my first week."

**Verdict.** Version 1 does the job better. Its paragraphs are easier to read, and its left edge ties the text to the logo. Version 2's centred multi-line paragraphs make every line start in a different place, so the paragraphs that make the case are harder to read. That costs more than the empty space Version 1 leaves on the right. Version 1's biggest remaining problem is that empty right half, which looks like a missing picture.

**What changed, and why it matters**
- **Version 2 centres paragraphs of two and three lines** (the "Tell Weekplate…" and "Swap any meal…" blocks, rows ~250–400). At the end of each line the eye has to hunt for where the next one starts, so the two sentences that explain the product read more slowly. Those two sentences are the reason anyone clicks. Centring is fine for a short headline or a few short lines, and not for running text (Tschichold, *The New Typography*; Bringhurst, 2.1.3). Defect / convention.
- **Version 2's centring is a habit, not a choice.** Every block sits on one centre axis, but the logo and nav stay pinned to the left and right edges. The hero therefore relates to nothing above it. Tschichold argues for an asymmetric layout that follows the order of the content. Version 1 does exactly that: logo, headline, paragraphs, claims and button all share the x=100 edge, so the page reads as one column from the brand down to the action. Convention.
- What Version 2 gains is balance: its weight sits in the middle, so nothing tips. That gain is real but smaller than the reading cost above.

**What would move Version 1 most**
1. **The right ~40% of the hero is empty and the bottom third is blank.** All the weight sits in the left column, so the page tips left, and the gap looks like an image that never loaded rather than space left on purpose (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). If a picture belongs there, check it in a live browser. If nothing does, put something there that does work: a sample week of seven dinners with its shopping list. That also shows the "ten minutes" promise instead of just stating it. Defect.
2. **The page never shows the product.** The ask "Plan my first week" is specific and good. But nothing on screen lets the viewer judge the value against what they already have, such as their own weeknight routine or a real shopping list. Showing a real planned week next to the button (the same fix as #1) turns the claim into something the viewer can check for themselves. Taste.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline breaks "ten / minutes", which splits the number from its unit and leaves one word on line 2. Widen the headline box a little, or force the break before "in ten minutes" (Bringhurst 2.4.6, on keeping a number with its unit). This applies to both versions.

**What's working.**
- In Version 1, the single left edge at x=100 runs from the logo through the button. Keep it: it gives the page its order.
- The copy speaks to the viewer and ends on what the product does for them: "so less food goes to waste by Friday," and "sorted by the aisles of your usual shop."
- There is one clear primary button, filled in the brand green, and its label names the exact thing you get.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Hover and focus states on the button and nav links.
- Whether an image was meant to fill the empty right side of Version 1 and failed to load.
