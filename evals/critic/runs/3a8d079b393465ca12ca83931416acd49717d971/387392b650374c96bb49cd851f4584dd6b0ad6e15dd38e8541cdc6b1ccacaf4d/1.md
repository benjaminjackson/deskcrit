**First read.** The eye goes to the bold title, then to the tall Organic search bar, then steps down the bars from left to right. It reads straight away as an internal growth report: Q3 sign-ups by channel, for a marketing or growth team. The side note gets read last, and it is the only part that says what any of this means. The page feels calm, clean and trustworthy. The figures add up: 5,400 total, and 1,840 is a third of it.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is an internal dashboard page that tells a growth team which channels brought accounts in Q3 and backs up a budget decision for Q4.

**Verdict.** It's a well-made, honest chart, and it does the "what happened" half of the job well. What gets in the way is that the page's two real claims sit in a small side note the chart doesn't back up. Organic search grew since Q2, and the events budget moves to referrals, yet the chart shows only one quarter and doesn't mark any bar the note talks about.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart can't show the claim the page is making.** The note's headline fact is a change: organic went "up from a quarter in Q2". But the chart shows Q3 alone, so the reader has to take the trend on trust. They can't tell whether referral, the channel getting more money, is rising or falling. A decision page needs the comparison next to the number (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design": give each figure the change from the last period). The usual fixes are a thin Q2 marker or outline bar behind each Q3 bar, or a small "+/– vs Q2" under each value label. Convention, and a defect for anyone using this to judge the Q4 budget move.
2. **The note and the chart don't point at each other.** The title "New sign-ups by channel" names the subject, not the finding. The finding sits in 15 px text in the right column, which is the last thing the eye reaches. All six bars are the same blue, so nothing in the chart says "this is the one that matters". Fix: keep the blue for Organic search, and maybe Referral as the channel that gains budget, and turn the rest grey. That follows Few's fix of one colour for the series and a second for the point the reader must notice. Optionally, have the title or subtitle state the takeaway ("Organic search now brings a third of sign-ups"). Then someone who only glances at the chart still leaves with the point. Convention for the highlight; the takeaway title is taste.

**Smaller things.**
- **Missing reason in the note:** "The events budget moves to referrals in Q4" gives the decision without the why, and doesn't say who decided. One clause would fix that, such as "…because referrals brought 4× as many accounts per dollar", if that's the reason. Without it, the reader can't judge whether it's the right move.
- **Doubled-up chart labels:** every bar carries its exact value, so the y-axis and gridlines repeat the same information. You could drop them, or keep only the baseline (taste, Tufte's data-ink argument).
- **Note placement:** the top of the note ("What changed this quarter", around y 174) lines up with nothing. It sits between the subtitle and the chart's top gridline at around y 195. Align it to the top of the chart, or to the title.

**What's working.**
- **An honest, readable chart:** bars sorted by value, starting from zero, labelled directly with values and category names, pale gridlines, and one colour. Every number can be read without the axis.
- **A source line that does real work:** it gives the date range and the total (5,400), so the reader can check the "a third" claim themselves. Keep it.
- **A note written as findings:** it uses concrete numbers and plain sentences instead of a bulleted summary. Keep that voice when you add the comparison and the reason.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Nav link hit areas look about 20 px tall but are widely spaced; check them in a browser.
