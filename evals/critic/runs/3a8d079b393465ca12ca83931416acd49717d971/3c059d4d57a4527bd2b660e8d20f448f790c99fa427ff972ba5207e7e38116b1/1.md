**First read.** The eye lands on "Plan the week with your whole team", jumps to the blue "Create account" button, then goes back up the form. Within a second it reads as a sign-up page for a team-planning tool. It feels calm, tidy and competent, and also like a hundred other SaaS sign-up pages.

**What it's for.** No brief was given, so I'm assuming this is the sign-up page of a team-planning product. Its job is to get someone from a small team to create a free workspace now.

**Verdict.** The layout is sound and the hierarchy is clear. The form is the weak part: every field is labelled only by placeholder text, so the instructions disappear as soon as someone types. The copy also leaves the one commercial fact, what "free" covers, open to two readings.

**What would move it most**
1. **Placeholders are the only labels on all five fields (defect, convention).** "Enter first name", "name@company.com", "At least 10 characters" and "Company name" vanish on the first keystroke.
   - The password field is the worst case. Its only label is the rule "At least 10 characters", so once someone starts typing, the field no longer says it's a password or what the rule was.
   - People checking their entries before they press the button see five filled boxes with no names on them.
   - NN/g (Sherwin, "Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful") and Nielsen's heuristic 6, recognition rather than recall, both say labels must stay visible.
   - Fix: put a short label above each field ("First name", "Work email", "Password", "Company"). Move the 10-character rule into helper text under the password field that stays visible while typing.
   - In the same pass, say whether "Company name" is required. A free workspace for five people may not have a company, and someone stuck on that field is the one who gives up. Mark it "(optional)" if it is optional; otherwise put an asterisk on each required label and explain it once (Budiu, NN/g).
2. **The offer can be read two ways (defect).** The headline promises "your whole team". The line under it caps the free plan at "up to five people", then says "You can invite the rest of the team later."
   - A reader with a 12-person team can take this two ways: the other seven join free later, or they cost money. This is the line a buyer later holds you to, and if they guess wrong they feel tricked at the moment they invite people.
   - Fix: say plainly what happens at person six, for example "Free for up to 5 people. Add more on a paid plan whenever you're ready." The other option is to drop "whole" from the headline so the two lines stop arguing.
   - Taste: the button repeats the card heading ("Create your account" / "Create account"). Make the button carry the offer instead, such as "Start your free workspace", so the click confirms what the viewer is getting.

**Smaller things.**
- The input borders look pale grey-blue on a white card. They are the only thing that marks the fields, and they look below the 3:1 that WCAG 1.4.11 asks for. Measure the border colour against white and darken it if it falls short.
- In "Already have an account? Sign in", the link looks the same grey and weight as the rest of the sentence, so nothing shows it's clickable (WCAG G183/F73). Underline it or use the brand blue.
- The "Tallyboard" wordmark (baseline about y 151) and the "Create your account" heading (about y 148) are a few pixels apart across the two columns. They look as if they were meant to line up and missed. Align them exactly, or offset them clearly.

**What's working.**
- The hierarchy is clean, with one heavy headline, one quiet paragraph and one saturated button. The eye has a clear start point and a clear destination, so keep the blue reserved for the wordmark and the button.
- The body text is easy to read: a plain sans at about 65 characters per line with comfortable leading. Keep it.
- Putting first and last name side by side is the right exception to the one-column form. Leave the rest stacked as it is.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Focus rings on the fields and the button.
- Whether the password field shows the 10-character rule or an error when the rule isn't met.
- How the two-column layout stacks on a phone.
