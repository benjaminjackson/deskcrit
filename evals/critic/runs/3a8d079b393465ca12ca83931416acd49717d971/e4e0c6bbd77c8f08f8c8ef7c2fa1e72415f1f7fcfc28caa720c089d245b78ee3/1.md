**First read.** The eye lands on the big serif headline, "The finest bread in the valley", then drops to the one word "valley" alone on the second line. It goes up to the small brown "HARTLEY'S BAKERY · SINCE 1962" and then to the two lines of body copy. After that it stops: the lower third and the right side of the screen are empty, and there is nothing to do. It feels warm, calm and old-fashioned in a good way, but also unfinished, like a hero section whose image and button never loaded.

**What it's for.** My assumption: this is the top of a local bakery's homepage, for nearby people deciding whether to visit or order. It needs to make the bread sound worth the trip and tell them how to get it.

**Verdict.** The type and palette are well made, but the screen doesn't do its job. It makes a claim any bakery could make and then leaves the visitor with nothing to act on. The biggest problem is that there is no next step: no hours, no way to order, no directions. The most specific and convincing facts are hidden in the smallest text.

**What would move it most**
1. **The page is a dead end.** After the body copy the eye has nowhere to go. There's no button, no opening hours, no full address and no "order for collection". The empty lower third makes it worse: everything sits in the top half with nothing below to pull the eye down. So the page feels top-heavy and as if it ends early (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). A local bakery visitor's real questions are "is it open now, where exactly, can I reserve a loaf?", and this screen answers none of them. **Defect.** Fix: put one specific ask in that empty lower area that names one exact action, such as "Reserve tomorrow's sourdough, collect from 7am" or "Open today 7–2 · 14 Mill Street". Give it enough weight to be the clear second stop after the headline.
2. **The headline could belong to any bakery, and the good material is buried.** "The finest bread in the valley" is a claim the visitor has to take on trust, and every rival can make it too. The specific, believable facts are in the 18 px body line and the brown eyebrow: wood-fired oven, baked every morning, Mill Street, since 1962. Those are what tell this bakery apart. **Taste, argued:** a specific claim the visitor can picture is more convincing than a superlative they're asked to believe. Lead with one of them, for example "Wood-fired on Mill Street every morning since 1962", or give the superlative something to measure against. There's also no picture of the bread. For a bakery, a crust photo does a lot of the persuading this text is trying to do. Pair it with the headline so each adds something the other doesn't, rather than captioning a loaf with "bread".
3. **The headline break leaves "valley" alone.** Line one fills nearly the whole width and line two is a single short word, so the largest thing on the screen reads as one long line plus a stub. It also splits "the valley" across lines. **Convention** (Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* 2.4.2, on a short word alone on a last line, applied here to a heading). Fix: break on the phrase ("The finest bread / in the valley"), or size and set the width so the two lines are closer in length.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline spacing: at this size the word spaces look wide ("bread in the"). Tighten the word spacing a little for display sizes (Bringhurst 2.1.1; Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*, p. 34).
- The apostrophe in "HARTLEY'S" looks like a straight typewriter mark. Use a curly ’. This is common sense; the reference gives no checked source for it.

**What's working.**
- The type pairing is clear. A classical serif in a large size carries the heritage, and a plain sans at a comfortable size carries the facts. There are three distinct sizes (eyebrow, headline, body), each with an obvious job. Keep this system when you add the ask and the practical details.
- The body copy runs about 60 characters a line with relaxed line spacing, so it's easy to read. Keep that width when it changes.
- The palette is restrained: a cream background, near-black text and one warm brown kept for the eyebrow. It feels like a bakery without any cliché. The brown is a good colour for the action you add, as long as nothing else competes with it.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether a hero image or a button is meant to fill the empty lower area and didn't load in this capture. Check in a live browser. If it's there, finding 1 shrinks to whether that button names a specific action.
