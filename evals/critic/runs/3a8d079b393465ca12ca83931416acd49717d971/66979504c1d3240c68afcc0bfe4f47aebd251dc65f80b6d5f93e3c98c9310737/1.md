**First read.** The eye goes straight to the big black headline, drops to the dark green button, then jumps right to the small invoice card floating in the pale green field. The three white cards below barely register; they look like empty frames. It reads as an invoicing tool for freelancers and small studios: calm, neat, trustworthy, and very close to a stock SaaS template.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about the piece, so I'm assuming it's the top of a marketing landing page for freelancers. Its job is to get them to start a trial in the first screen.

**Verdict.** The top of the page works. It has one clear focal point, a vivid promise and a single clear action. The main weakness is that nothing on the page shows the promise. The only picture is a small, finished invoice that says "invoice" again instead of showing the speed, the auto-fill or the chasing. Below the hero, three half-empty cards make the page look unfinished.

**What would move it most**

1. **The invoice card doesn't back up the headline.** The headline promises speed: "the time it takes to pour coffee". The subhead promises that clients, rates and tax get filled in for you and that late payments get chased. The card on the right (about 340 × 200 px, roughly a sixth of the hero's width) shows neither. It's a static, completed invoice, so it repeats the word "invoice" and leaves the speed claim to be taken on trust. The viewer also gets no first-hand look at the one thing that sets this product apart. Its small size and the wide empty band between it and the text column make it feel tacked on rather than like the proof. *Taste* (the source behind this rule isn't on the approved list), but it decides whether the hero persuades or only asserts. Direction: make the visual show the claim working, such as the client and tax lines auto-filling, a "sent" timestamp, or a reminder going out. Then give it enough size to stand as the hero's second half.

2. **The feature cards are mostly empty boxes.** In each of the three cards along the bottom, the title and one or two lines of text sit in the top third. Below that is about 130–150 px of blank white inside a border. That space isn't gathered into one open area that gives the eye a rest. It's split into three identical dead zones, so the row looks like images failed to load or the copy is missing (Ruder, *Typography: A Manual of Design*, "Contrasts"; Kunz). *Defect*: it makes the product look unfinished at the very moment you're asking for trust. Direction: fit the cards to their content, or fill them with something that earns the space, like a small view of each feature (a reminder email, a pay-by-card screen, an export file). If this was captured before images loaded, check it in a live browser. Either way the fix is the same.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline: "coffee." sits alone on the third line, and the break "in the / time" splits the phrase. Re-break as "Send an invoice / in the time it takes / to pour coffee." (Bringhurst 2.4.2 on short last-line words; the phrase break is common sense.)
- The button says "Start free for 14 days". That's concrete about the terms but not about what you'd do. "Send your first invoice free" names one exact thing the viewer can do and ties back to the headline's promise. If no card is needed, say so under the button. (Copy, taste.)

**What's working.**
- The left edges align cleanly at 48 px from the logo down through the headline, the button and the card text, and the invoice card's right edge lines up with "Sign in". The page feels ordered without trying. Keep this grid.
- The type scale has a few clear levels: a heavy, tightly set display headline, a grey subhead, bold card titles and smaller grey body text. There's one primary action, and the headline dominates without a fight.
- The invoice details hold up: the figures are right-aligned, the total adds up correctly ($640 + $45 + 8% tax = $739.80), and "Harbor Print Co. · 200 units" gives it a real-world feel. If you rework the visual, keep that level of detail.

- Whether the empty card bottoms are reserved space for images or animations that hadn't loaded when the screenshot was taken.
