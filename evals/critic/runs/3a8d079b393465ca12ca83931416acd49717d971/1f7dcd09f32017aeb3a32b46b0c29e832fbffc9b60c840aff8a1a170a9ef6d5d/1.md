**First read.** The eye lands on the serif headline "Savings deposits rose every month", drops to the tall June bar, then moves right to "$4.82M". It reads as a credit union's half-year report to its members. It feels calm, orderly and trustworthy, if a little like a stock dashboard template.

**What it's for.** My assumption: a public page telling members, at a glance and honestly, how savings deposits did in the first half of 2026.

**Verdict.** It's clean and well built, but the chart's story doesn't match the page's own numbers. The bars show deposits nearly doubling from January to June. The cards say the half-year was up only 6.1% on last year, and a footnote says June was inflated by a one-off bonus payroll. A member who trusts the picture comes away with the wrong idea, and on a credit union's report that is the costliest mistake it can make.

**What would move it most**
1. **The chart shows a seasonal pattern as if it were growth** (defect; common sense, since no approved source covers it). Six bars from January to June climb steadily, and the headline says "rose every month." But the top card says the half-year total is up just 6.1% on January to June 2025. So most of the climb is probably what happens every year, not something new. The viewer can't tell, because last year isn't on the chart. The fix: put last year's January to June next to this year's, as pale grey paired bars or a grey line. Then the honest comparison sits in the picture, not only in a card. If the climb really is new, the chart will show it. If not, the headline should claim what is true, such as "Deposits up 6% on last year."
2. **June's jump has no label where it happens** (defect; common sense). The June bar, $1,150k against $868k in May, is the strongest shape on the page. The only explanation, "June includes the mid-year bonus payroll", is in small grey type under the panel, which most people won't read. Two of the three cards ("Average deposit, Jun 2026" and "Accounts that made a deposit, Jun 2026") also use that one unusual month, while the first card covers the whole half-year. So the cards quietly mix periods. The fix: add a short note at the June bar ("includes one-off bonus payroll"), or shade June differently. Then give all three cards the same period, either January to June or a typical month.
3. **The comparison lines need a second read** (defect for the copy; Nielsen, heuristic 2: use the audience's own words). "Up $11 on Jun 2025" and "Up 402 on Jun 2025" can be read as "up on the date June 2025" rather than "compared with June 2025". "Up 402" also has no unit. These are the figures members remember and will hold the credit union to. The fix: say it outright, e.g. "$11 more than June 2025" and "402 more accounts than June 2025."

**Smaller things.**
- Type scale: I count about four sizes between 13 and 16 px (eyebrow, axis labels, card labels and changes, nav, subtitle, chart title). They don't read as separate levels (Bringhurst, 3.1.1). Settle on two small sizes and use weight and grey to separate the rest.
- Mixed units: the bars and axis say "$1150k" and "$1200k", while the card says "$4.82M". Write $1.15M and $1.2M so the June bar and the total can be compared directly (Tufte; Few).

**What's working.**
- The headline states a finding rather than a topic, and the subtitle says exactly what is being counted ("New money paid into member savings accounts, by month"). Keep that habit and point it at a true claim.
- The chart itself is honest and quiet. The bars start at zero, the gridlines are pale, values are labelled on each bar, and there's one colour. Keep it this restrained when you add last year's data.
- Each card gives one number with its comparison and a label that names the period. The structure is right; only the periods and the wording need fixing.
