**First read.** The eye lands on "River ferry timetable", then gets caught in the black grid. Every cell sits in its own 2 px near-black box, so the rules are the darkest and busiest thing on the page, and the times have to be picked out from behind them. It reads as a clean, plain, one-direction ferry timetable. It feels sturdy but closer to a spreadsheet printout than a timetable designed for passengers.

**What it's for.** Assumption: a passenger, on screen or on a printed notice at the landing, wants to find the next sailing from North landing and when it reaches market quay on the day they're travelling.

**Verdict.** It mostly works. The content is right, the type is calm and legible, and the single title leads clearly. What holds it back is the heavy cell-by-cell ruling, which caps the times and splits each sailing into four boxes, plus a few copy gaps a stranger would trip on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The black cage around every cell.** This is a defect. All 2 px near-black borders, outer frame and every inner horizontal and vertical rule, carry the same weight as the text. The eye notices the grid before the departure times, and the vertical rules cut each row (one sailing) into four separate compartments, when a row should read as one unit: *leaves 12:40, arrives 12:52, weekdays*. The columns are also stretched to fill 1080 px for five-character values, so you track about 250 px of empty cell between a departure and its arrival with nothing to guide you except those rules. Lupton puts it directly: table rules "should illuminate the relationships among data, not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell" (*Thinking with Type*, p. 157), and Tufte's data-ink argument says the same. Fix: drop the vertical rules completely and turn the horizontals into thin pale-grey lines or a light zebra tint. Keep one firmer rule under the header. Then narrow the columns to fit their content, so each row reads as one line.

2. **"Spring season" is a date the viewer acts on, and it doesn't say which dates.** This is a defect (common sense: it's copy, not a catalogue rule). Someone reading this in late May or early June can't tell if it still applies. That's the line people hold the operator to. Fix: give the date range in the subtitle ("1 March – 31 May"), and say what happens outside it, or where the summer timetable is.

3. **The Period column takes the first-read slot and says nothing the times don't.** This is taste. "Morning, Morning, Midday, Midday…" sits at the left edge, where the eye starts each row, so the departure time, the thing people actually scan for, only comes second. It also repeats itself on every pair of rows. Fix: put "Leaves north" first. Then either drop Period, or turn it into a small group label or a bit of extra space between the morning, midday, afternoon and evening blocks, so it does grouping work instead of taking up a column.

**Smaller things.**
- "Runs" makes a weekend rider scan the whole column to find out the 12:40 and 17:10 boats don't sail and the 19:30 only does. A quiet marker on the exceptions (with "Daily" as the default) would let the days that differ stand out. This is taste.
- "Arrives quay" is a clipped heading. "Arrives market quay" matches the subtitle and the landing's real name.

**What's working.**
- One sans family, two sizes plus bold for headers, and a muted grey subtitle. The type hierarchy is clear and quiet, and nobody has to work out how to read the type.
- A table is the right form for looking up exact times, the rows run in time order, and the 24-hour times are consistent and unambiguous. Keep all of that.
- Generous margins and a single left edge shared by title, subtitle and table give it a calm, ordered frame.

**Couldn't judge from a still.**
- Whether the times use tabular figures. They look close to aligned, but the difference is too fine to confirm from this image. Check the `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` setting.
- How the table behaves on a narrow phone screen, where four wide columns may force sideways scrolling.
