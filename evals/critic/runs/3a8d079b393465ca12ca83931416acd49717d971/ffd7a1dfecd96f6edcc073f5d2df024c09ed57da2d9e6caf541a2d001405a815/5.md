**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers" and then moves down three even paragraphs of dark grey text. It reads as a plain, calm info page, close to a well-set document. It is easy to read, but nothing on it pulls ahead as the thing a passenger should take away, and the right half of the frame is empty.

**What it's for.** Assumed: a web page that helps ferry passengers plan a crossing: when boats run, how long a crossing takes, what to do with a bike, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** The typography is clean and does its job, but the page fails at its job: it talks about the timetable without giving one. A passenger who reads every word still can't say when the next boat leaves or when the last one goes.

**What would move it most**
1. **The copy leaves out the facts a passenger acts on (defect).** In paragraph 1, "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" gives no first or last departure and no minute past the hour. In paragraph 3, "one boat each hour" doesn't say at what minute, and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" gives a time that changes every day and that the reader has to guess. Departure times are exactly what people remember and later hold the operator to. As written, "late evening" can be read as 9 pm or 11 pm, and someone who reads it the wrong way misses the last boat. The page then sends people to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which pushes the real information to a place the reader isn't. Fix: state the first and last departures, the minutes past the hour for summer and winter, and the date winter starts. Give the last winter boat as a time, or as a time range by month. If times change, link to the week's changes instead of pointing only to the noticeboard. This is common sense, not a published rule, but it decides whether the page is any use.
2. **Nothing is pulled out for scanning, so the one fact people came for has no home (taste).** Times, bikes, and winter all get the same paragraph treatment. Someone checking times at the quay has to read the whole thing to find the numbers. The paragraphs are short, so this passes the chunking rule (Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web"). Still, once real times are added (finding 1), set them apart where the eye goes first, as a small table or a short labelled list under the heading. Add short subheads ("Bicycles", "Winter") so each topic can be found without reading the rest.

**What's working.**
- **Type that is easy to read.** Body text is about 19 px with lines of roughly 65–75 characters and generous leading, flush left with an even rag. Keep this setting when you add content.
- **A two-level scale that is clear at once.** One bold heading over regular body text, with no near-duplicate sizes. Any subheads you add should sit clearly between the two, set apart by weight rather than by a third size close to either.
- **The voice.** The sentences are plain and complete ("Riders wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded"). Keep that tone when you add the hard facts.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the column behaves at other widths, and whether the empty right half is meant for a sidebar or a map. If it is, that's the natural place for the timetable.
