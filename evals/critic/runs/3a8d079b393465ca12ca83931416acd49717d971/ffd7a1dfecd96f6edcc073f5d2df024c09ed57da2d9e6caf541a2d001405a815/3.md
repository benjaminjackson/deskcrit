**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading "Notes for ferry passengers", then drops into three even grey paragraphs and reads them in order, because nothing else is on the page. It looks like a plain, tidy help or notice page: calm, legible, no branding. Nothing in the text stands out, so the times a passenger needs have to be read for, not found.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web information page for people planning a river-ferry trip. Its job is to let them work out when they can cross, whether they can bring a bike, and what changes in winter.

**Verdict.** The page is well set but doesn't yet do its job. It is written to be read once like a letter, but passengers will come back to it for exact times, and it gives them none. The typesetting is fine; the problem is the content and how it is structured.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times are vague in the places a passenger will act on (defect).** Look at "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" in paragraph 1, "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "in winter" in paragraph 3. A passenger can't tell when the first boat leaves or when the last one does, and whether "before dusk" means 15:30 or 16:45. They also can't tell which months count as winter. These are the lines people plan around, and getting the last boat wrong leaves someone stranded on the wrong bank. The page then sends them to "printed timetables… in the shelter at each landing", which is only useful once they're already standing at the landing. The fix: give clock times for the first and last departures in both seasons, with dates for the winter timetable. If the times really do change with dusk, say how ("last boat 30 minutes before sunset, about 16:00 in December").
2. **The facts are buried in prose, so the page can't be scanned (defect, common sense; Krug's "don't make me think" is the principle).** Frequency, crossing time, first and last boat, the winter change and the bike rule all sit mid-sentence at the same weight. Someone coming back to check "when's the last boat?" has to reread whole paragraphs. Put the timetable facts at the top as a short list or a two-column summer/winter table. Keep the prose below for the bike rules and the "it takes longer against the tide" detail. The heading could also name the route ("North landing ↔ Market quay ferry"). "Notes for ferry passengers" could be about any ferry, and it doesn't tell a visitor from a search result that they're in the right place.

**Smaller things.**
- Balance: everything sits in the left 60% of the 1280 px frame, and the right half and lower third are empty, so the page looks unfinished rather than intended. The reading measure is right, so keep it, but either centre the column or use the right side for the timetable summary from finding 2 (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance").
- Heading spacing: the heading looks set at the same letter spacing as the body text, and at that size it looks slightly loose. Tighten the tracking a little (Bringhurst 2.1.1; Lupton warns against overdoing it).
- Missing fact: bicycles "at no extra charge" implies there is a fare, but the passenger fare isn't stated anywhere.

**What's working.**
- The body text is set very well: comfortable size, dark text on white, lines of about 60–70 characters, generous line spacing, and clear space between paragraphs. Keep this setting for the prose that stays.
- Two type levels separated by size and weight, with nothing decorative, suit a public-service notice. Don't add ornament when you restructure it.
- The bike paragraph is good copy: it says who does what and in what order ("wheel them on after foot passengers have boarded, and lock them to the rail"). Write the timetable lines to that standard.
