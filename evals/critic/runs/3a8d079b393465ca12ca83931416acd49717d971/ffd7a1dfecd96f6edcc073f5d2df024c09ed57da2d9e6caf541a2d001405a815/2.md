**First read.** The eye lands on the bold "Notes for ferry passengers" at top left and drops straight into three even paragraphs of body text. It reads as a plain information page for a small local river ferry. It feels calm, tidy and trustworthy, but also like a page that is still waiting for the rest of its content: everything sits in the top-left, and the right half and bottom third are blank.

**What it's for.** You didn't tell me, so I'm assuming it's a web page that helps passengers plan a crossing: when the boat runs, how long it takes, and the rules for bikes and for winter.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read, but the page doesn't do its job. A passenger comes for times, and the page gives them prose with no actual times in it. The biggest problem is that the facts people act on are buried in sentences and left vague.

**What would move it most**
1. **The facts a passenger acts on are vague, so nobody can plan from this page** (defect). "Between early morning and late evening", "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" and "every forty minutes" give no clock times. "In winter" gives no dates. A passenger reading this at 6 pm in November can't tell whether a boat is still coming. "Before dusk" can be read two ways: the last boat leaves while it's still light, or it leaves at whatever time dusk falls that day, which shifts by weeks. If they guess wrong, someone is stranded on the wrong bank. The page then sends them to the printed timetables "in the shelter at each landing", which means they have to go to the river to learn what the page should have told them. The fix is to give the first and last departure as clock times, the winter dates, and the actual last winter sailing, or else a link to the timetable. Without those, this page can't do its job.
2. **What a passenger needs is spread through running prose instead of set out to scan** (defect, common sense). Interval, crossing time, bikes free, boarding order and the winter change are each tucked mid-sentence. "No extra charge", the line people remember and hold the operator to, sits in the middle of paragraph two. Nielsen's research on how people read the web says they scan, and here there is nothing to scan for. The page already has the space for this: put a short block of facts in the empty right column or straight under the heading (departs every 40 min, crossing about 12 min, first/last boat, bikes free on the lower deck, winter hourly from [date]), and keep the paragraphs for the conditions behind them.

**Smaller things.**
- Balance: all the weight sits in one column pinned at 120 px from the left of a 1280 px frame, with nothing on the right to balance it (Arnheim), so the page looks lopsided and half-loaded. Either use the right side for the facts block above, or centre the reading column.
- Copy: "the noticeboard there lists any changes for the coming week" leaves an online reader with nowhere online to check for changes. If the page can't show changes itself, it should say where online they appear.

**What's working.**
- The body text is set well. It looks like about 18 px Inter, the longest lines run about 70 characters, the line spacing is generous, the right edge is ragged, and paragraphs are marked by space alone. Keep this setting when you add the facts block.
- There's a clear two-level hierarchy. One bold heading over one body style, in near-black on white, with no extra sizes or colours competing.
- The tone is calm and local ("a little longer when the tide turns against the boat"). It sounds like someone who runs the boat wrote it, so keep that voice in the paragraphs.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the page reflows on a phone, which is where most passengers will open it, standing on a landing.
