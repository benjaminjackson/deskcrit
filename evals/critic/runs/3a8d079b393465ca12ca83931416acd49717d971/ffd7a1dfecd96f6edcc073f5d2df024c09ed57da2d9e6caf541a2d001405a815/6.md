**First read.** The eye lands on the bold heading, "Notes for ferry passengers", and drops straight into the first paragraph. Nothing competes with it. It reads as a plain information page for a small local river ferry: calm, clean and trustworthy. It also reads like a document, so you start reading prose before you find what you came for.

**What it's for.** I'm assuming this is a web page passengers check before travelling, to learn when the boat runs, how long the crossing takes and whether they can bring a bike.

**Verdict.** The typography is sound and I found no defects in the setting. The weak part is the copy. A passenger comes here with one question, "when is the next boat?", and the page never gives a time they can act on.

**What would move it most**
1. **The timetable has no times (defect, in the copy).** Look at "every forty minutes between early morning and late evening" in paragraph 1 and "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk" in paragraph 3. A passenger can't tell from these when the first boat leaves, when the last one goes, or whether "before dusk" in December means 15:30 or 16:15. Those are the lines people act on and later hold the operator to. When a vague one is read the wrong way, someone gets stranded on the wrong bank. Give the first and last departure from each landing, and the winter dates and last sailing, as clock times. If the times really do vary, say so and send people to the printed timetable in plain words.
2. **The facts people act on are buried in prose (taste, with a basis in Nielsen's work on how people skim screens).** Five facts carry the page: the boat runs every 40 minutes, the crossing takes about 12 minutes, bikes go free, the winter service is hourly, and the last winter boat leaves before dusk. They sit mid-sentence, at the same weight as "in the shelter at each landing". Three short paragraphs aren't a wall of text, so this isn't a broken rule. Still, a passenger checking on their phone at the landing has to read every line to find one number. The usual fix is to pull the schedule out into a small set of times or a short list near the top and keep the prose for the bicycle and noticeboard detail. A subhead per topic ("Times", "Bicycles", "Winter") would also do it.

**What's working.**
- The type scale has two levels, a bold heading and a regular body, with a big step between them. There's no doubt where to start.
- The text column is set about 70 characters wide, with generous line spacing and space-only paragraph breaks. It reads comfortably. Keep these settings when you add structure.
- The plain, quiet tone suits a local public service and builds trust.
- Ending on where to find updates (the noticeboard, listing changes for the coming week) is useful. Keep it.
