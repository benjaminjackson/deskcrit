**First read.** The eye lands on the bold headline, drops straight into the first paragraph and reads down. Nothing competes for attention. It looks like a short, calm information page for people using a local river ferry, and it feels quiet, trustworthy and a bit bare. Once you've read it, you still can't say when the next boat leaves.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing, so I'm assuming this is a web page for people about to take the ferry who want to know when it runs, how long it takes and whether they can bring a bike.

**Verdict.** The typesetting is clean and easy to read, and there's no layout defect worth ranking. The problem is the words. The page talks about the timetable without giving it: there's not one clock time, so a passenger can't plan a trip from it.

**What would move it most**
1. **The times a passenger acts on are vague (defect, copy).** The first and third paragraphs say the boat runs "between early morning and late evening", that winter service is "one boat each hour", and that "the last crossing leaves the quay before dusk." Then the page sends the reader to "printed timetables… kept in the shelter at each landing." Someone reading at home wants exactly two things: the first boat and the last boat. They get neither. "Before dusk" can mean 4:15 pm or 4:55 pm in December depending on the reader, and the wrong guess leaves them stranded on the far bank. "Every forty minutes" doesn't help either without a starting time. Times are what people remember and later hold the operator to, so vagueness here is a defect, not a matter of style. Fix: give the first and last departures from each landing, for summer and for winter, in clock times. If the times change through the season, give them by month, or give the rule plus one example ("last boat 4:30 pm in December").

2. **The facts are buried in prose (defect, copy and structure).** The paragraphs are short and well set, but each one opens with scene-setting ("The river ferry runs from…", "In winter the timetable thins…"). Someone checking whether bikes are allowed or when the last winter boat goes has to read whole sentences to find the answer. Krug's point applies: a page like this is used by scanning, not reading. Fix: start each paragraph with the fact itself ("Boats leave every 40 minutes, 7:00 am to 9:20 pm.", "Bikes travel free.", "Winter: hourly, last boat 4:30 pm."). An alternative is to give each paragraph a short subhead (Times, Bicycles, Winter). Keep the explanation that follows each fact as it is.

**Smaller things.**
- The headline "Notes for ferry passengers" could sit on any ferry's page. Name the route ("North landing ↔ Market quay ferry") so a reader landing from search knows they've found the right boat.
- The noticeboard line points to changes "for the coming week" that you can only see at the landing. If there's a web version of the notices, link it here, because this page is where people check before they leave home.

**What's working.**
- The text setting is right for a page people read. Body text is about 19 px in a plain sans with roughly 1.5 line spacing, lines run about 65–72 characters, paragraphs are separated by space only and set flush left, and the rag is even. Keep all of this when you restructure the copy.
- There are only two type sizes, and the headline is set apart by both size and a heavy weight, so the hierarchy is obvious with no extra decoration. If you add subheads, make them body size in bold rather than adding a third size.
- The palette is restrained: dark grey on white and nothing else. That suits a public-service notice. Don't add colour unless it carries meaning, such as a "service changed" warning.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** How the left-aligned column behaves on a phone and on very wide screens. At 1280 px the right ~40% of the page is empty, which is fine for a reading column but worth checking at other widths.
