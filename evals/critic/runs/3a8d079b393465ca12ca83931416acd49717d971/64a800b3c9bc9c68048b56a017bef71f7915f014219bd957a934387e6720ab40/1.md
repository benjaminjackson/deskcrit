**First read.** The eye goes to the masthead, "The Wexley Gazette", then drops onto the three story headlines and moves left to right across them with nothing to hold it. It looks like the front of a small local news site. It feels calm and well-mannered, and so quiet that it could be filler.

**What it's for.** You told me nothing about it, so I'm assuming it's the front page of a local news site or newsletter, for residents scanning what has changed in town.

**Verdict.** It's cleanly set and readable, with almost no craft faults. What's missing is any sense of news. Three stories of equal weight and an undated masthead mean the reader can't tell what matters most today, or when "today" is.

**What would move it most**
1. **No lead story** (convention). Below the masthead, the three headlines are the same size, weight and colour, with blocks of almost the same length. So the eye checks all three, and none of them says "this is the news." This is the case Tschichold describes in *The New Typography*: order of importance should show through differences of size. Here it doesn't. For a front page, the cost is that a reader who reads only one headline won't read the one the editors would have picked. The fix: make one story clearly first by giving it a bigger headline and a wider column, or a full-width slot above the other two. The bridge reopening is the obvious choice, since it changes how people get around town. Then set the other two back as briefs.
2. **The dates can't be pinned down** (defect). The masthead has no issue date. The copy relies on relative timing: the market moves "from next month", the bridge opens "in April" to cars and "from March" for walkers and cyclists. A reader who lands on this later, or from a link, can't tell which month "next month" means or which April this is. These are exactly the details people act on and later quote back. Put a dateline under the masthead, and give the market move an actual date ("from 1 December") instead of "next month".

**Smaller things.**
- The double rule and the centred masthead are newspaper conventions and they work. There's nothing to change there.
- The bottom 45% of the frame is empty. On a web page that's normal for this much content, but if it's meant to be a full page, more stories or a fuller lead would fill it.

**What's working.**
- The type is under control. I count three sizes (masthead, headlines, body) with clear steps between them, all in one serif family. The headlines are bold and the body is regular, so each level has an obvious job.
- The three columns share one grid. Their gutters are equal and the body baselines line up across all three. Lines run roughly 38–44 characters, at or just inside Bringhurst's 40–50 for text set in several columns, and the leading suits that width.
- The copy is concrete and local: "sixty stalls, a small cafe, and a stage", "two of the five arches". It reads like a real paper, not a template. Keep that voice.
