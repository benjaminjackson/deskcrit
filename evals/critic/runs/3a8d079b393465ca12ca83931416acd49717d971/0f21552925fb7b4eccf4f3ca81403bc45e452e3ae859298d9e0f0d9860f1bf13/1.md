**First read.** The eye lands on the big headline, reads "Plan the whole trip in one place", drops to the grey subhead, then reaches two buttons, "Get started" and "Start now". It stops there to work out how they differ, and they don't. After that there is nothing left to look at: the whole right half and the bottom third are empty cream. It reads as a calm, competent, rather anonymous travel-planning start-up landing page.

**What it's for.** My assumption: the hero of a marketing home page for a group trip-planning app, aimed at people organising a trip with friends or family, with the job of getting them to sign up.

**Verdict.** The type and colour are well made, but the hero doesn't do its job yet. It offers the same action twice and shows nothing of the product, so the visitor is asked to commit before seeing anything worth committing to.

**What would move it most**
1. **Two buttons for the same action** (defect). "Get started" (filled) and "Start now" (outline) under the subhead are synonyms. The visitor reads the second button as a different option, stops to tell them apart, finds no difference, and then doubts the page. The duplicate also weakens the real call to action (Nielsen heuristic 8; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*). Remove the twin, or give the second button a genuinely different job a hesitant visitor would want, such as "See how it works" or "See pricing". The styling is already right: one filled button, one outline.
2. **Half the screen is empty and nothing shows the product** (defect). Everything sits in the top-left quadrant, and the right half (roughly x 720–1280) carries no weight at all, so the page tips left and looks unfinished (Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, "Balance"). It also costs the pitch. "Flights, stays, and day plans together" is a claim the visitor has to take on trust, when one picture could prove it: a shared itinerary with three friends' votes on a stay, for example. If an image was meant to load here, check the live page. If not, that space is where the product should be shown. That second point is my judgment, not a published rule.
3. **The ask doesn't name anything the visitor can do** (defect, in the copy). "Get started" could be on anyone's site. The subhead has a sharp, specific pain, "without a dozen chat threads", but the button doesn't follow through on it (WCAG 2.4.4 asks that a link's purpose be clear from its text). Name the concrete first step from the visitor's own world, such as "Start a trip with your group" or "Invite your group". Better still, set the product against what they use now: a line or picture showing the group chat versus one Wayfold plan lets them judge the value on terms they already accept.
4. **The headline is generic** (taste). "Plan the whole trip in one place" fits any travel or planning tool. The thing that makes this product different, *group* agreement, is buried in the subhead. Move the group idea into the headline so a stranger can tell in five words why this isn't just another itinerary app.

**Smaller things.**
- Headline break: "in / one place" splits the phrase. Break after "trip" ("Plan the whole trip / in one place") so each line reads as a unit. This is common sense: the catalog's source for it is unverified.

**What's working.**
- The type is disciplined: about four sizes with clear jumps (nav/body, subhead, button, a tightly set bold display headline). Each level is obvious at a glance. Keep that scale when you add content.
- The palette is restrained: warm cream, near-black ink, slate grey for the subhead, and one deep green shared by the logo and the primary button. Green already means "the thing to click", so keep it off decoration.
- Logo, headline, subhead, and buttons all share one left edge at x≈80, and the nav ends on the matching right margin. Line length in the subhead is comfortable at about 60 characters.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Whether a hero image or animation was meant to fill the right half and failed to load; check this on the live page. Button hover and focus states.
