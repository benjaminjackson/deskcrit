**First read.** The eye lands first on the dark navy sidebar running down every tile, then on the bold page title at top left, then on the blue button at top right. Seen as a set, the five screens read as one calm, tidy back office for a small business. Every tile has the same frame: the same "Ledgerly" wordmark, the same nav order, the same title and gray subtitle, one blue primary button pinned to the same right edge, and white cards starting at the same left line. It feels plain and trustworthy, and also a bit anonymous.

**What it's for.** Five screens of an invoicing web app for small businesses, judged as a set for consistency of components and alignment.

**Verdict.** As a set, it holds. On all five tiles, every repeated element looks and sits the same: sidebar width and active state, title and subtitle, primary button, card radius and padding, table header style, row rules, and status pills. No tile breaks the pattern. The weaknesses are inside screens and are shared across them: the money columns aren't aligned for comparison, and the Overview figures have no context.

**What would move it most**
1. **Money columns are left-aligned, so the amounts don't line up.** In the Amount column on tile 3 (Invoices) and tile 4 (Overview), and in the Top clients figures on tile 2 (Reports), "$860.00" starts at the same left edge as "$3,400.00". The decimal points and thousands places don't stack, so you can't see at a glance which invoice is biggest. You have to read each one. This is the core comparison in an invoicing tool. *Convention* (Bringhurst, 4.4.3: align columns of figures flush right or on the decimal). Fix: right-align every Amount column and its header, in the same way on every screen, and use tabular figures. Whether they're already tabular is too fine to tell from a screenshot.
2. **The Overview's headline numbers stand alone.** On tile 4, Outstanding $4,525.00, Paid this month $12,870.00 and Overdue $515.00 are the largest type in the product, but none says whether that's good or bad. Tile 2 already shows the right way: "18 days / Down from 23 days last quarter". *Defect*: the user sees the figure but not whether to worry. Fix: give each figure a time period where it lacks one and a comparison, such as the last period or a target, in the same caption style as tile 2 (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design").

**Smaller things.**
- Tile 3: rows INV-0942 to INV-0937 repeat the clients, amounts and Nov/Oct dates of INV-1042 to 1037. It reads as duplicated sample data, and it undercuts "21 invoices this quarter". Use realistic data before showing this. *(Common sense.)*
- Tile 2: the Revenue by month bars show no amounts, and the title only names the chart. Put each month's value at the end of its bar (Tufte, ch. 2), and consider a title that states what the chart shows.
- Tile 3: a long, growing invoice list has no search or status filter (Nielsen, heuristic 7). Even a filter by Paid, Due and Overdue would save a scroll.
- Tile 1: the input borders are a very pale gray on white and look below 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure them, and darken them if they fall short.

**What's working.**
- **One frame, used without exceptions.** The title block, the top-right primary button and the card left edge sit on the same lines on all five tiles. You can move between screens and only the content changes. Keep this when adding the Payments screen.
- **One table component.** Tiles 3, 4 and 5 share the same small-caps header row, hairline row rules and cell padding. Tile 4's "Recent invoices" is the same component as tile 3's full list, not a near copy.
- **Status pills say the status in words.** Paid, Due and Overdue are written in each pill, so the color backs up the meaning instead of carrying it alone.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 2: make the Overdue figure on tile 4 the one number that leads. It's the figure that tells a small-business owner to act, so give it the existing red status color and tie it to the Overdue rows below. *Taste*: an overview for this audience should answer "who do I chase?" first.
2. The sidebar's navy is the product's only strong brand color. Let the "Ledgerly" wordmark carry more of it, with a mark or more weight, so the product feels like a product and not a template. *Taste*: right now it could be anyone's admin panel.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the nav and buttons. Whether the tables scroll inside their cards or with the page (tile 3's last row is cut off at the frame).
