I've checked all five screens and the checklist, and the full critique is below.

**First read.** The eye lands on the dark navy sidebar, then the large page title at top left, then the blue button at top right. It reads at once as a quiet, standard small-business invoicing back office. Flipping through the five tiles, the frame holds still: the same sidebar, the title and subtitle in the same place, the button sitting on the same right edge, and white cards starting on the same left line. Only the content changes. It feels calm, competent and generic.

**What it's for.** As you told me: a set of screens from one invoicing web app for small businesses, judged on whether they are consistent with each other.

**Verdict.** As a set, it holds. Every tile uses the same sidebar, header, card, table, badge and button treatment on the same columns and margins, and no tile breaks the pattern. The weak spots are in the content, not the system: sample data that contradicts itself, and numbers set so they can't be scanned or read in context.

**What would move it most**
1. **The data contradicts itself across screens (defect, common sense).** In tile 3, rows INV-0942 to INV-0937 repeat the clients, dates and amounts of INV-1042 to INV-1037. They also sit in a list otherwise ordered newest-first, under a "Nov 18" date that is older than nothing above it. In tile 5, the subtitle says "6 active clients" and the table lists six, but Maple Street Bakery is missing even though it has an invoice in tiles 3 and 4. Someone reviewing the set will either trust the duplicated rows or start doubting every figure on the screens. Either way the screens stop showing what the product does and start raising questions. Fix: use one consistent data set across every screen, so the invoice list, client list, overview and reports all agree.
2. **Money columns aren't aligned for comparing (convention).** In tiles 3 and 4 the AMOUNT column is left-aligned, so $860.00 and $3,400.00 start on the same edge and their decimal points don't line up. In tile 2, the Top clients figures float mid-card instead of sitting on the right edge. Comparing amounts down a column is the main reading task in an invoicing tool, and here it takes a second look. Bringhurst says columns of figures should sit flush right or align on the decimal. Fix: right-align every money column and its header, with tabular figures, on every screen.
3. **The numbers have no context (convention).** In tile 4, the three summary cards (Outstanding, Paid this month, Overdue) show bare totals with no comparison, so a business owner can't tell whether $515.00 overdue is better or worse than usual. Tile 2 already does this well with "Down from 23 days last quarter." In tile 2, the four revenue bars have no values, so the reader can see October beat November but not by how much or what either earned (Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design"; Tufte on labeling). Fix: give each summary card the same kind of comparison line the average-days card uses, and put the amount at the end of each revenue bar.

**Smaller things.**
- The tile 1 input borders look very pale against white and may fall below 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11). Measure them and darken the border if needed.
- The tile 1 form doesn't say which fields are required. Mark required fields with an asterisk explained once, or label the optional ones "optional" (Budiu, NN/g).

**What's working.**
- **The page frame is truly shared.** On all five tiles, the title, subtitle, button and first card sit at identical positions, and there is one primary action per page. Keep this as a fixed page template that every new screen must use.
- **Tables are one component.** Tiles 3, 4 and 5 use the same table: same row height, header style, cell inset and divider rules. The tile 4 "Recent invoices" table is the same component as the full list in tile 3, not a lookalike.
- **Status badges always carry a word** (Paid, Due, Overdue), not just a color, and they look identical everywhere they appear.

**Where it could go further.**
1. Builds on finding 3: make the tile 2 "Average days to pay" card the model for every number in the app: a figure, its unit, and what it's compared to. For a small-business owner, that turns a page of totals into one that says whether things are getting better. Taste.
2. The blue already used for the buttons and the revenue bars could be the only accent outside the status colors. Every screen already almost does this, and committing to it would keep the eye on the one action per page. Taste.

**Couldn't judge from a still.** Hover and focus states on the sidebar, buttons and table rows, which matter in an app used every day. Also whether tile 3's list scrolls inside its card or the whole page scrolls.
